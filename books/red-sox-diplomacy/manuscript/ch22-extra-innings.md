# Extra Innings

Diplomatic careers end badly on paper.

There is always a final title, a final posting, a final day in office. Chronology supplies a clean edge where life does not.

Nicholas Burns left Beijing in January 2025.

That was an ending.

It was not the end.

A few months later he was back at Harvard, teaching again. He returned with another government behind him, another set of decisions to explain, another relationship whose meaning was still changing after he had left it.

In an April 2025 lecture reflecting on China, Burns reached for a relay race rather than baseball. He had carried the American baton in Beijing for a few years, he said in substance, and now he was handing it to his successor.

The image is better than a final score.

A diplomat inherits a relationship already in motion and leaves it unfinished. The next person receives both.

The U.S.-China relationship did not belong to the ambassador who occupied the residence. NATO did not begin when Burns arrived twelve days before September 11. The India nuclear agreement did not belong to one negotiator. Iran did not resolve itself because an Under Secretary left office. Greece did not stop having an argument with America when the embassy changed occupants.

The office is temporary.

The file is not.

Burns had already left government once. In 2008, after twenty-seven years in the Foreign Service, he moved to Harvard. The move looked like a clean transition from practitioner to teacher until President Joe Biden asked him to return as ambassador to China more than a decade later.

The professor became the representative again.

Then the representative became the professor again.

Careers look more intentional in retrospect than they feel while being lived. What matters is that the returns changed the meaning of the departures.

After China, Burns came back to Harvard with recent memories of crisis, constrained channels, prisoners, military risk and a relationship he described as the most difficult assignment of his career.

He also came back with outcomes small enough to restore scale.

One of the moments he later emphasized was the late-2024 release of Americans who had been detained in China for years. In a bilateral relationship measured in nuclear weapons, technology competition, Taiwan, trade and the balance of power, a handful of individuals can sound numerically trivial.

They were not trivial to their families.

Diplomacy has this double scale. The state scale produces communiqués, deterrence, sanctions, alliances and summits. The human scale produces a person walking out of a prison.

Both are foreign policy.

Burns's career kept moving between them.

That is one reason the obvious temptation at the end of this book has to be resisted.

The temptation is verdict.

Was Nicholas Burns a great diplomat?

Compared with whom? Measured by agreements signed, wars avoided, students taught, allies reassured, adversaries deterred, policies defended, policies regretted?

None of those measures is useless. None survives as a final statistic.

Diplomatic outcomes decay, reverse, compound and reappear. A successful agreement can be abandoned by a later government. A failed negotiation can establish a framework a successor uses. An alliance can look obsolete until the day it becomes indispensable. A judgment that appears defensible inside one moment can look badly wrong twenty years later.

This last point became explicit in Burns's own public record.

In September 2026, twenty-five years after the attacks of September 11, Burns returned publicly to the period when he had been ambassador to NATO. He had supported the 2003 invasion of Iraq at the time. Looking back, he said he regretted that support and believed the decision to invade had been wrong.

The later judgment does not erase the original one.

It should not.

An admission is not an escape hatch from responsibility. It is evidence of a harder professional habit: revisability.

Officials decide with partial information, institutional pressure, political assumptions and the confidence of the moment. History later supplies information they lacked, ignored or weighted badly and asks whether they are willing to revise the story they tell about themselves.

Experience alone does not guarantee that willingness.

Experience can make error more sophisticated.

Harvard gave Burns a place to turn former decisions into material students could question. Return to government exposed the professor's ideas to actual authority. Return to teaching exposed the latest exercise of authority to analysis again.

Practice did not purify reflection. Reflection did not excuse practice.

They corrected each other when they worked.

The same tension runs through patriotism. Burns spent a career representing the United States. Competent representation required loyalty without the assumption of infallibility.

The diplomat who believes the country is always right becomes incapable of reporting inconvenient reality.

The diplomat who believes the country is uniquely corrupt becomes incapable of representing it at all.

The work sits between those comforts.

Baseball can return here once, carefully.

Extra innings begin after the expected ending has passed. The game continues anyway.

For Red Sox fans of Burns's generation, continuation once carried a particular cruelty. Then 2004 changed the emotional grammar of the affiliation. The fandom remained. The premise changed.

Burns's public life is not a baseball game, and history keeps no comparable scoreboard. But his professional sequence has the same refusal to end where the biography wants it to. The Foreign Service career ended in 2008. Teaching followed. Public argument followed. Government returned. China followed. Teaching returned again. In his seventies, Burns was still publicly arguing about alliances, China, intervention and the lessons of September 11.

That is not a victory lap.

It is continued participation.

The distinction matters because participation permits correction.

This book has resisted calling Burns America's greatest diplomat because greatness is too convenient a word. It collapses craft, judgment, luck, institutions, presidents, colleagues, counterparts and history into a personal ranking nobody can actually compute.

The smaller claim is more useful.

Burns's career makes visible what diplomacy asks of a person over time: learn another country without pretending to become native to it; represent your own without confusing loyalty with infallibility; use relationships without imagining they erase interests; know when language must be exact and when ambiguity is useful; accept that some negotiations end without the result you wanted; leave a file another person can pick up; be willing to return years later and say where you were wrong.

And remain a person while carrying an institution large enough to erase one.

The Red Sox cap was never the answer to any of this.

It was evidence.

There was a person under the title.

A person could care irrationally about baseball, joke with reporters, argue with allies, misjudge a war, help negotiate an agreement, teach students, visit detained Americans, return to a classroom and revise what he thought he knew.

Diplomatic institutions need people because institutions have to act before history tells them whether the action was correct.

The people do not make that uncertainty disappear.

They carry it.

Then they hand it off.

Somewhere another ambassador opens the file.

Another class begins.

Another government sends another message that must be interpreted by a person on the other side.

The inning was never extra.

It was only next.
