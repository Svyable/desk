# Iran: When Patience Does Not Win

India could make patience look like the trick. Years of argument crossed one jurisdiction after another and eventually produced an agreement.

Iran was the dangerous correction.

Nicholas Burns was working the Iranian nuclear file at almost the same time, with many of the same diplomatic tools—coalitions, incentives, pressure, technical language, repeated negotiation—and with a very different result. The difference was not that India had been reasonable and Iran was not. The histories, domestic politics, nuclear status and bargains available were different.

Burns became Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs in 2005. President George W. Bush set policy; Condoleezza Rice was Secretary of State. The White House, National Security Council, Treasury Department, intelligence agencies and Congress all had parts of the file. Abroad, Britain, France and Germany had already built a negotiating track with Iran. Russia and China had Security Council votes and interests of their own. The International Atomic Energy Agency had the inspection and safeguards role. Iran had the nuclear program and the decision about what terms it would accept.

The geometry discouraged hero stories.

Britain, France and Germany—the EU3—had negotiated with Iran before Washington was prepared to join them directly. Their talks produced temporary suspension arrangements but no durable settlement. Iran insisted on its right to peaceful nuclear technology and enrichment. The Europeans sought confidence that the program would not move toward a weapon. Washington brought its own history with the Islamic Republic, its own suspicions, and a reluctance to enter talks on terms that appeared to legitimize enrichment before the dispute had been resolved.

Burns entered a negotiation in which the United States was powerful but not yet seated at the table it was trying to shape. Washington could pressure Iran, support European diplomacy, work through the IAEA and assemble a Security Council coalition. But every step toward an international strategy also meant negotiating American policy with the governments needed to enforce it. Russia did not become American because it shared concern about Iran's nuclear program. China retained commercial and strategic interests. Britain, France and Germany retained their own judgments about timing and escalation.

In May 2006, Washington changed its position. The United States said it was prepared to join negotiations with Iran alongside the Europeans if Iran verifiably suspended uranium enrichment and reprocessing activities.

The condition contained the dispute. Washington treated suspension as a test: Iran should not keep advancing the contested activity while negotiating over whether, and under what conditions, it would be permitted. Tehran treated the demand as a concession on the central issue before substantive negotiation had begun.

The gap is unusually clear in the diplomatic record. At the Security Council in July 2006, Iran's representative argued that its earlier suspension had been voluntary and that making suspension mandatory upset the balance of rights and obligations Iran claimed under the Non-Proliferation Treaty. Six weeks later, Secretary-General Kofi Annan said Iranian leaders had told him they would not suspend enrichment as a precondition to negotiations but were prepared to put suspension on the negotiating agenda.

The American offer mattered. Washington was publicly saying that direct participation in a negotiated nuclear settlement was possible. But it was not offering unconditional bilateral talks, and it was not accepting Iranian enrichment as a settled fact. Iran did not accept the threshold on the required terms.

The dispute moved increasingly through Vienna and New York. The IAEA supplied technical findings and safeguards reporting. The Security Council could do something different: issue demands with legal force and impose multilateral sanctions.

Resolution 1696 demanded suspension in July 2006. When Iran did not comply, Resolution 1737 imposed sanctions in December. Resolution 1747 expanded them in 2007; Resolution 1803 added further measures in March 2008. The sequence looked like accumulating pressure because it was. What it did not supply was the political choice Washington wanted from Tehran.

The December 2006 Council record makes the coalition's limits visible. Russia and China voted for sanctions while continuing to describe negotiation as the route out, and the sanctions themselves were structured to be suspended if Iran suspended enrichment and reprocessing and returned to diplomacy. Six governments could agree on a decision without acquiring the same motives.

Iran continued enriching uranium. Its officials continued to describe peaceful nuclear technology as a right under the Non-Proliferation Treaty. International concern did not rest only on distrust in the abstract; the IAEA safeguards record contained unresolved questions that had damaged confidence in the peaceful character of the program. But technical findings could not settle the politics. Inspectors could describe material, facilities, declarations and compliance. They could not decide what national humiliation meant in Tehran or what proliferation risk governments in Washington, Paris, London, Berlin, Moscow and Beijing were willing to accept.

Here the contrast with India becomes useful. Washington had been trying to create an exception to the existing nuclear order for India, a country outside the Non-Proliferation Treaty. With Iran, the coalition was trying to enforce and interpret an existing nonproliferation structure against a treaty member whose nuclear history had generated serious safeguards concerns. The India bargain required governments to decide an exception was worth making. Iran required governments that disagreed among themselves to keep deciding what compliance, confidence and acceptable nuclear capability meant.

Burns could work Russia, China and the Europeans. He could help shape packages of incentives and pressure. He could not make Iran suspend enrichment.

By the time he left government in 2008, no comprehensive agreement existed. Iran had not accepted the suspension condition. The United States had not entered the full nuclear negotiation envisioned by its 2006 offer. Enrichment continued, the Security Council had imposed multiple rounds of sanctions, and the possibility of military conflict remained.

The file was not where Burns had found it in 2005. Washington had publicly accepted that it could participate in a negotiated nuclear settlement under specified conditions. Britain, France, Germany, Russia, China and the United States had a functioning negotiating framework. The Security Council had built an international pressure architecture; the IAEA remained the technical institution around which verification arguments turned. Incentives and sanctions now occupied the same strategy. None of that was the bargain.

Years later, a different American administration entered direct secret diplomacy with Iran, and the P5+1 eventually negotiated the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action. Drawing a clean line from the Burns years to 2015 would make the earlier diplomacy tidier than it was. Different presidents made different choices. Iranian politics changed. American policy changed. The sanctions environment changed. Direct U.S.-Iranian diplomacy went further than the conditional posture Burns had defended.

Burns helped leave behind machinery later diplomats could use. He did not negotiate their agreement.

This is one of diplomacy's less cinematic facts. Elections intervene. Governments fall. Ministers change. Intelligence changes. Wars begin. Coalitions fray. Another official inherits the folder. A career spent inside those sequences cannot sensibly be counted only in signing ceremonies.

Baseball has little useful to say here. Patience is not waiting for a pitch, sanctions are not innings, and nuclear capability is not a scoreboard. The analogy would shrink the subject. The Red Sox fan recedes because charm, relationships and persistence cannot substitute for another government's political choice.

India had offered one version of the long game: years of work crossed jurisdictions and eventually produced an agreement whose final ceremony Burns did not attend as a government official. Iran left him with pressure, institutions, clearer choices—and the file still open.
