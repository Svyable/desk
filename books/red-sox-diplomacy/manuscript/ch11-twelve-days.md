# Twelve Days

Nicholas Burns had been the United States ambassador to NATO for twelve days when a Belgian employee came through a side door at lunch and told him that an airplane had hit the World Trade Center.

Burns's first thought was weather.

A small plane. Fog. An accident.

That was still a world in which the explanation might be ordinary.

Then the second plane hit.

The explanation changed before the job did.

Burns was sitting among the ambassadors of an alliance built during the Cold War around a simple promise: if Europe were attacked, the United States would come.

For fifty-two years, that promise had been the structural center of NATO.

American presidents had made it. American troops had embodied it. American strategy had assumed it. Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty reduced the commitment to one sentence of extraordinary consequence: an armed attack against one would be considered an attack against them all.

The scenario generations of planners had imagined was east-to-west.

Soviet armor through Europe.

An attack on an ally.

American reinforcement across the Atlantic.

On September 11, 2001, the geometry reversed.

The United States had been attacked.

Burns had barely unpacked the job.

Twelve days is not enough time to become the institution.

It is enough time to have the title.

That distinction became important almost immediately.

The American ambassador to NATO was supposed to carry Washington into the alliance. He was supposed to know what his government knew, what his president wanted, what his secretary of state intended, what the United States could commit to, and where the lines were.

Instead, Washington began disappearing from reach.

The White House was evacuated. The State Department was evacuated. The Pentagon had been hit.

Burns and his team tried to call home.

For a period, they could not get through.

The representative still existed.

The center he represented had gone quiet.

Then the phones began ringing from the other direction.

Canadian ambassador David Wright called.

Burns would remember him for it years later.

The suggestion was immediate and serious: had the United States considered Article 5?

Other allies called too.

The exact order shifts slightly across Burns's later retellings. The important point does not.

Allied governments and ambassadors were not waiting passively to see what favor Washington might ask.

They were offering solidarity before the American representative could fully reconnect with his own capital.

That must be stated carefully.

Solidarity is not yet policy.

An ambassador saying, *we are with you*, is not the same thing as a parliament voting, a cabinet instructing, a military deploying, or the North Atlantic Council taking a formal treaty decision.

Several NATO ambassadors did not yet have final instructions from their capitals that night.

Denmark, Burns later recalled, needed parliamentary consultation.

The alliance had not magically become one nervous system.

But the direction was becoming visible.

That afternoon, Burns's job changed faster than his briefing book could.

He was no longer simply the newest American permanent representative to an organization whose internal rituals predated his arrival by decades.

He had become the person in Brussels through whom allies were trying to tell the United States something before the United States had fully decided what it needed to hear.

This is the part of diplomacy that is easy to miss when one reads only finished statements.

Finished statements make governments look synchronized.

They remove ringing phones.

They remove unanswered calls.

They remove the interval between an ambassador hearing one government's instinct and another government's authorization.

They remove the fact that, for several hours on September 11, Burns was working in that interval.

He had authority without complete information.

He had standing without full instructions.

He had allies speaking to him before he could reliably speak for Washington.

The instinct in a moment like that can be to fill the silence.

Diplomatic professionalism often requires the opposite.

Listen first.

Know what has actually been offered.

Know what has not.

Do not turn sympathy into a commitment before a government has made the commitment.

Do not turn confusion into certainty because your title suggests you should already know.

Burns had spent years in jobs that trained those reflexes.

Cairo had taught him what it meant to work near power without possessing it.

Jerusalem had taught him to function inside institutional ambiguity.

At the National Security Council, he had worked on problems where public headlines concealed years of implementation.

At the State Department podium, he had learned that an official voice becomes dangerous when it blurs the line between what is known and what is merely assumed.

None of those experiences prepared anyone for September 11.

They did prepare a diplomat for not pretending certainty where certainty did not exist.

That night, the nineteen NATO ambassadors met.

Secretary General George Robertson chaired.

Burns was asked to speak first about the attack on his country.

The casualty numbers were still unstable and terrible.

The full structure of the plot was unknown.

No one in the room could know yet how much of the next two decades had already begun.

Burns reported what he could.

Then the question moved around the table.

Would the allies support invoking Article 5?

Most answered yes.

Some were still waiting for instructions.

The symbolism of the moment was enormous, but its mechanism was procedural.

An alliance is not a feeling.

It is a system for converting political will into shared action.

The evening of September 11 supplied the first part.

The second would require capitals, instructions, lawyers, military planners, presidents, ministers, and another morning.

Burns's later retellings emphasize the offers of solidarity because that is what stayed with him.

The memory is understandable.

He had entered NATO as the representative of its strongest member.

Twelve days later, he encountered the alliance from the opposite direction.

America had spent half a century promising not to leave Europe alone.

Now Europe and Canada were telling America it would not be left alone.

The inversion mattered more than sentiment.

It revealed something that could be obscured during peacetime.

Power and alliance are not the same thing.

The United States was stronger than any of its NATO allies individually. It had more military capability, more global reach, and more freedom of action.

On September 11, none of that made the country less attacked.

None of it made a ringing phone from an ally meaningless.

This would become one of Burns's durable arguments about American power: strength without allies is not the maximum version of strength.

But that argument belongs partly to hindsight.

On September 11 itself, there was no polished lesson yet.

There were buildings burning in the United States.

There were people trying to determine whether more attacks were coming.

There were evacuated institutions.

There were uncertain casualty figures.

There was a new ambassador in Brussels trying to reconnect with Washington.

And there were allied ambassadors calling him.

That is enough.

The book does not need baseball here.

Major League games stopped after the attacks. Baseball later became part of America's return to public life. The sport would acquire its own September symbolism.

But there is no evidence that Burns experienced those first NATO hours through baseball, and there is no reason to make him do so on the page.

A metaphor becomes dishonest when it insists on surviving every event.

September 11 did not need help becoming meaningful.

For Burns, the diplomatic meaning began with something simpler.

He had been in the job twelve days.

Washington went silent.

The allies called.

By the end of the night, the alliance had offered the United States something it had never before been asked to give in that direction.

Solidarity had moved faster than procedure.

By morning, procedure would have to catch up.
