# Preface — The Red Sox Came Along

Nicholas Burns spent his career speaking for the United States in rooms built to make the speaker larger than the person: flags, seals, titles, protocol, a place at the table that told everyone who represented whom. The machinery matters. Diplomacy depends on knowing who has authority to speak, what a sentence commits, and which words belong to a government rather than the man saying them.

The Red Sox belonged to Burns.

He was a fan long before the affiliation became public shorthand. Reporters knew it. Colleagues knew it. By 2021, while his nomination to become ambassador to China was still pending, somebody could interrupt a State Department briefing with five words—*but he's a Red Sox fan*—and the room understood the joke.

That is about the right scale for baseball in this story. Fenway Park did not teach Burns how to negotiate with India or Iran. The Yankees are not a useful substitute for a geopolitical adversary once the stakes become coercion, sovereignty or war. A baseball game begins with mutually accepted rules, an umpire, a schedule and the assumption that there will be another game. Diplomacy is often necessary because some or all of those assurances are missing.

The metaphor gets smaller as the diplomacy gets larger. Good.

Baseball is more interesting here as provenance than instruction: a piece of home that survived the titles. It gives the career a second clock. Administrations change. Wars begin and end. Alliances enlarge. Countries disappear from maps. April comes. Fenway fills. A score reaches an embassy in another time zone.

Sometimes the record catches the two clocks crossing. In Greece, Burns helped connect a developing national baseball program with American baseball institutions and a wider Greek-American network before the 2004 Olympics. In Brussels in 2003, the record places him in front of Armed Forces television for a Red Sox loss to the Yankees. Other times the scene refuses to appear. Burns was in Jerusalem during the 1986 World Series, but the archive does not tell us where he watched, or whether he watched at all. When Boston finally won in 2004, the perfect biographical scene goes missing again.

The missing scenes stay missing. This book has no interest in manufacturing them.

Burns's diplomatic life does not need baseball to make it consequential. Baseball enters when Burns or the record puts it there. What the Red Sox offer is smaller and, for that reason, more revealing. They keep returning the diplomat to the fact that he was from somewhere.

Wellesley. A family. A game he had played. A team his father said had broken his heart in the 1930s and warned his children not to love.

Nicholas Burns loved it anyway.

America got the diplomat. The Red Sox came along.
