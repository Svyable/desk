# Preface — The Red Sox Came Along

Diplomacy can make a chair official. Put a flag behind it, a place card in front of it, and the person sitting there is no longer merely himself. Nicholas Burns spent decades in rooms arranged to make this obvious. The suit was his. The sentence, once cleared, belonged to the United States.

The Red Sox were another matter.

Burns was a fan long before anyone found the fact useful. Reporters knew it. Colleagues knew it. No committee had approved the allegiance, and no memo could revise it. It simply followed him from job to job, one of the few pieces of biography that survived contact with official Washington.

That is about the right scale for baseball in this story. Fenway Park did not teach Burns how to negotiate with India or Iran. The Yankees are not a sovereign adversary, however persuasive Boston may find the comparison. Baseball gives everyone the rules before the first pitch, an umpire, a schedule and the reasonable expectation that there will be another game. Diplomacy is often necessary because one or more of those conveniences is missing.

The metaphor gets smaller as the diplomacy gets larger. Good.

Baseball is more useful here as something Burns carried. Administrations changed. Wars began and ended. Alliances enlarged. Countries disappeared from maps. April kept arriving. Fenway filled. Somewhere, eventually, a score reached an embassy.

Sometimes the record catches the two clocks crossing. In Greece, Burns helped connect a developing national baseball program with American baseball institutions and a wider Greek-American network before the 2004 Olympics. In Brussels in 2003, the U.S. ambassador to NATO watched the Red Sox lose to the Yankees on Armed Forces television. Diplomatic rank offered no immunity.

Other times the archive refuses to cooperate. Burns was in Jerusalem during the 1986 World Series, but the record does not tell us where he watched, or whether he watched at all. When Boston finally won in 2004, the perfect biographical scene goes missing again. The archive had no sense of occasion.

The missing scenes stay missing. This book does not get to invent a television set because the scene would be good.

Burns's diplomatic life does not need baseball to make it consequential. Baseball enters when Burns or the record puts it there. What the Red Sox offer is smaller and more revealing: proof that the diplomat was from somewhere.

Wellesley. A family. A game he had played. A team his father said had broken his heart in the 1930s and warned his children not to love.

It was sensible advice. Nicholas Burns ignored it.

America got the diplomat. The Red Sox came along.
