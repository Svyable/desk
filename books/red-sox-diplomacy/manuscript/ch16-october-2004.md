# October 2004

There is a scene this book does not have.

On October 27, 2004, the Boston Red Sox completed their World Series sweep of St. Louis and won their first championship since 1918.

Somewhere, Nicholas Burns learned that the waiting was over.

That is all the evidence presently allows.

We do not know, from a reliable record, where he watched the final out. We do not know who was beside him. We do not know what he said or did in the immediate moment.

A biographer can feel the missing scene pulling at the page.

It should remain missing.

Burns had made the Red Sox unusually visible inside his public life. Reporters knew. Colleagues knew. Presidents knew. Greek journalists knew. Baseball had appeared in briefing-room jokes, his Athens office, public diplomacy and his description of himself.

And yet the championship night—the scene most perfectly shaped for a book called *Red Sox Diplomacy*—is undocumented.

The archive gives us almost the scene we want one year earlier.

But first it gives us a prediction.

At Boston College's commencement in May 2002, Burns stood in Alumni Stadium and predicted that, after an eighty-four-year pause, Boston would be home to the World Series champions that October.

It was exactly the kind of prediction Red Sox fans had been making for generations.

It was wrong.

Then came 2003.

Burns was the American ambassador to NATO in Brussels when the Red Sox and Yankees reached Game 7 of the American League Championship Series. Boston led late. The game stretched deep into the European morning. Burns later described following it on Armed Forces television in Brussels, all the way to another Boston loss.

That scene belongs to 2003.

It cannot be moved twelve months forward because 2004 supplies the better ending.

This is one of the small disciplines of factual biography. History is full of events that happened and scenes that would have happened around them. The second category is not automatically available to the writer.

So leave Burns out of the room.

Watch the baseball instead.

The Yankees took the first three games of the 2004 American League Championship Series. No Major League Baseball team had recovered from a three-games-to-none deficit to win a best-of-seven postseason series.

Boston then won four straight.

The opponent mattered. The Red Sox did not merely reach the World Series. They passed through the Yankees to get there, reversing the result of the previous October and temporarily rearranging the emotional architecture of the rivalry.

Then Boston swept St. Louis.

On October 27, the drought ended.

The country around the celebration was not waiting quietly for baseball history. American forces were fighting in Iraq and Afghanistan. September 11 still organized much of the country's political language. The presidential election was six days away.

The championship resolved none of that.

It was not national reconciliation.

It was local exultation inside a divided country.

That is enough historical scale. Baseball did not rise above the moment. It happened inside it.

For a fan identity that had spent decades feeding on disappointment, the scoreboard finally offered no ambiguity.

Champion.

Five months later, Burns supplied the language the missing October scene cannot.

He had just begun his job as Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs. At the U.S. Institute of Peace in March 2005, the subject turned briefly to baseball. Burns identified himself as a native-born member of Red Sox Nation and said the community had found “redemption.”

The word is funny because it is too large.

That is why it works.

A year later, at Northeastern's commencement in Boston, he made the joke more precise. New Englanders, Burns said, had been delivered from a belief “embedded deep in our regional DNA”: the Red Sox must always lose. Then he named what had broken the belief—the “glorious autumn of 2004.”

Regional DNA was not biology. It was comic shorthand for an expectation that had become hereditary: disappointment handed down long enough to feel like destiny.

The championship disproved the destiny.

It did not erase the memory.

Years later, speaking in Chicago in 2012, Burns noted Boston's championships in 2004 and 2007 and then said that the thing about Red Sox Nation was that “we identify with suffering.” The line was offered in sympathy with Cubs fans, but its usefulness here is narrower.

The suffering had survived the winning.

Not as forecast.

As memory.

The sequence is unusually clean.

Redemption.

Deliverance from inevitable losing.

Identification with remembered suffering.

Victory changed the story without requiring the fans to abandon the identity they had built while losing.

That matters for this biography because it tests an easy explanation.

The easy version of *Red Sox Diplomacy* would argue that decades of baseball disappointment trained Nicholas Burns for diplomatic patience.

The evidence does not justify that claim.

He did not need Fenway Park to teach him that governments move slowly. Cairo, Jerusalem, Washington, Athens and Brussels were sufficient instructors. There is no documentary basis for turning the Red Sox into a secret graduate school of statecraft.

The more defensible connection is identity.

Burns repeatedly carried a small piece of home into places where his job required him to speak for something much larger than himself. Losing made that identity comic and durable. Winning tested whether the identity had been only a performance of suffering.

It had not.

In October 2005, he was still joking about shutting down the State Department so employees could watch Boston play the Chicago White Sox. Years later the Red Sox remained standard shorthand in profiles, speeches and banter.

The drought ended.

The affiliation did not.

That is why the missing championship-night scene ultimately matters less than it first appears to.

If we had the perfect account—the room, the television, the final out, the exact sentence—it would give the chapter cinematic satisfaction. It would not necessarily tell us more about the man.

The public record afterward gives us something quieter.

He remained a fan.

The offices kept changing. Spokesman. Ambassador. NATO representative. Under Secretary. Professor. Ambassador again.

Boston did not require reappointment.

We know what happened to the Red Sox on October 27, 2004.

We know what Burns later called it.

Redemption.

We know what he said it broke.

The belief that they must always lose.

The room remains his.
