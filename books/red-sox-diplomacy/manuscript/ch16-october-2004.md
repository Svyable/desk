# October 2004

There is a scene this book does not have.

On October 27, 2004, the Boston Red Sox completed their World Series sweep of St. Louis and won their first championship since 1918.

Somewhere, Nicholas Burns learned that the waiting was over.

That is all the evidence presently allows.

We do not know, from a reliable record, where he watched the final out. We do not know who was beside him. We do not know what he said or did in the immediate moment.

A biographer can feel the missing scene pulling at the page.

It should remain missing.

Burns had made the Red Sox unusually visible inside his diplomatic life. Reporters knew. Colleagues knew. Presidents knew. Greek journalists knew. Baseball had appeared in his briefing-room jokes, his office, his public diplomacy and his description of himself. By 2004, the fandom was part of the public Nicholas Burns.

And yet the championship night—the scene most perfectly shaped for a book called *Red Sox Diplomacy*—is undocumented.

The temptation is obvious because the archive gives us almost the scene we want one year earlier.

In October 2003, Burns was the American ambassador to NATO in Brussels. The Red Sox and Yankees reached Game 7 of the American League Championship Series. Boston led late. The game stretched deep into the European morning. Burns later described following it on Armed Forces television in Brussels, all the way to another Boston loss.

That scene belongs to 2003.

It cannot be moved twelve months forward because 2004 supplies the better ending.

This is one of the small disciplines of factual biography. History is full of events that happened and scenes that would have happened around them. The second category is not automatically available to the writer. A person was somewhere. Emotion almost certainly existed. None of that gives us permission to furnish the room.

So leave Burns out of the room.

Watch the baseball instead.

In 2004, Boston's route to the championship seemed briefly designed to make the old identity unbearable before destroying it.

The Yankees took the first three games of the American League Championship Series. No Major League Baseball team had recovered from a three-games-to-none deficit to win a best-of-seven postseason series. Boston then won four straight.

The comeback mattered because the opponent mattered. The Red Sox did not merely reach the World Series. They passed through the Yankees to get there, reversing the result of the previous October and temporarily rearranging the emotional architecture of the rivalry.

Then Boston swept St. Louis.

On October 27, the drought ended.

For a fan identity that had spent decades feeding on complication, the scoreboard finally offered no ambiguity.

Champion.

The public Burns had long been useful partly because his baseball suffering was legible. The joke required almost no explanation. A senior American official could speak for the most powerful government in the world and then describe himself as a member of a constituency accustomed to disappointment. The asymmetry made him less polished without making him less official.

There was inheritance in it too. Burns had told the State Department briefing room that his mother supported the Red Sox and that his father, having been disappointed by the club long before Nick was born, had warned his children away. Burns ignored the advice.

That family story changes after October 2004.

Not because the past disappears. It does not.

But inherited disappointment is no longer the ending.

Five months after the championship, Burns supplied the line the missing October scene cannot.

He had just begun his job as Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs. At the U.S. Institute of Peace in March 2005, the subject turned briefly to baseball. Burns identified himself as a native-born member of Red Sox Nation and said that the community had found “redemption.”

The word is funny because it is too large.

That is why it works.

Redemption converts a baseball championship into mock theology while acknowledging that generations of supporters had already behaved as though something larger than a season was at stake. Burns was not claiming salvation. He was making fun of the emotional scale of the fandom from inside it.

The significant thing is not that the diplomat used a baseball joke.

It is that victory did not retire the identity.

The Red Sox had been useful material when they lost. Burns kept talking about them after they won.

In October 2005, before briefing reporters on an upcoming trip to Brussels and the Balkans, he joked that the State Department would shut down at four so employees could watch Boston beat the Chicago White Sox. The old long-suffering fan had become, at least for the moment, a fan capable of institutional swagger.

The joke was still recognizably Burns. The premise had changed.

This is where sports identities are stranger than political identities. They can survive the destruction of their organizing grievance.

For decades, Red Sox fandom had carried an argument with history. The Yankees supplied the antagonist. Fenway supplied continuity. Families supplied transmission.

Then the team won.

Nothing essential vanished.

The rival remained. Fenway remained. Boston remained. Family memory remained. The old losses became stories rather than forecasts.

Burns's later baseball language makes more sense in that light. The fandom increasingly appears not merely as a badge of endurance but as a community he carries with him. Official biographies call him a member of Red Sox Nation. Public appearances assume the affiliation. Baseball becomes less about explaining suffering and more about declaring provenance.

That is a subtle shift, but it matters for this biography.

The easy version of *Red Sox Diplomacy* would argue that decades of baseball disappointment trained Nicholas Burns for diplomatic patience.

The evidence does not justify that claim.

He never needed the Red Sox to teach him that governments move slowly. Cairo, Jerusalem, Washington, Athens and Brussels were sufficient instructors. There is no documentary basis for turning Fenway Park into a secret graduate school of statecraft.

The more defensible connection is identity.

Burns repeatedly carried a small piece of home into places where his job required him to speak for something much larger than himself. The Red Sox gave other people a way to locate the person inside the office. Losing made that identity comic and durable. Winning tested whether the identity had been only a performance of suffering.

It had not.

This may be why the missing October scene ultimately matters less than it first appears to.

If we had the perfect account—the room, the television, the final out, the exact sentence—it would give the chapter cinematic satisfaction. It would not necessarily tell us more about the man.

The record after the championship tells us something quieter.

He remained a fan.

The drought ended. The affiliation did not.

That is what durable identity does. It survives the explanation people have given for it.

By 2004 Burns had already spent decades representing the United States abroad. Governments had changed. The Soviet Union had disappeared. NATO had enlarged. September 11 had transformed the alliance he represented. Iraq had divided it. His own assignments kept changing.

Boston stayed in the luggage.

Not literally every time. The point is not the cap.

The point is continuity.

A diplomatic career requires repeated reinvention. New capital. New title. New brief. New counterpart. New administration. New hierarchy. The representative is expected to arrive prepared to embody an institution whose policies may have changed since the previous posting.

Personal identity operates on a different clock.

Burns could become spokesman, ambassador, NATO representative, Under Secretary and professor without needing to become a different person each time. The Red Sox were one of the public clues that the offices had accumulated around a continuing human being.

October 2004 altered one of those clues.

The fan who had been easy to tease because his team never won now belonged to the team that had completed the most famous comeback in its history and ended eighty-six years without a championship.

The jokes would continue.

So would the work.

The biographer does not get to watch the final out with him.

Perhaps that is fitting.

Fandom is public until suddenly it isn't. Millions can watch the same pitch. The meaning of it still belongs, in some measure, to the person watching.

We know what happened to the Red Sox on October 27, 2004.

We know what Burns later called it.

Redemption.

The room remains his.
