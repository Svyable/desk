# The Professor in the Cheap Seats

For twenty-seven years, Nicholas Burns had worked inside the sentence.

Governments speak in sentences that have consequences. A phrase at a State Department podium can become policy. An ambassador's cable can alter what Washington believes is happening abroad. A negotiating formulation can preserve a coalition or expose a disagreement. Burns had spent a career learning where the nouns mattered, where the verbs could move, and where one adjective too many could create a problem.

Then, in 2008, he left government and went to Harvard.

The change was larger than geography.

A diplomat speaks with delegated authority. A professor has none.

That is not quite true, of course. Professors possess authority of several kinds—expertise, institutional standing, reputation, command of a room. Burns arrived at Harvard Kennedy School with an unusual supply of all four. He had served in Cairo and Jerusalem, worked at the National Security Council, spoken for the State Department, represented the United States in Greece and at NATO, and served as Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs. He had negotiated with allies, adversaries and countries that fit neither category cleanly.

But none of that gave him the right to tell a student what history meant.

That was the interesting part.

Harvard appointed Burns Professor of the Practice of Diplomacy and International Politics after his retirement from the Foreign Service. The title contained the experiment. He was not being asked to stop being a practitioner. He was being asked to make practice teachable.

A few years later, Burns explained what he thought professors of practice could contribute. People who had spent most of their careers in public service, he argued, had seen directly how governments succeed and fail.

The important word was not *succeed*.

It was *fail*.

Government memoirs have a natural gravitational pull toward vindication. Officials remember the constraints they faced, the information they had, the irresponsible alternatives they rejected. Critics remember the consequences. Historians acquire documents the participants did not possess. Time exposes assumptions that once looked like facts.

A classroom can put all of those things in the same room.

That changes what experience is for.

At the State Department podium, Burns had been required to protect institutional confidence. He could correct himself, distinguish what was known from what was alleged, and refuse to speculate beyond policy. But he could not turn every briefing into a seminar on the government's uncertainty. His job was to speak for the United States.

At Harvard, uncertainty was useful material.

A negotiation that failed could be more educational than one that ended in a photograph and signatures. An agreement could be examined for what each side thought it was buying. A successful coalition could be taken apart to discover the compromises hidden inside the word *unity*. A former official could be asked not merely what happened, but what he believed at the time, what he did not know, and what he would now describe differently.

The professor of practice had to do something the spokesman could rarely afford to do in public.

He had to expose the seams.

Burns built an institutional version of that idea in the Future of Diplomacy Project. The project brought diplomats and other practitioners into Harvard, supported research and teaching on negotiation and statecraft, and eventually helped construct an unusually revealing archive: long interviews with former American secretaries of state about consequential negotiations.

Henry Kissinger could explain his negotiations.

So could George Shultz.

James Baker, Madeleine Albright, Colin Powell, Condoleezza Rice, Hillary Clinton, John Kerry and others could explain theirs.

The value was not that former secretaries possessed the final word.

They possessed a word.

Students could hear how powerful officials reconstructed decisions after the fact. They could compare those reconstructions with documents, scholarship, other participants and consequences. Memory itself became evidence rather than verdict.

That is a subtle transformation for someone who had spent much of his adult life inside hierarchy.

The Foreign Service is built around rank, responsibility and authority. A junior officer can disagree with an ambassador, but the ambassador is still the ambassador. The Secretary of State is not merely another participant in a seminar. A president's decision ends arguments that may continue intellectually for decades.

A university reverses part of that structure.

The former Under Secretary can be challenged by someone who was in middle school when he held the job.

The challenge can be good.

There is some evidence of what individual students took from Burns's teaching, and it is better to keep that evidence small rather than inflate it into testimonials about a beloved professor.

Hainer Sibrian, who attended the Kennedy School before entering the Foreign Service, later described Burns's diplomacy course as a useful counterpart to another course that examined what happens when diplomacy fails. The pairing mattered to him because it exposed both the instrument and its limits.

Christy Vines, another Kennedy School graduate, recalled that a diplomacy class with Burns changed her academic direction toward foreign policy and national security.

Two students are two students.

They do not establish a universal classroom effect. They do, however, show the kind of bridge a professor of practice can create. The profession becomes imaginable because someone who has done it is willing to take the machinery apart in public.

Burns's mature courses made the method explicit. Negotiation was studied through successes and failures. Students examined how officials use diplomacy alongside political, economic and military pressure; why apparently intractable conflicts remain intractable; how negotiators diagnose barriers; and how participants themselves describe what they thought they were doing.

This was not diplomacy as etiquette.

It was diplomacy as decision under constraint.

The distinction matters because the public mythology of diplomats tends to split in two directions. One version treats them as elegant conversationalists who prevent wars through sophistication. The other treats them as professional temporizers who talk while harder instruments produce results.

Burns had accumulated enough experience to know that neither caricature survives contact with an actual negotiation.

India had required political ambition, congressional legislation, technical negotiation, international approvals and domestic risk in both countries. Iran had demonstrated that persistence and coalition-building could still leave the central bargain unavailable. NATO after September 11 had shown how quickly solidarity could become operational. NATO over Iraq had shown how quickly solidarity could become argument.

Those were no longer merely episodes in a résumé.

They were cases.

This may be one of the stranger privileges of leaving government: your mistakes become syllabus material.

Burns did not withdraw from policy while teaching. He wrote, spoke, advised and argued about current affairs. He led projects on diplomacy, Europe and transatlantic relations. Practitioners moved through his programs. Students encountered crises while they were happening, not only after archives opened.

The professor was in the cheap seats only in one specific sense.

He no longer controlled a piece of the field.

From outside government, Burns could recommend. He could criticize. He could explain. He could tell a president what he thought should happen in an article or an interview, but the sentence did not carry delegated authority. No foreign ministry was required to parse a Harvard professor's wording as the position of the United States.

That freedom has a cost.

Officials know things outsiders do not know. Outsiders can say things officials cannot say. Each side is tempted to confuse its advantage with superior wisdom.

A practitioner-teacher sits uncomfortably between them.

He remembers what classified information and institutional responsibility feel like. He also knows that secrecy and responsibility can become excuses. He can see the confidence of commentators who have never had to execute policy. He can also see the confidence of officials who believe execution protects them from criticism.

The useful professor of practice belongs fully to neither camp.

He translates between them.

There was another continuity from the old Burns that Harvard did not erase.

He was still from Boston.

The Red Sox identity survived the transition from diplomat to professor because it had never depended on office. By then the team had won championships. The old suffering joke no longer carried the same premise. Burns could still talk baseball, still advertise allegiance, still make himself legible through a piece of ordinary American life.

Then, in 2013, that ordinary piece of Boston life changed function again.

On Patriots' Day, two bombs exploded near the finish line of the Boston Marathon. People were killed and hundreds were injured. The attack struck one of the city's defining civic rituals—the day when the marathon, an early Red Sox game and the public holiday collapse into a single long Boston calendar.

Burns was no longer an American representative abroad. He was back in Massachusetts, teaching at Harvard.

The documentary record used for this book does not give us his private reaction to the bombing or to the Red Sox season that followed. It should not be invented.

The public history is enough.

The Red Sox placed `Boston Strong` into the team's visible identity. Fenway became one of the places where the city mourned, thanked first responders and insisted on ordinary civic life after violence. That October Boston reached the World Series. On October 30, the Red Sox clinched the championship at Fenway Park—the first time the club had won a World Series on its home field since 1918.

The 2004 championship had destroyed the old claim that Red Sox identity required permanent losing.

The 2013 season did something different.

It showed how deeply a baseball club could be embedded in a city's public language even after winning had become familiar.

The team was not therapy. A championship did not repair the physical or psychological damage of the Marathon attack. Sport is too small for that claim.

But a civic institution does not have to cure grief to become one of the places where grief is expressed.

That distinction belongs in a biography about representation.

Burns had spent much of his professional life thinking about how countries make themselves visible through ceremonies, speeches, buildings, symbols and people. Boston in 2013 supplied a hometown example with no foreign-policy content at all. A community under shock used a ballpark, uniforms, signs, rituals and a team already saturated with local identity as part of the way it represented itself to itself.

Baseball did not explain Boston.

It gave Boston a stage.

Harvard changed the function of Burns's own baseball persona too.

At a State briefing, baseball had been a momentary register shared with reporters before the spokesman returned to policy. In Greece, it had sometimes become public diplomacy and network-building. In a classroom, the important form of accessibility was different. The professor had to make experience available without making experience sacred.

That is harder than telling a good story.

A career produces stories automatically. The temptation is to teach from them as parables: I was there, therefore here is the lesson.

The better question is what the story cannot prove.

Burns spent thirteen years at Harvard before returning to government.

Thirteen years is long enough for a former official to acquire another professional identity. Students came and went. Administrations changed. The wars after September 11 changed shape. The financial crisis arrived. The Arab uprisings began. Russia seized Crimea. The Iran nuclear agreement was negotiated, signed and later abandoned by the United States. China became more powerful and the American argument about China became harder.

Burns watched these developments partly as an analyst and partly as a former participant in the institutions responding to them.

Then the distance ended.

In 2021, President Joe Biden nominated him to become United States ambassador to the People's Republic of China.

The professor was going back inside the sentence.

This time the assignment was a relationship more consequential, more suspicious and more structurally competitive than any bilateral posting he had held before.

For thirteen years, Burns had asked students to examine how governments succeed and fail.

Now a government was asking him to represent it again.

The seminar was over.
