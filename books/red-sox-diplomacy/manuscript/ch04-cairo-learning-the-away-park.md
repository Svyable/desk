# Chapter 4 — Cairo: Learning the Away Park

The first useful fact about Nicholas Burns's first Foreign Service posting is that he had two jobs.

He was a vice consul in Cairo. He was also staff assistant to the American ambassador.

One job pointed toward the individual. The other pointed toward the institution.

At the consular end of an embassy, foreign policy becomes stubbornly specific. There is a person at the window. A passport. A visa. An American in trouble. A rule that may have been written thousands of miles away and now has to be applied to a case with a name attached to it. A 1999 profile of Burns, looking back on those Cairo years, reduced some of the work to its practical dimensions: stamping passports, helping American workers who had landed in jail.

There is not much grandeur in that description.

Good.

Diplomatic biographies tend to begin where the photographs begin. The young officer disappears so the later ambassador can enter on cue. But the state is not experienced only in summit rooms. For many people, a consular officer is the United States government in its most immediate form.

Burns was learning that form while also sitting close to another one.

The staff assistant works near the ambassador, but proximity is not power. It is exposure. The aide sees how information reaches the front office, which questions the ambassador asks twice, which sections of the mission know what Washington does not, which visitors matter, which cables require attention, and how a large bureaucracy turns fragments into something that can be called an American position.

Cairo was a large place to learn this.

Robert Beecroft, a political officer there during the same 1983–85 period, later remembered Embassy Cairo as the largest American embassy in the world at the time, roughly two thousand people, with an enormous USAID mission alongside it. Beecroft does not mention Burns in his account of those years, and there is no reason to pretend that two officers in a mission that size must have been close.

The scale is the point.

This was not an apprenticeship in a tidy little outpost where everybody gathered around one table. It was a government inside a government: political officers, economic officers, consular officers, public-affairs specialists, military personnel, aid officials, intelligence officers, administrators, local employees, visiting delegations, Washington principals moving through, all operating under the same flag and not always seeing the same problem in the same way.

The ambassador when Burns arrived was Alfred Atherton, one of the senior American diplomats most closely associated with the long Arab-Israeli negotiating process. Nicholas Veliotes succeeded him in November 1983. Burns spent most of his Cairo tour as Veliotes's staff aide.

Their embassy occupied a peculiar moment in the history of American diplomacy in Egypt.

The great breakthrough had already happened.

Egypt and Israel had signed peace. Sinai had been returned. The photographs from Camp David belonged to an earlier chapter. What remained was maintenance.

Maintenance is where the romance usually drains out of diplomacy and the profession becomes visible.

Beecroft, whose portfolio covered Egypt-Israel relations, remembered a cold peace. The agreement existed. The relationship did not become warm merely because signatures had been placed on paper. Egypt was also trying to restore its standing in an Arab world that had punished it for the separate peace with Israel. The United States was pouring assistance into the relationship. Military cooperation mattered enormously. So did Egyptian suspicion of foreign military presence.

Veliotes's oral history gives the contradiction physical form.

Washington had considered a large American military facility at Ras Banas on Egypt's Red Sea coast. From one angle it was logistics: access, infrastructure, the ability to move American forces in a dangerous region. From Cairo it carried history. Egyptians had lived with British power and then Soviet presence. A permanent foreign footprint could not be understood only as a line in a Pentagon plan.

Veliotes later complained that senior officials in Washington did not fully grasp the depth of that Egyptian sensitivity.

That is diplomacy in one sentence, although Burns did not write it and we should not assign the lesson to him after the fact.

A policy leaves Washington as an abstraction.

It arrives somewhere with a past.

The embassy stands at the collision point.

Burns was a junior officer inside that machinery. The documentary record does not put him into the Ras Banas negotiations, a Mubarak meeting, or a specific military discussion. It would be easy to improve the story by doing so. It would also be fiction.

What can be said is more useful. As staff assistant, he was serving the ambassador in a mission where such arguments moved through the building while he also performed consular work at the other end of the diplomatic spectrum.

The combination matters.

A career can become distorted by specialization. The policy officer sees policy. The consular officer sees cases. The aid officer sees programs. The military attaché sees capabilities. The public-affairs officer sees audiences. The ambassador has to see enough of all of them to decide what the embassy is actually doing.

A staff aide gets an early education in the seams.

Beecroft had held a similar job earlier in his own career, as staff aide to an ambassador in Paris. His recollection of that work is not evidence of Burns's daily routine in Cairo, but it explains the craft of the position. The aide, Beecroft said, needed discreet relationships across the embassy—people in political, economic, administrative, consular, public-affairs and defense sections who could answer questions without every inquiry becoming a bureaucratic production.

The aide learned whom to call.

That sounds minor until government stops working because nobody knows whom to call.

The job also teaches an uncomfortable fact about institutions: the organizational chart is not the same thing as the organization. Formal authority matters. So do trust, competence, memory, responsiveness and the person who actually knows where the file went.

Burns would spend much of his later career operating inside that distinction.

There is one witness from Cairo who brings the young Burns out from behind the résumé.

Ted Osius was not yet a diplomat. He was a young American interested in international affairs and associated with the American University in Cairo. Decades later, after his own Foreign Service career had taken him to an ambassadorship in Vietnam, Osius remembered getting to know Burns in Cairo.

He remembered Burns as Veliotes's staff aide. More importantly, he remembered the manner.

Burns was friendly to the interns. Inclusive. Kind. He took them seriously.

At some point Burns urged Osius toward the Johns Hopkins School of Advanced International Studies and the Foreign Service. Osius later described the suggestion as a seed Burns had planted.

Osius went to SAIS.

Then he joined the Foreign Service.

It is a small story. That is why it belongs here.

Burns was not yet the American ambassador inviting young people into public service from a commencement stage. He was a junior officer himself. He had no famous title to lend the advice. To an intern, though, the ambassador's aide was already somebody inside the profession.

Representation works downward as well as outward.

A diplomat represents the United States to another country. An officer also represents the institution to the people deciding whether they want any part of it.

The Foreign Service is perpetuated partly this way: one person notices another person early.

Burns's later speeches would return so often to public service that the theme can sound polished in retrospect. Cairo gives it a less polished origin. We cannot say that he had already formed the philosophy he would articulate decades later. We can say that one future ambassador remembered young Nick Burns encouraging him to become a diplomat.

That is enough.

The Cairo around them was not calm in any broad historical sense. Lebanon remained violent. The Iran-Iraq War continued. The Soviet Union occupied Afghanistan. American military planners were thinking hard about access to the Gulf. Egypt under Hosni Mubarak was consolidating after Anwar Sadat's assassination and trying to manage its separate peace with Israel without surrendering its claim to Arab leadership.

Yet Beecroft remembered the period in Cairo itself as, in some respects, a long slog rather than a sequence of dramatic crises.

That distinction is important.

The young officer's education was taking place after the breakthrough and before the next headline.

He was learning the part of diplomacy that survives the photograph.

A treaty has to be maintained. An aid program has to be administered. A congressional delegation arrives. An American gets detained. A military proposal has to be translated into the politics of the country where the concrete would actually be poured. The ambassador needs an answer. Someone has to find it.

There is a baseball version of this story available, and it is mostly wrong.

Cairo was not Burns learning to play an away game. Egypt was not an opposing ballpark. The United States and Egypt were partners on some questions, frustrated with one another on others, and embedded in a relationship too consequential to reduce to a scoreboard.

The useful part of the title is narrower.

Away from home, assumptions become visible.

Beecroft remembered arriving in Egypt with no previous Middle East experience and slowly recognizing the force of Egyptian historical self-consciousness. Veliotes remembered Washington failing to appreciate how foreign military presence could sound to Egyptians whose modern history included British and Soviet power. These were not lessons about exotic difference. They were warnings against believing that American intent determines foreign meaning.

A government can know what it means to say.

It cannot dictate what another country hears.

That gap is where diplomats work.

Burns would later become unusually comfortable explaining America in public: at the State Department podium, in Athens, at NATO, in negotiations with India and Iran, eventually in Beijing. It would be convenient to trace that ability cleanly back to Cairo.

The record does not permit it.

What Cairo gives us is earlier and rougher.

A junior officer working both ends of an embassy. Individual cases and institutional flow. A mission large enough to demonstrate that American policy is not made by one mind. A host country powerful enough to demonstrate that partnership does not erase memory. An ambassador's office close enough to observe but not yet command.

And an intern who remembered being welcomed.

That last detail deserves more weight than it appears to carry.

Burns would spend decades arguing that diplomacy depends on relationships built before they are urgently needed. In Cairo, long before he wrote about tending George Shultz's diplomatic garden, he was already part of somebody else's beginning.

Osius remembered the invitation.

The profession got another diplomat.

No box score records that kind of result.

The Foreign Service does.
