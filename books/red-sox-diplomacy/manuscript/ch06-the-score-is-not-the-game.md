# Chapter 6 — The Score Is Not the Game

By the beginning of 1994, the problem looked simple enough to fit inside a date.

August 31.

Russian troops were supposed to be out of Latvia and Estonia by then.

[![Documentary deadline object marking August 31, 1994 as the Baltic troop-withdrawal date](../media/ch06-deadline-1994-08-31.svg)](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/statement-withdrawal-russian-forces-from-eastern-europe)

*DEADLINE OBJECT 01 — August 31, 1994. President Clinton’s statement that day recorded completion of Russian military-force withdrawals from Estonia and Latvia under bilateral agreements. The date is clean enough to become historical shorthand; the object deliberately leaves the temporary Skrunda arrangements and other implementation terms outside the headline. [Presidential statement.](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/statement-withdrawal-russian-forces-from-eastern-europe) [GovInfo record.](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/PPP-1994-book2/PPP-1994-book2-doc-pg1514/context)*

A deadline gives officials something to repeat, newspapers something to print and opponents something to measure. Either the troops leave or they do not. Making that sentence true was the untidy part.

Nicholas Burns had reached the National Security Council after Cairo, Jerusalem, the State Department Operations Center, and Soviet and Eastern European work. By the Clinton administration he was the senior White House official for Russia, Ukraine and Eurasia. The Soviet Union had disappeared. Its consequences had not.

Fifteen independent states occupied the map where one superpower had stood. Nuclear weapons had to be accounted for. Economies were collapsing and being rebuilt. Borders that had once divided parts of a single state had become international borders. In the Baltics, Russian troops were still sitting in countries that regarded them as an occupying force.

Lithuania got the last Russian troops out in 1993. Latvia and Estonia were harder. Withdrawal was the visible objective; underneath it sat military installations, minority rights, pensions, housing, domestic politics and the practical question of where departing officers and their families would go.

Russian officials linked withdrawal to the rights of Russian-speaking minorities in the Baltic states. Latvia wanted control over its territory but still had the Skrunda early-warning radar complex on its soil, a facility Moscow considered militarily important. Estonia had unresolved questions involving Russian military pensioners and Soviet installations. Washington wanted the troops out without handing Boris Yeltsin's domestic opponents another argument that Moscow was surrendering too much.

In early February 1994, a Latvian delegation came to Washington carrying much of that argument with it. Foreign Minister Georgs Andrejevs was joined by leaders of all eight political factions in Latvia's parliament. One session in the White House Situation Room lasted roughly an hour and a half with Burns. The unusual composition of the delegation mattered: Latvia was negotiating with Russia abroad while deciding at home what kind of compromise a country recently out from under Soviet control could accept.

Skrunda concentrated the problem. Russia wanted to keep the radar operating longer than Latvia wanted to permit. Latvia wanted Russian military infrastructure gone. The gap could be counted in years, but neither side experienced it as arithmetic. For Latvia, the installation belonged to a history it was trying to end. For Russia, it remained part of a security system it was not prepared to abandon overnight.

President Clinton proposed four more years of operation, followed by eighteen months for dismantling. Burns communicated the proposal to the Latvian side.

No secret channel. No lone envoy carrying a final offer through the night. A delegation, a conference table, domestic pressures, technical details and a White House official trying to keep one component from swallowing the objective.

The Latvian negotiations eventually produced an August 31 withdrawal agreement. Clinton publicly credited the engagement of both sides and emphasized Sweden's constructive role as well as that of the United States. Washington mattered. It was not alone.

Estonia remained more difficult. The Clinton administration pressed Moscow while confronting the argument Yeltsin kept making about Russian minorities. Washington did not accept a continued Russian military presence as the answer to minority-rights concerns, but it treated those concerns as politically real. In Riga in July 1994, Clinton publicly pressed for complete withdrawal while urging the Baltic states to build inclusive democratic societies. He was speaking to several audiences at once.

[![Implementation ledger for selected U.S. support measures connected to the 1994 Baltic troop withdrawals](../media/ch06-implementation-ledger.svg)](https://clintonwhitehouse6.archives.gov/1994/07/1994-07-10-backgrounder-on-clinton-yeltsin-meeting.html)

*IMPLEMENTATION LEDGER 01 — Selected U.S. support measures documented by the White House in July 1994: 2,500 housing vouchers for departing Russian military officers at a stated value of $25,000 each; $4 million to help dismantle the Skrunda radar facility in Latvia; and $2 million for environmental cleanup assistance at Paldiski in Estonia. The voucher figures imply $62.5 million at the stated value. These are implementation mechanisms, not an exhaustive accounting of Baltic policy or the cost of the diplomacy. [White House background briefing.](https://clintonwhitehouse6.archives.gov/1994/07/1994-07-10-backgrounder-on-clinton-yeltsin-meeting.html)*

The ledger makes the machinery visible. The United States increased support for housing vouchers that could help Russian officers resettle. It backed environmental cleanup at the former Soviet nuclear training site at Paldiski. It worked the remaining differences between Moscow and Tallinn and carried proposals from Estonian president Lennart Meri to Yeltsin. A withdrawal deadline that looked binary from a distance depended on people finding apartments, dismantling radar, cleaning contaminated sites and making bargains that could survive politics on both sides of a border.

The result would be remembered as a date.

August 31, 1994.

The last Russian troops left Estonia. Russian forces also completed their withdrawal from Latvia, apart from the temporary arrangements connected to Skrunda. Clinton described the larger marker more precisely: for the first time since the end of World War II, there would be no Russian troops in Germany or elsewhere in Eastern Europe.

History could keep the line. Burns had spent years inside what the line discarded: a Latvian parliamentary delegation that needed persuading; an Estonian government trying to keep the problem from drifting; Russian officers who needed somewhere to go; a radar station that was both military hardware and historical residue; a Russian president facing his own parliament and military; Baltic governments whose recovered sovereignty could not yet enforce itself automatically.

That summer, another American institution people liked to describe as permanent stopped in the middle of its calendar. Major League Baseball players went on strike after play on August 11. The season never resumed. The postseason and World Series disappeared with it.

The juxtaposition should not be asked to do more than chronology can bear. A labor dispute in professional sports was not remotely equivalent to the end of a military occupation. It belongs to the American atmosphere of 1994, nothing more. On August 31, Russian troops completed the Baltic withdrawals while the baseball season remained suspended: one calendar acquired a geopolitical milestone; the other acquired a hole.

The score was unusually legible. The troops left.

What came after was not. NATO enlargement, Russia's place in Europe and the violence in the Balkans would keep the larger argument open for years. August 31 measured an outcome. It could not show the housing vouchers, pensioners, radar station, cleanup money, domestic opponents and negotiated delays that made the outcome possible.
