# Yankees, Rivals, Adversaries

Nicholas Burns once told a State Department briefing room that the rules were simple.

The Red Sox were good.

The Tigers were acceptable.

The Yankees were hated.

Everyone laughed.

That was the point.

No one in the room believed the United States government had adopted a position on the American League East. The joke worked because the hostility was theatrical. A Yankees fan could be threatened with expulsion from the briefing room and still know where the door joke ended and the relationship began.

The teams played inside a shared system.

That fact is so obvious that sports fans rarely mention it.

The Red Sox and Yankees use the same rulebook. They recognize the same league. They accept the same standings. They know there will be another series, another season, another chance to complain about the strike zone. One team's identity depends partly on the other remaining available as an opponent.

Sports rivalry therefore contains an assumption that international politics cannot safely borrow.

The game itself will survive.

A state cannot assume that.

Burns's career eventually placed him inside relationships where the words *rival*, *competitor*, *adversary* and *enemy* were not interchangeable decorations. They described different structures of danger.

The distinctions mattered because diplomatic mistakes often begin with classification.

Treat an ally's disagreement as betrayal and you can weaken an alliance.

Treat a competitor as an enemy and you can accelerate hostility.

Treat an adversary as if it were merely another team on the schedule and you can miss preparations for coercion or war.

A diplomat does not solve this problem by choosing the friendliest word.

He solves it, if he can, by naming the relationship accurately enough that policy is built for the world that exists rather than the one officials prefer.

The State Department podium had supplied Burns with the harmless version first.

In 1997, baseball banter recurred often enough that reporters knew the ritual. A visiting intern admitted to being a Yankees fan and Burns theatrically pointed toward the doors before granting clemency for the day. A Dutch journalist knew enough to say the Red Sox played at Fenway, and Burns pressed him on whether Boston's club was more famous than New York's.

This was rivalry as social language.

The content mattered less than the pattern. Everyone knew the antagonism had limits.

Ten years later, in Paris, Burns used the same language with another American diplomat.

He had come to commemorate the sixtieth anniversary of the Marshall Plan with Craig Stapleton, the United States ambassador to France. Burns opened by saying the two men shared a special bond: both were fans of the Boston Red Sox, and both opposed what Burns jokingly called the evil empire of the New York Yankees.

Then he turned immediately to the actual relationship in the room.

He praised Stapleton for helping soothe and calm U.S.-French relations and for building a stronger connection with the French government and people.

The baseball line did not explain the diplomacy. It located the diplomats inside it.

Two American officials could share a theatrical enemy because nobody mistook the Yankees for a strategic problem. The joke created a small piece of common identity before Burns moved into a speech about alliance history, European recovery, and transatlantic cooperation.

That distinction is useful because it shows how rivalry language can be harmless only when everyone knows the boundaries around it.

Years later, Burns would use similar vocabulary in a very different register.

As ambassador to China, he described the United States and China as engaged in historic competition and rivalry. The stakes were no longer a pennant race. Technology, military power, Taiwan, trade, artificial intelligence, human rights, alliances and global influence all sat inside the relationship.

The word *rivalry* survived.

Its meaning had changed.

A strategic rival can compete inside some shared systems while contesting others. The United States and China trade on enormous scale while restricting technologies from one another. Their diplomats meet while their militaries prepare for contingencies. Their students cross the Pacific while their governments accuse each other of destabilizing conduct. They cooperate on selected problems and contest basic questions of power.

No commissioner can end the dispute with a ruling.

No umpire can eject a government from the field and restore order.

International law matters. Treaties matter. Institutions matter. Norms matter. So do military capabilities, economic leverage and the willingness of governments to bear costs.

That is where the baseball analogy stops being cute and becomes clarifying.

The Yankees joke works because the shared system is stronger than the hatred.

Diplomacy cannot assume the same of states.

Burns had seen the opposite error inside NATO.

France, Germany and Belgium disagreed bitterly with Washington over the Iraq crisis and over the timing of defensive planning for Turkey. The dispute became severe enough to produce a procedural crisis inside the alliance.

Yet those countries did not become American adversaries because they blocked an American preference.

They remained allies.

That distinction was not sentimental. It was strategic.

If every severe disagreement transforms an ally into an enemy, alliances become impossible precisely when they are most needed.

The same career produced the reverse case with Iran.

Washington regarded the Iranian government as profoundly adversarial. The nuclear issue involved sanctions, proliferation risk, regional power and the possibility of military conflict.

Yet diplomacy still occurred.

Negotiation did not mean friendship.

It meant that the disagreement was serious enough to require communication.

This is one of the recurring confusions in public argument about diplomacy. Talking is treated as a reward. Refusing to talk is treated as toughness. Engagement is mistaken for trust. Contact is mistaken for concession.

Professional diplomacy rests on a less theatrical premise.

Sometimes you talk because you do not trust the other side.

Sometimes the channel is most necessary because the relationship is adversarial.

The China posting brought all of these distinctions together.

Burns described the relationship as structural competition. He spoke about military competition, technological competition, economic competition and a contest over political ideas.

He also argued that the United States should not demonize the Chinese people and should keep the two societies connected.

Those positions fit together only if competition is something more precise than enmity.

That precision matters.

A government that calls every competitor an enemy eventually begins to interpret ordinary competition as evidence of hostile intent. Every investment becomes penetration. Every student becomes a risk. Every negotiation becomes appeasement. Every concession becomes defeat.

The category eats the evidence.

The reverse mistake is just as dangerous.

A government that insists all disputes are manageable rivalry may discount coercion, conceal military risk or reassure itself with personal relationships that do not alter strategic incentives.

The category can eat the evidence from the other direction.

Diplomacy sits between those failures.

It asks a harder question.

What kind of disagreement is this?

The answer can change.

Countries that were enemies can become allies. Allies can drift into rivalry. Competitors can cooperate in one domain and threaten each other in another. Governments change. Leaders change. Wars alter categories quickly. Domestic politics can harden public language long before institutions catch up.

A representative therefore inherits labels but cannot rely on them blindly.

He has to report behavior.

What is the other government doing?

What does it appear to want?

What capabilities does it possess?

Where does it compromise?

Where does it refuse?

Which signals are public theater and which are operational preparation?

That work is less satisfying than declaring who the good guys are.

It is also more useful.

Baseball fandom offered Burns one language for antagonism without annihilation. The Yankees could be hated forever because nobody wanted the league to disappear.

That proposition does not transfer cleanly to geopolitics.

And that failure is the lesson.

A sports rivalry presupposes another game.

Diplomacy exists partly to make another meeting possible when no such guarantee exists.

The diplomat's task is not to turn adversaries into Yankees.

It is to know the difference.
