# Yankees, Rivals, Adversaries

Nicholas Burns once told a State Department briefing room that the rules were simple: the Red Sox were good, the Tigers acceptable, the Yankees hated.

Everyone laughed because everyone knew where the hatred ended. A Yankees fan could be threatened with expulsion from the briefing room and remain entirely safe. Boston and New York played inside the same league, under the same rules, with another series already on the schedule.

International politics makes no such promise.

Burns's career put him inside relationships where *ally*, *competitor*, *adversary* and *enemy* were not synonyms for people on the other side. France could block an American preference and remain an ally. Washington could negotiate with an Iranian government it regarded as adversarial. The United States and China could trade, deter, accuse, bargain and educate one another's students at the same time. The category mattered because the behavior did.

The State Department podium supplied the harmless version first. In 1997, the baseball banter recurred often enough that reporters knew the ritual. A visiting intern admitted to being a Yankees fan and Burns theatrically pointed toward the doors before granting clemency. A Dutch journalist knew enough to identify Fenway, and Burns pressed him on whether Boston's club was more famous than New York's. Nobody mistook the performance for policy.

Ten years later in Paris, Burns opened a Marshall Plan commemoration with Ambassador Craig Stapleton by noting their shared Red Sox allegiance and a line no diplomat could safely use about an actual adversary:

> **NICK / THE EVIL EMPIRE**
>
> ### “We are opponents of the evil empire, the New York Yankees.”
>
> *Marshall Plan commemoration, Paris · June 12, 2007 · [State Department transcript](https://2001-2009.state.gov/p/us/rm/2007/87176.htm)*

Then he changed registers and turned to U.S.-French relations.

The distinction became less funny in Beijing. As ambassador, Burns described the United States and China as engaged in historic competition. Technology, military power, Taiwan, trade, artificial intelligence, human rights, alliances and global influence all sat inside one relationship. The two countries traded at enormous scale while restricting technologies from one another. Their diplomats met while their militaries prepared for contingencies. Students crossed the Pacific while both governments accused the other of destabilizing conduct. Cooperation did not cancel deterrence; deterrence did not end contact.

There was no commissioner to settle the dispute.

Burns had already lived through the classification problem at NATO. France, Germany and Belgium fought Washington bitterly over Iraq and the timing of defensive planning for Turkey in 2003. Their opposition was real. It did not make them American adversaries.

Iran supplied the reverse case. Washington regarded the Iranian government as profoundly adversarial; the nuclear dispute carried sanctions, proliferation risk and the possibility of military conflict. Diplomacy still occurred because talking was not a prize for good behavior. It was one of the instruments available when the disagreement was dangerous enough.

China contained pieces of both problems without being reducible to either. Burns argued for sustained communication while describing structural competition and warning against demonizing the Chinese people. Precision mattered in both directions. Call every competitor an enemy and ordinary competition starts to look like evidence of hostile intent. Call every adversarial act a rivalry and coercion disappears inside a comfortable word.

The label has to follow the behavior, not replace it. What is the other government doing? What does it want? Where does it compromise, and where does it refuse? Which signals are theater and which are preparation? The answers can change. Enemies become allies. Allies quarrel. Competitors cooperate in one domain while threatening each other in another.

Baseball gave Burns a language for antagonism without annihilation because the league itself was never in doubt. That is exactly where the analogy stops being cute and starts being useful.

A Red Sox–Yankees game presupposes another game.

Diplomacy sometimes has to make the next meeting possible.
