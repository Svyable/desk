# Epilogue — The Next Inning

The Red Sox arrived in Nicholas Burns's life before diplomacy did. He inherited a team already deep into its own argument with Boston: players he had never seen, losses that people older than he was could still locate by inning, and eventually a championship that changed the meaning of the old story without erasing it.

Baseball changes while insisting on continuity. Rules, rosters, owners, workers, audiences and technologies change. A game long defended as purely American becomes more visibly international with every generation. The Red Sox story Burns received was no cleaner than the country around it: devotion and exclusion, civic pride and civic failure, labor fights, reinvention, championships and public grief.

Ken Burns has spent decades arguing that baseball matters as an American subject because the country's contradictions keep turning up inside it: race, labor, class, immigration, celebrity, commerce, belonging. The game does not sit outside American history as relief from the complicated parts. It carries them.

A diplomat enters history in much the same inconvenient place: the middle. No ambassador gets a country at the beginning. The host government remembers an earlier promise, insult, war or ambassador. Washington has instructions; the other side has its own. For a while, one person carries the title through all of that accumulated memory.

The Red Sox cap never negotiated a treaty, rescued an alliance or made another government agree with the United States. It did something smaller. It made the wearer visible. Boston remained beneath the protocol, along with family, humor and the irrational loyalty of a baseball fan. The American representative had not stopped being a particular American because the seal was behind him.

There are limits to that particularity. Personality does not repeal instructions, friendship does not erase incompatible interests, and a joke cannot solve a nuclear dispute. But the title cannot enter the room by itself either. The United States cannot shake a hand. NATO cannot remember who kept a promise. An embassy cannot hear hesitation in a sentence. People do those things while carrying institutions larger than themselves.

Burns spent a career inside that tension: recognizable enough to another person to be read as human, constrained enough by office to know he was not speaking only for himself. Then other people took the offices he had held and inherited conversations he had once carried.

That is less majestic than institutional continuity usually sounds. Work survives because somebody else picks it up.

The book began with a fan because fandom made the diplomat easier to see. By the end, the cap matters less than the exchange underneath it: a government needs a person to speak for it, and another person has to decide what those words mean.

Then somebody else gets the file.
