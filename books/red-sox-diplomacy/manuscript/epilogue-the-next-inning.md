# Epilogue — The Next Inning

The Red Sox arrived in Nicholas Burns's life before diplomacy did.

A baseball fan inherits a story already underway. Parents remember players their children never saw. A city carries losses forward until people born later can still name the inning. Then one result changes the meaning of what came before without erasing it.

The inheritance is not clean. The Red Sox story Burns received contained devotion and exclusion, civic pride and civic failure, labor fights, reinvention, championships and public grief. That messiness belongs to the American backdrop of this book.

The fan enters in the middle.

So does the diplomat.

No ambassador gets a country at the beginning. The argument is already happening. The host government remembers an earlier promise, insult, war or ambassador. Washington has instructions. The other side has instructions too.

Then, for a while, one person wears the title.

The Red Sox cap never negotiated a treaty, rescued an alliance or made another government agree with the United States. It did something smaller: it made the wearer visible.

Boston was still there beneath the protocol. Family was there. Humor was there. Irrational loyalty to a baseball team was there. The American representative had not stopped being a particular American because the seal was behind him.

That particularity has limits. Personality does not repeal instructions. Friendship does not erase incompatible interests. A joke cannot solve a nuclear dispute.

But the title cannot enter the room by itself either.

The United States cannot shake a hand. NATO cannot remember who kept a promise. An embassy cannot hear hesitation in a sentence. People do those things while carrying institutions larger than themselves.

Burns spent a career inside that tension. The job required him to be legible enough to another human being without forgetting that he represented a country rather than himself.

Neither personality nor profession guaranteed good judgment.

Nothing does.

Experience can accumulate without becoming wisdom. Officials defend positions they later revise. Institutions remember some things and lose others. Another decision arrives before history has finished grading the last one.

After Beijing, Burns returned to Harvard. Other people took the offices he had held. Students who had never worked in an embassy began deciding what they thought diplomacy was for. Officials Burns would never meet inherited conversations he had once carried.

Public service survives the public servant that way—not as a monument, but as work passed forward.

The book began with a fan because fandom made the diplomat easier to see.

It ends with the ordinary fact underneath the titles.

A government needs a person to speak for it.

Another person has to decide what those words mean.

Then the conversation continues.
