# Nicholas Burns personal-social Red Sox chronology

This file keeps `@RNicholasBurns` evidence separate from the official ambassadorial `@USAmbChina` corpus.

That separation is substantive, not cosmetic. A personal-account post can establish continuity of fandom and self-description before or after government service, but it cannot be silently imported into the ambassadorial record or used to prove a China-era event that has its own evidentiary requirements.

## Evidence rule

Personal-social material is graded as follows:

- **personal-social primary** — recoverable original `x.com/RNicholasBurns/status/...` or `twitter.com/RNicholasBurns/status/...` URL with clear authorship;
- **personal-social preservation** — a stable third-party capture that preserves Burns's account attribution and the relevant text/profile state but not the original status ID;
- **institutional continuity evidence** — an authoritative biography or institutional page describing his Red Sox identity; useful for continuity, not a tweet.

Do not upgrade a preservation record into a primary tweet by guessing a status ID or date.

## Relationship to older primary evidence

The social chronology should be read alongside stronger older non-social primary evidence already in the book's research corpus:

- **1997:** Burns publicly called himself a long-suffering Boston Red Sox fan.
- **2005:** he described himself as a native-born member of Red Sox Nation in public remarks.
- **2005:** during a State Department briefing, he joked about the department shutting down so employees could watch a Red Sox postseason game.

Those sources matter because they remove any need to ask the social record to carry the entire continuity argument. The personal account instead shows that Burns later chose to make the same identity legible in digital public life.

## Evidentiary synthesis

The safest chronology is therefore:

1. long-standing Red Sox identity is established by pre-social-era primary remarks;
2. before Beijing, `@RNicholasBurns` publicly described its owner as a loyal member of Red Sox Nation;
3. during Beijing, the separate official `@USAmbChina` account repeatedly allowed Boston sports identity to enter diplomatic communication;
4. after Beijing, Burns returned to `@RNicholasBurns` and continued talking about the Red Sox in an ordinary fan register.

This is stronger than a claim that Burns adopted baseball as a diplomatic branding exercise. The record instead supports continuity: an identity already present for decades was allowed to remain visible when he became an ambassador again.
