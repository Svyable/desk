# October 19, 2004 — Boston skips Yankee Stadium batting practice and a clubhouse ritual starts

## Finding

A familiar 2004 story about the Red Sox taking a pregame drink becomes more useful when reconstructed as clubhouse and manager history rather than folklore. MLB's later oral-history-style account preserves Kevin Millar's first-person explanation of how the ritual began before ALCS Game 6 at Yankee Stadium.

Millar said the weather was misty, rainy, and cold and that he did not want Boston taking visiting batting practice on the field while Yankee Stadium played "Yankeeography" material over the sound system. He told manager Terry Francona the team would hit in the indoor cages instead. According to Millar, while walking downstairs he encountered a bottle of Jack Daniel's, grabbed it as a joke, and players took a symbolic pregame drink. Boston won Game 6. The ritual then continued through the remaining postseason games.

Authoritative retrospective source:
- Major League Baseball / Ian Browne, “How the 2004 Red Sox became the 'Idiots',” May 19, 2021: https://www.mlb.com/news/2004-red-sox-idiots-nickname-explained

A later account of Francona's tenure adds an important managerial boundary. Francona said he did not know about the ritual until he walked into the clubhouse before World Series Game 3 in St. Louis. That means the episode should not be narrated as a Francona-created motivational device. His observable role at the beginning was narrower: Millar told him the players were going to hit in the cages rather than take outdoor batting practice; the drinking ritual developed among players without the manager initially knowing about it.

Supporting retrospective source:
- Major League Baseball / Ian Browne, “Book details highs, lows of Francona's tenure,” January 22, 2013: https://www.mlb.com/news/book-details-highs-lows-of-terry-franconas-tenure-with-red-sox/c-41169896

MLB's 2024 reporting on *The Comeback: 2004 Boston Red Sox* supplies unusually strong retrospective corroboration for the physical ritual. Director Colin Barnicle said Millar eventually located personal camcorder footage showing the large bottle and players taking the drink from Gatorade cups; the documentary used that footage. This is not contemporaneous press coverage, but it is retrospective audiovisual evidence originating with a participant rather than memory alone.

Additional audiovisual provenance:
- Major League Baseball / Ian Browne, “Spoiler! 2004 Red Sox tale worth retelling, even with the ending known,” October 21, 2024: https://www.mlb.com/redsox/news/documentary-recounts-2004-red-sox-comeback-vs-yankees/

There is also a provenance problem worth preserving rather than smoothing away. Pedro Martínez later described the substance and start of the ritual differently, saying a Dominican mamajuana mixture was involved and placing an earlier version of the practice around Game 4. Contemporary-to-2004 public retellings by Millar emphasized Jack Daniel's and Game 6. The exact liquid and first occurrence therefore should not be narrated with false certainty unless the manuscript explicitly attributes the competing memories.

## Why this is narratively valuable

The useful story is not that alcohol made Boston win. It is the operating boundary inside a famous clubhouse.

Before Game 6, a veteran player says the club will move batting practice indoors rather than follow the normal visiting routine at Yankee Stadium. Francona allows the change. Downstairs, a player-generated ritual develops that the manager later said he did not even know about until the World Series. The team keeps repeating it as the wins accumulate.

That distinction rewards knowledgeable Red Sox readers because it separates three things often collapsed into one legend: the decision to skip outdoor batting practice; the players' private ritual; and the manager's later discovery of it. It also gives Francona texture without manufacturing a leadership lesson. He did not have to author every clubhouse behavior to manage the club.

The physical evidence is unusually useful. Twenty years later, Millar's own camcorder footage surfaced and was incorporated into an MLB-associated documentary, showing players with the bottle and Gatorade cups. That does not settle every remembered detail about when the practice began or what was in every cup, but it moves the core ritual beyond anecdote.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- Millar retrospectively said Boston skipped outdoor batting practice before ALCS Game 6 and hit in the cages instead;
- Millar said he told Francona about the batting-practice change;
- Millar later described encountering Jack Daniel's downstairs and players beginning a symbolic pregame ritual;
- Francona later said he did not know about the drinking ritual until World Series Game 3;
- MLB reported in 2024 that Millar supplied personal camcorder footage showing the bottle and players drinking from Gatorade cups;
- participant memories differ about the exact substance and when the ritual first began.

Not safe:

- saying alcohol improved performance or caused any win;
- claiming Francona designed, approved, or initially knew about the drinking ritual;
- reconstructing private dialogue beyond sourced recollections;
- claiming every player participated or consumed the same substance;
- smoothing the Millar/Martínez chronology conflict into a single certain account;
- presenting the ritual as evidence that professional preparation did not matter;
- turning it into a generic lesson about looseness, culture, leadership, diplomacy, or superstition.

## Manuscript recommendation

Use as a compact clubhouse beat if the manuscript needs to show the limits of managerial control during the 2004 comeback. The clean sequence is: bad weather and the Yankee Stadium visiting environment; Millar tells Francona the players will hit inside; a player ritual starts downstairs; Francona later says he does not learn about that part until the World Series; participant footage eventually corroborates the physical ritual.

Keep the disputed chronology explicit if the drink itself matters. The strongest narrative fact is not what was in the cup. It is that a famous postseason ritual operated for several games below the manager's awareness.

## Evidentiary level

**Authoritative MLB retrospective with named participant recollections + later MLB reporting on participant-originated camcorder footage.** Strong for the broad clubhouse mechanics and Francona's stated lack of initial knowledge. The exact substance and first game remain contested in retrospective accounts and should be attributed rather than harmonized.
