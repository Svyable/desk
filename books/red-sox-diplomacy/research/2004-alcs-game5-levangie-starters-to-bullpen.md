# October 18, 2004 — the bullpen catcher sends the starters out

## Finding

Late in Game 5 of the 2004 ALCS, with Boston facing elimination for a second straight night, a small piece of clubhouse/bullpen machinery became visible.

SABR's reconstruction of the 14-inning game records that after Keith Foulke held New York scoreless in the top of the ninth, three Red Sox starters — Curt Schilling, Derek Lowe, and Tim Wakefield — walked across the field to the Boston bullpen. The source for why they appeared there is unusually specific: bullpen catcher Dana LeVangie later told Bill Nowlin that he called from the bullpen and had a message relayed telling the starters to get their gloves and walk across the field. LeVangie described the move retrospectively as intensifying the park and sending New York a message that Boston was prepared to use everyone available.

Authoritative historical synthesis with named first-person oral-history provenance:
- Society for American Baseball Research, “October 18, 2004: David Ortiz’s walk-off single in 14th lifts Red Sox in Game 5”: https://sabr.org/gamesproj/game/october-18-2004-david-ortizs-walk-off-single-in-14th-lifts-red-sox-in-game-5/
- SABR identifies the underlying source as Dana LeVangie interview with Bill Nowlin, June 7, 2013, used in Allan Wood and Bill Nowlin, *Don’t Let Us Win Tonight*.

The baseball context makes the gesture operational rather than merely theatrical. Boston had already used Pedro Martínez and Mike Timlin. Foulke pitched the ninth. Bronson Arroyo followed for two innings, then Alan Embree. Wakefield ultimately entered in the 12th and finished the game, working three scoreless innings before David Ortiz's 14th-inning single ended it. SABR's reconstruction also records that Doug Mirabelli had caught Wakefield warming in the bullpen, even though Jason Varitek remained in the game and had almost no regular-season experience catching Wakefield — the battery problem documented separately in the Wakefield dossier.

## Why this is narratively valuable

The familiar shorthand for the 2004 comeback is “all hands on deck.” This supplies an actual institutional mechanism behind the phrase.

The person initiating the visible movement was not Francona, Epstein, Schilling, or another star. According to LeVangie's later account, it was the bullpen catcher. He used the communication chain available to him to get three starting pitchers, gloves in hand, from the dugout/clubhouse side of the operation to the bullpen during an elimination game. One of them, Wakefield, was eventually needed.

That is useful Red Sox history because it makes the organization larger than its famous decision-makers. A bullpen catcher recognized what the game had become, sent a message, and the pitching staff physically reconfigured itself around the possibility of emergency work.

The sequence complements, but does not duplicate, the recent Wakefield dossier. That note explains why Wakefield was available and the unusual Varitek battery. This note explains how the club visibly moved starting pitchers into the bullpen before Wakefield was called.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- SABR records Schilling, Lowe, and Wakefield walking to the Red Sox bullpen in the ninth inning of Game 5;
- SABR attributes the reason for the move to a 2013 interview with bullpen catcher Dana LeVangie;
- LeVangie retrospectively said he called from the bullpen and had a message relayed telling the starters to get their gloves and walk across the field;
- his stated purpose and interpretation of the gesture are retrospective and should remain attributed to him;
- Wakefield eventually entered in the 12th and pitched through the 14th;
- Boston won on Ortiz's walk-off single after 5 hours, 49 minutes.

Not safe:

- inventing who answered LeVangie's call or the exact relay chain beyond his sourced account;
- reconstructing dialogue beyond any verified quotation in the oral history;
- claiming Francona ordered the three starters out unless a source establishes that instruction;
- claiming the gesture intimidated the Yankees or changed their behavior;
- assigning thoughts or emotions to Schilling, Lowe, Wakefield, Francona, or New York players;
- turning the episode into a generic lesson about leadership, morale, diplomacy, or teamwork.

## Manuscript recommendation

Use if the 2004 section needs one compact clubhouse/dugout image that is both surprising and operational. The strongest shape is to keep LeVangie visible: in the ninth inning, Boston's bullpen catcher called in and asked for three starters with their gloves. Schilling, Lowe, and Wakefield crossed the field to the bullpen. Hours later, Wakefield was the one still pitching when Ortiz ended the game.

Do not claim the movement caused anything. The payoff is that the famous “everyone was available” posture had a literal communication chain and a named staff member behind it.

## Evidentiary level

**SABR Games Project historical reconstruction + named first-person retrospective oral history (Dana LeVangie, interviewed by Bill Nowlin in 2013).** Strong for the observable movement and LeVangie's later account of initiating it; retrospective for purpose and perceived effect, and not evidence of Yankees reaction or managerial instruction.
