# Chapter 17 visual archive — The Professor in the Cheap Seats

## Editorial job

Chapter 17 is about a change in the status of experience.

Inside government, Burns spoke with delegated authority. At Harvard, his experience still carried weight, but it became material students and scholars could compare, question and revise. The visual sequence should make that change legible without pretending that leaving government made Burns neutral or that practitioner experience became automatically correct.

## ROLE SHIFT 01

Asset: `../media/ch17-role-shift.svg`

The left side represents delegated government authority: a spokesman's answer can become the government's answer; an ambassador's communication carries institutional standing and responsibility.

The right side represents practitioner authority in the university: experience supplies access, evidence and credibility, but not sovereign control over interpretation.

Burns's own useful formulation comes from Harvard Kennedy School's 2011 `Practice Makes Perfect` interview. He argued that professors of practice who spent most of their careers in public service can give students a perspective rooted in having seen directly how governments **succeed and fail**.

Source:
- Harvard Kennedy School, `Practice Makes Perfect`, Autumn 2011: https://www.hks.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/magazine/archives/autumn_2011.pdf

The object is editorial synthesis, not a Burns-authored taxonomy.

## TEACHING ARCHIVE 01

Asset: `../media/ch17-teaching-archive.svg`

Harvard's American Secretaries of State Project preserved extended videotaped interviews with former secretaries about consequential negotiations. HKS describes those interviews as teaching material used in graduate courses on diplomacy, negotiation and leadership.

Sources:
- Harvard Kennedy School, `Research Footprint: Diplomacy programs at HKS`: https://www.hks.harvard.edu/more/hks-magazine/research-footprint-diplomacy-programs-hks
- Harvard Kennedy School, `Negotiation and Diplomacy`: https://www.hks.harvard.edu/courses/negotiation-and-diplomacy

The diagram adds an editorial step the chapter itself argues for: practitioner memory becomes more useful when compared against documents, other witnesses, scholarship and consequences before being treated as interpretation.

The project interviews are therefore evidence, not verdict.

## Student witnesses

Two named HKS profiles provide limited but useful evidence about individual student experience:

- Hainer Sibrian described Burns's diplomacy course as a useful counterpart to a course about what happens when diplomacy fails and emphasized the limits of available instruments.
  - https://www.hks.harvard.edu/more/student-life/student-stories/upholding-scaffolding-liberal-democracy
- Christy Vines said a diplomacy class with Burns changed her academic direction toward foreign policy and national security.
  - https://www.hks.harvard.edu/alumni/connect/community-stories/christy-vines-mcmpa-2010-leading-empathic-intelligence

These are two student witnesses, not evidence of a universal classroom effect. They remain in prose rather than being promoted into a broad impact graphic.

## Guardrails

- Do not imply academics have no authority; the distinction is between delegated state authority and contestable scholarly/practitioner authority.
- Do not portray practitioner recollection as inherently more accurate than documents or other witnesses.
- Do not infer general teaching effectiveness from two student profiles.
- Do not back-project the current `Negotiation and Diplomacy` syllabus onto every course Burns taught from 2008 to 2021.
- Do not imply Burns left policy argument behind when he entered Harvard.

The chapter's visual thesis is simple: the same experience that once constrained Burns to speak carefully for an institution could later be opened up, compared and argued with.
