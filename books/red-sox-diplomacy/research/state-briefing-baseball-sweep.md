# State Department Baseball Sweep — 1997

This file isolates Nicholas Burns's baseball exchanges at the State Department podium during his final months as spokesman. The purpose is not to collect every joke. It is to establish frequency, function, and context: when baseball entered the briefing, what Burns did with it, and how quickly he returned to policy.

## Why this matters

The surviving 1997 archive shows a recurring pattern rather than a one-off persona. Baseball repeatedly appears in the opening minute or two of briefings, often before Burns welcomes foreign journalists or pivots into NATO, Turkey, Cyprus, the Middle East, Zaire, or other substantive business.

That pattern supports a narrow claim for *Red Sox Diplomacy*: Burns used baseball as a stable informal register inside an extremely formal representative role. It created familiarity without confusing the joke for policy.

## January 6

Burns responds to sports chatter with: “This is not a sports briefing — right?” The line is useful precisely because it acknowledges the running joke.

## April 1

On April Fools' Day Burns explicitly says the briefing team avoided a foreign-policy prank because it could create real consequences and instead “decided to stick to baseball.”

This is unusually revealing. Baseball is not merely recreation here; it is treated as the safe rhetorical zone adjacent to policy.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9704/970401.html

## April 2

Burns opens by greeting visiting Cypriot journalists and asking: “Do you have baseball in Cyprus?” The exchange leads into Secretary Madeleine Albright's ceremonial first pitch in Baltimore.

The scene matters because baseball becomes the first conversational bridge to foreign visitors inside an official briefing.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9704/970402.html

## April 3

Burns notes a ninth-inning Red Sox comeback and says it was “a good day for baseball” before moving immediately into Secretary Albright's White House schedule and foreign-policy business.

This is a clean example of register switching.

## May 9

Burns opens with Red Sox distress and jokes that his tie is being worn for luck: “we need it very badly.” He then welcomes an international visitor participating through USIA and begins the briefing.

## May 13

Burns introduces a Dutch journalist and immediately asks whether he knows the Red Sox and Fenway Park. After a reporter supplies a Bert Blyleven trivia question, Burns jokes: “This is the baseball briefing. You thought you'd come into a foreign policy briefing. We only talk about baseball here.” Then: “Okay, foreign policy?”

This is among the strongest scenes for the book because Burns himself narrates the transition between the personal and official registers.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9705/970513.html

## June 4

The Roger Clemens exchange produces the most vivid cluster of lines:

- “We're still bitter in Boston.”
- “We're poor losers in Boston.”
- “We're praying. We're saying our Novenas for the Red Sox.”

Later Burns welcomes the room to the State Department “daily briefing on baseball and foreign policy” before announcing Richard Holbrooke's appointment as special presidential emissary on Cyprus.

This scene is useful because comedy and consequential policy literally share the same transition.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9706/970604db.html

## June 13

Baseball returns through discussion of Secretary Albright's Opening Day baseballs and the press-room culture around the game.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9706/970613db.html

## June 16

This is one of the most valuable newly surfaced exchanges.

After discussion of a Red Sox win Burns says, “Really, I'm speaking for Red Sox nation.” A reporter asks why Red Sox fans are always described as rabid. Burns replies that they “have to be foolhardy to be Red Sox fans,” then launches into a joking explanation involving Calvinism, predestination, Puritan Boston, and the team's destiny to fail.

Most importantly, Burns volunteers a family detail: his mother was a Red Sox fan; his father said the team had broken his heart in the 1930s and advised his children never to root for them. Burns adds: “But I didn't follow his advice.”

This is our best sourced material so far on the intergenerational origin of his fandom. It should replace speculation in any childhood/family section.

The briefing then pivots directly into Turkey and NATO.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/www/briefings/9706/970616db.html

## July 16

The State Department-Senate baseball game appears in the briefing, documenting that baseball also functioned as part of Washington institutional culture, not only Burns's personal fandom.

## Editorial conclusions

### Supported

- The Red Sox identity was habitual and publicly recognizable by 1997.
- Reporters expected and actively elicited baseball exchanges with Burns.
- Burns often placed baseball immediately beside substantive policy rather than in separate sports settings.
- He used self-deprecating fandom more often than triumphalist fandom in the pre-2004 period.
- Baseball repeatedly helped him greet or include foreign visitors.
- The June 16 exchange provides direct evidence of family transmission and resistance: mother a fan; father disillusioned; Burns choosing the team anyway.

### Not yet supported

- First Fenway visit.
- Specific childhood games, players, radio habits, or rituals.
- A precise age at which Burns became a fan.
- A claim that his family fandom caused later diplomatic habits.

## Manuscript implications

The material justifies a full chapter such as **The Briefing Room Bleachers**. It can also strengthen **Boston Before Washington** with the father/mother material, provided the scene is attributed to Burns's own 1997 remarks rather than reconstructed as childhood memory.
