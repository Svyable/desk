# State Department Baseball Sweep — 1997

This file isolates Nicholas Burns's baseball exchanges at the State Department podium during his final months as spokesman. The purpose is not to collect every joke. It is to establish frequency, function, and context: when baseball entered the briefing, what Burns did with it, and how quickly he returned to policy.

## Why this matters

The surviving 1997 archive shows a recurring pattern rather than a one-off persona. Baseball repeatedly appears in the opening minute or two of briefings, often before Burns welcomes foreign journalists or pivots into NATO, Turkey, Cyprus, the Middle East, Zaire, Cuba, or other substantive business.

That pattern supports a narrow claim for *Red Sox Diplomacy*: Burns used baseball as a stable informal register inside an extremely formal representative role. It created familiarity without confusing the joke for policy.

The deeper sweep also shows something more: reporters learned the register and actively participated in it. They prompted Burns for Clemens reactions, tested visitors' team loyalties, supplied scores and trivia, and expected the spokesman to respond. The persona was therefore relational rather than a series of isolated prepared lines.

## January 6

Burns responds to sports chatter with: “This is not a sports briefing — right?” The line is useful precisely because it acknowledges the running joke.

## February 14 — spring-training prediction and Clemens grievance

Burns welcomes Ellen McHugh from his hometown of Wellesley, identifies her as a major Red Sox fan, notes that pitchers and catchers are reporting, repeats the Roger Clemens “traitor” joke, and predicts the Red Sox will win in 1997.

The prediction is wrong, but that is useful. Burns is not merely invoking a team as hometown branding; he is following the baseball calendar and making ordinary fan predictions in public.

## March 7 — the rules

In banter with the room, Burns lays out a hierarchy: Red Sox first, Tigers tolerated, Yankees hated. The joking formulation matters because the rivalry has already become an understood press-room convention.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9703/970307.html

## March 11 — “we” are Red Sox fans

Burns welcomes students from Delaware and jokes about whether they belong to Orioles or Phillies territory. He then says the briefing room has already decided that “we” are Red Sox fans.

This is a small but revealing linguistic move: a personal loyalty becomes a temporary collective identity for the room.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9703/970311.html

## April 1

On April Fools' Day Burns explicitly says the briefing team avoided a foreign-policy prank because it could create real consequences and instead “decided to stick to baseball.”

This is unusually revealing. Baseball is not merely recreation here; it is treated as the safe rhetorical zone adjacent to policy.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9704/970401.html

## April 2

Burns opens by greeting visiting Cypriot journalists and asking whether they have baseball in Cyprus. The exchange leads into Secretary Madeleine Albright's ceremonial first pitch in Baltimore.

The scene matters because baseball becomes the first conversational bridge to foreign visitors inside an official briefing.

## April 3

Burns notes a ninth-inning Red Sox comeback and calls it a good day for baseball before moving immediately into Secretary Albright's White House schedule and foreign-policy business.

This is a clean example of register switching.

## April 15

A routine briefing includes Red Sox/Orioles discussion and Tim Wakefield banter before substantive policy. Burns responds to current-game detail about Wakefield's knuckleball and pitch count.

## April 17 — fandom as social biography

Burns introduces a friend who is leading visiting French and American students and identifies her, among other things, as a huge Red Sox fan. He then turns directly to Secretary Albright's work.

This is a different use from scorekeeping: baseball becomes a compact way to tell the room who a person is.

## May 9

Burns opens with Red Sox distress and jokes that his tie is being worn for luck. He then welcomes an international visitor participating through USIA and begins the briefing.

## May 13

Burns introduces a Dutch journalist and immediately asks whether he knows the Red Sox and Fenway Park. After a reporter supplies a Bert Blyleven trivia question, Burns jokingly reframes the room as a baseball briefing and then explicitly pivots back to foreign policy.

This is among the strongest scenes for the book because Burns himself narrates the transition between the personal and official registers.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9705/970513.html

## June 3 — current-season statistical fluency

A joke about the State Department website receiving 1.7 million hits turns into a Red Sox pitching joke. Burns adds that Boston's pitching is third worst in the American League.

That specificity matters. He is not merely repeating inherited Red Sox mythology; he is following the current club closely enough to discuss its standing relative to the league.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9706/970603db.html

## June 4

The Roger Clemens exchange produces the most vivid cluster of lines. Burns jokes about Boston bitterness, poor losing, prayers, and the team's need for resuscitation.

Later he welcomes the room to a briefing on baseball and foreign policy before announcing Richard Holbrooke's appointment as special presidential emissary on Cyprus.

This scene is useful because comedy and consequential policy literally share the same transition.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9706/970604db.html

## June 13

Baseball returns through discussion of Secretary Albright's Opening Day baseballs and the press-room culture around the game.

## June 16 — family history and fan fatalism

After discussion of a Red Sox win Burns says he is speaking for Red Sox Nation. A reporter asks why Red Sox fans are described as rabid. Burns replies with a joking explanation involving foolhardiness, Calvinism, predestination, Puritan Boston, and a destiny to fail.

Most importantly, Burns volunteers a family detail: his mother was a Red Sox fan; his father said the team had broken his heart in the 1930s and advised the children never to root for them. Burns says he ignored that advice.

This is the strongest sourced material so far on the intergenerational origin of his fandom. It should replace speculation in any childhood/family section.

The briefing then pivots directly into Turkey and NATO.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/www/briefings/9706/970616db.html

## July 8 — New England fan geography

Burns identifies Waterville, Maine, as Red Sox territory and asks a visiting intern whether he is a fan. When the intern admits to supporting the Yankees, Burns jokingly threatens to expel him from the briefing before granting an All-Star Game reprieve.

This is useful evidence for the territorial idea Burns later develops more explicitly: the Sox are not only Boston's team but a regional identity across New England.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9707/970708db.html

## July 14 — baseball becomes foreign policy

A reporter asks about Cuba canceling a baseball team's planned visit to Connecticut. Burns argues that Castro should let the players travel and suggests that fear of player defections is the real issue. He then jokes that more Cuban players would be welcome in the American League, so long as they join the Red Sox rather than the Yankees.

This is materially different from ordinary sports banter. Baseball is the actual subject through which Burns discusses freedom of movement, defection, state control, and U.S.-Cuba relations.

It therefore belongs in the book as an example of the line between baseball-as-icebreaker and baseball-as-substantive-diplomatic subject.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9707/970714db.html

## July 16

The State Department-Senate baseball game appears in the briefing, documenting that baseball also functioned as part of Washington institutional culture, not only Burns's personal fandom.

Source: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9707/970716db.html

## The Roger Clemens running story

Contemporaneous reporting from December 1996 records Burns calling Clemens a “traitor” after the pitcher left Boston for Toronto and joking that the move would have been even worse had Clemens joined the Yankees.

That line returns in February 1997. By June, reporters themselves are prompting Burns for reactions to Clemens's success in Toronto. The running exchange shows that the press corps had learned Burns's baseball persona well enough to sustain it over months.

## Editorial conclusions

### Supported

- The Red Sox identity was habitual and publicly recognizable by 1997.
- Reporters expected and actively elicited baseball exchanges with Burns.
- Burns followed current games, standings, pitching performance, personnel moves, and individual players rather than relying only on generic team references.
- He often placed baseball immediately beside substantive policy rather than in separate sports settings.
- He used self-deprecating fandom more often than triumphalist fandom in the pre-2004 period.
- Baseball repeatedly helped him greet or include foreign visitors.
- He used regional baseball geography — Boston, Maine, Connecticut, New Jersey — as social shorthand.
- The June 16 exchange provides direct evidence of family transmission and resistance: mother a fan; father disillusioned; Burns choosing the team anyway.
- The July 14 Cuba exchange shows baseball could become a substantive foreign-policy subject rather than merely a rapport device.

### Not yet supported

- First Fenway visit.
- Specific childhood Red Sox games, radio habits, or rituals.
- A precise age at which Burns became a fan.
- A claim that his family fandom caused later diplomatic habits.
- A claim that every baseball exchange was strategically planned rather than spontaneous press-room culture.

## Manuscript implications

The material justifies a full chapter such as **The Briefing Room Bleachers**. It can also strengthen **Boston Before Washington** with the father/mother material and **The Useful Fan** with the evidence that reporters themselves co-created the register.

The Cuba episode should be kept distinct. It is not merely comic texture; it demonstrates that a sport Burns loved could also become entangled with migration, authoritarian control, defection, and international politics.
