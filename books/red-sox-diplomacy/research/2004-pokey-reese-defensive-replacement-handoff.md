# October 2004 — Francona keeps handing late innings to Pokey Reese

## Finding

The 2004 postseason is remembered through Boston's stars, but Terry Francona repeatedly used a player who had lost his starting job as a late-inning defensive instrument. Pokey Reese's postseason is a clean example of roster depth becoming managerial machinery.

SABR's biography of Reese records the sequence. Reese opened 2004 as Boston's shortstop while Nomar Garciaparra was injured, moved to second when Garciaparra returned, then missed time with an oblique injury. By September, Garciaparra had been traded, Orlando Cabrera was at shortstop, and Mark Bellhorn had established himself at second. Reese no longer had an everyday position. SABR says Francona nevertheless had a postseason role for him: replacing Bellhorn defensively in late innings when Boston had a lead.

Authoritative historical source:
- Society for American Baseball Research, “Pokey Reese”: https://sabr.org/bioproj/person/pokey-reese/

That role appears repeatedly in October. Reese entered ALDS Games 1 and 2 as a defensive replacement. In ALDS Game 3 he instead ran for Bellhorn in the tenth and scored on David Ortiz's series-ending home run. In the ALCS he replaced Bellhorn in Games 4, 6, and 7. In the ninth inning of Game 7 at Yankee Stadium, Reese was at second when Ruben Sierra hit the final groundball; Reese fielded it and threw to first to finish Boston's unprecedented comeback from three games down.

SABR's Game 7 reconstruction and MLB's tenth-anniversary history independently preserve the final-out mechanics.

- Society for American Baseball Research, “October 20, 2004: ‘Hell freezes over’; Red Sox complete historic ALCS comeback over Yankees in Game 7”: https://sabr.org/gamesproj/game/october-20-2004-hell-freezes-over-red-sox-complete-historic-alcs-comeback-over-yankees-in-game-7/

The pattern continued in the World Series. Historical postseason records show Reese appearing in all four games despite receiving only one plate appearance, closing each game in the field. Boston's bench was therefore not merely unused depth during the sweep: Francona repeatedly changed the defensive configuration after Boston obtained a lead.

Supporting historical record:
- RedSoxDiehard player chronology, “Pokey Reese — 2004 World Champions”: https://www.redsoxdiehard.com/worldseries/players/reese.html

## Why this is narratively valuable

The famous final defensive substitution of 2004 is Doug Mientkiewicz taking over at first before Keith Foulke records the World Series' last out. Reese supplies a longer and less familiar pattern behind that kind of move.

He began the season as an everyday middle infielder and ended it as a specialist. Francona used him to replace Bellhorn when the score made defense more valuable than Bellhorn's next plate appearance. That assignment put Reese on the field for the final out of the ALCS and in the field at the end of every World Series game.

For knowledgeable Red Sox readers, the useful detail is the repetition. Reese did not accidentally happen to be standing at second for Ruben Sierra's grounder. His presence was the product of a role the manager had used throughout the postseason. The player who had lost his starting job after injury and the deadline remake remained part of Boston's late-game operating system.

This also complements the recent July 31 trade dossier. That note shows the roster changing when Cabrera and Mientkiewicz arrived. This one shows what happened to a displaced incumbent afterward: rather than disappearing, Reese was reassigned into a narrow postseason job that Francona repeatedly used.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- SABR records Reese opening 2004 as Boston's shortstop, later moving to second, suffering an oblique injury, and returning after Cabrera and Bellhorn occupied the everyday middle-infield jobs;
- SABR explicitly says Francona had a postseason role for Reese as a late defensive replacement for Bellhorn when Boston led;
- Reese entered ALDS Games 1 and 2 defensively and ran for Bellhorn in Game 3;
- Reese appeared in ALCS Games 4, 6, and 7 and was playing second for the final out of Game 7;
- SABR and MLB record Reese fielding Sierra's grounder and throwing to first for the final out of the pennant-clinching game;
- historical postseason records show Reese appearing in all four World Series games while receiving only one plate appearance and finishing each in the field.

Not safe:

- claiming Francona promised Reese this role or reconstructing any conversation about accepting it;
- assigning Reese disappointment, pride, resentment, or satisfaction after losing his starting job;
- claiming Reese's defensive substitutions caused Boston's postseason wins;
- claiming Bellhorn welcomed or resisted being removed;
- treating every Reese appearance as the same tactical decision without checking the individual game state;
- turning the specialist role into a generic lesson about humility, teamwork, diplomacy, or leadership.

## Manuscript recommendation

Use as a compact manager/bench thread if the manuscript needs to show that Francona's 2004 postseason management extended beyond pitchers and famous pinch runners. Establish Reese's changed status after the deadline, then let the October appearances accumulate briefly: defensive replacement in the Division Series; late-inning work during the ALCS comeback; second base for Sierra's final grounder; all four World Series games closed in the field.

The best payoff is procedural rather than sentimental. By Game 7, a groundball reaches the position Boston had deliberately upgraded for the late innings. Reese fields it, throws to first, and the pennant is won. No claim about what he felt is necessary.
