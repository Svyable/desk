# Baltic Withdrawal Dossier — Chapter 6

## Why this case matters

The 1993–94 Russian troop-withdrawal negotiations in Latvia and Estonia provide the strongest documented case yet for Chapter 6, *The Score Is Not the Game*.

The visible result was binary: Russian forces either left or did not. The diplomatic work was not binary. It involved troop timetables, minority rights, housing for returning Russian officers, the Skrunda radar installation in Latvia, environmental cleanup at Paldiski in Estonia, Russian domestic politics, Baltic security fears, and repeated presidential engagement.

Burns's role is specific enough to support a chapter without inflating him into the sole architect of the outcome.

## Burns's formal position

From 1990 to 1995 Burns served on the National Security Council staff. Under President George H. W. Bush he was Director for Soviet and then Russian Affairs. Under President Clinton he became Special Assistant to the President and Senior Director for Russia, Ukraine and Eurasia Affairs, with lead White House responsibility for relations with the fifteen former Soviet republics.

## Burns-specific evidence

### Latvian delegation / Situation Room

Ojars Kalnins's account of U.S.-Latvian diplomacy identifies Burns and Strobe Talbott as central American officials on Latvia and says both played prominent roles in the policy that removed Russian troops.

Kalnins records that in early February 1994 a Latvian delegation including Foreign Minister Georgs Andrejevs and leaders of all eight parliamentary factions spent roughly ninety minutes in the White House Situation Room with Burns. The trip was designed to build Latvian political support for a compromise package with Russia.

The same account says Burns informed Kalnins that President Clinton had proposed a compromise on the disputed Skrunda radar timetable: four years of operation followed by an eighteen-month dismantling period.

Source:
https://www.mfa.gov.lv/media/115/download

### Estonian channel

A contemporaneous Washington Post retrospective credited Burns with urging intensified White House attention to the Estonian problem after meeting Estonian ambassador Toomas Hendrik Ilves through Swedish diplomatic channels.

The article's broader claim is that Clinton repeatedly pressed Yeltsin while Burns and other officials worked the diplomatic details around Estonia.

Source:
https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/opinions/1994/09/04/clintons-secret-successes/565344c7-547f-4bb6-b53c-8bc29218ca40/

### Burns's later public account

As State spokesman in 1996, Burns publicly described the Clinton administration and Sweden as having worked productively with Yeltsin to secure withdrawal from Estonia and Latvia in 1993–94.

Source:
https://www.nato.int/en/news-and-events/events/transcripts/1996/12/10/press-briefingby-nicholas-burns-spokesman

A later scholarly treatment based on Burns's recollections states that he was personally involved in the troop-removal negotiations and quotes him describing Clinton's insistence on visiting Riga in 1994.

Source:
https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-12815-7_3

## The policy mechanics

### Latvia

The withdrawal problem was entangled with:

- a target date of August 31, 1994;
- Russian claims concerning the treatment of Russian-speaking minorities;
- pensions and status for retired Soviet/Russian military personnel;
- the Skrunda early-warning radar complex;
- disagreements over how long Russia could continue operating the radar;
- the political difficulty inside Latvia of accepting compromise with Moscow.

President Clinton's April 30, 1994 statement explicitly said the United States had played an active role with both parties and singled out Sweden as another important contributor.

Source:
https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/statement-the-agreement-withdraw-russian-military-forces-from-latvia

### Estonia

The Estonian problem remained unresolved after Latvia reached agreement. Clinton carried proposals from Estonian president Lennart Meri to Yeltsin at the July 1994 Naples meeting and encouraged a direct Meri-Yeltsin meeting.

The White House also backed:

- housing vouchers for Russian officers returning from Latvia and Estonia;
- $2 million in support for environmental restoration at Paldiski;
- continued pressure for an August 31 deadline;
- minority-rights language aimed at addressing Russian concerns without accepting continued Russian military presence.

Sources:
https://clintonwhitehouse6.archives.gov/1994/07/1994-07-06-president-in-press-conference-with-baltic-presidents.html
https://clintonwhitehouse6.archives.gov/1994/07/1994-07-10-backgrounder-on-clinton-yeltsin-meeting.html

## What the chapter can safely claim

- Burns held the NSC portfolio that directly covered Russia, Ukraine, Eurasia, and the Baltic states.
- He participated personally in diplomacy around Russian troop withdrawal.
- A Latvian diplomatic account places him in extended direct talks with a broad Latvian political delegation and shows him communicating a Clinton compromise proposal on Skrunda.
- Contemporary commentary credits him with helping elevate the Estonia problem inside the White House.
- The outcome required more than insisting on withdrawal; it required dealing with Russian officer resettlement, infrastructure, environmental issues, minority politics, and domestic political constraints in multiple countries.

## What the chapter must not claim

- Burns alone negotiated or caused the withdrawals.
- The United States dictated the settlement.
- Russian minority concerns were merely invented or irrelevant.
- Baltic governments accepted compromise easily.
- The troop withdrawals "solved" Baltic security.
- The Soviet collapse itself was an American diplomatic victory.

## Narrative center

The strongest documented scene is February 1994: a Latvian delegation representing the foreign minister and every parliamentary faction spends about ninety minutes with Burns in the Situation Room while Washington tries to make a politically difficult compromise acceptable enough to preserve the larger objective.

That scene illustrates the chapter's title. The scoreboard says: Russian troops out by August 31. The game consists of everything required to make that line possible.

## Editorial thesis

Diplomatic success often appears cleaner after it happens than while it is being made. The public sees the deadline met. The diplomat spends months on the pieces that do not fit neatly into the headline.
