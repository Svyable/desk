# October 2004 — Wakefield gives up a start and changes the rotation

## Finding

The 2004 ALCS comeback contains a useful piece of manager/clubhouse machinery that is easy to lose beneath the famous individual moments.

In Game 3, New York was routing Boston 19–8. Tim Wakefield, ordinarily a starter, volunteered for mop-up relief work. MLB's later reconstruction records the consequence plainly: by absorbing innings in the blowout, Wakefield gave up his expected Game 4 start and helped preserve Boston's bullpen. Derek Lowe remained available to start Game 4. Lowe later described the larger pitching-staff behavior in similarly practical terms: at that point, pitchers worked when needed and "guys like Wakefield were stepping in."

The handoff kept propagating. MLB's postseason reconstruction notes that Boston used Lowe in Game 4, Pedro Martinez in Game 5, Curt Schilling in Game 6, then Lowe again in Game 7 on two days' rest. Lowe threw six innings of one-run, one-hit baseball on 69 pitches in Game 7 and earned the win. MLB also records that Lowe wound up as the winning pitcher in all three of Boston's postseason clinchers.

A separate MLB retrospective supplies another revealing consequence of Wakefield's role change. When Wakefield entered Game 5 in extra innings, Jason Varitek had almost no recent experience catching him: Wakefield's MLB player record says Doug Mirabelli caught all but 2.1 of Wakefield's 188.1 regular-season innings in 2004. In the 13th, Varitek was charged with three passed balls, yet with runners on second and third he held the strikeout pitch that ended the inning. Wakefield then finished the 14-inning win.

## Why this is narratively valuable

This is not another comeback summary. It exposes the roster mechanics beneath it.

The 19–8 Game 3 loss looked like the point at which Boston's season had become absurd. Inside that loss, however, a starter taking low-leverage innings altered the next game's pitching assignment and protected relievers. That forced a chain of later decisions: Lowe in Game 4; Martinez in Game 5; Schilling in Game 6; Lowe again, on short rest, in Game 7. Wakefield then reappeared in Game 5, where the emergency battery itself became part of the drama because Varitek almost never caught his knuckleball.

For knowledgeable Red Sox readers, the reward is the continuity between an apparently meaningless mop-up assignment and the rotation Boston actually used to survive four elimination games. The manuscript need not call this leadership. The sequence is interesting enough as baseball operations.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- MLB retrospectively reports that Wakefield volunteered to work in relief during the 19–8 Game 3 loss and thereby sacrificed his expected Game 4 start;
- MLB connects that move to preserving the bullpen and Lowe remaining in line for Game 4;
- Lowe later described pitchers such as Wakefield as stepping in where needed;
- Boston subsequently started Lowe, Martinez, Schilling, and Lowe in Games 4–7;
- Wakefield's MLB record says Mirabelli caught all but 2.1 of his regular-season innings in 2004;
- MLB records Varitek's three passed balls in the 13th inning of Game 5 and the inning-ending strikeout of Ruben Sierra;
- Lowe threw six innings in Game 7 on two days' rest and earned the win.

Not safe:

- inventing the conversation in which Wakefield volunteered;
- claiming Francona ordered or resisted the move unless a source supplies that exchange;
- assigning Wakefield a motive beyond the retrospective description of volunteering to save the bullpen;
- claiming the Game 3 relief appearance caused Boston to win the series;
- reconstructing dugout or clubhouse reactions;
- turning the sequence into a generic lesson about sacrifice, diplomacy, teamwork, or crisis management.

## Manuscript recommendation

This is strong enough for a bounded Red Sox-side insertion around the 2004 comeback if the manuscript needs more manager/roster mechanics.

The useful shape begins in the worst game, not the best one: Boston is being beaten 19–8; Wakefield takes innings and relinquishes the next start; Lowe becomes the Game 4 starter; the rotation is effectively re-threaded from there. When Wakefield returns in Game 5, the club is deep enough into improvisation that Varitek is catching a pitcher he had caught for only 2.1 regular-season innings.

Do not tell the reader what the sequence means. Let the handoffs carry it.
