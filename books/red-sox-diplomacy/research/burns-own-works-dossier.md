# Nicholas Burns in His Own Words — Research Dossier

This dossier mines Nicholas Burns’s own speeches, essays, testimony, interviews, and institutional publications for recurring ideas that can give *Red Sox Diplomacy* an intellectual spine deeper than baseball color.

The quotations below are intentionally short. Before publication, revisit the original source, confirm wording, and preserve context.

## 1. 1997: engagement, optimism, public service

### Worcester Polytechnic Institute commencement — May 24, 1997

Burns’s 1997 commencement address is unusually useful because it shows his worldview before the Greece ambassadorship, before September 11, before India and Iran, and long before Beijing.

**Quote:** “America must be a leader.”

**Quote:** “We must be engaged in the world.”

**Quote:** “Be patriotic.”

**Quote:** “Dare to be great.”

**Use:** Establishes early Burns language: engagement over withdrawal, optimism as an American trait, patriotism connected to public service, and an almost Kennedy-era belief in national responsibility.

**Important tension:** The speech contains a very confident post-Cold War formulation of American primacy. Later chapters should let subsequent history complicate rather than silently preserve that confidence.

**Source:** https://1997-2001.state.gov/policy_remarks/970524.burns.html

## 2. 2002: service after September 11

### Boston College commencement — May 20, 2002

Burns frames his own Foreign Service career explicitly as a choice of service.

**Quote:** “I do not have a single regret.”

**Use:** Strong biographical line. It can connect Boston, vocation, post-9/11 public service, and the emotional cost of a career spent abroad.

He also describes the central American foreign-policy choice as a recurring contest between isolation and engagement.

**Use:** This language echoes the 1997 speech and gives us a recurring Burns binary that can be traced forward.

**Source:** https://www.bc.edu/content/bc-web/bcnews/campus-community/alumni/r--nicholas-burns-2002-commencement-address.html

## 3. 2002: NATO after September 11

### NATO Defense College graduation address — February 8, 2002

Burns addresses diplomats, defense officials, and military officers months after September 11 and argues for a renewed core commitment to NATO.

**Use:** This is useful less for a single epigram than for the continuity of his alliance thinking. He treats NATO as an institution that must adapt rather than as a sentimental inheritance.

**Source:** https://avalon.law.yale.edu/sept11/burns_003.asp

## 4. 2006: citizen diplomacy

### National Council for International Visitors — February 17, 2006

Burns explicitly elevates “citizen diplomacy” and international exchange as part of the State Department’s mission.

**Use:** This is central to *Red Sox Diplomacy*. The Greece baseball story should be placed inside Burns’s broader belief that diplomacy is not confined to formal negotiations. Exchanges, visitors, sports, universities, and ordinary Americans can all represent the country.

**Source:** https://2001-2009.state.gov/p/us/rm/2006/61953.htm

## 5. 2006: commencement and public service

### Northeastern University commencement — May 6, 2006

Burns receives an honorary degree specifically in public service and addresses graduates in Boston.

**Use:** Mine full text during manuscript drafting for Burns’s language about Massachusetts, public service, international engagement, and generational responsibility.

**Source:** https://2001-2009.state.gov/p/us/rm/2006/66226.htm

## 6. 2007: strategic patience and India

### “America’s Strategic Opportunity With India” — Foreign Affairs / State archive

Burns writes that the rise of democratic India is a major strategic opportunity for the United States and makes a long-horizon argument for partnership.

**Useful phrase:** “strategic upside”

**Use:** This is a direct source for the India chapters and for the book’s recurring idea that diplomacy is often about building relationships before an immediate crisis requires them.

The article also lets us compare Burns the negotiator with Burns the retrospective policy author: what did he think the bargain meant while still in government?

**Source:** https://2001-2009.state.gov/p/us/rm/2007/93728.htm

## 7. 2013: diplomacy as an antique art that still matters

### Chautauqua — “The Return of American Diplomacy,” August 5, 2013

**Quote:** “a venerable and antique art”

Burns immediately adds that diplomacy is “of great value to all of us.”

**Use:** Excellent chapter epigraph material. This is Burns reflecting after government service and before returning for China. It shows him consciously defending the profession itself.

He describes himself as having begun as a “lowly intern” in Mauritania and ended as Under Secretary.

**Use:** Natural biographical bridge into career arc without heroic inflation.

**Source:** https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/return-american-diplomacy

## 8. 2014: keep the lines open

### Interview on India-Pakistan and difficult relationships

Burns generalizes from U.S. experience with Iran, Cuba, and Venezuela:

**Quote:** “keep the lines open.”

He says countries are “almost always better off” talking even when relations are difficult.

**Use:** This may be one of the most important recurring lines for the entire manuscript. It anticipates his later Beijing role and supports the “human channel” thesis without importing language from *The Long Table*.

**Source:** https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/us-has-closer-relationship-india-pakistan-nicholas-burns

## 9. 2014: Passage to India

### “Passage to India” — Foreign Affairs

Burns argues that U.S. strategic interests will align increasingly with India’s and stresses bipartisan continuity across Clinton, Bush, and Obama.

**Use:** Important evidence for how Burns understood long-term diplomatic investment and bipartisan statecraft. Also useful for examining where his forecasts proved strong, weak, or incomplete.

**Source:** https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/passage-india

## 10. 2015–2017: the arena and public service

### Loyola University Maryland commencement — 2015

**Quote:** “purpose and passion”

Burns joins those qualities to intelligence, drive, and public service.

**Use:** This is more than commencement boilerplate because similar public-service language recurs throughout his career.

**Source:** https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/faculty-director-r-nicholas-burns-delivers-loyola-university-maryland-2015-commencement

### American University of Paris commencement — May 23, 2017

Burns uses Theodore Roosevelt’s “arena” language to tell students that changing the world requires entering public life.

**Quote:** “you must go into the arena of public life and public service.”

**Use:** Strong late-career articulation of service as participation rather than commentary. This becomes especially resonant when he later leaves Harvard to return to government for Beijing.

**Source:** https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/nicholas-burns-gives-commencement-address-and-receives-honorary-degree-american

## 11. 2017: defense of the Foreign Service

### “Dismantling the Foreign Service” — New York Times

Burns calls the Foreign Service an “irreplaceable asset” for understanding and interacting with a dangerous world.

**Use:** Excellent evidence for his institutional loyalty. In *Red Sox Diplomacy*, this can balance the emphasis on personality: Burns’s human style operated inside a profession whose discipline he defended vigorously.

**Source:** https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/dismantling-foreign-service

## 12. 2019: history and family

### Versailles 1919–2019 conference remarks

Burns opens a historical conference with humor, then pivots into a personal reflection on Paris and his wife Libby’s family history.

**Use:** A good example of the same register-switching seen in the baseball material: humor → family → history → diplomacy. It can help us show that this is broader speaking craft, not merely a sports habit.

**Source:** https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/remarks-ambassador-ret-nicholas-burns-conference-versailles-1919-2019

## 13. 2020: diplomacy’s time

### “The Indispensable Power” — Harvard Magazine

**Quote:** “diplomacy’s time has come”

Burns argues that global crises require international cooperation and that American leadership cannot rest on military strength alone.

**Use:** A mature statement of his professional creed. This is especially useful for chapters connecting alliances, public diplomacy, and nonmilitary forms of American power.

**Source:** https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2020/07/features-forum-indispensable-power

## 14. 2020: Foreign Service reform

### “A U.S. Diplomatic Service for the 21st Century” — Burns, Marc Grossman, Marcie Ries

The report argues that career diplomats need support, funding, training, and institutional reform to represent Americans effectively.

**Use:** This is Burns not merely defending the old institution but arguing it must change. Important antidote to nostalgic treatment of diplomacy.

**Source:** https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/us-diplomatic-service-21st-century

## 15. 2020: leadership in a pandemic

### “How to Lead in a Time of Pandemic” — Foreign Affairs

Burns argues that major crises test the ability of countries to work together and require institutions capable of convening, agenda-setting, and collective action.

**Use:** Reinforces his conception of leadership as coalition-building rather than unilateral command.

**Source:** https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/how-lead-time-pandemic

## 16. 2021: the diplomat as gardener

### “The Diplomat as Gardener” — Foreign Affairs

This is probably the single most important Burns-authored text for the manuscript’s philosophy of diplomacy.

Burns recalls accompanying Secretary George Shultz on a long Asia trip in 1988 and learning from Shultz’s commitment to “tending the diplomatic garden.”

**Use:** The gardening metaphor complements rather than competes with baseball. Both are about maintenance, patience, return, relationships, and work whose payoff may not be immediate.

The article gives us a way to resist the dramatic bias of biography: much diplomacy matters because it occurs before the crisis.

**Source:** https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/diplomat-gardener

## 17. 2021: return to public service

### Senate confirmation statement for China — October 20, 2021

Burns begins by thanking his wife Libby and explicitly situating their family inside decades of overseas public service in Mauritania, Egypt, Israel, Greece, and Belgium.

**Quote:** “returning to public service”

**Use:** This is the dramatic answer to the 2017 “arena” speech: after thirteen years teaching diplomacy, Burns goes back into the arena.

The statement should be mined in full for his own framing of China, competition, cooperation, human rights, Taiwan, and alliances at the moment before he assumed the Beijing post.

**Source:** https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/statement-senate-committee-foreign-relations-nicholas-burns-us-ambassadorial-nominee

## 18. 2024: public service at HKS while serving in Beijing

### Harvard Kennedy School graduation address — May 22, 2024

Burns returns to the “arena” motif and tells students that public service is part of the school’s DNA.

**Quote:** “Change the world, change it for the better.”

**Use:** Important late-career continuity. The same themes found in 1997—optimism, public service, national responsibility—remain after nearly three decades of wars, failed negotiations, polarization, and strategic competition.

This continuity should be examined, not merely celebrated. What has experience changed in Burns’s language, and what has remained remarkably intact?

**Source:** https://www.hks.harvard.edu/faculty-research/policy-topics/public-leadership-management/rise-enormous-challenges-you-ambassador

## 19. 2025: walking-away lesson from China

### Foreign Affairs conversation after Beijing

Burns says that alliances “multiply our power” and calls his core lesson after roughly forty-five years of diplomacy:

**Quote:** “Don’t forsake your allies.”

**Quote:** “Keep them close.”

**Use:** This is potentially the late-book summation. It links NATO, Asia, Beijing, and his entire professional career in Burns’s own language.

He also rejects simple bilateral measurements of U.S.-China power because American alliances are part of American power.

**Source:** https://www.foreignaffairs.com/podcasts/bonus-room-xi-jinping-china-nicholas-burns

## 20. Recurring Burns vocabulary

Across these works, a strikingly stable lexicon appears:

- **engagement** rather than isolation;
- **public service** rather than career advancement;
- **allies** as multipliers of American power;
- **lines open** even in difficult relationships;
- **gardening** as maintenance before crisis;
- **leadership** as coalition-building and responsibility;
- **optimism** as an American disposition;
- **the arena** as the place where consequential public work occurs.

For *Red Sox Diplomacy*, this suggests a productive counterpoint. Baseball is the recurring personal language. These ideas are the recurring professional language. The book becomes strongest when the two registers touch naturally rather than when baseball is forced to carry the argument alone.

## 21. Manuscript implications

### “Boston Before Washington”
Use the 1997 commencement address to establish that Burns’s Massachusetts identity, optimism, patriotism, internationalism, and public-service ethic were already tightly linked before his first ambassadorship.

### “The Road Team”
Use the 2002 Boston College address and Shultz/gardener essay to describe the psychological structure of repeated overseas service: leave home, build relationships, move again, maintain continuity.

### “Speaking for America” / “Briefing Room Bleachers”
Pair baseball banter with Burns’s insistence on engagement. The humor should reveal how he made official authority more human without weakening it.

### Greece section
Place baseball diplomacy inside his explicit belief in citizen diplomacy and exchange programs, not as an isolated personal hobby.

### NATO section
Use 2002 NATO speech and the 2025 “Don’t forsake your allies” formulation as bookends, showing how one conviction survived decades.

### India section
Use the 2007 and 2014 India essays to compare practitioner and professor: what Burns believed while negotiating versus what he believed years later.

### Beijing section
Use “keep the lines open,” gardener logic, 2021 testimony, and the 2025 alliance lesson. This gives Beijing philosophical continuity without repeating *The Long Table*.

### “Extra Innings” / epilogue
Return to public service: 1997 “Dare to be great,” 2017 “arena,” 2021 return to government, 2024 “change the world,” 2025 “keep [allies] close.” The arc is compelling precisely because Burns’s language changes less than the world around him.
