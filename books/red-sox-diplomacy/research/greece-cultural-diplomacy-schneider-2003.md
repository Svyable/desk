# Greece baseball — Cynthia Schneider 2003 cultural-diplomacy evidence

## Why this source matters

Cynthia P. Schneider's 2003 Center for Arts and Culture paper, *Diplomacy That Works: 'Best Practices' in Cultural Diplomacy*, is an unusually useful near-contemporaneous source for Nicholas Burns's Greece baseball project. Schneider had served as U.S. ambassador to the Netherlands and wrote the paper as a study of practical cultural-diplomacy programs. The surviving PDF identifies Schneider, Georgetown University, the Center for Arts and Culture, and a 2003 copyright on its title page.

Source: https://www.interarts.net/descargas/interarts645.pdf

A contemporaneous IFACCA notice dated January 1, 2003 also identifies the Center for Arts and Culture as publisher/releaser of Schneider's research paper:
https://ifacca.org/news/2003/01/01/diplomacy-that-works-best-practices-in/

## What Schneider reports

On pp. 5–6, Schneider uses Burns's Greek baseball effort as a concrete example of cultural diplomacy suited to local circumstances. Her account says:

- Burns learned in discussions connected with preparations for the 2004 Olympics that Greece lacked a baseball team to field for the Games.
- Burns, whom Schneider identifies as a lifelong Red Sox fan, secured assistance from Greek-American political and sports figures.
- Those efforts helped form a nonprofit called **Friends of Greek Baseball**.
- Schneider attributes to the combined efforts of Burns and others a developing amateur infrastructure that she quantified at the time as twenty baseball teams, ten softball teams, and Little Leagues, while the Greek national team trained for the Olympics.
- She places the initiative specifically against the hostile political climate in Greece during the Kosovo war.
- She pairs baseball with a separate Burns-backed Athens marathon initiative as examples of "people to people diplomacy" in which American expertise addressed a locally identified problem.
- Schneider quotes Burns describing cultural diplomacy as a way to make headway in a hostile climate.

This is stronger for the manuscript than a later generic characterization of Burns as a sports fan because it records the baseball project while its institutional consequences were still developing and explicitly situates it inside a theory of diplomatic practice.

## Evidentiary classification

**Near-contemporaneous expert/institutional account, with apparent participant access.**

The paper is not a State Department after-action report and Schneider does not footnote every sentence in the Burns passage to a named interview. The nearby footnotes identify conversations with other diplomats for adjacent examples, but the Burns passage itself should not be silently converted into a verbatim Burns interview unless the paper's research files or another source establishes that provenance.

The direct Burns quotation at the end of the passage is usable as a quotation from Schneider's 2003 paper, attributed through Schneider. It should not be described as coming from a recovered transcript.

## What this strengthens

### 1. The project was more than Olympic-team recruitment

The 2004 *Christian Science Monitor* account already establishes Burns bringing Peter Angelos and MLB International into the Greek baseball effort. Schneider adds a distinct dimension: by 2003 she described a wider amateur ecosystem of baseball, softball, and Little League activity. That supports treating the project as grassroots/public-diplomacy infrastructure as well as elite Olympic-team construction.

### 2. "Friends of Greek Baseball" has a near-contemporaneous institutional witness

The nonprofit name is not merely a later retrospective label. Schneider's 2003 paper records it while Burns's Greece tenure was still recent and before the Athens Olympics.

### 3. Burns's own diplomatic framing can be bounded more precisely

The safest manuscript claim is not that baseball measurably changed Greek opinion. Schneider's account instead supports a narrower argument: Burns used baseball as one of several people-to-people initiatives in a period of unusually difficult U.S.-Greek relations, and Schneider presented the program as a cultural-diplomacy best practice.

## What this does **not** establish

Do not use this source to claim:

- that the Boston Red Sox organization supplied a particular shipment, grant, employee, coach, clinic, or other deliverable;
- that the Red Sox formally sponsored Friends of Greek Baseball;
- that baseball measurably changed Greek public opinion or "tempered hostilities" as an empirically demonstrated outcome rather than Schneider's evaluative framing;
- that all twenty baseball teams, ten softball teams, or Little Leagues were created solely by Burns;
- that Burns personally founded or legally incorporated the nonprofit without corroborating organizational records;
- that the quoted Burns sentence came from a surviving transcript or recording;
- any baseball behavior, reaction, dialogue, or opinion by Libby Baylies.

The source therefore strengthens the **Burns / cultural-diplomacy / grassroots-infrastructure** side of the Greece chapter while leaving the specific **Boston Red Sox club contribution** unresolved.

## Manuscript opportunity

This evidence is strong enough to support a short revision where the Greece narrative moves from elite recruitment to local infrastructure. The scene should remain documentary: hostile Kosovo-war political climate; a country preparing to host the Olympics without an established baseball tradition; Burns and Greek partners assembling outside expertise; and, by Schneider's 2003 account, a network extending beyond the Olympic roster into amateur teams and youth play.

The important argument is institutional rather than sentimental. Baseball gave the embassy a practical, non-policy arena in which Americans and Greeks could build something together while disagreeing sharply over Kosovo and other issues. The manuscript should attribute Schneider's judgment rather than turn that interpretation into omniscient narrator fact.
