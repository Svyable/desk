# October 2004 — the medical handoff behind the bloody sock

## Finding

The familiar image from 2004 ALCS Game 6 is Curt Schilling's blood-stained sock. The less familiar and more narratively useful history is the Red Sox medical operation that made the start possible at all.

MLB's tenth-anniversary reconstruction reports that Schilling had torn the tendon sheath in his right ankle and had already been ineffective in Game 1. Before Game 6, Red Sox team physician Dr. Bill Morgan devised a temporary procedure to keep the loose tendon from moving during Schilling's delivery: sutures would hold the tissue in place. MLB reports that Morgan first tested the improvised procedure on a cadaver. The procedure was not public before the game; when blood became visible through Schilling's sock, viewers were seeing a consequence of the sutures rather than a conventional treatment whose outcome was known in advance.

Authoritative source:
- MLB, “Schilling's bloody sock the bridge to history,” October 19, 2014: https://www.mlb.com/news/curt-schillings-bloody-sock-the-bridge-to-history/c-98978666

SABR's Game 6 reconstruction, drawing on later oral-history material, preserves the uncertainty inside the organization. Theo Epstein later recalled that the club did not know whether Schilling would be able to throw normally, field his position, or even remain upright once the game began. That recollection is retrospective and should remain labeled as such.

Historical synthesis:
- SABR Games Project, “October 19, 2004: Curt Schilling keeps Red Sox alive in ‘Bloody Sock Game’”: https://sabr.org/gamesproj/game/october-19-2004-curt-schilling-keeps-red-sox-alive-in-bloody-sock-game/

The result is firmly documented. MLB records Schilling throwing seven innings, allowing one run on four hits, as Boston won 4–2 and forced Game 7. MLB's game archive preserves the performance.

## Why this is a better Red Sox detail

The bloody sock is famous enough to risk becoming iconography instead of history. The cadaver test restores the institutional mechanics.

Boston was not merely asking an injured ace to be brave. A team physician confronted a mechanical problem with no established baseball protocol, tested a proposed temporary fix on a cadaver, performed it on Schilling, and sent the club into an elimination game without knowing whether the repair would survive the delivery. The uncertainty remained operational: could he throw with normal velocity, cover his position, withstand repeated push-offs, or stay in the game?

That is a useful Red Sox-side scene because it reveals a hidden handoff among player, physician, baseball operations and manager without needing invented clubhouse dialogue. The visible artifact—the sock—comes last. The interesting history is what the organization had to do before the cameras could see it.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- Schilling had a torn tendon sheath and was ineffective in ALCS Game 1;
- MLB retrospectively reports that Dr. Bill Morgan devised the temporary suturing procedure before Game 6 and first tested it on a cadaver;
- the procedure was not publicly known before the game;
- SABR preserves Epstein's later recollection that the organization did not know how Schilling's ankle would respond under game conditions;
- Schilling pitched seven innings of one-run, four-hit baseball in Boston's 4–2 Game 6 win.

Not safe:

- reconstructing Morgan's cadaver test as a scene beyond the sourced fact that it occurred;
- inventing dialogue among Morgan, Schilling, Epstein, Terry Francona, trainers or other staff;
- assigning private fear, confidence or certainty except where a named participant supplies it retrospectively;
- saying the procedure was medically routine or guaranteed to work;
- claiming the medical improvisation caused Boston to win the series;
- turning the episode into a generic lesson about courage, innovation, diplomacy or leadership.

## Manuscript recommendation

This is strong enough for a bounded insertion if the 2004 sequence needs more institutional Red Sox texture. Start with the problem after Game 1, not with the sock. Keep Morgan and the medical staff visible. The best surprising detail is that the proposed fix was tested on a cadaver before being used to get Schilling through an elimination-game start.

Then let the box score close the sequence: seven innings, four hits, one run, Boston alive for Game 7.

Do not over-narrate the blood. Readers know the image. Give them the machinery they probably do not know.
