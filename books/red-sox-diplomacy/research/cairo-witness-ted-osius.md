# Cairo Witness — Ted Osius

## Why this is different

This is the first direct Cairo witness located in the current research sequence who actually remembers Nicholas Burns personally during the 1983–85 posting.

Ted Osius later became a career Foreign Service officer and U.S. ambassador to Vietnam. In his ADST oral history, recorded decades later, he recalls knowing Burns while Osius was associated with the American University in Cairo.

## What Osius remembers

Osius identifies Burns as staff aide to Ambassador Nicholas Veliotes. He says he got to know Burns, although he only met Veliotes. His characterization of the young Burns is specific: friendly, kind, inclusive, and welcoming toward the American University in Cairo interns.

More consequentially, Osius remembers Burns encouraging him to attend SAIS and join the Foreign Service. Osius says Burns planted that seed. Osius later attended SAIS and did join the Foreign Service.

This is a small episode, but it is unusually valuable because it is not retrospective self-description by Burns. It is another diplomat remembering what Burns was like before Burns was famous.

## What it adds to Chapter 4

Until this witness, the Cairo material risked making Burns disappear inside embassy machinery. Osius gives us a human-scale action that fits the book's larger subject without requiring invention: a junior diplomat, himself early in his career, taking young interns seriously enough to encourage one toward the profession.

The episode also complicates the hierarchy. Burns was not ambassador. He was the ambassador's aide. Yet to an intern, he could already function as the visible human face of the profession.

That is a better use of the “representation” theme than another baseball analogy.

## Possible scene use

Do not manufacture the physical setting of the conversation. We do not yet know exactly where Burns and Osius spoke, how often they interacted, who else was present, or the exact date.

A chapter can safely say that Osius, while at AUC, got to know Burns and later remembered him as unusually welcoming to the interns. Burns urged him toward SAIS and the Foreign Service. Osius eventually followed both suggestions.

The point can land quietly: before Burns represented the United States as an ambassador, he was already representing the Foreign Service to someone standing just outside it.
