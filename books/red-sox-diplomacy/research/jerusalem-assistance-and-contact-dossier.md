# Jerusalem Assistance and Contact Dossier

## Purpose

Augment the Chapter 5 evidence base before manuscript drafting. The goal is to understand what Nicholas Burns's documented responsibility for U.S. economic assistance to Palestinians meant institutionally and historically, without projecting later Oslo-era structures backward onto 1985–87.

## Burns's portfolio

Multiple Burns biographies independently preserve the same unusually specific résumé line: as a political officer at the American Consulate General in Jerusalem from 1985 to 1987, he coordinated U.S. economic assistance to Palestinians in the West Bank (with some versions explicitly including East Jerusalem).

This is more than generic political reporting. It places Burns at an intersection of political representation, development assistance, Palestinian civil society/economic life, and the peculiar diplomatic status of the Jerusalem consulate.

## Scale of assistance during the tour

Later Congressional Research Service tables, drawing on State/USAID figures, report U.S. West Bank and Gaza assistance of approximately:

- FY1985: $12.141 million
- FY1986: $13.950 million
- FY1987: $8.400 million

These figures do **not** establish that Burns personally controlled those sums. They establish the approximate scale of the assistance environment in which his coordination role sat.

The amounts are useful because they keep the chapter away from two opposite errors: describing the portfolio as symbolic, or implying that Burns personally ran a vast modern aid mission.

## The consulate as a political channel

Contemporary reporting from July 1986 documents Vice President George H. W. Bush meeting Palestinian political and civic figures at the U.S. Consulate in Jerusalem. Participants included East Jerusalem editor Hanna Siniora, Bethlehem mayor Elias Freij, acting Nablus mayor Hafez Touqan, and Gaza activist Hatem Abu Ghazaleh. Some invitees stayed away amid boycott pressure and threats.

No source yet establishes Burns's personal role in organizing or attending that meeting. Do not put him in the room without evidence.

The event is nevertheless valuable institutional context: during Burns's tour, the consulate was an active American channel to Palestinian personalities at a time when direct U.S. contact with the PLO remained constrained by policy.

## Leadership transition

Morris Draper began as consul general in February 1986, replacing Wat Cluverius. Contemporary reporting described the consul general unofficially as an American diplomatic representative to the occupied/administered territories, while noting the unusual U.S. position on Jerusalem and the consulate's separate diplomatic function.

Draper's oral history likewise stresses that the Jerusalem mission was not subordinate to Embassy Tel Aviv and that the post's structure flowed from the unresolved status of Jerusalem.

This matters for Burns's development as a representative: his second overseas tour placed him inside a diplomatic institution whose organizational chart was itself foreign policy.

## What we can say now

A defensible Chapter 5 can say that Burns:

1. served as a political officer in Jerusalem in 1985–87;
2. coordinated U.S. economic assistance to Palestinians in the West Bank/East Jerusalem;
3. worked during a period when annual U.S. assistance was measured in the low tens of millions of dollars;
4. served at a consulate that functioned as a distinct American political channel to Palestinians and whose institutional status reflected unresolved sovereignty questions;
5. left before the First Intifada began in December 1987.

## What remains unproven

We still do not have documentary support for:

- a named aid project Burns personally designed or approved;
- a named Palestinian counterpart who explicitly remembers working with Burns;
- Burns's attendance at the July 1986 Bush-Palestinian meeting;
- a Burns-authored cable from the Jerusalem tour;
- Burns's private political views during the posting;
- any baseball episode in Jerusalem.

## Narrative implication

The chapter title `Rivalry Is Not Hatred` should remain provisional. The evidence is pointing toward something more precise and potentially better: **representation without recognition** or **diplomacy in the seams**.

The Jerusalem consulate embodied ambiguity. Burns's assistance portfolio dealt with people whose political future was unresolved. American officials maintained channels without resolving the underlying conflict. That is a stronger documentary theme than comparing Middle Eastern conflict to sports rivalry.
