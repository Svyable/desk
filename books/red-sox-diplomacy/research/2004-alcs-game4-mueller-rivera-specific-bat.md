# October 17, 2004 — Bill Mueller brings a Rivera-specific bat to the plate

## Finding

The ninth inning of 2004 ALCS Game 4 is usually reduced to Dave Roberts's steal. A later *Boston Globe* oral-history-style reconstruction preserves a less familiar piece of hitter-level preparation immediately behind it: Bill Mueller said he kept a special, lighter bat specifically for facing Mariano Rivera.

Mueller told the *Globe* that instead of his usual roughly 31.5- to 31.7-ounce bat, the Rivera bat weighed about 30.9 or 31 ounces. He also said he choked up slightly against Rivera, something he did not normally do, because he needed extra quickness against Rivera's cutter. This is retrospective first-person evidence and should remain attributed to Mueller rather than narrated as contemporaneous access to his plan.

Participant-based retrospective source:
- Bob Ryan, *The Boston Globe*, “In ’04, Bill Mueller's glory was stolen amid Red Sox comeback,” October 13, 2011: https://www.bostonglobe.com/sports/2011/10/13/mueller-glory-was-stolen-roberts-theft/mlM945B7NnSOZMsU7cbyLM/story.html

The same reconstruction preserves the tactical handoff around Roberts. Francona said he had gone over the inning with Roberts in the tunnel before the ninth; when Kevin Millar walked, Roberts knew he was entering. Roberts stole second on Rivera's first pitch to Mueller. Rivera later said he was surprised Boston did not bunt. Francona's explanation was specific: he did not want to surrender an out against Rivera.

Mueller's own account makes the next decision equally concrete. With Roberts on second and nobody out, he said his initial objective was to move Roberts to third, even if that meant a productive out. Rivera left the cutter far enough over the plate for Mueller to send it back through the middle instead. Roberts scored the tying run.

Contemporaneous reporting corroborates the observable game mechanics: Roberts pinch-ran after Millar's walk, Rivera threw to first three times, Roberts stole second, Mueller briefly showed bunt on the next pitch, Francona removed the bunt, and Mueller singled Roberts home. The *Washington Post* also records that Roberts had stolen 38 bases in 41 attempts during the regular season and quotes Francona after the ALCS placing the steal among the series' biggest plays.

Contemporaneous source:
- Dave Sheinin, *The Washington Post*, “It's a Red October,” October 24, 2004: https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/sports/2004/10/24/its-a-red-october/d072ea38-3eee-4821-9151-b2c976112446/

## Why this is narratively valuable

The steal has already generated abundant mythology. The better addition is the preparation sitting in the batter's hands.

Boston's tying run required several distinct jobs to line up. Millar reached. Francona deployed the specialist runner. Roberts executed the steal while Rivera and the Yankees plainly expected it. Francona declined to give Rivera a free out. Mueller came to the matchup with equipment and stance adjustments he later said were reserved for this particular pitcher, then produced the hit that actually brought Roberts home.

For knowledgeable Red Sox readers, the Rivera-only bat is the surprising detail. It turns a famous highlight into opponent-specific craft without claiming anyone foresaw the comeback. The useful story is not destiny. It is a hitter shaving roughly half an ounce from his bat and changing his grip for one exceptional cutter, then getting exactly that pitcher in the elimination inning.

This complements rather than duplicates the recent Roberts material. Roberts's role explains the base-running weapon. This note recovers Mueller's preparation and Francona's decision not to trade an out for third base once Roberts had stolen second.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- Mueller retrospectively told the *Boston Globe* that he used a lighter bat specifically against Rivera and choked up slightly, unlike his normal approach;
- Mueller described the weight difference approximately and connected the adjustment to getting to Rivera's cutter;
- Francona retrospectively said he had gone over the inning with Roberts before Millar walked;
- Roberts pinch-ran, Rivera threw over three times, and Roberts stole second on the first pitch to Mueller;
- contemporaneous reporting records Mueller showing bunt and Francona taking the bunt off;
- Francona later explained that he did not want to surrender an out against Rivera;
- Mueller singled through the middle and Roberts scored the tying run.

Not safe:

- presenting Mueller's remembered bat weights as contemporaneous equipment records;
- inventing a batting-cage, dugout, tunnel, or clubhouse scene around the special bat;
- inventing dialogue among Mueller, Francona, Roberts, Millar, Rivera, or coaches beyond sourced quotations;
- claiming the lighter bat caused the hit;
- claiming Francona, Mueller, and Roberts had rehearsed this exact sequence;
- assigning private confidence, fear, certainty, or motive beyond attributed retrospective statements;
- turning the sequence into a generic lesson about preparation, diplomacy, specialization, or leadership.

## Manuscript recommendation

Use as a compact Game 4 detail if the manuscript needs to reward readers who already know the steal. The clean shape is: Roberts reaches second; Boston does not give Rivera an out; Mueller is facing the one pitcher for whom he later said he kept a lighter bat and changed his grip; the cutter stays reachable; the ball goes through the middle; Roberts scores.

Keep the special-bat detail explicitly retrospective. It is vivid enough without reconstructing when Mueller selected the bat or what anyone said in the dugout.

## Evidentiary level

**Named first-person retrospective reporting from Mueller, Francona, Roberts, Rivera, and others in the Boston Globe + contemporaneous Washington Post game/series reporting.** Strong for the observable ninth-inning sequence and the participants' later accounts of their own tactical preparation. The exact bat-weight memory is retrospective and should be labeled accordingly.
