# Boston geography map pass 06

## Purpose

This pass adds a publication-safe geographic source for the Boston/baseball side of *Red Sox Diplomacy*. The useful question is not “what old Boston map looks good?” but whether a map can explain where the club’s physical history sits inside the city without turning the book into decorative baseball nostalgia.

## Approved source: 1912 Boston annexation/base map

**Object:** *Directory map showing small annexations, 1883*  
**Repository:** Library of Congress, Geography and Map Division  
**Date:** cataloged as `[n.p., 1912?]`; LOC dates the object 1912  
**Base map:** Sampson, Davenport & Co., *Map of Boston for 1883*  
**Scale:** 1:19,800; 100 rods to an inch  
**LCCN:** 76692161  
**Digital ID:** `http://hdl.loc.gov/loc.gmd/g3764b.ct005985`  
**Canonical record:** https://www.loc.gov/item/76692161/  
**Rights:** Library of Congress states that content in its Geography and Map Division digitized collections is free to use and reuse unless an item carries a contrary Rights Advisory. The catalog record for this item shows no contrary advisory.  
**Required/preferred credit line:** `Library of Congress, Geography and Map Division.`  
**Commercial-publication assessment:** APPROVED. The item-level record is in an LOC collection explicitly described as free to use and reuse and supplies a credit line.

### Why this object earns space

The map is contemporaneous with Fenway Park’s opening year rather than a modern reconstruction. Its half-mile circles from City Hall also give an unusually legible measure of Boston distance. It should not be reproduced as a full-page antique curiosity. Its best use is as source geometry for a restrained original redraw showing the relationship among central Boston, the Fenway/Kenmore area, Huntington Avenue Grounds, and Fenway Park.

The historical map itself does **not** identify those baseball sites for the reader. Any site markers in a redraw therefore need independent location evidence and must be labeled as editorial overlays, not features copied from the 1912 map.

This is useful as a cross-check for the pre-Fenway urban geography and transport network. Do not reproduce its bicycle-route coloration merely because it is visually attractive; those routes are irrelevant unless the manuscript develops a transportation point.

## Separate photographic context, not a map substitute

The Library of Congress also catalogs *[Fenway Ball Park, in preparation for World Series]* (1912), George Grantham Bain Collection, LCCN 92513261, showing men constructing bleachers at Fenway. Canonical record: https://www.loc.gov/item/92513261/. It is historically strong, but the current web record surfaced in this pass does not expose a sufficiently explicit item-level reuse statement to improve on already approved Fenway photography in the project. **HOLD for final-media use until the item-level rights advisory is captured cleanly.**

A separate September 1912 Boston ball-grounds panorama, LOC item 2001704407, does carry the explicit advisory **“No known restrictions on reproduction.”** Canonical record: https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2001704407/. It may be used if a panoramic stadium view is narratively necessary, but the map/redraw is higher value because the project already has strong early-ballpark imagery.

## Proposed original figure: BOSTON GROUND 01

**Working title:** `Two ballparks, one changing city`

**Form:** original editorial map abstraction derived from the rights-clean LOC maps, not a traced facsimile and not a modern proprietary basemap screenshot.

**Minimum content:**

- simplified Boston/Cambridge water and land outline sufficient for orientation;
- central Boston / City Hall reference point;
- Huntington Avenue Grounds marker;
- Fenway Park marker;
- a simple connector indicating the club’s move from Huntington Avenue to Fenway;
- dates only where independently verified in the manuscript research;
- no team logos, trademarked marks, faux scoreboards, baseball stitching, sepia texture, or decorative pennants.

**Caption draft:** `Boston baseball moved only a short distance on the map when the club left Huntington Avenue Grounds for Fenway Park. The move matters because the park that later accumulated a century of memory began as a specific piece of a changing city, not as a timeless shrine. Editorial map based on historical Boston maps in the Library of Congress Geography and Map Division.`

**Alt text draft:** `Simplified historical map of Boston marking Huntington Avenue Grounds and Fenway Park, with a short connector showing the club’s move west to Fenway.`

**Credit/source note:** `Editorial map by the author. Historical geography based on Library of Congress, Geography and Map Division, “Directory map showing small annexations, 1883” (cataloged 1912), LCCN 76692161, and Geo. H. Walker & Co., “Road map of the Boston district showing the metropolitan park system” (1905).`

## Reader-facing production rules

For print, keep the map, caption, and credit together and do not let the caption orphan onto the following page. Use a horizontal figure only if the trim width preserves readable labels at normal reading distance; otherwise use a compact portrait crop centered on the two sites. Design must survive grayscale without relying on color to distinguish old/new locations.

For EPUB, place the figure immediately after the paragraph that first needs the geographic comparison, followed by caption and source note in reading order. Alt text should explain the geographic relationship, not repeat the caption. Avoid tiny street labels; the figure should communicate at ordinary phone width without pinch-zoom.

Links belong in notes/provenance rather than inside the artwork. Preserve the LOC LCCN and canonical item URLs so production can re-check rights at final export.

## Evidentiary boundaries

The historical maps document Boston geography, roads, parks, annexation context, and distance conventions. They do not establish why the Red Sox moved, what owners intended, what fans felt, or what later generations made Fenway mean. Those are separate historical claims requiring separate sources.

Likewise, a short distance between the two parks is a geographic observation, not evidence that the institutional or cultural transition was small. The figure should help the reader locate the move, not manufacture a metaphor about continuity.

## Integration recommendation

Use `BOSTON GROUND 01` in the early Boston/baseball material or Chapter 19 only where the prose moves from rivalry or Fenway mythology back to physical place. Prefer this map over another archival stadium photograph. The book now has enough early baseball imagery; geographic clarification adds a different kind of evidence.

Do not add the figure to the manuscript until the exact Huntington Avenue Grounds and Fenway coordinates/footprints are independently verified from authoritative sources and the redraw is generated from those checked points.
