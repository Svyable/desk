# Chapter 3 Brief — The Road Team

## Function

Move the book from identity to profession. Chapter 2 establishes that Burns's fandom could become useful social language; Chapter 3 must show what kind of institution he entered, what repeated movement does to a diplomatic life, and why relationships accumulated across postings matter more than a baseball metaphor.

## Documentary spine

- Raised in Massachusetts; studied at Boston College, the Sorbonne, and Johns Hopkins SAIS.
- Before entering the Foreign Service, served as an intern at the U.S. Embassy in Nouakchott, Mauritania, and worked for A.T. International.
- Entered the Foreign Service and served in Cairo (1983–85), Jerusalem (1985–87), State's Operations Center/Secretariat, the counselor's office, the National Security Council, the State spokesman job, Athens, NATO, and later as Under Secretary.
- Burns later described his career as running from a “lowly intern” in Nouakchott to Under Secretary of State.
- In 2021 Burns wrote about watching George Shultz practice “tending the diplomatic garden” during a long 1988 Asian trip. His retrospective emphasis is on maintaining relationships before crises require them.
- Burns repeatedly framed public service as a life choice rather than a temporary credential. Boston College and later commencement addresses are useful for his own language about service without turning the chapter into a speech anthology.

## Argument

Foreign Service life is a road-team profession in a limited sense: the diplomat repeatedly enters other people's political, linguistic, and social home fields. But the metaphor should remain bounded. Unlike a sports team, the embassy is not there to defeat the host country. Success often depends on becoming legible, reliable, and useful inside someone else's environment while still representing Washington.

The stronger concept is cumulative relationship maintenance. The postings are not isolated episodes. Habits learned in one place travel: reading a room, respecting hierarchy without worshipping it, learning enough language to show effort, keeping contact with people who may matter later, and understanding that a relationship built during ordinary time may become valuable during crisis.

## Necessary restraint

Do not invent Burns's emotions on arrival in Mauritania, Cairo, or Jerusalem. Do not fabricate first-day scenes, family conversations, airports, apartments, or baseball listening habits abroad.

Do not claim that baseball taught him how to be a diplomat. The chapter can observe that both fandom and Foreign Service life involve durable affiliation carried across changing locations, but the causal claim is unsupported.
