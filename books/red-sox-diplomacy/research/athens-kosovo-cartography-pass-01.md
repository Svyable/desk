# Athens–Kosovo Cartography Pass 01 — Red Sox Diplomacy

This bounded media/research pass supports the Athens chapters without changing manuscript prose. Its purpose is to give the reader period-correct geographic evidence for the diplomatic pressure surrounding Ambassador Nicholas Burns in Greece during the Kosovo crisis, while avoiding decorative war imagery and rights ambiguity.

## Recommended figure — ATHENS 01: Greece beside the Balkan crisis, 1998–1999

**Editorial purpose:** show, at a glance, the geography that prose otherwise has to keep re-explaining: Athens and Greece sit immediately south of the former Yugoslav conflict zone, while Kosovo, Albania and Macedonia form the northern regional field relevant to the 1998–1999 crisis. The figure should clarify proximity and political geography; it should not imply that geography alone explains Greek public opinion, Greek government policy, NATO decisions or Burns's actions.

**Preferred production method:** original redraw using the federal/Library of Congress maps below as period reference. Do not copy decorative relief, typography or graphical styling. Use a restrained political outline with only locations needed by the chapter.

Suggested labels: Athens; Greece; Albania; Macedonia (use the period label appropriate to the dated figure, with a note if necessary); Kosovo; Serbia/Yugoslavia as period-appropriate; Adriatic Sea; Aegean Sea. Add NATO/KFOR operational information only if the surrounding manuscript passage specifically discusses it.

**Caption:** *Greece beside the Balkan crisis, 1998–1999. An original orientation map based on contemporaneous U.S. government cartography. Athens lay immediately south of the conflict zone, but proximity is context, not an explanation for Greek policy or public opinion.*

**Credit line:** *Map by the author, based on U.S. Central Intelligence Agency maps held by the Library of Congress, Geography and Map Division.*

**Alt text:** *Political orientation map showing Athens and Greece south of Albania, Macedonia, Kosovo and Serbia/Yugoslavia during the 1998–1999 Balkan crisis.*

### Print / EPUB treatment

- Keep the figure and caption together; never orphan the credit line onto the next page.
- Design for grayscale first. Borders and any highlighted crisis area must remain distinguishable without color.
- Do not use flags, fighter-aircraft silhouettes, explosion icons, NATO logos or baseball graphics.
- In EPUB, place the figure immediately after the paragraph that first requires regional orientation, followed by caption and credit in reading order.
- Minimum label size should remain legible on a six-inch e-reader display. If it does not, simplify rather than shrink.
- Preserve period names in the map itself and explain later naming changes in the caption/note rather than silently modernizing the historical map.

## Held, not recommended — Athens-centered world projection, 1970

The CIA's *Azimuthal equidistant projection centered on Athens, 37°59′N., 23°44′E. 10-70* is visually striking and rights-clean under the same LOC reuse framework (LCCN 75693302; https://www.loc.gov/item/75693302/). It shows radial distances from Athens.

Do **not** promote it into the manuscript merely because an Athens-centered projection is clever. It predates Burns's ambassadorship by twenty-seven years and does not clarify the Kosovo-era political geography nearly as well as the 1998–1999 sources. Retain it as a possible design/reference object only if a later passage genuinely needs an Athens-centered distance concept.

## Evidence and attribution boundaries

These maps can establish period labels, relative location, mapped operational areas and—where explicitly dated—what a U.S. government cartographic product represented at that moment. They cannot establish what Burns knew at a particular meeting, why Greek citizens opposed NATO action, what the Greek government privately intended, or whether proximity caused a diplomatic outcome. Those claims require diplomatic records, contemporaneous reporting or identified testimony.

The manuscript should also keep different kinds of map evidence distinct. A CIA operational map is a U.S. government representation produced for a purpose; it is not a neutral aerial view. When a boundary, operational area or sector matters to an argument, identify the map's creator and date rather than presenting the graphic as omniscient geography.
