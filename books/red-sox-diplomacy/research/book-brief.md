# Book Brief — Red Sox Diplomacy

## Working package

**Title:** *Red Sox Diplomacy*

**Subtitle:** *Nicholas Burns, Baseball, and the Art of Representing America*

**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson

## The experiment

This is intentionally the second full-length narrative treatment of Nicholas Burns in Sven Hardy Benson’s Desk.

*The Long Table* asks how American professional diplomacy works and uses Burns’s career to illuminate the institution.

*Red Sox Diplomacy* asks a different question: how does a recognizably American human being carry a superpower abroad without becoming a cardboard embodiment of it?

The factual substrate can overlap. The selection, rhythm, scenes, argument, and emotional center should not.

## Governing idea

Burns’s documented lifelong Red Sox fandom provides an unusually human route through a career dominated by large abstractions. Baseball is useful where it illuminates memory, rivalry, patience, public diplomacy, identity, humor, bounded disagreement, continuity across postings, and the distinction between representing America and performing Americanness.

The deeper research now establishes that baseball is more than a decorative biographical hook. The record supports that Burns:

- played organized youth baseball in the Wellesley Babe Ruth League;
- inherited a complicated family Red Sox tradition — a mother who rooted for Boston and a father who said the team had broken his heart;
- followed current Red Sox personnel, pitching, standings, and individual games closely enough to argue baseball in public rather than merely repeat a hometown affiliation;
- watched the 2003 ALCS Game 7 from Brussels on Armed Forces television and later remembered the exact local time of Aaron Boone’s walk-off;
- kept Red Sox objects from multiple generations in his NATO office;
- used baseball habitually in State Department briefings as a social register shared with reporters and foreign visitors;
- personally participated in Greek baseball development through television demonstrations, playing catch with children, lobbying for a government budget, and connecting Greek organizers to American baseball networks;
- later used his own word, “redemption,” for the 2004 championship;
- remained enough of a current fan in 2007 to analyze Boston’s bullpen, rotation, schedule, and postseason prospects in an official State interview;
- continued using Red Sox–Yankees talk as immediate rapport decades later.

That evidence allows the manuscript to treat baseball as a genuine biographical strand. It still does **not** justify claiming that baseball caused his diplomatic temperament or that every sports reference was strategically planned.

Do not force baseball onto episodes where it does not belong.

The book should survive if every explicit baseball metaphor is challenged. Its real subject is representation.

## Reader promise

A reader who would never purchase a book called *Nicholas Burns and the American Practice of Diplomacy* should be able to pick this up because the title promises personality, baseball, Boston, and a human story—and finish with a serious understanding of diplomatic craft.

A foreign-policy professional should recognize the substance beneath the accessibility.

A baseball reader should never feel tricked into buying a textbook. The baseball history therefore needs to be as exact as the diplomatic history: not generic “curse” language, but the real sequence of 1967, 1975, 1978, 1986, 2003, the 2004 comeback, Fenway, Red Sox Nation, and the Yankees rivalry where those histories illuminate Burns’s own documented vocabulary.

## Evidentiary standard

No invented dialogue, interiority, composite characters, scenes, baseball encounters, family memories, or motives.

For baseball material, prioritize direct Burns remarks, official transcripts, Harvard material, contemporaneous reporting, MLB/club/institutional records, Greek baseball/Olympic records, and attributable interviews.

Separate documented fact from interpretation. Do not infer childhood game attendance, family rituals, favorite players, reactions to famous games, or private feelings unless sourced.

### Scene hierarchy

Use a simple hierarchy when deciding what can carry narrative weight:

1. **Direct/primary:** Burns quotation, official transcript, contemporaneous document, photograph, letter, calendar, recording.
2. **Named near-primary:** reporter or colleague recording what Burns told them; usable with attribution.
3. **Contextual overlap:** Burns’s age/posting coincides with a Red Sox event; useful as background only.
4. **Research hypothesis:** a chronology or baseball fact strongly suggests a scene or date; keep in research notes until corroborated.

Example: 2003 Brussels/Armed Forces TV is a documented Burns scene. The April 2004 CENTCOM/Yankees–Red Sox anecdote is a named secondary account. April 25 as the likely CENTCOM date is a research hypothesis. The 1967 Impossible Dream occurring when Burns was eleven remains cultural context until Burns or another direct source connects him personally to it.

## Structural principle

The current architecture contains 22 chapters plus prologue and epilogue. It can grow.

Do not make the entire book nine innings merely because baseball has nine innings. The device is available for section-level architecture if it improves pacing, but documentary chronology and dramatic centers take priority over cleverness.

A stronger recurring structure is now available: **where was the fan while the diplomat was working?** This should be used only where direct evidence answers the question — for example, Brussels at 6:16 a.m. in 2003 — rather than as a device that forces reconstruction into every posting.

## Distinctive scenes to research deeply

- Wellesley youth baseball: Babe Ruth League roster, team, position, coaches, teammates, and whether any local records survive.
- Burns using Red Sox references at the State Department podium and in public appearances; especially how reporters learned and co-produced the baseball register.
- The July 1997 Cuba/baseball exchange, where the sport becomes an actual foreign-policy subject involving travel, state control, and defection.
- Greece: the Greek federation, MLB, Peter Angelos, the Red Sox, Paul Sarbanes, television demonstrations, youth leagues, and Burns’s role connecting institutions.
- The 2003 Brussels ALCS Game 7 scene and NATO-office Red Sox objects.
- The April 2004 CENTCOM Qatar anecdote: corroborate Friedman’s account and test the April 25 candidate date without promoting inference into fact.
- The 2004 Red Sox championship: locate Burns’s immediate reaction and viewing circumstances rather than importing the documented 2003 setup.
- The 2007 championship: exploit the unusually good before-and-after record — Burns’s August prediction and detailed pitching analysis, then his official State Department Red Sox Nation moment the morning after the title.
- Baseball banter as rapport in classrooms, speeches, interviews, and diplomatic settings where documented.
- Rivalry language, especially Red Sox/Yankees, as a bounded form of opposition; never pretend this is equivalent to geopolitical conflict.
- Beijing-era evidence of sports, personal identity, public diplomacy, and human contact amid strategic competition.

## Tone

Warm without hagiography. Funny when the record is funny. Serious without announcing seriousness. Boston can provide texture, but do not manufacture Boston-ness. Baseball should open doors into diplomacy rather than become a running pun.

The book should feel like a life, not a framework wearing a cap.
