# Book Brief — Red Sox Diplomacy

## Working package

**Title:** *Red Sox Diplomacy*

**Subtitle:** *Nicholas Burns, Baseball, and the Art of Representing America*

**Author:** Sven Hardy Benson

## The experiment

This is intentionally the second full-length narrative treatment of Nicholas Burns in Sven Hardy Benson’s Desk.

*The Long Table* asks how American professional diplomacy works and uses Burns’s career to illuminate the institution.

*Red Sox Diplomacy* asks a different question: how does a recognizably American human being carry a superpower abroad without becoming a cardboard embodiment of it?

The factual substrate can overlap. The selection, rhythm, scenes, argument, and emotional center should not.

## Governing idea

Burns’s documented lifelong Red Sox fandom provides an unusually human route through a career dominated by large abstractions. Baseball is useful where it illuminates memory, rivalry, patience, public diplomacy, identity, humor, bounded disagreement, continuity across postings, and the distinction between representing America and performing Americanness.

Do not force baseball onto episodes where it does not belong.

The book should survive if every explicit baseball metaphor is challenged. Its real subject is representation.

## Reader promise

A reader who would never purchase a book called *Nicholas Burns and the American Practice of Diplomacy* should be able to pick this up because the title promises personality, baseball, Boston, and a human story—and finish with a serious understanding of diplomatic craft.

A foreign-policy professional should recognize the substance beneath the accessibility.

A baseball reader should never feel tricked into buying a textbook.

## Evidentiary standard

No invented dialogue, interiority, composite characters, scenes, baseball encounters, family memories, or motives.

For baseball material, prioritize direct Burns remarks, official transcripts, Harvard material, contemporaneous reporting, MLB/club/institutional records, Greek baseball/Olympic records, and attributable interviews.

Separate documented fact from interpretation. Do not infer childhood game attendance, family rituals, favorite players, reactions to famous games, or private feelings unless sourced.

## Structural principle

The current architecture contains 22 chapters plus prologue and epilogue. It can grow.

Do not make the entire book nine innings merely because baseball has nine innings. The device is available for section-level architecture if it improves pacing, but documentary chronology and dramatic centers take priority over cleverness.

## Distinctive scenes to research deeply

- Burns using Red Sox references at the State Department podium and in public appearances.
- Boston/Massachusetts formation and what can actually be documented about early fandom.
- Greece, Olympic baseball, the Greek baseball federation, Major League Baseball, Peter Angelos, and Burns’s role connecting institutions.
- The 2004 Red Sox championship in relation to Burns’s public/academic life only where direct evidence exists.
- Baseball banter as rapport in classrooms, speeches, interviews, and diplomatic settings where documented.
- Rivalry language, especially Red Sox/Yankees, as a bounded form of opposition; never pretend this is equivalent to geopolitical conflict.
- Beijing-era evidence of sports, personal identity, public diplomacy, and human contact amid strategic competition.

## Tone

Warm without hagiography. Funny when the record is funny. Serious without announcing seriousness. Boston can provide texture, but do not manufacture Boston-ness. Baseball should open doors into diplomacy rather than become a running pun.

The book should feel like a life, not a framework wearing a cap.
