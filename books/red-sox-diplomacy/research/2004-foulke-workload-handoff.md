# Keith Foulke: the workload behind the final out

## Why this belongs in the research layer

The familiar image is the last one: Edgar Renteria's ground ball back to Keith Foulke, Foulke underhanding to Doug Mientkiewicz, and Boston's eighty-six-year wait ending. The more useful managerial history is what Terry Francona had already asked Foulke to do before that ball came back to him.

MLB's 2014 Game 6 retrospective fixes the most striking part of the sequence. Foulke entered the ninth at Yankee Stadium on October 19 after having already worked heavily in Games 4 and 5. With two runners on and two outs, Tony Clark represented the winning run. Foulke struck him out on an elevated 88-mph fastball. MLB identifies that pitch as Foulke's **100th pitch in three days**.

A later MLB reconstruction supplies the game-by-game split: **50 pitches in Game 4, 22 in Game 5, 28 in Game 6**, with no runs allowed across those three appearances. This is retrospective arithmetic, not a contemporaneous clubhouse account, and should be labeled that way in prose.

SABR's Foulke biography independently records the same three-day 100-pitch workload and adds a small piece of clubhouse mechanics worth preserving: when Boston went to New York for Game 6, Foulke had forgotten his glove and borrowed Curt Leskanic's. That detail is retrospective biographical reporting, not evidence of anyone's state of mind.

The workload did not end with the pennant. SABR records that Foulke appeared in **11 of Boston's 14 postseason games**, allowing one earned run in 14 innings, and pitched in all four World Series games. MLB's surviving game video fixes two of the World Series handoffs: Foulke got Mike Matheny to ground out to end Game 2, then on October 27 got Renteria to ground back to the mound for the final out of Game 4.

## Narrative value

This offers Chapter 16 a more specific baseball counterweight to the missing Burns championship-night scene. The final out was not simply a closer arriving fresh for the ninth. Francona had repeatedly extended the same reliever through the elimination sequence, including 100 pitches across three consecutive ALCS days, and then used him in every World Series game. The championship's last play returned the ball to the pitcher who had absorbed that workload.

That is a decision chain rather than a leadership metaphor:

**Game 4 extended relief → Game 5 return → Game 6 return on a third consecutive day → World Series appearances in Games 1–4 → final ground ball back to Foulke.**

If promoted to manuscript prose, keep the distinction between the sources visible. MLB's 2014 Game 6 account is an authoritative retrospective with participant recollection. MLB's 2024 documentary companion supplies retrospective pitch-count arithmetic. SABR provides an independently researched biographical synthesis. MLB video pages establish the recorded final outs.

## Documentary limits

Do **not** claim that Francona knew in Game 4 that Foulke would be used three straight days, that Foulke wanted or resisted any particular workload, or that this usage caused Boston to win the series. Do not turn the borrowed glove into symbolism. Do not invent bullpen conversations.

The 100-pitch total is unusually vivid because it is verifiable workload, not because it licenses interiority.
