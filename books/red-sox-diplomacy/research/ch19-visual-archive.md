# Chapter 19 visual archive — Yankees, Rivals, Adversaries

## Editorial job

Chapter 19 is where the baseball organizing device is most likely to become glib. Its visual language therefore has to demonstrate the **limit** of analogy rather than decorate geopolitics with sports vocabulary.

Two objects are sufficient:

1. a real Burns register-switch in Paris, where the Red Sox/Yankees joke is immediately bounded by actual alliance language;
2. an editorial comparison showing which institutional assumptions make sports rivalry structurally different from rivalry among sovereign states.

## REGISTER SWITCH 01

Asset: `../media/ch19-register-switch.svg`

Primary source:
- R. Nicholas Burns, `Commemoration of the 60th Anniversary of the Marshall Plan`, Paris, June 12, 2007: https://2001-2009.state.gov/p/us/rm/2007/87176.htm

Burns opens by describing a `special bond` with U.S. Ambassador to France Craig Stapleton: both are Red Sox fans and both oppose the joking `evil empire` of the Yankees. He then immediately praises Stapleton's work calming U.S.-French relations and turns to the Marshall Plan and transatlantic cooperation.

The design does not claim baseball caused rapport or alliance repair. Its narrative job is to make the register boundary visible: theatrical sports hostility can be summoned and dropped because everyone in the room understands its limits.

The two exact quoted fragments used in the object are intentionally short.

## ANALOGY BREAK 01

Asset: `../media/ch19-analogy-break.svg`

This is **editorial synthesis**, not a Burns-authored taxonomy and not a claim that Major League Baseball and international politics are comparable institutions.

The left side records structural assumptions behind Red Sox-Yankees rivalry:
- jointly accepted league;
- shared rulebook and standings;
- external game authorities;
- repeated scheduled competition;
- expectation that the institutional system survives a loss.

The right side records the chapter's contrast for sovereign states:
- international law, treaties and institutions matter;
- enforcement is partial and power unequal;
- coercion and war remain possible;
- no commissioner guarantees another meeting;
- labels such as ally, competitor and adversary have strategic consequences.

The point is the **breaking point**, not resemblance.

## Primary baseball anchor

The chapter's opening jokes are directly grounded in the State briefing archive.

On March 7, 1997 Burns welcomed Michigan students by saying the briefing room liked the Red Sox, tolerated the Tigers and hated the Yankees.

Source:
- https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9703/970307.html

Similar repeated examples include:
- May 13, 1997 Dutch-journalist / Fenway exchange: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9705/970513.html
- July 11, 1997 Yankees-renunciation joke: https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9707/970711db.html

These establish the shared social register; they do not establish a geopolitical theory derived from baseball.

## Mature China vocabulary

The chapter's serious rivalry/competition case is documented in `ch19-rivalry-language-dossier.md` and Chapter 18's China source set. Burns paired strong competition with continued communication and societal connection rather than collapsing competition into enmity.

The chapter should retain that as a policy case, not make it another infographic taxonomy. The two visual objects already do enough.

## Placement

- `REGISTER SWITCH 01` belongs immediately after the manuscript's 2007 Paris passage, before it states that the baseball line did not explain the diplomacy.
- `ANALOGY BREAK 01` belongs after the chapter has established the serious China case and states that the Yankees joke works because the shared system is stronger than the hatred.

## Guardrails

- Do not equate the Yankees with China, Iran, Russia, France, Germany, Belgium or any state.
- Do not call the sports structure a model for international order.
- Do not turn `rival`, `competitor`, `adversary` and `enemy` into fixed universal boxes.
- Do not imply communication means friendship or trust.
- Do not treat severe allied disagreement as evidence that an ally became an adversary.
- Do not use sports vocabulary around war casualties, nuclear danger or coercion.

The chapter's visual argument is that classification matters—and baseball becomes useful only once the reader can see why its safest form of rivalry does not transfer cleanly to sovereign power.
