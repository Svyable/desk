# Deep Baseball Primary-Source Pass 01

This research pass pushes *Red Sox Diplomacy* from general baseball context toward a Burns-specific documentary record. It prioritizes primary transcripts, contemporaneous reporting, and direct quotations from Nicholas Burns. The objective is to distinguish three different things that must not be blurred in the manuscript:

1. **Burns's own baseball life and statements** — what he personally says he played, watched, believed, or did.
2. **Baseball as an observed public register** — how reporters, colleagues, institutions, and audiences interacted with his Red Sox identity.
3. **Historical baseball context** — team history that makes his language legible but is not itself evidence about his personal experience.

The strongest new finding in this pass is an ESPN profile from the run-up to the 2004 Athens Olympics. It gives direct Burns testimony about his own playing background, his reaction to the 2003 ALCS, the baseball objects he kept in his NATO office, and the practical baseball work he performed as ambassador in Greece.

## 1. Burns played organized baseball in Wellesley

An ESPN Magazine profile of the Greek Olympic baseball project reports Burns describing his own playing career as having peaked in the **Wellesley, Massachusetts Babe Ruth League**.

This is the first directly sourced playing-history detail located in the project. It materially changes the evidentiary baseline for Chapter 1 and Chapter 2. The book no longer needs to describe Burns only as someone who inherited a fan identity; there is direct evidence that he played organized youth baseball in his hometown.

**What this supports:**
- Burns played organized baseball in Wellesley.
- His baseball fluency was experiential as well as spectatorial.

**What this does not yet support:**
- his position;
- team name;
- seasons or exact ages;
- batting or pitching statistics;
- coaches or teammates;
- whether he played high-school baseball;
- whether playing caused his later attachment to the Red Sox.

**Source:** ESPN Magazine, “Give 'Em Hellas.”
https://www.espn.co.uk/espn/magazine/archives/news/story?page=magazine-20040119-article32

## 2. The 2003 ALCS is now a documented Burns scene

The same ESPN profile supplies a direct personal memory of the 2003 American League Championship Series.

Burns, then U.S. ambassador to NATO in Brussels, said he watched **Game 7 on Armed Forces television** and could remember the exact local time — **6:16 a.m.** — when Aaron Boone's walk-off home run ended Boston's season.

This is a major upgrade over chronological inference. We previously knew only that Burns was ambassador to NATO when the 2003 Red Sox lost to the Yankees. We now have direct evidence that:

- he watched the decisive game from Brussels;
- he was using Armed Forces television;
- he stayed with the game until the walk-off in extra innings;
- the moment was memorable enough that he later supplied the exact time on his clock.

The baseball sequence is independently documented by MLB: Boston led Game 7 before the Yankees tied it and Boone homered in the eleventh.

**Sources:**
- ESPN Magazine, “Give 'Em Hellas.”
  https://www.espn.co.uk/espn/magazine/archives/news/story?page=magazine-20040119-article32
- MLB, 2003 ALCS recap.
  https://www.mlb.com/news/2003-alcs-recap

### Manuscript implication

The 2003 loss can now be written as a real expatriate scene without invented interiority. The facts are enough: Brussels, Armed Forces TV, a game extending into the early morning, and Burns remembering 6:16 a.m.

Do not add an emotional reaction that he did not state. The exact time itself is documentary evidence of salience; it is not permission to invent what he said or felt in the room.

## 3. His NATO office contained a Red Sox “minishrine”

The ESPN profile describes visitors to Burns's Brussels office encountering a small collection of Red Sox objects, including:

- an autographed photograph of **Tony Conigliaro**;
- a baseball signed by **Ted Williams**;
- a bat signed by **Nomar Garciaparra**.

This is stronger than generic institutional biography calling him a lifelong fan. It documents baseball as part of the physical environment in which Burns performed a senior diplomatic role.

The objects also span different Red Sox generations: Williams, Conigliaro, and Garciaparra. That makes the office less like current-team decoration and more like an archive of personal baseball identity.

**Source:**
https://www.espn.co.uk/espn/magazine/archives/news/story?page=magazine-20040119-article32

### Research target

Search photographs of the U.S. Mission to NATO ambassador's office, mission newsletters, embassy photography, and Burns papers for visual corroboration and exact placement. Do not assume the collection was identical across his Athens, NATO, Under Secretary, Harvard, or Beijing offices.

## 4. Greece: Burns's baseball diplomacy was hands-on

The ESPN profile adds first-person detail missing from the more general Associated Press and Christian Science Monitor accounts.

Burns recalled that the embassy brought American coaches and players to Greece for baseball demonstrations. He said he personally:

- appeared on **Greek television from a batting cage** to demonstrate hitting;
- **played catch with Greek children**;
- **lobbied the Greek government for a baseball budget**.

He also described growth over roughly two years to **20 youth baseball teams and 10 softball teams playing in two leagues**.

These are among the most important baseball-diplomacy facts yet located because they move the story from “ambassador made introductions” to observable public-diplomacy practice.

**Source:**
https://www.espn.co.uk/espn/magazine/archives/news/story?page=magazine-20040119-article32

### Corroborating network sources

A 1999 Washington Post item reports that Burns and the Hellenic Amateur Baseball Federation hosted a reception involving Orioles owner Peter Angelos; the commissioner's office and the Boston Red Sox were helping the effort. This places Burns's favorite team inside the institutional network, not merely in his private fandom.

https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/sports/1999/09/08/injuries-to-teens-are-still-a-concern/fe2eb9f0-7fc2-4b43-80a4-a9cb6ac94670/

An Associated Press report in January 2000 describes Burns as taking on an additional “baseball ambassador” role, coordinating efforts to build the Greek program. It records Burns explaining that most Greeks he spoke with found baseball difficult to understand.

https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-jan-02-mn-49873-story.html

A 2003 Athens News account says the federation contacted Burns in 1999 and that he circulated the problem through American baseball networks.

https://www.helleniccomserve.com/baseball.html

The Hellenic Baseball Federation's later history describes the Burns–Paul Sarbanes–Peter Angelos chain and records a 2003 meeting involving the federation, MLB, and the Orioles.

https://www.greekbaseball.com/history

In 2004, Burns called the project a joint U.S.-Greek venture and “one of the best things” he had done as ambassador.

https://www.csmonitor.com/2004/0818/p02s01-woeu.html

### Editorial conclusion

The strongest defensible formulation is now more specific:

> Burns used his own baseball competence, embassy visibility, political access, diaspora relationships, and connections to American professional baseball to help a small Greek sports initiative become a functioning Olympic-era network.

Do not overclaim that baseball materially changed Greek public opinion toward the United States without evidence. The project is demonstrably public diplomacy; its macro-political effect remains unproven.

## 5. The State Department briefing room was more saturated with baseball than the first sweep showed

The 1997 archive contains additional episodes beyond the initial research pass. Together they make Burns's baseball register look less like occasional banter and more like a recurring social grammar between spokesman and press corps.

### February 14, 1997 — prediction and Clemens grievance

Burns welcomes a visitor from Wellesley, identifies her as a Red Sox fan, notes pitchers and catchers reporting, predicts the Red Sox will win in 1997, and again calls Roger Clemens a “traitor.”

https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9702/970214.html

### March 7 — rules of affiliation

Burns states the press-room rules: like the Red Sox, tolerate the Tigers, hate the Yankees.

https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9703/970307.html

### March 11 — institutional “we”

Greeting students from Delaware, Burns says “We're Red Sox fans here,” treating fandom as a playful identity of the briefing room itself.

https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9703/970311.html

### April 17 — baseball as social introduction

Burns introduces a friend leading visiting French and American students partly by identifying her as a huge Red Sox fan before turning to Secretary Albright's work.

https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9704/970417.html

### June 3 — statistics and topical fluency

After joking that State's website received more hits than the Red Sox, Burns adds that Red Sox pitching is third worst in the American League. The exchange shows current-season fluency rather than only inherited identity.

https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9706/970603db.html

### July 8 — geography becomes baseball territory

Burns identifies Waterville, Maine, as Red Sox territory, asks a visiting intern his team, and jokingly threatens a Yankees fan with expulsion from the briefing.

https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9707/970708db.html

### July 14 — baseball, Cuba, and political freedom

A question about Cuba canceling a baseball team's planned trip to Connecticut produces something more consequential than Red Sox banter. Burns argues that Castro should allow Cuban players to travel and speculates that the real fear is defection. He then jokes that Cuban players would be welcome in the American League — preferably with the Red Sox, not the Yankees.

https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9707/970714db.html

This episode matters because baseball becomes an actual foreign-policy subject. The sport is not merely Burns's icebreaker; it is a vehicle for discussing mobility, repression, defection, and U.S.-Cuba relations.

## 6. Roger Clemens became a recurring press-room character

Contemporaneous December 1996 reporting records Burns calling Roger Clemens a “traitor” after Clemens left Boston for Toronto, adding that the move would have been worse had he joined the Yankees. Burns jokingly labeled this the official State Department position.

The joke carried forward into 1997. By June, after Clemens began dominating for Toronto, reporters actively solicited Burns's reaction. Burns responded with bitterness, prayer, and self-mockery.

That repetition is useful because it shows the press corps understood the bit and returned to it. The public persona was co-produced: Burns offered the identity, and reporters learned how to activate it.

## 7. Burns had genuine baseball opinions, not just scripted references

An official State Department policy podcast on August 6, 2007 ends with an extended Red Sox discussion. Burns talks about the club's August schedule, the previous year's collapse, the bullpen, the starting rotation, and the importance of pitching in postseason baseball. He predicts Boston will reclaim the World Series championship.

The Red Sox did win the 2007 World Series, sweeping Colorado after coming back from a 3–1 ALCS deficit against Cleveland.

The importance is not that Burns happened to predict correctly. It is that he could sustain an unscripted baseball argument about roster construction, pitching, schedule, and postseason dynamics while serving as Under Secretary for Political Affairs.

## 8. The rivalry remained a repeated form of bounded antagonism

Official records repeatedly show Burns using Yankees rivalry as social language:

- in 1997, Yankees fandom becomes a joking violation of briefing-room rules;
- in 2005, he calls the Yankees the “Evil Empire” while describing Boston's 2004 redemption;
- in 2007, he jokes about New Jersey/Yankees affiliation at an editorial-writers conference;
- in Paris in 2007, he bonds publicly with Ambassador Craig Stapleton over Red Sox allegiance and opposition to the Yankees;
- in 2025, a Davidson student reports that learning they were both from Boston led immediately to Red Sox–Yankees conversation and helped set an informal tone before substantive policy discussion.

The book's “rivalry is not hatred” idea therefore has direct behavioral support, but the analogy must remain bounded. Sports rivalry creates a socially safe form of opposition precisely because the stakes are not war, coercion, or national survival.

## 9. The direct-evidence timeline is now materially different

### Supported in Burns's own or near-primary record

- **Youth:** Burns says his playing career peaked in the Wellesley Babe Ruth League.
- **Family:** his mother was a Red Sox fan; his father said the team broke his heart in the 1930s and warned the children not to root for Boston; Burns says he ignored the advice.
- **1996–97:** he publicly follows roster moves, pitching, standings, individual players, and the Yankees rivalry with current-season specificity.
- **Athens:** he performs baseball demonstrations, plays catch with children, lobbies for public funding, recruits U.S. baseball institutions, and helps build a Greek baseball network.
- **Brussels / 2003:** he watches ALCS Game 7 on Armed Forces TV and remembers the exact time of Boone's walk-off.
- **Brussels office:** documented Red Sox memorabilia includes Williams, Conigliaro, and Garciaparra items.
- **2005:** he personally describes the 2004 championship as “redemption.”
- **2007:** he gives detailed opinions about the rotation, bullpen, schedule, postseason pitching, and predicts another championship.
- **2013:** he describes Boston sports as institutions that bind Massachusetts and create a New England “Nation.”
- **2019:** Red Sox identity still functions as an opening social register in New York.
- **2025:** Red Sox–Yankees talk still appears as immediate rapport with a student.

## 10. Important gaps after this pass

Despite the much richer evidence, several tempting scenes remain unsupported:

- no direct Burns account of the 1967 Impossible Dream was located;
- no direct Burns account of the 1975 Fisk game was located;
- no direct Burns account of the 1978 Bucky Dent tiebreaker was located;
- no direct Burns account of following the 1986 World Series from Jerusalem was located;
- no contemporaneous October 2004 Burns account of where he watched the championship-clinching World Series game was located in this pass;
- no direct Libby Red Sox quotation was located.

The absence matters. Those years can be used as Red Sox cultural context, but not as Burns memoir unless new testimony, correspondence, photographs, diaries, or interviews emerge.

## 11. Highest-value next archival moves

1. **Wellesley baseball records:** Babe Ruth League rosters, local newspapers, town recreation records, Wellesley High yearbooks, coaches, and teammates from roughly the late 1960s–early 1970s.
2. **Boston College, 1974–78:** student newspapers, yearbooks, alumni files, and Burns papers for 1975 and 1978 baseball references.
3. **Jerusalem, 1985–87:** correspondence, oral histories, embassy newsletters, and colleague interviews for the 1986 World Series.
4. **U.S. Mission NATO, 2001–05:** mission photography and office images that may capture the Williams/Conigliaro/Garciaparra objects; travel and internal newsletters around October 2003 and October 2004.
5. **Athens:** locate video or transcript of the Greek-TV batting-cage appearance and photographs of Burns playing catch with children.
6. **Sarbanes / Angelos / MLB / Red Sox institutional archives:** correspondence that identifies exactly which Red Sox officials or departments assisted Greek baseball and what material support they supplied.
7. **October 2004:** Boston press, NATO mission newsletters, Armed Forces Network archives, and personal correspondence for Burns's immediate championship reaction.
8. **Oral history interview:** ask Burns the narrow questions the record now makes possible rather than generic fandom questions: Wellesley Babe Ruth team/position, 1967, 1975, 1978, 1986 in Jerusalem, 2003 at 6:16 a.m., and October 2004.

## Editorial bottom line

The book no longer needs to rely primarily on a clever premise that a diplomat happened to like the Red Sox. The documentary record now shows a more substantial pattern: Burns **played baseball, followed the sport closely enough to argue personnel and pitching, carried Red Sox artifacts into diplomatic offices, watched decisive games while posted overseas, used baseball habitually at the State Department podium, and converted baseball knowledge into hands-on public diplomacy in Greece**.

That is enough evidence to make baseball a genuine biographical strand rather than a decorative metaphor. The remaining task is to keep each scene within the boundary of what Burns or contemporary witnesses actually document.
