# Source Note — “America’s Salesman”

A high-value Athens profile has now been identified rather than left as an anonymous excerpt.

## Provenance

**Author:** David L. Marcus  
**Title:** “America’s Salesman”  
**Publication:** U.S. News & World Report  
**Preserved in:** U.S. House Committee on International Relations, *Foreign Relations Authorization for Fiscal Year 2000–2001: Public Diplomacy Programs*, March 4, 1999, appendix.

Archival PDF:
https://chrissmith.house.gov/uploadedfiles/1999.03.04_foreign_relations_authorization_for_fy_2000_2001-_public_diplomacy_programs.pdf

## Why this source matters

This is a contemporaneous, scene-rich profile of Burns in Athens. It should become a core source for the Greece chapters because it captures the ambassador at work rather than reconstructing his tenure years later.

The profile opens inside the U.S. Embassy country-team meeting. It describes Burns asking how to pronounce “Missouri” because the governor was arriving to pitch Boeing F-15s to Greece. The small detail is revealing: the ambassador is shown worrying about pronunciation because trade, jobs, government advocacy, and personal presentation are all intertwined.

The article frames the modern ambassador as personnel manager, troubleshooter, master of ceremonies, recruiter, economist, political scientist, counterintelligence figure, promoter of American products, and diplomat.

## Boston inside the Athens office

The profile describes Burns in his second-floor office with a Red Sox cap signed by President Clinton and other Boston material around him. This is especially valuable for *Red Sox Diplomacy* because it documents the visual coexistence of local identity and official authority in the physical workspace.

The article records Burns explaining that the United States had to convince the Greek government and public that the era of intervention and America acting as “Big Brother” was over and that the goal was a normal, rational, mature, open relationship.

Use: this can anchor the transition from the personal register to Burns’s actual theory of representing America in Greece.

## Embassy architecture as a diplomatic signal

The article also describes the Athens chancery when Burns arrived: security accretions, vines obscuring the Walter Gropius-designed building, a fountain that had not worked for years, and a generally closed appearance. Burns ordered staff to cut back vines, fix the fountain, and light the facade.

This matches and strengthens the separate *Foreign Service Journal* account of Burns treating the building’s appearance as part of diplomatic representation.

Use: the physical embassy can become a recurring image in Chapter 9. The book can show Burns trying to make an institution literally more visible and legible while also trying to make American power appear less domineering.

## Family and career texture

The profile includes material on Burns’s White House years, his family, his path to Athens, and the choice of the Greece post. Some OCR is poor in the congressional scan, so every factual detail should be cross-checked before manuscript use.

## Public-facing ambassadorial style

The article describes Burns speaking to high-school students, Greek-American civic organizations, Rotary clubs, and other public audiences. It reports heavy use of the ambassador’s residence for meals and receptions and includes Greek observers reacting to his unusually public style.

Use: this corroborates the Barbara Nielsen oral history describing Burns as intensely interested in public diplomacy and as the person who “set the tone.”

## Sports and informal representation

The profile’s photo/caption material includes Burns playing basketball in a charity game and otherwise participating visibly in public life. This broadens the sports context beyond baseball without diminishing baseball’s special role.

## Commercial diplomacy

A substantial portion of the profile concerns Burns’s work helping American companies compete in Greece and navigating the tensions between commercial advocacy, defense sales, regulation, and political interests.

Use: useful corrective to a soft-focus public-diplomacy portrait. Burns’s representation of America included trade and hard strategic interests as well as cultural connection.

## Editorial rule

This source is exceptionally useful but the congressional reproduction has degraded OCR. Before quoting any long line or reconstructing a detailed scene, verify against a clean U.S. News & World Report copy or page image. Short phrases visible clearly in the scan may be used provisionally in research notes, not treated as final transcript.
