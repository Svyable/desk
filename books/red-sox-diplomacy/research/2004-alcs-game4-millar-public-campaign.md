# October 17, 2004 — Kevin Millar takes the clubhouse argument public

## Finding

The famous “don’t let us win tonight” line is more useful when treated as an observable piece of clubhouse behavior rather than as prophecy.

Allan Wood and Bill Nowlin’s *Don’t Let Us Win Tonight*, an oral history built from interviews with more than fifty Red Sox players, coaches, medical staff, and front-office personnel, preserves Kevin Millar’s retrospective first-person account of the hours before 2004 ALCS Game 4. Millar said he was repeating the argument to anyone who would listen: if Boston survived that night, Pedro Martínez was available for Game 5, Curt Schilling was expected back for Game 6, and Game 7 would become its own problem. He described the purpose retrospectively as trying to get his teammates to believe the possibility was real.

Oral-history source and publisher provenance:
- Triumph Books, *Don’t Let Us Win Tonight: An Oral History of the 2004 Boston Red Sox’s Impossible Playoff Run*: https://www.triumphbooks.com/don-t-let-us-win-tonight-products-9781600789137.php
- Allan Wood and Bill Nowlin, *Don’t Let Us Win Tonight* (2014; later revised edition). The first-person Millar passage is preserved in searchable ebook editions; any verbatim manuscript quotation should be checked against the print/ebook text before publication.

The important detail is that Millar did not keep the pitch inside a closed clubhouse. Contemporary-era video and later reconstructions preserve him making the case publicly around batting practice and the Red Sox dugout. The argument was baseball-specific: survive one game, then Boston’s pitching sequence changed.

That makes this distinct from the recent Wakefield, Roberts, and Schilling dossiers. Those recover hidden operational machinery. This recovers the player-facing public campaign surrounding that machinery: one veteran reducing an unprecedented four-game requirement to the next assignment and naming the pitchers who made the claim plausible.

## Why it is narratively useful

The phrase has become so famous that it is often stripped of its baseball logic and repackaged as generic inspiration. The underlying argument is better.

Boston was down three games to none after losing Game 3 by 19–8. Millar’s case was not that history, destiny, or the “curse” would reverse itself. It was a pitching map. Win Game 4; Martínez could start Game 5; Schilling might return for Game 6; then reach Game 7 and accept the uncertainty.

That is a knowledgeable-fan detail because it restores the roster mechanics underneath a slogan. It also sits cleanly beside the Wakefield dossier: Wakefield’s Game 3 relief work had helped make Derek Lowe available for Game 4, while Millar’s public argument looked forward to Martínez and Schilling behind him. The two pieces should not be collapsed into a claim that the club had a master comeback plan. They show different actors working with the next available baseball problem.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- Millar retrospectively said he repeated the “don’t let us win tonight” argument widely before Game 4;
- his stated baseball logic was that surviving Game 4 put Martínez in Game 5, Schilling in position for Game 6, and made Game 7 possible;
- his retrospective account says he wanted teammates to believe the possibility;
- the oral history is based on named interviews with Red Sox players, coaches, medical staff, and front-office personnel;
- Boston did survive Game 4 and subsequently used Martínez in Game 5 and Schilling in Game 6.

Not safe:

- claiming every teammate believed Millar;
- treating the slogan as the cause of the comeback;
- inventing a closed-door speech, team meeting, clubhouse reaction, or dialogue not preserved by a source;
- saying Terry Francona instructed Millar to make the argument;
- presenting Millar’s later account of his purpose as contemporaneous evidence of every player’s state of mind;
- turning the episode into a generic lesson about optimism, leadership, diplomacy, morale, or resilience.

## Manuscript recommendation

Use only if the 2004 sequence needs a player/clubhouse beat between the Game 3 rout and the Game 4 mechanics. Do not quote the slogan and move on. Give the reader the pitching argument attached to it.

A defensible shape is: after the 19–8 loss, Millar spent the next afternoon telling teammates, reporters, and people around the field that New York needed to finish Boston that night. His case was concrete rather than mystical: get through Game 4, then Martínez, then—if the ankle allowed it—Schilling, then whatever Game 7 became.

Keep “if the ankle allowed it” as narrator qualification rather than Millar quotation unless the exact source wording is checked. The recent Schilling medical dossier establishes how uncertain that Game 6 assumption actually was.

## Evidentiary level

**Named first-person retrospective oral history with publisher-documented interview base, supported by the established postseason pitching chronology.** Strong for what Millar later said he was arguing and why; retrospective for motive/purpose and not evidence that the argument caused teammate belief or series outcome.
