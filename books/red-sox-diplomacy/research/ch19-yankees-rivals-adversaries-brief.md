# Chapter 19 Brief — Yankees, Rivals, Adversaries

## Status

Research brief after Chapter 18.

## Working question

What does Burns's lifelong joking language of sports rivalry reveal—and, more importantly, fail to reveal—about the distinction between rivals, competitors and adversaries in diplomacy?

## Editorial danger

This chapter can become glib very quickly. Yankees–Red Sox rivalry is voluntary entertainment inside a shared system. Strategic rivalry between states can involve coercion, war, nuclear weapons and incompatible political objectives. The chapter must use the contrast to expose the limits of sports analogy, not flatten geopolitics into it.

## Documentary threads

- State briefing-room Yankees jokes and reporter banter.
- Burns's repeated public Red Sox identity after 2004, when rivalry survives without the old losing premise.
- NATO: allies can disagree bitterly without becoming adversaries.
- India: states can have serious differences while pursuing strategic partnership.
- Iran: negotiation can occur without friendship.
- China: Burns repeatedly used the language of competition while arguing for communication and guardrails.
- Burns's broader post-government writing and teaching on great-power competition, alliances and diplomacy.

## Conceptual distinctions to earn

**Rival:** competes within a relationship or system whose continued existence both sides generally accept.

**Competitor:** seeks relative advantage; competition can coexist with exchange, rules and selective cooperation.

**Adversary:** interests and actions are sufficiently opposed that coercion, deterrence and serious security conflict become central.

These are analytical tools, not fixed labels. States can move between categories, and different policy domains can contain different relationships simultaneously.

## Baseball's useful limit

The Yankees joke works because neither fan wants baseball destroyed. Rivalry presupposes a shared game, rules, seasons, institutions and another meeting.

International politics offers no comparable umpire with final authority. Rules exist, institutions matter and norms constrain behavior, but enforcement and power are radically different.

The chapter's strongest baseball move may therefore be the moment the analogy breaks.

## Possible thesis

Diplomacy begins by identifying what kind of disagreement actually exists. Treating every competitor as an enemy can manufacture hostility. Treating every adversary as merely a sporting rival can conceal danger. The representative's job is not to like the other side. It is to understand accurately what relationship he is standing inside.

## Guardrails

Do not claim Burns derived geopolitical theory from baseball. Do not equate the Yankees with China, Russia, Iran or any state. Do not use sports language around war casualties or nuclear risk. Do not imply personal rapport eliminates strategic conflict. Do not define states permanently as enemies based on one administration's rhetoric.

4. Use China-era competition language as the mature policy case.

## Draft threshold

Medium. Concept is strong, but it needs Burns's own vocabulary and should avoid becoming a retrospective essay detached from the biography.
