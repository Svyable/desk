# Athens Witness Pass 03 — Embassy as Representation

## Purpose

Raise Chapter 9 from High-Medium to draft-ready by adding direct colleague testimony and cleaner evidence for the embassy-openness episode. Keep Chapter 10's baseball network separate.

## John Koenig — direct colleague witness

John Koenig served in Athens during Nicholas Burns's ambassadorship and later described working with Burns very closely for roughly three years as head of political-military affairs.

Koenig identifies two large projects in his portfolio during the period:

- major U.S. arms sales to the Hellenic armed forces, including F-16s and Patriot systems;
- work to reduce Greek-Turkish tensions, including military-to-military issues.

Koenig's testimony is especially useful because it gives an observed judgment rather than a retrospective Burns self-description. He calls Burns unusually skilled at face-to-face persuasion and says Burns operated effectively both in public communication and direct meetings with Greek officials. Koenig specifically remembers repeatedly accompanying Burns to meetings with Defense Minister Akis Tsochatzopoulos.

Use carefully. Koenig admired Burns and later worked for him again. His testimony is a colleague's perspective, not neutral proof that every persuasion effort succeeded.

## Barbara Nielsen — public diplomacy witness

Barbara H. Nielsen served as Cultural Affairs Officer in Athens from 1999 to 2001. Her ADST oral history says Burns was very interested in public diplomacy, knew how he wanted it conducted because of his spokesman background, and "set the tone."

She describes mission programs including:

- an annual American studies seminar;
- public programming around biotechnology;
- explaining the 2000 U.S. election to Greek media and audiences;
- engagement around American-style higher education;
- cultural programming;
- partnership with organized Greek-American groups.

Nielsen also supplies an important corrective to flattening Greece into a uniformly hostile environment. She remembered loud anti-American demonstrations and a strongly critical press during Kosovo, but said day-to-day dealings with Greeks were generally workable and distinguished bureaucratic obstacles from anti-American ones.

## Embassy facade — clean corroboration

Jane C. Loeffler's June 2000 Foreign Service Journal article, "The Identity Crisis of the American Embassy," contains a dedicated Athens sidebar, "A Light Shines in Athens."

The article reports that Embassy Athens used existing funds and private contributions to increase apparent openness and make the chancery more welcoming without adding exposure. The work included cleaning exterior marble, painting stucco, replacing dense hedges and vines with local plantings, and restarting a fountain that had been off for years.

Crucially, the article itself supplies the interpretive link: Burns made improving the property a priority as a way of garnering respect and signaling U.S. respect for Greece. It also records a permanent exhibit on the U.S.-Greek relationship and a George C. Marshall sculpture in the newly visible garden.

This means Chapter 9 does not need to invent symbolism around the physical work. Contemporary professional reporting already understood it as representational.

## Historical pressure test — Kosovo and Clinton visit

The openness/public-diplomacy thesis should not be written as easy charm. In 1999, after NATO's Kosovo air campaign, anti-American protest in Greece was intense enough to affect President Clinton's planned Athens visit. Contemporary Washington Post reporting says Burns and Foreign Minister George Papandreou renegotiated visit arrangements after disputes over demonstrations near the embassy.

This gives the chapter a necessary counterweight: making an embassy look more open did not dissolve disagreement over American policy. Representation could be more legible without becoming more popular.

## Stronger Chapter 9 architecture

1. **Country team / work:** Ambassadorial representation begins in the machinery of the mission, not ceremony. Use the Marcus country-team scene if cleanly verified; otherwise open with the institution and Koenig's account of the mission's substantive work.
2. **The building:** the Gropius chancery, vines, fountain, facade, historical exhibit, Marshall sculpture. The physical post as a public object.
3. **The people:** Nielsen's account of Burns setting the public-diplomacy tone; cultural and educational programming; Greek-American engagement.
4. **The hard edge:** arms sales, Greek-Turkish tensions, Kosovo, terrorism, protests. Openness was not softness.
5. **The test:** Clinton's 1999 visit amid protests. The embassy remained a target and symbol even while the mission tried to make the relationship more mature and reciprocal.
6. **Handoff:** one form of public diplomacy would become unusually literal in Greece—baseball—but save the network and causal chain for Chapter 10.

## Evidentiary boundaries

Do not claim:

- that Burns's facade changes caused improved bilateral relations;
- that public diplomacy overcame Greek opposition to Kosovo;
- that Koenig's admiration is an objective performance rating;
- that every arms sale was personally negotiated by Burns;
- that Tsochatzopoulos's later corruption conviction implicated the U.S. arms sales Koenig describes;
- that Nielsen speaks for Greek public opinion as a whole;
- that architectural openness meant reduced security.
