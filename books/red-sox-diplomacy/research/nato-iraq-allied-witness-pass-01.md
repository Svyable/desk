# NATO Iraq Allied-Witness Pass 01

## Purpose

Strengthen Chapter 13 with non-American evidence for the February 2003 Turkey-defense dispute, so Nicholas Burns is not allowed to narrate the alliance's disagreement by himself.

## What the allied record changes

The strongest corrective is not a memoir from a single opposing ambassador. It is the combination of NATO Secretary General George Robertson's near-contemporaneous reconstruction and public statements from German and Belgian officials.

Robertson's February 20 account is unusually useful because he represented the alliance as a whole. His description is materially less accusatory than Burns's April Senate testimony. Robertson said the dispute was over **when** to begin defensive planning for Turkey, not over whether Turkey should ultimately be defended. Sixteen allies wanted to proceed; three believed moving then would send the wrong signal immediately before Hans Blix reported to the UN. Robertson explicitly described the disagreement as arising from substantive policy differences rather than caprice.

This should govern the manuscript's neutral description of the dispute.

## Belgium: Louis Michel

Contemporary reporting records Belgian Foreign Minister Louis Michel openly saying Belgium would break NATO's silence procedure and block the proposed planning. His stated rationale was that moving NATO military preparations forward would imply acceptance of the logic of war while diplomatic efforts at the United Nations were still under way.

This is important because it shows that Belgium did not publicly frame its position as indifference to Turkey's security. The objection was to timing and to the political meaning of military preparations in the wider Iraq crisis.

Later in the DPC negotiation Belgium proposed amendments to the decision text. Robertson's February 16 press conference says those amendments were considered at length before all eighteen DPC members agreed.

## Germany: Joschka Fischer

German Foreign Minister Joschka Fischer's public position likewise separated defense of Turkey from participation in a war against Iraq. Contemporary reporting has him arguing before the decision that peaceful means had not been exhausted and military moves were premature.

After the compromise, Fischer publicly emphasized that German participation in AWACS and Patriot-related support for Turkey was defensive and confined to alliance territory. Germany's behavior therefore supplies an especially useful fact for the chapter: a government that resisted the timing of NATO planning nevertheless contributed materially to Turkey's defense once consensus was reached.

Robertson made the same point in his February 20 account, explaining that Germany's bilateral willingness to send Patriots was intended to demonstrate that Berlin did not dispute the substantive obligation to defend Turkey.

## France

France's institutional position needs precision. France opposed moving the measures when the North Atlantic Council could not reach consensus. It was then absent from the Defence Planning Committee because at the time it did not participate in NATO's integrated military structure.

Robertson was explicit after the DPC decision that France's absence should not be read as lack of commitment to Turkey's ultimate defense. France believed the measures were not yet opportune.

The chapter should therefore avoid the easy formulation that NATO simply 'went around France.' The DPC was an established alliance body responsible for the integrated military structure. Robertson later said he delayed moving the issue there until he concluded that nineteen-member agreement was impossible. NATO's current institutional history says the transfer occurred with the concurrence of all member countries.

## Exact procedural sequence

1. NATO had discussed possible defensive support for Turkey since December 2002.
2. On February 6, Robertson used the silence procedure on a package of planning measures.
3. France, Germany and Belgium broke silence.
4. Turkey formally requested Article 4 consultations on February 10.
5. The North Atlantic Council met repeatedly but could not reach consensus among nineteen allies.
6. Robertson concluded on February 16 that further progress in the Council was not possible.
7. The issue moved to the Defence Planning Committee, then composed of eighteen allies because France was outside the integrated military structure.
8. Germany and Belgium ultimately joined consensus after a long DPC session; Belgium proposed amendments that were incorporated/handled in the final negotiation.
9. On February 19 the DPC authorized urgent implementation of defensive measures under Operation Display Deterrence.
10. The resulting measures included AWACS support, air/missile defense and chemical/biological defense capabilities for Turkey.

## The best neutral formulation

The February 2003 dispute was not fundamentally about whether Turkey was a NATO ally entitled to defense. It was about whether beginning NATO military planning at that moment would be prudent collective defense or a political signal that narrowed the remaining space for UN diplomacy over Iraq.

Burns considered the blockage a crisis of alliance credibility. Belgium, France and Germany considered premature action capable of prejudging the wider Iraq crisis. Robertson's alliance-level account validates the existence of both positions and says the differences were substantive.

That tension is much better for Chapter 13 than a heroes-and-obstructionists account.

## The repair evidence

Robertson's November 2003 retrospective gives the chapter an unusually clean post-rift test. Within months of the Turkey fight:

- NATO agreed unanimously to take command of ISAF in Afghanistan, an initiative Robertson attributed to Canada, Germany and the Netherlands;
- the alliance later agreed to support Poland in assembling a multinational stabilization division for Iraq.

Former opponents therefore did not need to agree retrospectively about the Iraq war in order to resume alliance work together.

This is the chapter's strongest evidence for the proposition that alliance durability is not the absence of disagreement. It is the ability to continue making decisions after disagreement.

## Burns-specific contrast

Burns's April 1, 2003 Senate testimony is much sharper. He said France, Germany and Belgium had produced a crisis of credibility and violated NATO's core fabric; he praised Germany and Belgium for eventually agreeing. That is valuable primary evidence of how the U.S. ambassador understood the fight, but it should be presented as Burns's judgment, not the book's omniscient verdict.

His testimony also records the seven invited future allies visiting his office and saying they would have supported assistance to Turkey. This can show how enlargement politics intersected with the crisis, but it should not be used to imply that the invitees settled the dispute's merits.

## Draft implication

Chapter 13 is now draft-ready.

A named opposing NATO ambassador's later oral-history recollection would still be valuable, especially Benoît d'Aboville of France, but it is no longer necessary to make the chapter balanced. The near-contemporaneous allied record already establishes the opposing rationale, the procedural mechanics, and the fact that Germany contributed to Turkey's defense despite having opposed the original timing.

## Attribution guardrail

Do not turn Robertson's institutional interpretation into the private motives of French, German or Belgian ambassadors. Do not call the DPC route a trick or procedural ambush without direct evidence. Do not claim that subsequent cooperation erased the Iraq disagreement.
