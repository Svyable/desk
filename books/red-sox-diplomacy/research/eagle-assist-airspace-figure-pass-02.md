## Why this pass exists

The existing Eagle Assist material has a clean political chronology and a cleared human photograph at Geilenkirchen. This pass adds the missing operational picture: what the NATO aircraft actually did over North America and how to show that without copying a protected map or implying that Nicholas Burns directed air operations.

## Primary operational facts

NATO's contemporary 30 April 2002 statement says the North Atlantic Council agreed to terminate Operation Eagle Assist effective 16 May 2002 after a U.S. evaluation found material improvements in the U.S. air-defence posture and enhanced civil-military cooperation. It dates the operation's beginning to 9 October 2001, following the Council's 4 October decision to operationalise Article 5, and reports 830 crew members from 13 NATO nations, nearly 4,300 patrol hours, and more than 360 operational sorties.

Source: NATO, "Statement by the Secretary General on the conclusion of Operation Eagle Assist," 30 April 2002.
https://www.nato.int/docu/update/2002/04-april/e0430a.htm

A NATO historical page adds a useful equipment fact: seven NATO Airborne Early Warning and Control System aircraft ultimately supported the mission. NATO's 18 March 2002 account says five aircraft were initially deployed and two more followed in January 2002. It also explains the function rather than merely naming the platform: AWACS aircraft provide airborne surveillance and early warning while passing data to command-and-control centres on land, at sea and in the air.

Sources:
- NATO, "AWACS in action," 2 November 2016: https://www.nato.int/cps/en/natohq/declassified_137124.htm
- NATO, "Geilenkirchen and Operation EAGLE ASSIST," 18 March 2002: https://www.nato.int/cps/en/natohq/news_18874.htm (current NATO routing may redirect by language/site revision)

A contemporaneous American Forces Press Service report, now migrated to DVIDS, supplies the deployment mechanics. It reports that the first five NATO AWACS aircraft deployed from Geilenkirchen, Germany, beginning 9 October for Tinker Air Force Base, Oklahoma. NORAD spokesman Capt. Ed Thomas said the aircraft would augment U.S. resources and provide radar coverage, surveillance, and situational awareness for continental defence. The article states that the NATO aircraft operated under NORAD command. This is a U.S. Department of Defense-origin news record; use its factual content as a primary federal source, not its page artwork.

Source: Gerald J. Gilmore and Rudi Williams, American Forces Press Service, "NATO Sends Radar Surveillance Planes to Protect United States," 9 October 2001; DVIDS migration posted 3 July 2025.
https://www.dvidshub.net/news/526646/nato-sends-radar-surveillance-planes-protect-united-states

## Date discrepancy that must remain visible

NATO's contemporary and historical accounts date operational deployment/start to **9 October 2001**. The U.S. Air Force Personnel Center's NATO Medal page gives **12 October 2001** as the beginning of the qualifying service period for the Article 5 NATO Medal. These are not interchangeable dates. The reader-facing chronology should use 9 October for the operation/deployment and, if the medal appears at all, label 12 October specifically as the medal-eligibility period start.

AFPC source: https://www.afpc.af.mil/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/421921/article-5-nato-medal-operation-eagle-assist/

### Figure: Europe sent radar west

Produce an **original schematic route/operations map**, not a traced NATO, NORAD, Air Force, Google, or commercial basemap.

Minimum geometry:

1. A simplified Atlantic outline sufficient to locate western Europe and the continental United States. Draw coastlines from a clearly reusable public-domain geographic dataset (preferred: Natural Earth public-domain vector data) or use an intentionally abstract ocean/continent silhouette created in-house.
2. Mark **Geilenkirchen, Germany** as the NATO AWACS home/departure base.
3. Mark **Tinker Air Force Base, Oklahoma** as the initial U.S. deployment base identified by the contemporaneous AFPS/DVIDS report.
4. Use one westbound route arrow labelled `9 OCT 2001 — initial five NATO AWACS begin deploying`.
5. Add a small January marker: `JAN 2002 — two additional aircraft; seven total`.
6. Do **not** draw patrol tracks, coverage circles, city targets, or a precise operational area unless an authoritative public source documents them. "U.S. skies" / North American airspace is adequately supported; exact flight paths are not established by the sources in this packet.
7. Pair the map with a compact terminal ledger rather than another decorative aircraft silhouette: `830 crew / 13 nations / nearly 4,300 hours / 360+ operational sorties / ends 16 MAY 2002`.

This figure clarifies a point the political chronology alone cannot: Article 5 became aircraft, crews, an Atlantic deployment, a U.S. operating base, and months of surveillance work.

## Reader-facing caption

**Operation Eagle Assist, October 2001–May 2002.** NATO AWACS aircraft began deploying from Geilenkirchen, Germany, to Tinker Air Force Base, Oklahoma, on October 9, 2001, after the Alliance moved to operationalize Article 5. Five aircraft deployed initially; two more followed in January. By the operation's end on May 16, NATO reported 830 crew members from thirteen nations, nearly 4,300 patrol hours, and more than 360 operational sorties. *Original schematic; geography simplified. Sources: NATO and American Forces Press Service/DVIDS.*

## Alt text

Simplified map of the North Atlantic showing a westbound deployment from Geilenkirchen, Germany, to Tinker Air Force Base, Oklahoma. ## Claim boundaries

This material supports the mechanics and scale of Eagle Assist. It does **not** support claims that Burns selected the bases, commanded the aircraft, chose patrol routes, or personally directed NORAD operations. Burns belongs in the book's political/alliance story; the air-operation figure belongs beside that story as the consequence of an alliance decision.
