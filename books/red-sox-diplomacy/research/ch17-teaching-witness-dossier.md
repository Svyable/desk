# Chapter 17 Dossier — Teaching Diplomacy

## Documentary center

Nicholas Burns's Harvard years are not an intermission between government jobs. They are a change in professional vantage point: the former official turns experience into material that students are expected to question.

## Burns's own theory

In a 2011 Harvard Kennedy School interview, Burns described what professors of practice can add to an academic institution. His emphasis was direct experience of how governments succeed and fail. That formulation matters because it does not say experience makes practitioners automatically right. The value is access to a class of evidence: what governing looks like from inside decisions.

## Institutional form

Burns joined Harvard Kennedy School after retiring from the Foreign Service in 2008. He founded and chaired the Future of Diplomacy Project, which developed teaching, research, practitioner programming and public discussion around diplomacy, negotiation and statecraft.

By 2021, HKS described the project as using extensive videotaped interviews with former secretaries of state in graduate courses on negotiation and diplomacy. The American Secretaries of State Project turned practitioner recollection into an archive students could compare rather than a single memoir they had to accept.

That is narratively important. Burns moved from being a source inside government to building a structure in which sources could be interrogated.

## Student evidence

Harvard Kennedy School's profile of Hainer Sibrian MPP 2020 records a student describing Burns's diplomacy course as a useful pairing with a course on what happens when diplomacy fails. This is modest but valuable evidence of how at least one student understood the course: diplomacy as one instrument with limits, not a universal solution.

An HKS alumni profile of Christy Vines MC/MPA 2010 says a diplomacy class with Burns changed her focus and led her to specialize in foreign policy and national security. Use only as evidence about this individual student's trajectory, not as proof of general teaching impact.

## Classroom architecture

The current HKS `Negotiation and Diplomacy` course description, taught by Burns and James Sebenius, explicitly studies successful and failed negotiations and emphasizes the `how` of diplomacy: how officials negotiate, how pressure interacts with negotiation, what barriers prevent agreement, and what can be learned from practitioners' recorded accounts.

The present course is not identical to every course Burns taught during 2008–21, so use it as evidence of the mature teaching method, not as a retroactive syllabus for all thirteen years.

## The professor's changed authority

At State, Burns's authority was delegated. At a podium, his answer could become the government's answer. As an ambassador, his communications carried official standing.

At Harvard, his experience gave him credibility but not sovereign authority over interpretation. Students could disagree. Other scholars could challenge his account. Recorded interviews could be compared with documents and other participants. Public commentary could be wrong and later revisited.

This is the chapter's useful transformation: experience becomes examinable.

## Active public life

Do not portray the Harvard period as retirement into observation. Burns wrote and spoke publicly on active foreign-policy crises, chaired projects on diplomacy and transatlantic relations, engaged practitioners, and remained part of the policy community. Distance from government removed delegated authority; it did not remove participation in argument.

## Bridge to China

Harvard states that Burns taught at HKS for thirteen years before his 2021 appointment as U.S. ambassador to China. The narrative question for the next chapter is therefore unusually clean: after thirteen years of explaining how governments succeed and fail, how does the professor behave when he again has to speak with delegated authority?

## Guardrails

Do not generalize from two student witnesses to all students. Do not claim Burns became more reflective merely because he became a professor. Do not invent classroom dialogue. Do not imply recorded oral histories are inherently accurate; their value is that recollection becomes available for comparison and challenge.
