# Chapter 5 Brief — Jerusalem: Rivalry Is Not Hatred

## Status

Research brief. Do not draft from the title alone.

## Burns's documented assignment

R. Nicholas Burns served as a political officer at the American Consulate General in Jerusalem from 1985 to 1987. Official State biographies say he coordinated U.S. economic assistance to the Palestinian population in the West Bank and East Jerusalem. Some later institutional biographies shorten this to assistance to Palestinians in the West Bank.

This is already enough to establish a significant change from Cairo: Burns moved from a huge bilateral embassy and ambassadorial staff role into the unusual institutional position of the Jerusalem consulate, with direct work touching Palestinian economic assistance.

## Institutional setting

The ADST Jerusalem country reader identifies Wat T. Cluverius IV as consul general through 1985, Douglas R. Keene as deputy principal officer from 1983 to 1986, and Morris Draper as consul general from 1986 to 1988. Burns's tour therefore appears to span another leadership transition.

The Jerusalem post was institutionally unusual. It was not simply a subordinate section of Embassy Tel Aviv. Contemporary and later diplomatic oral histories describe the consulate general as operating independently, with the consul general functioning in practice at ambassadorial level for Jerusalem and Palestinian affairs.

That structure matters for a book about representation: Burns moved into a post where American diplomatic geography itself embodied the unresolved political status of the place.

## The title problem

“Rivalry Is Not Hatred” is potentially strong but dangerous. It could become a facile baseball analogy imposed on Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

Do not write that chapter.

The defensible question is narrower: how does a diplomat work amid durable opposition without treating political conflict as proof that human contact is meaningless? Burns's actual portfolio—economic assistance to Palestinians—may give us a grounded way into that question, but only after we know what the programs were and whom he worked with.

## High-value research questions

1. Which USAID or consulate assistance programs did Burns coordinate?
2. Who supervised him directly—Keene, Cluverius, Draper, another section chief?
3. Do Keene or Draper oral histories mention Burns or the economic-assistance portfolio?
4. Which Palestinian officials, municipal figures, businesspeople, universities, NGOs, or development institutions interacted with the consulate on these programs?
5. Are there State cables, congressional hearings, GAO reports, or USAID documents from 1985–87 that describe the West Bank/East Jerusalem assistance architecture?
6. Did Burns later speak or write specifically about his Jerusalem posting?
7. Can we identify a direct witness comparable to Ted Osius in Cairo?
8. What was the exact institutional relationship among Consulate Jerusalem, Embassy Tel Aviv, USAID, and Washington on Palestinian assistance?

## Historical boundary

Burns left Jerusalem in 1987, before the First Intifada began in December of that year. Do not write him into the Intifada or use later events as though he experienced them in-post.

The chapter's immediate period includes the aftermath of the Lebanon war, a cold regional diplomatic environment, continued occupation of the West Bank and East Jerusalem, and the absence of the later Oslo framework. Those facts are context, not Burns biography, until tied to his work.

## Narrative opportunity

Cairo taught us to look at the seams inside an embassy. Jerusalem may let us look at seams in diplomatic geography itself.

Burns was an American political officer assigned to a consulate whose institutional independence reflected a U.S. position on Jerusalem, while his substantive work involved assistance to Palestinians living under Israeli occupation. That is inherently more interesting than forcing Yankees/Red Sox language onto the conflict.

## Guardrails

Do not invent:

- checkpoints or crossings Burns personally used
- meetings with Palestinians or Israelis not documented in sources
- private views about occupation, Israel, Palestinian nationalism, or religion
- a personal friendship across the conflict unless sourced
- baseball conversations in Jerusalem
- a claim that the posting taught Burns “rivalry is not hatred”
- scenes from the First Intifada
