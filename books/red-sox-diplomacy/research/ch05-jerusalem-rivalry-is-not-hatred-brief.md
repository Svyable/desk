# Chapter 5 Brief — Jerusalem: Rivalry Is Not Hatred

## Status

Research brief, now materially augmented. The evidence supports a chapter about diplomatic ambiguity, Palestinian assistance, and maintaining political channels. It still does **not** support a baseball analogy for the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

## Burns's documented assignment

R. Nicholas Burns served as a political officer at the American Consulate General in Jerusalem from 1985 to 1987. Official and later institutional biographies consistently say he coordinated U.S. economic assistance to Palestinians in the West Bank; some explicitly include East Jerusalem.

This is a significant change from Cairo: Burns moved from a huge bilateral embassy and ambassadorial staff role into the unusual institutional position of the Jerusalem consulate, with direct work touching Palestinian economic assistance.

## Institutional setting

The ADST Jerusalem country reader identifies Wat T. Cluverius IV as consul general through 1985, Douglas R. Keene as deputy principal officer from 1983 to 1986, and Morris Draper as consul general from 1986 to 1988. Burns's tour therefore spanned another leadership transition.

The post was institutionally unusual. It was not simply a subordinate section of Embassy Tel Aviv. Draper's oral history describes a separate mission whose status reflected longstanding U.S. policy that Jerusalem's final status remained unresolved. Contemporary reporting on Draper's arrival likewise described the consul general, unofficially, as a kind of American representative to the occupied/administered territories.

That structure matters for a book about representation: Burns worked in a post where the organization chart itself carried a diplomatic position.

## Assistance scale

Later Congressional Research Service tables drawing on State/USAID figures report West Bank and Gaza assistance of approximately:

- FY1985: $12.141 million
- FY1986: $13.950 million
- FY1987: $8.400 million

These figures establish the scale of the assistance environment, **not** Burns's personal budget authority.

A contemporary 1985 international-NGO report describes U.S.-government-funded projects administered through private voluntary organizations across economic development, social/educational services, and public works. It reports a large submitted project pipeline and also records the political/administrative constraints affecting which projects could actually be implemented.

A 1986 UN report on assistance to Palestinians shows the broader development environment Burns was working beside: water systems, agricultural development and training, universities and vocational education, health institutions, municipal equipment, small-scale industry, women's institutions, fisheries, and infrastructure. These UN projects are contextual; do not mislabel them as Burns programs or USAID programs.

## A documented political channel during Burns's tour

In July 1986 Vice President George H. W. Bush met Palestinian political and civic figures at the U.S. Consulate in Jerusalem. Contemporary accounts identify participants including East Jerusalem editor Hanna Siniora, Bethlehem mayor Elias Freij, acting Nablus mayor Hafez Touqan, and Gaza activist Hatem Abu Ghazaleh. Other invitees stayed away amid boycott pressure and reported threats.

No evidence yet places Burns personally in the meeting. Do not put him there.

The episode nevertheless demonstrates what the institution around him was doing: maintaining an American political channel to Palestinian figures before the Oslo framework and while U.S. policy sharply constrained official dealings with the PLO.

## The title problem

`Rivalry Is Not Hatred` remains potentially strong but dangerous. It risks trivializing a conflict involving occupation, sovereignty, violence, displacement, and competing national claims by importing Red Sox/Yankees language.

The research now suggests a stronger documentary center: **representation without recognition**, **diplomacy in the seams**, or **the unresolved address**.

The chapter can ask how diplomats operate when even the status of the office, the people represented, and the political destination of assistance are contested.

## Historical boundary

Burns left Jerusalem in 1987, before the First Intifada began in December. Do not write him into the uprising or use later events as though he experienced them in-post.

The immediate setting includes the aftermath of the Lebanon war, continued Israeli occupation of the West Bank and East Jerusalem, Palestinian political organization under substantial constraints, and the absence of the later Oslo institutional framework. Context must remain distinct from Burns-specific biography.

## Narrative opportunity

Cairo lets the book look at seams inside an embassy. Jerusalem can look at seams in diplomatic geography itself.

Burns was an American political officer in an institution whose separate status reflected an unresolved sovereignty question. His documented portfolio involved economic assistance to Palestinians. During his tour the consulate also served as a venue for direct American contact with Palestinian civic and political figures.

This is richer than the original sports-derived title. It also connects naturally to the book's larger question: how does a representative make official power legible when the categories themselves—capital, territory, recognition, counterpart—are disputed?

## Guardrails

Do not invent:

- checkpoints or crossings Burns personally used;
- meetings with Palestinians or Israelis not documented in sources;
- Burns's attendance at the 1986 Bush meeting;
- private views about occupation, Israel, Palestinian nationalism, religion, or the PLO;
- a personal friendship across the conflict unless sourced;
- baseball conversations in Jerusalem;
- a claim that Jerusalem taught Burns a specific later doctrine;
- scenes from the First Intifada;
- a claim that UN development projects were Burns-managed programs.

## Evidence still worth finding

1. A named Palestinian counterpart who remembers working with Burns.
2. A Burns-authored or Burns-cleared cable from Jerusalem.
3. USAID historical documentation identifying the exact U.S. project architecture he coordinated.
4. Douglas Keene testimony about the political/economic section and staff.
5. Burns's own later recollection of Jerusalem beyond the résumé line.
6. Evidence resolving whether East Jerusalem was formally inside the aid portfolio in every phase of his tour.
