# Jerusalem — Burns-Specific Witness Addendum

## Why this changes Chapter 5 readiness

The Jerusalem research has now produced the direct colleague witness the chapter was waiting for.

Douglas R. Keene, deputy principal officer at the U.S. Consulate General in Jerusalem during the first part of Burns's 1985–87 tour, later described the consulate's aid program and named Burns directly. Keene said the program was run through grants to nongovernmental organizations and that a junior officer normally served as its point of contact. He then identified “a young Nick Burns” as one of the officers who worked with him on it.

This is materially stronger than the résumé formulation that Burns “coordinated economic assistance.” It gives us a contemporaneous supervisor-level witness explaining the mechanism: NGO grants, a designated junior-officer point of contact, and Burns working directly with Keene.

## Keene's institutional picture

Keene's oral history also supplies the surrounding operating environment:

- Consulate officers traveled extensively through the West Bank and maintained a broad Palestinian contact network.
- The post had to manage relations with Embassy Tel Aviv, which Keene remembered as initially marked by mutual suspicion and clashing cables before improving through regular liaison and country-team participation.
- Officers interacted with Palestinian political and civic figures, Israeli officials, journalists, academics, American citizens, congressional delegations, and international organizations.
- Keene describes the consulate as consciously trying to understand both Israeli and Palestinian constituencies while operating in an intensely politicized environment.
- He recalls constant sensitivity over wording and representation because small signals could be interpreted as evidence that the post was either pro-Arab or anti-Israeli.

These observations describe the post and Keene's experience. They should not be converted automatically into scenes featuring Burns unless a source places Burns in the particular episode.

## Burns's own retrospective — 2025

A June 2025 Harvard/Belfer podcast transcript adds the first strong first-person retrospective located in this research program. Burns says that in Jerusalem he managed the economic-assistance program for Palestinians in the West Bank and East Jerusalem and calls the posting a “seminal” and very important experience. He also says he was an Arabic speaker and, at that stage, expected his career to remain in Africa and the Arab world.

This is important because it finally permits the manuscript to say that **Burns himself regarded Jerusalem as formative**. Earlier drafts correctly avoided making that inference from chronology alone.

## A useful personnel-directory detail

A historical State Department personnel directory for Jerusalem lists R. Nicholas Burns as `ECO/COM` under Consul General Morris Draper. That title is more specific than the generic later biography label `Political Officer` and fits the documented assistance/economic portfolio.

Treat this as a period-directory title for at least that directory snapshot, not necessarily his title during every month of the 1985–87 assignment.

## Editorial consequence

Chapter 5 can now be drafted without hiding behind institutional context. We have three levels of evidence:

1. **Burns's own later account:** he managed the Palestinian economic-assistance program and considered Jerusalem a seminal experience.
2. **A direct supervisor-level witness:** Keene identifies young Nick Burns as working with him as a point person on an NGO-grant aid program.
3. **Institutional evidence:** official biographies and period directories establish the posting, portfolio, East Jerusalem/West Bank geography, and economic/commercial function.

The chapter still must not invent named Palestinian meetings, travel routes, private reactions, or a baseball lesson.

## Best chapter center now

The strongest chapter is no longer “Rivalry Is Not Hatred.” A better center is the problem of **representation inside unresolved geography**.

Burns was learning to administer practical assistance in a place where almost every institutional category carried political meaning: Jerusalem's status, the consulate's independence, the relationship with Embassy Tel Aviv, the identity of Palestinian counterparts, the occupation, and the distinction between humanitarian/development work and political recognition.

The aid program makes that abstraction concrete. Money had to move through institutions. Projects had to reach people. NGOs had to operate. American officials had to maintain channels without pretending the sovereignty question was settled.

That is enough for a factual chapter with genuine tension.
