# Jerusalem 1985–87 — official-role anchor and baseball boundary

## Finding

A contemporaneous-era U.S. State Department biography preserved in the Department's 1997–2001 archive gives a clean official account of Nicholas Burns's Jerusalem posting. It states that Burns served as **Political Officer at the American Consulate General in Jerusalem from 1985 to 1987** and that in that position he **coordinated U.S. economic assistance to the Palestinian population in the West Bank and East Jerusalem**.

Primary official source:
- U.S. Department of State archived biography, “Biography: Amb. R. Nicholas Burns”: https://1997-2001.state.gov/about_state/biography/burns.html

A George W. Bush White House archived biography independently repeats the 1985–87 Jerusalem assignment and the West Bank/East Jerusalem economic-assistance responsibility:
- https://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/government/nburns-bio.html

## Why this matters

The manuscript's 1986 Jerusalem gap needs a documented professional frame before any family, baseball, or cultural texture is added. These official biographies establish Burns's title, institution, dates, and substantive portfolio without requiring retrospective scene reconstruction.

The safest narrative use is therefore institutional: in 1986 Burns was a relatively early-career Foreign Service officer at the American Consulate General in Jerusalem, working on U.S. economic assistance to Palestinians in the West Bank and East Jerusalem.

## Baseball search result

Targeted searches for combinations of Burns, Jerusalem, 1985–87, baseball, and the Red Sox did **not** surface a reliable contemporaneous or authenticated first-person source documenting baseball activity during the posting.

Later institutional biographies often place two facts on the same page — the Jerusalem assignment and Burns's lifelong Red Sox identity — but that juxtaposition is **not evidence** that baseball played a documented role in Jerusalem.

Do not bridge those facts narratively unless a diary, letter, interview, oral history, photograph, cable, colleague account, or other authenticated source supplies the missing connection.

## Attribution and family boundary

The official 1997–2001 State Department biography says Burns was married to Elizabeth Baylies and that they had three daughters. It does not provide a Jerusalem family anecdote, baseball memory, household scene, or statement by Baylies.

Accordingly, do **not** attribute to Libby/Elizabeth Baylies:
- Red Sox fandom or indifference;
- game watching or listening;
- opinions about Jerusalem, the posting, baseball, or Burns's fandom;
- dialogue, recollection, reaction, motive, or interiority;
- any first-person material not independently authenticated to her.

## What this does not establish

Do not use these sources to claim:
- where Burns lived in Jerusalem;
- where or whether he watched or followed the Red Sox in 1985, 1986, or 1987;
- that he listened to games by Armed Forces Radio or any other broadcast service;
- that colleagues knew or discussed his fandom;
- that baseball functioned as diplomacy during this posting;
- that a particular 1986 Red Sox game or World Series moment was experienced in Jerusalem;
- any causal interpretation of how the Jerusalem assignment shaped his later diplomacy.

## Next archival targets

1. Association for Diplomatic Studies and Training oral histories for Jerusalem Consulate officers serving 1985–87, searched for Burns and social/recreational life.
2. State Department Foreign Affairs Oral History Collection interviews with the consul general, deputy principal officer, political/economic officers, and USAID-linked personnel from the posting.
3. American Consulate General Jerusalem newsletters, CLO/community publications, photographs, and employee recreation material, if preserved.
4. Burns-authenticated interviews or correspondence specifically discussing the 1986 Red Sox while overseas.
5. Contemporary Boston press profiles, alumni notes, or hometown coverage that might have contacted Burns abroad during the 1986 pennant/World Series run.

Until such evidence appears, Jerusalem should remain a documented diplomatic setting rather than an invented baseball scene.
