# Athens Primary Archive Pass 02 — Red Sox Diplomacy

This pass narrows the Greece research toward documentary scenes rather than broad background. It is designed to support Chapters 9–10 and the larger public-diplomacy argument.

## 1. The embassy itself as public diplomacy

A June 2000 *Foreign Service Journal* feature, “A Light Shines in Athens,” describes Burns using the physical embassy as an instrument of representation. The article says that when he arrived in 1997 he made improving the appearance of the chancery a priority, as a way of signaling respect for Greece. It records that the embassy cleaned and restored the exterior, revived a fountain, established a permanent exhibit on the U.S.-Greek relationship, and commissioned a sculpture of George C. Marshall.

Burns is quoted saying the result was a “complete and dramatic change for the better” and that the building “actually gleams once again.”

Narrative use: this gives the Athens chapters a physical setting and a concrete example of Burns’s public-diplomacy instinct. Representation was not limited to speeches. He appears to have thought about what the building itself communicated.

Source: https://afsa.org/sites/default/files/fsj-2000-06-june_0.pdf

## 2. Personal objects inside official space

A 1999 congressional hearing record on public diplomacy reproduces or preserves material describing Burns in his Athens office surrounded by Boston objects, including a Red Sox cap signed by President Clinton and Celtics/Boston memorabilia, while he discusses changing the tenor of U.S.-Greek relations.

The quoted Burns line is that the United States had to convince Greeks that “the days of intervention” and “America as Big Brother” were over, and that the relationship should become “normal, rational, mature, open.”

This is potentially one of the most valuable scenes in the book because the personal and official registers literally occupy the same room: Boston memorabilia alongside an ambassador trying to make American power less overbearing.

Caution: before manuscript quotation, recover the underlying article or a clean page image and verify exact wording and provenance. The congressional PDF is a useful archival lead but should not be our only transcription source.

Source: https://chrissmith.house.gov/uploadedfiles/1999.03.04_foreign_relations_authorization_for_fy_2000_2001-_public_diplomacy_programs.pdf

## 3. Burns’s explicit relationship strategy

A 1998 Harvard Crimson account of Burns speaking at the Kennedy School reports that he made stronger U.S.-Greek relations his first priority and explicitly advocated strong personal relationships between leaders.

Useful line: “Our very first priority is to strengthen a relationship that should be as close as it can be.”

Narrative use: this helps establish that relationship-building was a stated policy method, not an interpretation imposed later by the baseball book.

Source: https://www.thecrimson.com/article/1998/11/3/ambassador-to-greece-urges-improved-relations/

## 4. Institutional public diplomacy around the embassy

An American Hellenic Institute account of an October 2000 “Celebration of the U.S.-Greek Relationship” documents a series of embassy-associated events: a joint press conference, briefings, a reception with the mayor of Athens, a Philhellenes monument rededication, tree planting, unveiling of a George C. Marshall statue, and an embassy reception.

Narrative use: Burns’s public diplomacy in Greece operated through repeated civic rituals and historical symbolism, not merely private government meetings. Baseball should be written as one especially vivid branch of this broader method.

Source: https://ahiworld.serverbox.net/media-center/newsletters/2000/1264-december-3-2000.html

## 5. Baseball project — verified chain

Contemporaneous Associated Press reporting from January 2000 describes Greece as having essentially no baseball infrastructure: only two neglected diamonds at a former U.S. base. Burns is described as taking on an additional “baseball ambassador” role to coordinate the effort, with Major League Baseball’s international division involved.

Burns explains the cultural gap directly: “Most Greeks with whom I’ve talked say, ‘Baseball—we can’t figure out what’s going on.’”

Source: https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-jan-02-mn-49873-story.html

A September 1999 *Washington Post* item documents a reception held by Burns and the Hellenic Amateur Baseball Federation involving Peter Angelos. The commissioner's office and Boston Red Sox were reported as helping.

Source: https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/sports/1999/09/08/injuries-to-teens-are-still-a-concern/fe2eb9f0-7fc2-4b43-80a4-a9cb6ac94670/

The Hellenic Baseball Federation’s retrospective records that federation president Panos Mitsiopoulos approached Burns for help, after which Burns contacted Senator Paul Sarbanes; Sarbanes connected the effort to Orioles owner Peter Angelos.

Source: https://greekbaseball.com/history

A 2004 *Christian Science Monitor* retrospective quotes Burns calling himself “a confirmed, unconditional, rabid fan of the Boston Red Sox” and saying that bringing baseball to Greece was “one of the best things I ever did as ambassador.”

Source: https://www.csmonitor.com/2004/0818/p02s01-woeu.html

## 6. Sports beyond baseball

A 1998 *Washington Post* report notes Burns meeting with U.S. basketball players after a game in Greece. This is not central enough by itself to carry narrative weight, but it is a useful signal that Burns’s sports-facing representation in Athens was not limited to baseball.

Source: https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/sports/1998/08/10/us-earns-bronze/4b65d471-b0e4-420b-bd1b-cfaac9480b00/

## 7. Athens thesis now supported by the record

The evidence supports a stronger claim than “Burns liked baseball while ambassador to Greece.”

Burns appears to have treated representation as an ecosystem: personal relationships, civic events, cultural exchange, the embassy’s physical presentation, historical symbolism, media work, and—in the baseball case—a transnational institutional network linking Greek organizers, diaspora figures, a U.S. senator, an MLB owner, MLB’s international operation, and the Red Sox.

Baseball matters because it is the most vivid case where his personal identity and the embassy’s public-diplomacy function visibly converge.
