# Chapter 11 Brief — Twelve Days

## Status

Draft-ready research brief. This replaces the working title **September Baseball** unless later evidence genuinely reconnects baseball to Burns's September 2001 experience.

## Why the title changes

The documentary record for Nicholas Burns on September 11, 2001 is unusually strong. The documentary record for baseball in his personal 9/11 experience is not.

Major League Baseball did suspend play after the attacks, and baseball's later return became part of the national cultural response. But there is no current evidence that Burns himself used baseball to understand, narrate, or endure those days in Brussels.

Do not force the book's organizing metaphor into a chapter where Burns's own record gives us something better.

The strongest fact is that he had arrived as U.S. ambassador to NATO only **twelve days** before September 11.

## Documentary center

Burns's later first-person accounts are consistent on the central sequence:

- He was at NATO headquarters in Brussels on September 11, only twelve days into the job.
- In his 2024 Foreign Service Journal account, he says he was at the weekly ambassadors' lunch when a Belgian employee told him a plane had hit the World Trade Center.
- After the second strike, the possibility of accident disappeared.
- The White House, State Department, and Pentagon were evacuated, and Burns's team could not immediately reach Washington.
- Allied ambassadors began contacting him anyway.
- Burns repeatedly credits Canadian ambassador David Wright as the first or among the first to raise invoking Article 5; later accounts also name Danish, Norwegian, Icelandic, German, British, Greek, and other allied ambassadors as part of the rapid solidarity.
- The North Atlantic Council met that night.
- Burns had to report on an attack whose full scale was still unclear while the alliance considered the most consequential collective-defense decision in its history.

## Chapter 11 / Chapter 12 division

Chapter 11 should be about **shock, isolation, instinct, and the first calls**.

Chapter 12, **Article 5**, should be about the formal alliance decision, Washington authorization, the September 12 vote, operational consequences, AWACS, Afghanistan, and Burns's later interpretation of what collective defense meant.

That division prevents Chapter 11 from consuming the strongest material intended for Chapter 12.

## Dramatic center

The chapter can open at the ambassadors' lunch.

Burns had barely arrived. He was not yet an old NATO hand in this post. A Belgian employee comes through a side door with the first report. Burns initially wonders whether weather or an accident caused the crash. Minutes later, the second plane ends that possibility.

Then comes the institutional rupture: the U.S. ambassador to NATO cannot reach the institutions he formally represents because Washington is being evacuated.

The phone begins ringing from the other direction.

That reversal is the chapter.

Normally an American ambassador at NATO carries Washington into the alliance.

For several hours on September 11, the alliance begins carrying itself toward Washington before Burns can fully reach Washington.

## Burns-specific first-person evidence

### 2024 Foreign Service Journal / AFSA HTML

Burns describes:

- the weekly ambassadors' lunch;
- the Belgian employee entering through a side door;
- his initial thought that weather might explain the first plane;
- inability to reach evacuated U.S. institutions;
- worry over whether every ally would support a collective response;
- the late-night meeting of the nineteen ambassadors;
- his report on the attack;
- the fact that several allies still needed instructions from capitals.

This is the strongest scene-level retrospective because it is written by Burns and contains concrete sequence without requiring invented reconstruction.

### Harvard Magazine, 2020

Burns says he could not reach Washington for several hours and remembers allied ambassadors calling in succession. He gives special credit to Canadian ambassador David Wright for raising Article 5.

### 2026 Amanpour retrospective

Burns again says he had been ambassador only twelve days. He recalls being unable to reach the White House, State, or Pentagon while allied ambassadors called to say they were with the United States and prepared to invoke Article 5.

Use this as late corroboration, not as the sole source.

## Narrative argument

This chapter is about a diplomat discovering that institutional authority is not the same as institutional control.

Burns represented the United States at NATO, but for part of September 11 he could not reliably communicate with the U.S. government. He still had to listen, report, reassure, and judge what allied offers meant.

The deeper reversal is geopolitical:

NATO's Cold War architecture had been designed around an American commitment to cross the Atlantic if Europe were attacked.

On September 11, the United States was the attacked ally.

Burns had arrived expecting to represent American leadership of NATO. Within twelve days, he was receiving offers of defense from the alliance the United States had spent half a century promising to defend.

## Guardrails

Do not:

- invent Burns watching television images unless a source explicitly places him in front of a television at a given moment;
- invent his emotional state beyond his own stated recollections;
- place Condoleezza Rice's `It's good to have friends in the world` line on September 11; Burns consistently places that call on the morning of September 12 and it belongs primarily in Chapter 12;
- say Article 5 was formally invoked on September 11;
- imply all nineteen allies had final instructions that night;
- use baseball merely because the book is titled *Red Sox Diplomacy*;
- turn alliance solidarity into a claim that every later NATO policy choice was unified or uncontested.
