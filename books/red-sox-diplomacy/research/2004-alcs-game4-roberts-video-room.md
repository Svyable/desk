# October 17, 2004 — Dave Roberts prepares for one job

## Finding

The famous ninth-inning steal in Game 4 of the 2004 ALCS was not merely a burst of improvisation. Dave Roberts later gave MLB a specific account of the preparation behind it.

In a March 31, 2020 MLB interview, Roberts said he had not played in ten days and knew that if Boston needed a late run he was likely to be used as a pinch-runner. During Game 4, he went from the dugout back to the Red Sox clubhouse between roughly the fifth and seventh innings and studied video of Yankees relievers who might enter the game.

Primary/authoritative first-person source:
- MLB, “Roberts on The Steal, and how Mo helped it happen,” March 31, 2020: https://www.mlb.com/news/dave-roberts-2004-alcs-steal-baserunning

SABR’s Game 4 reconstruction adds the staff handoff. Video/advance scouting coordinator Billy Broadbent recalled that Roberts specifically asked to see Mariano Rivera’s throws to first base. Broadbent called up Rivera’s clip and Roberts watched it. SABR attributes the recollection to Allan Wood and Bill Nowlin’s oral history of the 2004 Red Sox.

Authoritative historical synthesis with named oral-history provenance:
- SABR Games Project, “October 17, 2004: ‘Don’t Let Us Win Tonight!’ Red Sox begin ALCS comeback in Game 4”: https://sabr.org/gamesproj/game/october-17-2004-dont-let-us-win-tonight-red-sox-begin-alcs-comeback-in-game-4/

The National Baseball Hall of Fame preserves the physical endpoint: the spikes Roberts wore when he stole second are in the museum collection. Its account records that Kevin Millar opened the ninth with a five-pitch walk from Rivera; Roberts entered for his first appearance of the ALCS; Rivera made three pickoff throws; Roberts stole on Rivera’s first pitch to Bill Mueller; and Mueller’s single brought him home with the tying run.

Authoritative museum source:
- National Baseball Hall of Fame, “Starting Nine: Run to the Title”: https://baseballhall.org/discover/starting-nine-boston-red-sox

MLB’s game archive independently preserves the steal and tying run:
- https://www.mlb.com/video/roberts-steals-game-4-c33289221
- https://www.mlb.com/video/mueller-ties-game-4-c30566311

## Why this is a high-value Red Sox detail

The manuscript does not need another summary saying that Roberts stole second and Boston came back. Knowledgeable readers know that sequence.

The better detail is the machinery before the famous moment: a reserve player who had not appeared in the series left the dugout during an elimination game to study the opposing closer’s pickoff move because his likely assignment, if a baserunner appeared late, was extremely narrow. The video coordinator then retrieved Rivera’s throws to first on request. When Millar walked, Roberts entered. Rivera threw over three times. Roberts still went on the first pitch to Mueller.

That is dugout/clubhouse history with an institutional handoff: manager/bench role -> player anticipation -> video staff retrieval -> opponent-specific study -> pinch-running assignment -> execution.

It also offers a useful structural rhyme for *Red Sox Diplomacy* without requiring a baseball-as-leadership sermon. Elsewhere the book is strongest when diplomacy is shown through preparation, staff work, handoffs, and decisions rather than retrospective abstractions. Here baseball itself supplies exactly that kind of scene.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- Roberts later said he had not played in ten days and expected a late pinch-running role if Boston needed a run;
- he said he spent part of Game 4 in the clubhouse studying video of Yankees relievers;
- SABR, citing the 2004 Red Sox oral history, records Billy Broadbent saying Roberts requested video of Rivera’s throws to first and Broadbent retrieved it;
- the Hall of Fame records Millar’s five-pitch walk, Roberts entering, three Rivera pickoff throws, the steal, and Mueller’s tying single;
- MLB preserves video of the steal and tying hit.

Not safe:

- claiming Roberts knew Millar would walk;
- reconstructing the video room’s appearance, sound, dialogue, or atmosphere beyond the sourced recollections;
- claiming Broadbent or Francona told Roberts exactly when to run unless a source supplies the instruction;
- assigning Roberts fear, certainty, calm, or other interior state beyond his own attributed retrospective statements;
- saying the video study caused the steal to succeed;
- turning the sequence into a generic lesson about preparation, intelligence work, diplomacy, or leadership.

## Manuscript recommendation

This is strong enough for a bounded Red Sox-side narrative insertion wherever the manuscript treats 2004, The Steal, or the difference between the visible play and the work behind it.

The scene should begin before Roberts reaches first base. Keep the institutional mechanics visible: he is on the roster for a specialized late-game use; he anticipates the likely closer; he uses the club’s video operation to study Rivera’s throws; then the actual ninth inning arrives.

The payoff is not “preparation matters.” The payoff is that one of the most replayed seconds in Red Sox history had a less replayed prehistory inside the clubhouse.
