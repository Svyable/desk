# Baseball Diplomacy Comparative Context — Burns was distinctive, not unique

A trade book built around Nicholas Burns and baseball can easily imply that Burns invented the idea of using baseball as diplomacy. The record does not support that. This note places his Greece work inside a wider Foreign Service and American cultural-diplomacy tradition so the manuscript can make the stronger, more defensible claim: **Burns made baseball unusually personal, sustained, and visible across a long diplomatic career.**

## 1. Baseball already had a Foreign Service life before Burns's Greece project

A March 1988 *State Magazine* article by Foreign Service officer **Thomas Krajeski** describes embassy personnel in Warsaw helping develop baseball in Poland. Krajeski, himself a dedicated Red Sox fan, recounts Polish baseball organizers approaching the U.S. Embassy, embassy officers assembling teams, and American baseball expertise and equipment entering the relationship.

The article's own headline treats the activity as an American export — “baseballski.” It mentions American baseball figures and institutional help, including Stan Musial, and presents baseball as part of ordinary Foreign Service improvisation rather than a formal grand strategy.

Source:
- Thomas Krajeski, “A leading American export: ‘baseballski’ — In Poland, U.S. diplomats take 'em out to the ball game,” *State Magazine*, March 1988.
  https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/ce/State_1988-03-_Iss_309_%28IA_sim_state-magazine_1988-03_309%29.pdf

### Why this matters for Burns

Krajeski later remembered Nicholas Burns as a longtime friend and joked in his ADST oral history that Burns remained his friend primarily because both men were Red Sox fans.

Source:
https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Krajeski-Thomas.pdf

That creates a useful institutional continuity: a Red Sox-fan diplomat who had personally participated in baseball outreach in Poland later belonged to Burns's professional circle. It does **not** show that Krajeski taught Burns baseball diplomacy or caused the Greek initiative.

## 2. Burns's Greece project belongs to cultural diplomacy, not just sports promotion

Cynthia P. Schneider's 2003 study *Diplomacy That Works: “Best Practices” in Cultural Diplomacy* uses Burns's Greek baseball effort as a case study.

Schneider writes that Burns used baseball diplomacy in the hostile atmosphere surrounding the Kosovo war, secured help from Greek-American political and sports figures, and helped form the nonprofit **Friends of Greek Baseball**. She describes a network of twenty baseball teams, ten softball teams, and Little Leagues.

Most importantly, Schneider attributes to Burns the conclusion that cultural diplomacy could make headway in a hostile climate.

Source:
- Cynthia P. Schneider, *Diplomacy That Works: “Best Practices” in Cultural Diplomacy*, Center for Arts and Culture / Georgetown University, 2003.
  https://www.interarts.net/descargas/interarts645.pdf

### Evidentiary caution

Schneider is a near-contemporary practitioner-scholar and former U.S. ambassador, but this is still a secondary account. Her wording that baseball diplomacy helped “temper” hostility should not be inflated into a claim that the program measurably shifted Greek national opinion.

The strongest use is narrower:

- contemporaries understood the project as cultural / people-to-people diplomacy;
- the program was situated explicitly against a difficult Kosovo-war political climate;
- Burns's baseball effort was being cited as a public-diplomacy “best practice” before the 2004 Olympics had even occurred.

## 3. The Greek project survived Burns

Burns's successor in Athens, Ambassador Thomas J. Miller, later told the Association for Diplomatic Studies and Training that the baseball effort had started under Burns, whom he called a “baseball nut,” and that Miller continued it.

Miller describes helping engineer an arrangement with Major League Baseball for training and for identifying U.S.-based players with Greek ancestry. His testimony is important because it shows the initiative becoming institutional rather than remaining an ambassador's personal hobby.

Source:
https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Miller%2C%20Thomas%20J.toc.pdf

## 4. The Red Sox club itself later appears in a clean diplomatic-post case

An April 2008 *State Magazine* article documents a case in which the **Boston Red Sox organization itself** participated visibly in U.S. consular public outreach in Halifax.

A local Red Sox fan club approached the U.S. Consulate General for help persuading the team to bring the **2007 World Series trophy and mascot Wally** to Nova Scotia. Consul General Harold Foster and Consul Elizabeth Schwefler wrote the Red Sox in support of the proposal, and the team accepted.

The resulting visit included:

- a welcome reception at the Nova Scotia Legislature;
- a consul-general's residence event with fans, politicians, and community leaders;
- visits to a children's hospital and elementary school;
- a public fan event at a shopping mall.

The article explicitly situates the visit inside the long New England–Atlantic Canada relationship, including Massachusetts aid after the 1917 Halifax explosion and Halifax's reception of diverted U.S.-bound aircraft after September 11, 2001.

Source:
- Marcia Seitz-Ehler, “Red Sox Pitch In — Consulate General in Halifax Celebrates 175 Years,” *State Magazine*, April 2008.
  https://2001-2009.state.gov/documents/organization/102927.pdf

### Why Halifax matters to the Greece question

Halifax provides a useful **control case**. Here, the documentary record clearly tells us what the Red Sox club did: it accepted a diplomatic post's invitation, sent the championship trophy and mascot, and participated in a sequence of official/community events.

The Greece record is different. Contemporary reports say the Red Sox were helping and had pledged support, but the public sources located so far do not describe the club's concrete contribution.

That difference should make the manuscript more disciplined, not less:

- **Halifax:** club-level contribution is explicit and observable.
- **Greece:** club-level participation is reported, mechanics unresolved.

Do not use the Halifax case to infer what the Red Sox did in Greece. Use it to show what a well-documented club/post collaboration looks like and therefore why the missing Greece details matter.

## 5. The distinctive Burns contribution

The comparative evidence argues against easy uniqueness claims. Burns was not the first U.S. diplomat to discover that baseball could create contact abroad, and the Greek project depended on many actors.

What is distinctive in the Burns record is the combination:

1. **Personal depth** — he played organized youth baseball in Wellesley, followed the Red Sox in detail, and retained the identity across postings.
2. **Public visibility** — reporters, colleagues, students, and foreign audiences already knew the Red Sox persona before and after Greece.
3. **Hands-on behavior** — Burns says he demonstrated hitting on Greek television, played catch with children, and lobbied for a baseball budget.
4. **Network brokerage** — the project connected Greek organizers to Greek-American politics, Peter Angelos, MLB, and reportedly the Red Sox.
5. **Institutional persistence** — successor testimony shows the project continuing into training and roster development after Burns left Athens.
6. **Career continuity** — baseball remained visible in Brussels, Washington, Harvard, and Beijing rather than being a one-post gimmick.

That is a better thesis than “Burns invented baseball diplomacy.”

## 6. What the book should resist

Avoid these formulations:

- “Burns pioneered baseball diplomacy.”
- “Burns brought baseball to Greece” without acknowledging Greek organizers and the broader network.
- “Baseball won Greeks over to America.”
- “The Red Sox taught Burns diplomacy.”
- “Sports diplomacy was Burns's strategy everywhere he served.”

Prefer formulations such as:

- Burns made baseball one of several human registers through which he represented the United States.
- In Greece, a lifelong personal interest became useful public-diplomacy infrastructure when local organizers had a concrete Olympic problem.
- The project worked because it connected a host-country need with American expertise, diaspora networks, and a diplomat willing to participate personally.

## 7. Broader historical frame for later research

A full comparative chapter is probably unnecessary, but a few pages of context may prevent the book from becoming self-mythologizing. Future research can test Burns against:

- Cold War sports and cultural diplomacy;
- State Department baseball outreach in Latin America and Asia;
- Foreign Service officers who used local or American sports as relationship-building tools;
- MLB International's expansion efforts in the 1990s and early 2000s;
- direct club/post collaborations such as the Red Sox–Halifax case;
- the place of sport within post-USIA public diplomacy after the 1999 reorganization.

The aim is not to dilute Burns. It is to define what, precisely, was his.
