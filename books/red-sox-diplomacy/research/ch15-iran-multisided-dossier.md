# Chapter 15 Dossier — Iran, 2005–08

## Purpose

Build a multi-sided documentary spine for Chapter 15. The chapter is not a verdict on Iran policy and not a Burns-centered account of a multinational negotiation. It asks what diplomatic work means when coalition-building, incentives, pressure and repeated offers do not produce the desired bargain during the diplomat's tenure.

## Burns's institutional role

As Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs from 2005 to 2008, Nicholas Burns was a senior U.S. official working the Iran nuclear portfolio. His role included diplomacy with the European states already engaged with Iran, Russia and China, Security Council coalition-building, public explanation of the U.S. position, and work on the incentives-and-sanctions strategy.

Do not convert senior responsibility into sole authorship. President George W. Bush, Secretary Condoleezza Rice, the National Security Council, Treasury, other U.S. officials, European governments, the EU foreign-policy machinery, Russia, China, the IAEA and Iran all held authority the Under Secretary did not control.

## The inherited negotiation

The United States did not invent the European diplomatic track. Britain, France and Germany—the EU3—had been negotiating directly with Iran before the United States altered its own posture. Their efforts produced temporary suspension arrangements but did not settle the underlying dispute over enrichment and guarantees about the peaceful character of Iran's program.

This matters narratively. Burns entered an existing diplomatic structure. One of his tasks was to make American policy compatible enough with allied policy to sustain a common front without pretending the allies had identical preferences.

## 2006 policy shift

In May 2006, the United States publicly offered to join European negotiations with Iran if Iran verifiably suspended uranium enrichment and reprocessing activities. This was a meaningful shift because Washington had not been participating directly in the EU3 nuclear talks.

The offer was conditional, not an offer of unconditional bilateral talks. The suspension requirement became one of the central points of dispute.

The P5+1/EU3+3 package combined possible benefits with the prospect of escalating costs. The logic was to make negotiation more attractive while preserving pressure if Iran continued activities the coalition demanded it suspend.

## Iranian position

Iran repeatedly framed enrichment as a right associated with peaceful nuclear technology under the Non-Proliferation Treaty and rejected demands that it permanently surrender that capability. Iranian officials also argued that suspension as a precondition required Iran to concede the central disputed issue before substantive negotiations began.

Those claims should be represented as Iran's stated legal and political position, not adopted automatically as the narrator's conclusion. The IAEA record also contained unresolved safeguards questions and findings that drove international concern.

Iranian politics were not monolithic. Public statements by President Mahmoud Ahmadinejad, nuclear negotiators including Ali Larijani, Foreign Minister Manouchehr Mottaki and other officials reflected different institutional roles and sometimes different tactical registers. Do not invent a single Iranian mind.

## IAEA and Security Council

The IAEA is the factual anchor for safeguards findings. The Security Council became the arena for binding international demands and sanctions after the dispute was referred from Vienna.

The sequence matters more than a sanctions scorecard. Resolution 1696 in 2006 demanded suspension of enrichment-related and reprocessing activities. Resolution 1737 imposed sanctions later that year after Iran did not comply with the suspension demand. Resolution 1747 expanded measures in 2007, and Resolution 1803 added further measures in March 2008.

These were negotiated coalition outcomes. Burns was a prominent U.S. negotiator in that coalition; he did not personally impose UN sanctions.

## Europe, Russia and China

The coalition was never a unitary bloc. European governments generally favored sustained negotiation while also supporting increasing pressure after the suspension demand was not met. Russia and China had economic, strategic and nonproliferation interests that did not map neatly onto Washington's preferences and repeatedly affected the pace and scope of Security Council action.

That friction is part of the chapter's subject. Coalition diplomacy is not the accumulation of countries that agree with the United States. It is the work of producing a common position among governments that would otherwise choose different policies.

## What the strategy did and did not accomplish by Burns's departure

By early 2008 there was no comprehensive nuclear agreement. Iran had not accepted the coalition's suspension condition, and enrichment continued. The United States and Iran had not entered the full nuclear negotiation envisioned by the 2006 conditional offer.

At the same time, the diplomatic landscape had changed. The United States had publicly accepted the principle that it could join a negotiated nuclear settlement under specified conditions. A P5+1 negotiating structure existed. Multiple Security Council resolutions had established an international pressure architecture. The dispute had become simultaneously a negotiation, verification problem and sanctions regime.

None of that proves the strategy would eventually succeed. It is institutional inheritance, not victory.

## Relationship to the later JCPOA

The 2015 agreement should appear only as later context. It involved different governments, different negotiating choices and direct U.S.-Iranian diplomacy that went substantially beyond the Burns-era posture. It is fair to note institutional continuities in the P5+1 format and the long history of sanctions and nuclear bargaining. It is not fair to call the JCPOA the inevitable completion of Burns's work.

## Documentary tensions worth preserving

- Negotiation versus precondition: Washington called suspension the entry condition for full talks; Tehran argued that this required concession before talks.
- Rights versus verification: Iran emphasized peaceful nuclear rights; international concern focused on safeguards history, enrichment capability and confidence in peaceful intent.
- Unity versus compromise: every Security Council resolution required negotiation not only with Iran in mind but among the powers imposing pressure.
- Pressure versus outcome: sanctions can demonstrate coalition agreement without proving that pressure will produce the desired political choice.
- Persistence versus control: a diplomat can keep a negotiating architecture alive without controlling whether the other government finds the bargain acceptable.

## Chapter thesis candidate

India and Iran expose the same limitation from opposite directions. Diplomatic persistence matters, but persistence is not leverage by itself. A negotiator can clarify choices, assemble coalitions, alter incentives and preserve a channel. He cannot manufacture the other side's political decision.

The useful measure of diplomacy is therefore not simply whether the agreement arrives before the diplomat leaves office. It is whether the choices become clearer, the coalition remains usable, the record remains credible, and a future bargain—if political conditions change—has somewhere institutional to begin.
