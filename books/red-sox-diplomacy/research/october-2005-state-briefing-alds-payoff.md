# October 7, 2005 — the briefing-room prediction meets the box score

## Finding

A previous *Red Sox Diplomacy* research pass recovered Nicholas Burns's October 7, 2005 State Department briefing before travel to Brussels and the Balkans. Burns opened by joking that the Department would shut down at 4 p.m. so employees could watch Boston beat the Chicago White Sox. A reporter extended the joke by asking about B-Net; Burns answered in kind.

Primary diplomatic source:
https://2001-2009.state.gov/p/us/rm/2005/54610.htm

That pass correctly held the baseball result for authoritative verification. MLB now closes the hold cleanly.

MLB's official 2005 ALDS recap records that the White Sox defeated the defending champion Red Sox three games to none. Game 3 was played at Fenway Park on October 7, the same day as Burns's briefing. Chicago won the game and completed the sweep. MLB's preserved Game 3 video record identifies the final out as Bobby Jenks getting Edgar Renteria to ground out; another MLB record preserves the sixth-inning hinge, when Orlando Hernández entered with the bases loaded and nobody out and escaped without allowing a run.

## Why the correction is narratively useful

The point is not merely that Burns made a bad prediction. The timing is better than that.

He was the Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs, opening a briefing about imminent travel to Brussels and the Balkans, and publicly behaving like a fan of the defending champions: confident enough to announce Boston's victory before the game existed. Hours later, at Fenway, the season ended.

For knowledgeable Red Sox readers, the texture matters. This was not a random regular-season loss. Boston entered October as the reigning World Series champion. Chicago had already beaten the Red Sox twice. In Game 3 Boston tied the game on back-to-back David Ortiz and Manny Ramirez home runs, then loaded the bases with nobody out in the sixth. Orlando Hernández escaped the inning. Chicago held on and swept the series.

The manuscript does not need to turn that sequence into symbolism. Its value is anti-mythology. A book about a devoted fan should allow him the ordinary fan's privilege of being loudly, publicly wrong.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- Burns joked on October 7, 2005 that State would shut down at 4 p.m. so employees could watch Boston beat Chicago;
- a reporter asked about B-Net and Burns continued the exchange;
- the official transcript concerns Burns's upcoming Brussels/Balkans travel;
- MLB records that Chicago beat Boston that day at Fenway and completed a three-game ALDS sweep;
- MLB preserves the Game 3 final out and Hernández's bases-loaded escape.

Not safe:

- saying the State Department actually closed early;
- saying B-Net actually carried the game;
- saying Burns watched Game 3, where he watched it, or whom he watched with;
- inventing Burns's reaction to the loss or sweep;
- claiming the baseball result affected his Balkan travel or diplomatic work;
- turning the defeat into a lesson about diplomacy, prediction, humility, or leadership that Burns himself did not draw.

## Manuscript recommendation

If the Under Secretary years need a compact baseball beat, use the contradiction rather than another generic proof of fandom. Burns can be shown making the prediction in the official briefing; MLB can supply the correction. Stop there.

A defensible narrative shape is:

> On October 7, 2005, before briefing reporters on a trip to Brussels and the Balkans, Burns joked that State would shut down at four so everyone could watch Boston beat Chicago. The White Sox had other plans. That afternoon at Fenway, Orlando Hernández inherited a bases-loaded, nobody-out jam and escaped it. Bobby Jenks eventually got Edgar Renteria for the last out. Chicago swept the defending champions in three games.

Do not append an invented Burns reaction. The missing reaction is part of the discipline.

## Evidentiary level

**Primary official State Department transcript + authoritative MLB postseason history and game archive.** Strong for Burns's public prediction and the baseball correction; no evidence for actual Department closure, broadcast, Burns viewing behavior, private reaction, or diplomatic effect.
