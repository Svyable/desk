# Deep Baseball Primary-Source Pass 09 — the players left behind and the diaspora tradeoff

## Purpose

The Greece chapter has increasingly become a network story rather than an ambassador-as-founder story. One evidentiary problem remains: the Olympic-team construction can still read too smoothly if the only surviving voices belong to Burns, federation leaders, Greek-American organizers, Major League Baseball, and the professional players who made the team.

A contemporaneous August 2004 *Kathimerini* article and a 2024 retrospective from the Greek national baseball organization make the internal cost of the diaspora strategy much clearer.

The important conclusion is not that the diaspora strategy was wrong. The sources show why it was competitively necessary. The conclusion is that **Olympic competitiveness and domestic baseball development were different goals, and the project sometimes treated them as though they were the same.**

## 1. August 17, 2004 — a contemporaneous account of the local-player conflict

Alexandra Koroxenidis's *Kathimerini English Edition* article on Valerie Kontakos's then-unfinished documentary reports from Athens during the Olympic Games themselves.

Source:
https://www.ekathimerini.com/culture/25025/the-game-s-the-thing-or-is-it/

The article says Kontakos had begun filming roughly two years earlier after encountering committed Greek baseball players practicing in Athens. The federation initially selected local amateur players for Olympic training, while Greek-American players were later brought into the process.

By the final run-up to the Games, the federation had changed direction toward a roster dominated by experienced players from the diaspora.

The article reports that of **thirteen locally trained Greek players, only two were selected** for the Olympic team. It also says Greek coach **Dimitris Gousios resigned** after the shift.

### Why this matters

This is not a later distributor blurb or retrospective complaint. It is a contemporaneous Greek newspaper account published while the Olympics were underway.

The conflict therefore belongs inside the historical event, not merely inside a later documentary interpretation of it.

## 2. The grievance was not simply “we deserved roster spots”

Kontakos's reporting in the article is more nuanced than a simple local-versus-diaspora grievance.

She says the local players recognized that the experienced Greek-American players were better. The complaint, as she describes it, was about expectations and process: local players believed they were training for a genuine chance to make the team and later felt abandoned when the roster strategy changed.

The article includes one local player saying, in substance, that if the players had been told clearly from the beginning that they would not make the Olympic roster, they could still have used the program as a chance to train and learn.

### Editorial use

Paraphrase rather than overquote.

The manuscript can fairly state:

- local players did not need the book to pretend they were Olympic-caliber when they were not;
- their complaint concerned being encouraged to believe the roster path remained open;
- the competitive solution had a human cost even if the performance logic was real.

## 3. The federation's competitive logic was also real

The current Greek baseball history gives the other side unusually bluntly.

It says that when international officials evaluated the local player pool, they concluded Greece could not field a credible Olympic-level team from locally developed players alone. The federation therefore turned heavily to players of Greek descent trained in North America.

Source:
https://greekbaseball.com/history

The same historical account says the qualifying team included **22 North American players and two players from the local Greek league** and credits that roster with winning the necessary European qualifier.

For the final 2004 Olympics, it describes the team as 19 Greek Americans, two Greek Canadians, and two players from the local Greek league.

### Guardrail

This is the federation / national-program history. It has an institutional interest in defending the competitive logic of the roster and uses celebratory language elsewhere. Treat it as the federation's account, not neutral adjudication.

Placed beside *Kathimerini*, however, it explains why the roster decision was made.

## 4. Rob Derksen complicates the conflict rather than fitting neatly on one side

A July 2004 *Washington Post* report after Rob Derksen's death says native Greek players competing for team spots **adored Derksen because he taught them the game**.

Source:
https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/sports/2004/07/04/greece-to-pay-tribute-to-its-beloved-baseball-coach/8a77e2e2-581b-41e4-8b4f-056e4f2dbac4/

This matters because Derksen was simultaneously:

- the professional scout/manager charged with finding stronger diaspora players;
- a teacher valued by local Greek players;
- the operational figure whose roster-building work ultimately made many of those local players unlikely to make the Olympic team.

The human reality is therefore less clean than “professionals displaced locals.”

The same person could expand local baseball knowledge and participate in a competitive structure that narrowed local players' roster opportunities.

## 5. 2024 — the national program itself calls the development model a missed opportunity

A 2024 article published by the Greek national baseball organization interviews general manager Tom Mazarakis and current federation leadership twenty years after Athens.

Source:
https://greekbaseball.com/news/are-there-baseball-teams-in-greece

Mazarakis describes the Olympic era with both admiration and a sense of missed opportunity. He says the federation had effectively been built around creating an Olympic team and calls that development model a **mistake**, arguing that youth recruitment should have begun from day one rather than relying primarily on athletes brought from the United States and given Greek eligibility.

The same article makes clear that the diaspora strategy remains essential to competitive Greek baseball: the modern national team is still made up overwhelmingly of players based abroad.

### Why this is important

This is not a clean reversal of the 2004 strategy.

The article acknowledges both truths:

- without diaspora players, Greece would struggle to compete internationally;
- without domestic youth development and facilities, Greece cannot build a self-sustaining baseball culture.

Those goals can coexist, but they are not the same project.

## 6. The “one resident” detail is a useful afterlife marker

The 2024 article says the **2004 Olympic roster had only one player resident in Greece** and describes the current team as similarly dependent on Greek-American players, with only one current player living locally.

This should be used cautiously because the federation's separate historical page describes two players from the local Greek league on the 2004 roster. “Local league player” and “resident of Greece” are not necessarily identical categories.

Do not harmonize the two statements by assumption.

The safe conclusion is narrower: **the Olympic roster was overwhelmingly diaspora-based**, and the modern national team remains heavily dependent on players raised or based outside Greece.

## 7. A twenty-year consequence of the original tradeoff

The same 2024 retrospective describes a chronic domestic-development problem:

- Olympic-era infrastructure did not produce a broad baseball culture;
- baseball fields later disappeared, including the remaining Elliniko stadiums in 2018;
- youth recruitment and local facilities remain weak;
- current federation leaders are trying to rebuild youth participation and fields.

This gives Chapter 10 a more rigorous afterlife than a generic “the program survived” sentence.

The Olympic team survived as a national-team institution.

The domestic sport did not become self-sustaining at the same scale.

Both facts can be true.

## 8. The chapter's central distinction should now be explicit

The evidence supports separating **four different goals** that were often grouped under “building baseball in Greece”:

1. **Teach the sport locally** — children, adult amateurs, coaches, fields, leagues.
2. **Create a credible national federation** — governance, funding, eligibility, international membership.
3. **Qualify and field an Olympic team** — high-level roster, passports, scouting, European competition.
4. **Build a durable domestic baseball culture** — youth pipeline, facilities, participation after the Olympics.

Success in one category did not guarantee success in another.

Burns's brokerage can be credited where the evidence supports it without calling the entire enterprise a singular success or failure.

## 9. Manuscript consequence for Chapter 10

The current paragraph about the diaspora roster tradeoff should become more concrete.

A defensible sequence is:

- explain why locally trained players were not yet competitive enough for Olympic play;
- state that contemporaneous *Kathimerini* reporting says only two of thirteen locally trained players made the roster and coach Dimitris Gousios resigned after the shift;
- note that the local players, as Kontakos described them, understood the skill gap but objected to how expectations had been managed;
- then state that the federation's later history defends the diaspora-heavy strategy as necessary to qualify;
- use Derksen as the complication: the recruiter of diaspora talent was also remembered by local players as a valued teacher;
- end the tension rather than resolving it artificially.

The book does not need to choose which group was “right.”

It needs to show that Olympic competitiveness and domestic development produced conflicting incentives.

## 10. Interview / archive targets

### Valerie Kontakos

Ask for:

- access to *Who's on First?*;
- transcript, participant list, production notes and unused interviews;
- names of the locally trained players interviewed;
- details of Dimitris Gousios's resignation;
- whether federation officials were interviewed on the roster decision;
- what changed in her interpretation between beginning filming and finishing the documentary.

### Dimitris Gousios

If available, seek direct testimony on:

- the initial training plan for local players;
- when he learned the final roster would be diaspora-dominated;
- why he resigned;
- his view of Derksen, federation leadership and the local players' development.

### Local players from the original thirteen

The highest-value missing perspective is their own, preferably through contemporaneous interviews or the documentary rushes.

### Federation records

Look for:

- 2002–2004 training rosters;
- selection criteria;
- minutes around roster strategy;
- Gousios correspondence/resignation;
- communications explaining expectations to local athletes.

## Documentary rule

Do not turn a real roster conflict into a morality play.

The local players' disappointment is documented. The competitive inferiority of the local player pool is also documented. The diaspora roster successfully qualified Greece for the Olympics. The domestic sport then struggled to become self-sustaining.

The tension is the history.
