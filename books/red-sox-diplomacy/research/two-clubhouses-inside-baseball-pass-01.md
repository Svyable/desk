# Two clubhouses: inside-baseball research pass 01

## Purpose

This pass answers a specific editorial request: give *Red Sox Diplomacy* more of the feeling of being inside a baseball team **and** inside the State Department.

The book already has strong public surfaces: the State Department podium, speeches, formal negotiations, alliance votes, embassy representation, Red Sox fandom, the 2003 loss and the 2004 championship. The missing texture is often the machinery immediately behind those surfaces: the watch floor before a senior official sees a cable, the paper route before a Secretary acts, the morning press-guidance process before a spokesman walks to the podium, the political-military team behind an ambassador, the people in a dugout waiting for one specific late-inning job.

The aim is not to create a baseball/diplomacy leadership framework. It is to let readers spend time inside two institutions whose public actions are produced by groups of people working under pressure.

No manuscript prose is changed in this pass. The document identifies scenes, evidence quality, limits, and an integration order.

## Evidentiary rule for this lane

Use three source classes and keep them visibly distinct.

**A — Burns-specific documentary evidence.** A contemporary record involving Burns, Burns's own attributable retrospective account, or a firsthand colleague describing work with him. This can support Burns-specific narrative if chronology and retrospective status are stated honestly.

**B — period institutional witness.** A firsthand account of the same office or process in the same period, but not a record of Burns performing the particular act. This can establish how the machinery worked. It cannot silently become a scene starring Burns.

**C — institutional or baseball context.** Official histories, later organizational descriptions, SABR research, MLB retrospectives, and reported recollections. These can establish procedure, chronology, or another participant's scene. They do not establish Burns's private reaction or causal lessons he drew from them.

The manuscript's existing prohibition remains controlling: no invented dialogue, composite characters, reconstructed sensory detail, or unobserved interior thought.

## Scene card 1 — The watch floor, 1987–88

### What is Burns-specific

The State Department's 1997 biography says Burns served as a staff officer in the Department's Operations Center and Secretariat in 1987–88.

Primary biography:
https://1997-2001.state.gov/about_state/biography/burns.html

John Koenig's ADST oral history independently places himself in the Operations Center in 1987–88 and says it was the first place he worked with Nick Burns. Koenig remembers rotating shifts, pressure during a busy watch, a need-to-know culture, and the network of officers who passed through the Center.

Firsthand retrospective:
https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Koenig.John.pdf

Relevant PDF areas: pp. 17–18 in the PDF's printed pagination / web extraction around the Operations Center discussion. The useful Burns-specific point is narrow: Koenig says he first worked with Burns there. His description of his own shift belongs to Koenig unless independently attached to Burns.

### Period mechanics

Mark Tauber entered the Operations Center in 1988, immediately after Burns's listed 1987–88 assignment. Tauber describes watch officers distributing large volumes of cables to the appropriate bureaus, translating foreign media, producing situation reports on unfolding crises for the Secretary and senior officials, working a rotating 24-hour schedule, and briefing the incoming watch at shift change.

Firsthand period witness:
https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Tauber.Mark.pdf

Relevant discussion: pp. 57–58 in the PDF's printed pagination / extraction beginning `Watch Officer, Operations Center`.

Janet Bogue describes the Operations Center a few years earlier under George Shultz. Junior watch officers/editors handled incoming issues, alerted people, and compiled morning briefings from overnight cables and phone traffic before 24-hour television news became the default external feed.

Firsthand period witness:
https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Bogue.Janet.pdf

Relevant discussion: printed pp. 46–47.

### Institutional continuity

The Office of the Historian says the Executive Secretariat was established to regulate information flow at the Department's highest levels and had managed the Operations Center since 1962.

https://history.state.gov/departmenthistory/people/principalofficers/executive-secretary

A 1998 State reorganization report, close enough to Burns's service to be useful as continuity evidence but not a substitute for 1987 procedure, describes the Secretariat as managing and tracking information and work flows among the Secretary, principals, bureaus, and interagency community. It describes the Operations Center as the around-the-clock crisis, alerting, sensitive-information and communications component.

https://1997-2001.state.gov/global/general_foreign_policy/rpt_981230_reorg10.html

### Manuscript opportunity

Chapter 3 currently moves from Jerusalem into Washington largely at résumé speed. A bounded expansion can slow down at exactly the right moment: Burns comes home from posts where Washington sent instructions and joins the place where information from posts enters the building, gets noticed, routed, summarized, handed off and escalated.

The reader should understand what a watch floor is before the book asks them to follow Burns into the NSC, the spokesman's office, embassies and the Seventh Floor.

The passage should not say: `Burns distributed hundreds of cables a day` unless a Burns-specific source is found. It can say that Burns served in the operation and then explain, through identified period witnesses, what officers on that floor were doing.

## Scene card 2 — How paper reaches the Secretary, 1989–90

Burns's official biography says that after the Operations Center / Secretariat assignment he became Special Assistant to the Counselor of the Department in 1989–90, responsible for Soviet and Eastern European affairs.

https://1997-2001.state.gov/about_state/biography/burns.html

Mark Tauber's oral history is unusually valuable here because he worked in Secretary James Baker's staff operation at the same time and explicitly identifies Nick Burns as working for Counselor Robert Zoellick.

Tauber then explains the path of a hypothetical action memo: a bureau drafts and clears it; Executive Secretariat assigns a tracking number; inner-office staff follows it; the Counselor's office may revise or add concerns; affected bureaus are alerted; scheduling intersects with substance; the paper reaches the Secretary in a priority order; after a decision, the result travels back outward. Tauber's example is illustrative, not a Burns case.

Firsthand period witness:
https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Tauber.Mark.pdf

Relevant discussion: printed pp. 61–63, `How a Memo Becomes a Secretarial Action`.

### Manuscript opportunity

This can give Chapter 3 a compact progression inside Washington:

- Operations Center: incoming information, alerting, continuity across shifts.
- Executive / Counselor machinery: cleared paper moving upward toward decision.
- Burns then moves into the NSC and later returns to State as the person at the podium.

The point is not that bureaucracy is heroic. The point is that an official American sentence or decision has a physical and institutional prehistory. Somebody drafts it. Other offices object. Somebody tracks the paper. Somebody decides which disagreement must be elevated.

That understanding will pay off later in Chapters 7, 14 and 15 without having to re-explain the entire building.

## Scene card 3 — The briefing before the briefing

This is the cleanest new Burns-specific backstage material.

On April 28, 1997, Burns welcomed Romanian government spokesman Eugan Serbanescu and told the room that Serbanescu had observed the press-guidance process that morning. Burns called the morning process messy and described the task as trying to anticipate reporters' questions.

Primary transcript:
https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9704/970428.html

On May 14, 1997, Burns was handed press guidance during the briefing, called it unhelpful, read its cautious formulation to the room, and then tried to give a more useful answer while preserving the policy boundary.

Primary transcript:
https://1997-2001.state.gov/briefings/9705/970514.html

A State Magazine account from the same year shows the process from the bureau side: a Consular Affairs public-affairs office reviewed the day's consular news and received a call from the Department press office asking for press guidance for the spokesman's briefing.

Contemporary institutional account:
https://1997-2001.state.gov/publications/statemag/statemag_nov-dec97/bom.html

A period State job description for a special assistant to the Deputy Spokesman adds a useful personnel-level view: track foreign-policy media, brief the Deputy Spokesman, prepare him for the daily briefing, and help shape the administration's foreign-policy message.

Contemporary State personnel description:
https://1997-2001.state.gov/careers/rpmirot.html

### Manuscript opportunity

Chapter 7 already explains the grammar of the podium. It can now show the morning before the grammar becomes public.

This should be a short scene, not an organizational tutorial. The revealing details are that guidance was produced before the briefing, that substantive bureaus fed the press operation, that the office tried to predict questions, and that the written answer could still prove inadequate once an actual reporter asked an actual question.

That last point matters. `Cleared` does not mean `useful`. Burns's May 14 exchange lets the reader see the spokesman operating inside institutional language rather than merely reciting it.

## Scene card 4 — The embassy is a team, not an ambassador

Chapter 9 already uses John Koenig, but it can potentially go one step deeper into how the mission assembled work.

Koenig's ADST oral history says he worked closely with Burns in Athens as head of political-military work. He describes major arms-sales work, Greek-Turkish confidence-building efforts, and close cooperation with the embassy's Defense Attaché Office and Office of Defense Cooperation. He calls the group effective and describes repeatedly accompanying Burns to meetings with Greek Defense Minister Akis Tsochatzopoulos.

Firsthand retrospective:
https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Koenig.John.pdf

Relevant discussion: printed pp. 42–43.

### Manuscript opportunity

The best addition is not another paragraph praising Burns's persuasion. The current chapter has that. The new value is division of labor: the ambassador at the meeting is the visible tip of work that includes political-military officers, defense offices, colleagues in Ankara, regional military commands, companies on arms-sale matters, and Washington.

Use only if the passage adds mechanics rather than another testimonial.

## Scene card 5 — What disagreement looks like in the NATO room

Koenig later became Burns's DCM at the U.S. Mission to NATO. In his oral history he remembers the post-Iraq split in physical, almost comic terms. French permanent representative Benoît d'Aboville would sometimes make a visible show of disengagement while Burns spoke, including opening *Le Monde*, swiveling, and talking to his delegation. Koenig presents it as part of months of tense alliance relations.

Firsthand retrospective:
https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Koenig.John.pdf

Relevant discussion: printed p. 65.

### Manuscript opportunity

Chapter 13 already explains French, German, Belgian and American positions carefully. A few sentences of witnessed room behavior could make the institutional dispute physical without changing the substantive balance.

The scene must be attributed to Koenig's later recollection. It is useful precisely because it is small: alliances can have treaty language, formal procedure and sovereign equality while also containing twenty people in a room who know exactly when another ambassador is theatrically not listening.

## Scene card 6 — Grady Little, Pedro, and the game Burns actually watched

The current Chapter 16 correctly preserves Burns's documented 2003 viewing scene in Brussels and refuses to invent the equivalent 2004 room. That gives the chapter permission to make the *baseball* more specific.

SABR's Game 7 account records the decisive eighth inning: Boston led, relievers were warm, Martínez remained in, the Yankees tied the game, and Little later defended the choice by saying Martínez had been Boston's man all year.

SABR game account:
https://sabr.org/gamesproj/game/october-16-2003-aaron-boones-home-run-wins-the-pennant-for-the-yankees/

MLB's retrospective similarly identifies the decision as one of the postseason's most memorable pitching decisions and notes that Martínez believed he was finished after 100 pitches before returning for the eighth.

MLB retrospective:
https://www.mlb.com/news/controversial-postseason-pitching-decisions

Pedro Martínez's SABR biography records the mound conversation as later understood: Little asked if he had anything left; Martínez said he did.

https://sabr.org/bioproj/person/pedro-martinez/

### Manuscript opportunity

Chapter 16 currently moves quickly from Burns watching the game to `another Boston loss`. A compact account of the managerial decision would give the reader the exact agony Burns was watching without claiming to know Burns's words or thoughts.

This is a case where the book can become more cinematic **by documenting the game**, not by manufacturing the diplomat's room.

## Scene card 7 — Roberts waiting for one job, 2004

Dave Roberts told MLB in 2020 that he had not played in ten days before Game 4 of the 2004 ALCS. During the game he moved between dugout and clubhouse, stretched and studied video of Yankees relievers because he knew he might be needed to pinch-run late. When Kevin Millar walked in the ninth, Roberts looked at Terry Francona; Roberts remembered Francona simply winking. Roberts stole second and scored the tying run.

First-person retrospective via MLB:
https://www.mlb.com/news/dave-roberts-2004-alcs-steal-baserunning

MLB's later Francona retrospective confirms the larger sequence and also notes that Francona stuck with struggling second baseman Mark Bellhorn, who homered in Games 6 and 7.

https://www.mlb.com/news/terry-francona-manager-top-moments

### Manuscript opportunity

This belongs directly after Chapter 16's existing instruction: `Watch the baseball instead.`

It is almost the inverse of the missing Burns championship room. The book cannot see Burns on October 27, but it can see Roberts preparing for a job that might last seconds, the manager signaling, and a play whose consequences are fully documented.

Do not append a leadership moral. Let the sequence work.

Goal: add the Operations Center / Secretariat and paper-flow machinery without turning the chapter into an org chart. The movement should be Jerusalem → watch floor → Counselor/inner-office paper flow → NSC. Target: Chapter 16 only.

Goal: retain the missing Burns scene and deepen the documented baseball around it. The 2003 loss gets the managerial decision; the 2004 comeback gets Roberts preparing for the steal and Francona's minimal signal.

This pass should probably add **no more than two baseball scenes**. It should not add Francona/Ramirez in the same chapter.

### Pass D — Working teams abroad

Targets: Chapters 9 and 13, separately or as one coherent diplomatic-teams batch.

Goal: show the embassy and alliance as workplaces. Athens gets division of labor around pol-mil work. NATO gets one room-level witness detail if it materially improves the already careful procedural narrative.

Do not reopen Chapters 17, 18 or 22 while PR #2003 remains open.

## Stop conditions

1. Do not add a baseball anecdote merely because it has a recognizable manager. It must change what the reader understands about the team, the period, or the documented game Burns was following.
2. Do not make baseball stories into management parables. The book is biography and institutional narrative, not a leadership manual.
3. Do not use another officer's Operations Center routine as though it were a diary of Burns's shift.
4. Do not use a later State organizational description to claim an unchanged procedure in 1987. Use it only for continuity where the historical record supports continuity.
5. Do not make every diplomatic chapter prove that institutions are teams. Once Chapter 3 earns the machinery, later chapters should reveal a new mechanism rather than repeating the same idea.
6. Prefer scenes with a visible decision or handoff: a cable routed, a memo elevated, guidance found wanting, a reliever left warming, a pinch-runner sent.
7. Preserve retrospective status. Koenig, Tauber, Roberts, Francona and Martínez recollections are valuable because they are firsthand or near-firsthand; they are still later recollections.
8. Keep the two sides asymmetrical. A State Department is not a baseball club, NATO is not a clubhouse, and a manager's authority over a roster is fundamentally different from an ambassador's authority in an alliance or embassy interagency team.

## Editorial finding

The strongest new angle is not `baseball teaches diplomacy`.

It is access to **production**.

The public sees a Secretary's decision, a spokesman's answer, an ambassador's meeting, a manager's substitution and a stolen base. The interesting material is often one layer back: who was watching, preparing, clearing, objecting, handing off, warming up, waiting to be used, or deciding that the prepared answer was no longer good enough.

That is the version of `inside baseball` this manuscript can own without inventing access it does not have.
