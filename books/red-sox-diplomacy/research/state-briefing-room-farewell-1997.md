# State briefing-room farewell, 1997 — baseball as working-room culture

## Finding

Two contemporary *Washington Post* items from Nicholas Burns's departure as State Department spokesman preserve something the manuscript has not yet captured: not merely Burns making Red Sox jokes from the podium, but the briefing-room community answering him in baseball.

On Burns's final day as spokesman in July 1997, Thomas W. Lippman reported that President Bill Clinton sent Burns an autographed Boston Red Sox cap inscribed, "To Nick, you're still Number One." Veteran Associated Press diplomatic correspondent Barry Schweid gave Burns a Red Sox alarm clock, turning Burns's habit of starting briefings late into a baseball-themed newsroom joke. Greek journalist Lambros Papantoniou, a frequent sparring partner over Greek-Turkish questions, gave him ouzo as Burns prepared for his Athens ambassadorship.

A second *Post* item, published October 1997 while Burns awaited Senate confirmation for Greece, records an even sharper baseball exchange. Earlier, Burns had jokingly called Roger Clemens a "traitor" from the State Department podium after Clemens left Boston for Toronto and then added that this was the Department's official position. At Burns's farewell, CBS State Department producer Charles Wolfson — himself identified as a Red Sox fan — arranged for the Toronto Blue Jays to obtain a signed photograph from Clemens in his new uniform. Clemens wished Burns luck in the new job and thanked him for his "kind words."

## Why this is different from the existing briefing-room evidence

The manuscript and recent research already establish Burns's habit of carrying Red Sox material into official remarks. These contemporary accounts add the other side of the room.

The important fact is not that Burns told another baseball joke. Reporters and colleagues had learned the register well enough to answer in kind. A presidential gift, a correspondent's alarm clock and a producer-engineered Clemens photograph turn fandom into observable social traffic inside the institution surrounding the briefing podium.

That is stronger than describing Burns as "relatable" or claiming baseball built trust. The objects and exchanges are documented. Any psychological or diplomatic effect is not.

## Red Sox value

The Clemens episode lands in a particularly charged Boston baseball moment. Clemens had left the Red Sox after the 1996 season and signed with Toronto. Burns's podium joke therefore belongs to a real fan argument of the period rather than generic team loyalty. The later signed photograph is funny precisely because the press corps found a way to send the joke back through the player Burns had publicly denounced.

Before manuscript publication, any added baseball chronology beyond what the contemporary *Post* items state — contract terms, front-office quotations, season statistics, or later Clemens history — should be checked against MLB, Red Sox, SABR or another authoritative baseball source.

## Documentary boundary

Safe:

- contemporary reporting says President Clinton sent Burns an autographed Red Sox cap on his last day as spokesman;
- Barry Schweid gave Burns a Red Sox alarm clock tied to Burns's briefing start times;
- Lambros Papantoniou gave Burns ouzo as he prepared for Greece;
- Burns had jokingly called Roger Clemens a traitor from the podium after Clemens left Boston for Toronto and called that the State Department's official position;
- CBS producer Charles Wolfson arranged a personalized Clemens photograph for Burns's farewell;
- these objects and exchanges show that Burns's baseball register had become legible and participatory among people around the State briefing room.

Not safe:

- claiming Clinton personally selected or wrote the cap inscription without additional White House evidence;
- claiming the gifts prove Burns was beloved by the entire press corps;
- claiming baseball improved press relations, briefing accuracy or diplomatic outcomes;
- inventing Burns's reaction when he received any gift;
- reconstructing a farewell-room scene, applause, laughter, body language or dialogue beyond the reported words;
- treating Burns's "official position" line as an actual Department policy statement.

## Manuscript recommendation

This material belongs naturally in Chapter 8, **The Briefing Room Bleachers**, if that chapter currently tells the story mainly from Burns's side of the podium.

The best narrative move is a compact reversal: Burns had spent years importing Boston baseball into the official briefing; when he left, the room handed baseball back to him. The Red Sox cap, the alarm clock and the Clemens photograph are physical receipts. They let the reader see a culture forming around repeated public behavior without the narrator having to announce that Burns possessed charm or rapport.

Do not turn the beat into a leadership lesson. Let the gifts do the work.

## Evidentiary level

**Contemporary national reporting by reporters covering the State Department.** Strong for the reported gifts, podium joke and participants; secondary rather than primary for the exact farewell exchanges. Highest-value upgrade targets are State Department briefing transcripts containing the Clemens remark, White House/Clinton gift records, or contemporaneous television footage of the farewell.
