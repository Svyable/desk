# Chapter 19 Dossier — Rivalry, Competition, Adversaries

## Purpose

Anchor Chapter 19 in Burns's own documented vocabulary and make the baseball analogy useful by showing exactly where it fails.

## 1997: rivalry as joke

At the State Department podium, Burns used Red Sox–Yankees rivalry as a recurring social register with reporters and visitors.

On March 7, 1997, Burns jokingly described the local rules as liking the Red Sox, tolerating the Tigers and hating the Yankees. On July 8, when an intern admitted to being a Yankees fan, Burns joked that the intern might have to leave and pointed to the doors before immediately relenting. In May he pressed a visiting Dutch journalist on whether the Red Sox were more famous than the Yankees in Holland.

These episodes matter because everyone in the room understood the frame. The hostility was performative. The game, the rules, the league and the expectation of another season were shared.

Do not carry that emotional vocabulary directly into state conflict.

## 2023–25: rivalry as policy language

As ambassador to China, Burns used `competition` and sometimes `rivalry` in a serious strategic sense. In a 2023 National Committee on U.S.-China Relations town hall, he described the United States and China as being in a historic competition and rivalry. In a 2024 CSIS interview he described structural competition across technology, economics, military affairs and ideas while also insisting that the societies should remain connected.

After leaving Beijing, Burns told Harvard in 2025 that the relationship was difficult and competitive and argued that the United States needed to stabilize it because the objective was coexistence rather than war.

This gives the chapter an evidentiary bridge across nearly three decades. The same word—rivalry—can describe both a joke and a major-power relationship, but the institutional content is radically different.

## What the baseball analogy can illuminate

A sports rival is usually a rival inside a jointly accepted system.

The Red Sox need the Yankees to exist as an opponent. Neither club seeks to destroy Major League Baseball. Both accept schedules, standings, umpires, contracts and the fact that a loss today does not end the relationship.

This shared structure is what makes joking hatred safe.

International competition can contain analogous elements—treaties, institutions, diplomatic protocols, trade rules, military hotlines—but no authority stands above sovereign states in the same way an umpire stands above a game. Enforcement is partial, power matters, and the consequences of misreading intent can include coercion or war.

The analogy therefore becomes most useful at the point where it breaks.

## Analytical distinctions

### Rival
A rival seeks advantage against another actor across repeated interaction. Rivalry can coexist with mutual recognition and stable rules.

### Competitor
A competitor seeks relative advantage in one or more domains. Competition can coexist with commerce, diplomacy and selective cooperation.

### Adversary
An adversary is an actor whose interests, policies or actions are sufficiently opposed that deterrence, coercion and serious security conflict become central.

### Enemy
Use sparingly. In policy rhetoric the term can imply a more totalized relationship than the evidence supports. Burns's China-era language generally emphasized competition and rivalry rather than depicting the Chinese people as enemies.

These are not permanent boxes. The same bilateral relationship can contain different categories across different domains.

## NATO counterexample

Chapter 13 provides the useful reverse case. France, Germany, Belgium and the United States fought bitterly over Iraq-related NATO policy, yet the governments remained allies. Severe disagreement did not automatically create adversaries.

That distinction matters because diplomacy can fail if disagreement is misclassified.

## Iran counterexample

Negotiation does not imply friendship. The Burns-era Iran file shows diplomacy with a government Washington treated as deeply adversarial. Contact can occur because conflict is serious, not because relations are warm.

## China mature case

Burns's China language is particularly useful because he paired hard competition with a warning against demonizing the Chinese people. He argued simultaneously for military, technological, economic and ideological competition and for keeping societies connected.

That is a mature version of the distinction this chapter needs: compete accurately, communicate continuously, and do not convert the existence of rivalry into permission for caricature.

## Thesis candidate

Diplomacy begins with classification.

If a government mistakes a competitor for an enemy, it may manufacture escalation. If it mistakes an adversary for a sports rival, it may conceal danger. If it mistakes an ally's disagreement for betrayal, it may destroy useful institutions.

The representative's job is not to like the other side. It is to understand what relationship actually exists and communicate inside it without making the category worse through carelessness.
