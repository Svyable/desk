# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Ubiquitous* argues that general-purpose AI raises the floor of competent digital work while changing the scarcity value of narrow expertise. People who begin without a marketable specialist skill can reach useful first outputs faster. Workers whose advantage consists mainly of routine digital production, interface navigation, standardized synthesis, or stable symbolic procedures face pressure as those capabilities move into model-and-tool harnesses.

The book's stronger claim is not that "trades are safe" or that "generalists win." The claim worth testing is:

> As general cognitive capability becomes cheap and embedded in software, durable human advantage migrates toward work that combines consequence, verification, difficult observation, system integration, local trust, accountable judgment, and the ability to connect multiple real domains.

The counterclaim is serious. AI and robotics could progress quickly enough that many of these supposed complements become automatable within the same decade. Organizations could also use AI to narrow physical and professional jobs into centrally directed exception handling, causing workers to lose bargaining power even when the occupations remain. Evidence of those outcomes should change the argument rather than be explained away.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Floor raising:** Do weaker, novice, or less-experienced workers receive larger productivity or quality gains from generative AI than stronger workers in controlled or field studies?
2. **Expertise compression:** Which professional tasks are becoming available directly to non-specialists through general models, retrieval, tools, connectors, and agent harnesses?
3. **Jagged capability:** Do users become more accurate across the board, or can AI increase error on tasks outside a model's effective frontier?
4. **Task versus job exposure:** Is near-term generative-AI exposure concentrated in digital tasks while whole jobs remain bundles of exposed and unexposed work?
5. **New-task creation:** Does AI primarily substitute for existing labor tasks, or does it create new valuable tasks and demand that can support worker gains?
6. **Physical durability:** Which physical occupations are projected to grow, and which are already pressured by automation, prefabrication, or improved equipment?
7. **Infrastructure complementarity:** Does the buildout of AI, electrification, automation, and data centers create additional demand for electrical, HVAC, construction, maintenance, and engineering capacity?
8. **Apprenticeship:** If routine junior work is automated, what evidence shows that organizations can or cannot preserve the learning paths that produce future experts?
9. **Verification:** As generation becomes cheaper, do testing, quality, commissioning, audit, security, provenance, and evaluation become more central in deployed systems?
10. **Bargaining power:** Are productivity gains captured by workers, consumers, employers, or owners of models, data, workflows, and customer relationships?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

The manuscript should change if strong evidence supports any of the following:

- Generative AI consistently benefits already-high performers more than novices across a wide range of work, undermining the "rising floor" mechanism.
- Models become reliably capable across open-ended professional tasks without requiring meaningful domain verification, making the jagged-frontier framing obsolete.
- General-purpose robotics becomes economical for variable service and field environments at a pace that materially erodes the physical-work distinction within the book's ten-year horizon.
- Occupational data show broad declines in the electrical, HVAC, industrial-maintenance, construction-management, and civil-engineering roles used as examples, for reasons that cannot be explained by short cyclical effects.
- AI adoption creates enough new entry-level tasks and formal training mechanisms that the junior-rung problem proves temporary or small.
- Organizations systematically share AI productivity gains with workers and expand worker discretion, weakening the concern that AI can narrow jobs while broadening organizational capability.
- New interfaces make model evaluation, permission design, and verification so automatic that these no longer constitute meaningful human work.
- Cross-domain workers do not outperform or create differentiated value once models can integrate specialized domains more reliably than people.

## Evidence discipline

- Prefer peer-reviewed papers, NBER working papers, government labor statistics, standards, official product documentation, and original institutional reports.
- Treat occupational projections as projections, not guarantees. Preserve the projection period and publication vintage.
- Distinguish task exposure from job elimination.
- Distinguish technical feasibility from economic deployment at acceptable cost, reliability, safety, and scale.
- Do not use current product names as permanent architecture claims. Claude Desktop and MCP are examples of a broader harness pattern, not predictions that one vendor or protocol will dominate.
- Separate augmentation from worker capture of gains. Higher productivity does not imply higher wages or bargaining power.
- Do not treat licensing or regulation as permanent technological moats; discuss them as current accountability and institutional structures.
- Do not treat "physical" as synonymous with "safe." Controlled repetitive physical tasks may automate faster than variable service work.
- For every chapter, retain a "What would make this false?" note in the chapter briefs.
