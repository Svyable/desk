# The Floor Rises

There is an old way to think about skill that depends on scarcity.

A person knows how to write the memo, reconcile the account, model the spreadsheet, search the cases, sketch the logo, debug the script, read the contract, prepare the presentation, or explain the regulation. Another person does not. The first person can therefore charge for crossing a gap the second person cannot cross alone.

For most of modern office work, this is so ordinary that we barely notice the economic structure inside it. A large portion of professional life consists of being the person who can turn an ambiguous request into an acceptable artifact. Some of the artifacts are sophisticated. Some are not. The important thing is that the client, manager, colleague, or customer cannot reliably produce them at the same quality, speed, or confidence without help.

Generative artificial intelligence attacks that arrangement from below.

It does not begin by making the best lawyer better than every lawyer, the best programmer better than every programmer, or the best engineer better than every engineer. The first large effect is simpler. It raises the floor. It gives people who could not previously produce a competent first draft, basic analysis, working script, structured plan, or plausible explanation a way to get one.

That is why the most important early evidence about AI and work is not only about average productivity. It is about who improves most.

In a study of 5,179 customer-support agents, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond found that access to a generative AI assistant increased productivity by roughly fourteen percent on average. The interesting number was underneath the average. Novice and lower-skilled workers improved far more, while the most experienced and highest-performing workers saw little benefit. The tool appeared to spread some of the practices of stronger workers to people who had not yet learned them through experience.

A different experiment by Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang gave college-educated professionals writing tasks and access to ChatGPT. The people using the tool finished faster and produced higher-quality work. Again the distribution mattered. The weaker performers benefited more. The technology compressed differences.

This is a strange kind of technological progress if your identity is built around being comfortably above average.

For the person who begins with no marketable expertise in a domain, the development is extraordinary. The gap between wanting to do something and being able to produce a first useful version shrinks. A restaurant manager who has never written software can produce a small scheduling script. A tradesperson who dislikes office work can draft a professional estimate and customer follow-up. A student can ask for an explanation at the exact level where the textbook lost them. A small-business owner can interrogate a lease, compare insurance clauses, clean a spreadsheet, or build a rough website without hiring four different specialists for the first pass.

None of those outputs is guaranteed to be correct. Some are dangerous precisely because they look more competent than they are. But economically, that does not erase the effect. A capability does not have to become perfect to become much less scarce.

The printing press did not make everyone a great author. Spreadsheets did not make everyone a great accountant. Cameras did not make everyone a great photographer. Search engines did not make everyone a scholar. Each technology nevertheless changed what ordinary competence cost and therefore changed the shape of the professions around it.

AI is unusually broad because its interface is language and its output can be language, code, images, structured data, plans, classifications, explanations, and increasingly actions taken through tools. The same general system can move across tasks that used to belong to separate software categories and separate occupational categories. That breadth matters more than any single benchmark result.

The floor rises across rooms at once.

There is a temptation to describe this as democratization, which is partly true and partly too cheerful. A higher floor is good for the person who could not reach the old floor. It is less obviously good for the person whose wage depended on being the floor.

Consider the professional who spent ten years becoming reliably competent at a narrow digital task. Maybe she is a copy editor who can clean business prose, a junior financial analyst who can build a conventional model, a paralegal who can summarize documents, a designer who can produce acceptable campaign variations, a developer who can implement familiar application patterns, or a consultant who can turn interviews into a standard deck. These are real skills. They took work to acquire. They may still matter. But their market value came partly from the fact that many other people could not perform them quickly.

Once a general-purpose model can perform a useful fraction of the task, the scarcity changes.

The specialist is not competing only against the model. She is competing against every adjacent worker who now has the model.

That distinction is important.

A good accountant may not lose work because a business owner trusts an AI to file complicated taxes unattended. She may lose some work because a bookkeeper with an AI can now handle cases that previously escalated to her. A designer may not be replaced by an image model. She may discover that a marketer can now produce the first thirty variations alone and hires the designer only for the one campaign where taste, brand stakes, and final judgment matter. A programmer may remain essential while the number of programmers needed to build a conventional internal tool falls because product managers, analysts, and smaller engineering teams can produce more themselves.

The economic threat often arrives sideways.

This is also why the phrase “AI will replace jobs” is too blunt. Jobs are bundles of tasks, privileges, relationships, accountabilities, habits, and institutional boundaries. Technology can flatten one part of a job while making another part more valuable. The International Labour Organization’s 2025 global index of generative-AI exposure reached a similar conclusion at a much larger scale. Clerical work remains highly exposed, and exposure has expanded into digitized professional and technical occupations, but the report argues that task transformation is a more likely near-term effect for most jobs than complete elimination.

Transformation can still be brutal.

If thirty percent of a job becomes cheap, the remaining seventy percent does not automatically receive the old salary. The organization may redesign the role. It may combine two jobs. It may stop hiring juniors. It may expect one person to supervise several automated workflows. It may buy software that contains the best-known procedure and then pay a smaller number of people to handle exceptions.

The floor can rise while the ladder loses rungs.

This book is about what becomes valuable in that environment.

The title is Ubiquitous because that is the direction that matters. Intelligence is moving from a scarce service we visit toward a background capability embedded in ordinary tools. The exact interfaces will change. Today one person may use Claude Desktop with local extensions, another may work inside ChatGPT, another may use an AI coding environment, another may use an enterprise agent connected to company systems. Names and products will churn. The durable fact is that more software will be able to interpret intention, call tools, retrieve context, produce artifacts, and attempt multi-step work.

The useful question is therefore not which interface wins.

It is what happens when competent digital execution becomes an expected property of the environment.

Three groups are often treated as if they occupy opposite ends of a hierarchy, yet they may all be advantaged by the same change.

The first group is people who begin without a valuable specialist skill. They gain access to a large library of procedural competence. They can attempt work that used to be blocked by vocabulary, software syntax, formatting conventions, or the need to know which first step professionals considered obvious. Their risk is dependence. If they cannot tell when the system is wrong, they can become confidently incompetent at greater speed. But their starting options expand.

The second group is people with hard physical and field skills: electricians, plumbers, mechanics, machinists, HVAC technicians, construction managers, civil engineers, technicians, operators, repair specialists, inspectors, and others whose work has to meet a stubborn physical world. Their tasks will change too. Some will be automated. Prefabrication will move work offsite. Better diagnostics will reduce troubleshooting time. Robotics will take portions of repetitive or dangerous labor. Yet the physical world imposes friction that pure information work does not. Wires must be terminated. Pipes must fit existing buildings. Bearings fail. Tolerances stack. Concrete cures. Water leaks. Equipment vibrates. Permits belong to places. Someone is responsible when the breaker trips, the pump cavitates, the wall moves, or the machine crashes.

The third group is harder to name because modern education and hiring systems often make them look unfocused.

They are the curious generalists who become serious about several things at once.

Not dabblers in the sense of collecting trivia. Not people who refuse the discipline required to learn anything deeply. They are builders who move across domains, carry concepts with them, notice analogies, ask why a process exists, learn enough economics to understand the constraint, enough engineering to respect the mechanism, enough software to automate the repetition, enough design to see the user, enough history to recognize the old pattern, and enough humility to call the specialist when the edge of their competence arrives.

The Renaissance ideal returns in a different form.

For centuries, one argument against broad curiosity was opportunity cost. Every hour spent learning a new domain was an hour not spent mastering the narrow craft that paid you. Specialization was economically powerful because knowledge was expensive to acquire, tools were hard to learn, and coordination across fields was slow. A person who knew a little about everything could be charming and unemployable.

AI changes the price of crossing intellectual distance.

It can explain unfamiliar vocabulary, generate examples, translate between professional dialects, sketch a prototype, summarize a standard, suggest experiments, expose missing prerequisites, and help a curious person get far enough into a neighboring field to ask a much better question. It does not abolish the need for depth. It lowers the cost of reaching the point where depth can connect to something else.

That is a large advantage for people whose natural mode is synthesis.

The vertical expert uses AI mainly to move faster inside a column. The renaissance worker can use it to move sideways between columns and then build at the intersection.

This is not automatically superior. Most intersections contain nothing valuable. Curiosity can become procrastination with better vocabulary. A person who moves endlessly between interests may never accumulate enough tacit knowledge to make a correct judgment under pressure. The physical trades remind us that competence is often stored in the body, in timing, in sounds, in the feel of resistance, in the memory of what failed last winter. A model can describe a weld without developing a welder’s eye.

The point is narrower.

When execution inside established categories becomes cheaper, value tends to migrate toward choosing the category, connecting categories, handling exceptions, and taking responsibility for what happens next.

That pattern is already visible in software. Writing code remains valuable, and demand for software developers remains substantial. But the boundary around who can make software is weakening. A strong developer with product judgment can now build more. A domain expert who could never code can prototype. A founder can explore without assembling a full team. A mediocre developer who only translates tickets into conventional code faces a different future than a developer who understands architecture, customers, security, operations, and the weird production failure nobody has documented.

The same logic appears in engineering. Drafting tools did not eliminate engineers. They changed where engineering value sat. Computer-aided design made lines cheaper. Simulation made some calculations cheaper. Standard components made many decisions reusable. The valuable engineer increasingly owns system behavior, tradeoffs, interfaces, safety, verification, and consequence.

It appears in medicine. Information is no longer scarce in the old way. Patients can access explanations, literature summaries, symptom information, and increasingly sophisticated decision support. That does not make physicians irrelevant. It changes what patients can arrive knowing and increases the premium on diagnosis, judgment, procedural skill, accountability, communication, and deciding what matters among too many plausible possibilities.

It appears in law. Generating clauses and summarizing authorities gets cheaper. Understanding the client’s actual exposure, negotiating against another party, appearing before a court, designing a strategy under uncertainty, recognizing which fact will destroy the argument, and putting a professional name behind advice remain different kinds of work.

The recurring distinction is between producing an artifact and owning an outcome.

AI is very good at making artifacts abundant.

A memo can be generated. A plan can be generated. Code can be generated. A drawing can be generated. A recommendation can be generated. A checklist can be generated. An explanation can be generated.

The world still contains the thing the artifact was supposed to change.

A building has to stand. A customer has to buy. A machine has to run. A patient has to improve. A bridge has to carry load. A budget has to balance eventually. A team has to coordinate. A permit has to clear. A product has to survive contact with people who did not read the documentation.

As artifacts become cheaper, the distance between artifact and outcome becomes easier to see.

This can be uncomfortable for professions that used artifacts as proof of value. The deck looked expensive because producing it was expensive. The report looked substantial because gathering and formatting the information took weeks. The codebase looked impressive because thousands of lines implied thousands of hours. When a machine can produce similar surfaces quickly, the surface stops certifying the labor behind it.

The response should not be to make the artifact more ornate.

It should be to move closer to consequence.

That is where the physical trades have always lived. An electrician cannot persuade a dead circuit with a better presentation. A plumber cannot wordsmith a drain into flowing. A machinist cannot argue a tolerance into existence. A civil engineer eventually meets gravity, traffic, water, soil, corrosion, procurement, and public use. These professions contain paperwork and routine tasks that AI will reduce, but their core education is unusually honest about the existence of an external judge.

Reality checks the work.

Knowledge work has reality too, but organizations can buffer people from it for surprisingly long periods. A strategy can be praised before it works. A report can circulate without anyone verifying the premise. A policy memo can be elegant and operationally impossible. A model can have twelve tabs and no causal value. A presentation can be excellent at presenting.

Ubiquitous intelligence may make those buffers harder to justify because polished cognitive output will no longer be evidence of unusual effort.

This is where the word skill itself needs repair.

We often use skill to mean a named competency: Excel, Python, copywriting, welding, financial modeling, prompt engineering, project management. Those are useful labels because schools and employers need labels. But durable economic value tends to live in combinations.

An electrician who also understands controls, networking, energy systems, permitting, estimating, and customer communication is not merely an electrician with extra hobbies. She can see a larger system and take on larger problems. A machinist who understands CAD, metrology, automation, fixture design, and the economics of setup time has more leverage than someone who only knows one machine. A civil engineer who can work across hydraulics, procurement, public meetings, construction sequencing, data, and regulation can move a project that a narrow calculation cannot.

The same is true for the curious knowledge worker. Writing plus statistics plus software plus domain knowledge plus sales can become a company. Biology plus computation plus instrumentation plus regulatory understanding can become a laboratory platform. History plus economics plus engineering can reveal why an infrastructure project keeps failing for reasons no single discipline owns.

AI does not create these combinations by itself.

It makes them easier to attempt.

That may be the defining career shift of the next decade. The old advice was to become T-shaped: broad across the top, deep in one vertical. It remains useful, but the geometry is changing. The horizontal bar becomes cheaper to build because AI helps with translation and access. Some verticals also become cheaper because standard expertise is increasingly embedded in models and tools. The advantage may belong to people with several real depths connected by unusually active curiosity.

A comb, perhaps. Or a messy workshop bench.

The shape matters less than the behavior.

Keep enough depth to know when the machine is bluffing. Keep enough breadth to notice when the problem is in the next field over. Keep enough physical or operational contact with reality that your judgments are tested. Keep enough ownership that you cannot outsource every error to the tool.

This is not a guarantee of safety.

There is no safe list of occupations that technology promises not to touch. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects strong growth for electricians, HVAC technicians, industrial machinery mechanics, industrial engineers, construction managers, and civil engineers over the coming decade. It also projects decline for some occupations that sound equally physical, including machinists and certain plant operators, because automation, prefabrication, and improved equipment change labor demand unevenly. “Work with your hands” is therefore poor forecasting advice by itself.

The better question is what kind of constraint the work encounters.

Is the task purely digital, standardized, and easy to verify? Expect its price to fall quickly.

Does the work require messy physical manipulation in changing environments? Automation becomes harder.

Does it require licensed responsibility, local knowledge, trust, or legal accountability? Replacement requires more than generating the answer.

Does it depend on tacit knowledge that is difficult to write down? Models may assist before they substitute.

Does it involve choosing among objectives rather than optimizing a stated objective? Judgment remains central.

Does it create new tasks, products, or systems rather than repeating known ones? Curiosity and invention gain leverage.

Most occupations contain several of these at once. Their futures will therefore be mixed.

The mistake would be to watch AI become more capable and conclude that human capability matters less.

Abundance changes what capability means.

When calculation became cheap, we did not stop needing mathematics. We needed fewer people to perform routine calculation by hand and more people able to decide what should be calculated, build systems around it, interpret the result, and notice when the inputs were nonsense. When information became abundant, we did not stop needing knowledge. We needed more judgment about sources, relevance, incentives, and context.

When competent intelligence becomes ubiquitous, we will not stop needing skilled people.

We will become less willing to pay merely for access to competence that everyone can summon.

That is a harsher sentence and a more hopeful one.

It is harsh because many careers were built around controlled access to procedure. It is hopeful because a person starting today may not need permission to begin nearly as often. The first useful draft, prototype, explanation, analysis, plan, or piece of code is closer than it has ever been.

The challenge is what to do after the first useful draft.

That is where the next decade begins.
