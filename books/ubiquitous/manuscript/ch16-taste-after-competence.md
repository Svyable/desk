# Taste After Competence

Competence used to filter the field before taste entered the room.

A person who could not draw had limited ability to propose a visual design. A person who could not code had limited ability to produce software. A person who could not write had limited ability to fill the world with finished prose. Production skill narrowed the number of people able to participate.

Generative systems weaken that filter.

More people can produce competent candidates.

That makes selection more important.

Taste is the word we often use for selection when the rules are not fully explicit. It can sound elitist, decorative, or impossible to teach. In some fields it is all three. Yet every practical craft contains a version of taste that has little to do with luxury.

An experienced carpenter can look at a detail and know it will be awkward even if it is technically buildable.

A machinist can recognize a drawing that was created without regard for how the part will be held.

A software engineer can see an architecture with too many moving pieces for the problem.

An editor can see the sentence that is correct and dead.

A product manager can recognize a feature that tests well in isolation and makes the product worse as a whole.

These judgments combine explicit constraints with accumulated preference about what tends to work.

That is operational taste.

AI makes it more valuable because candidate generation becomes abundant.

If a designer can generate fifty visual directions in the time once required for three, the scarce action becomes deciding which direction deserves development. If a programmer can generate several implementations, the scarce action becomes deciding which one fits the system’s future. If a marketer can produce hundreds of messages, the scarce action becomes knowing which one belongs to the brand and the customer rather than merely satisfying an engagement metric.

Abundance creates a garbage problem.

This is already visible in information. When publishing became cheap, the world did not run out of things to read. It ran out of attention. Search, recommendation, curation, reputation, and taste became more important because production no longer limited supply.

Generative AI applies that logic inside work itself.

A team can now generate more alternatives than it can seriously evaluate.

The person who responds by generating even more alternatives is not adding much value.

The valuable worker narrows.

Narrowing requires a point of view about what matters.

This is why taste cannot be reduced to asking an AI which option is best. The model can rank according to criteria. The difficult part is choosing criteria that reflect a real objective rather than the easiest measurable proxy.

A house can be optimized for square footage and become unpleasant to live in.

A website can be optimized for clicks and become exhausting.

A manufacturing process can be optimized for cycle time and become fragile.

A public project can be optimized for vehicle throughput and make walking miserable.

A business can be optimized for short-term conversion and destroy customer trust.

Taste often notices what the objective function omitted.

This is not magic. It is a form of systems judgment shaped by exposure.

The person has seen enough examples to know that one quality cannot be maximized independently forever.

Good taste therefore tends to come after competence, not instead of it.

A novice can have preferences. Expertise teaches which preferences survive constraints.

This matters in the AI era because the tools can make preference look like mastery. A user can ask a model for a “minimalist, sophisticated, premium” design and receive an object with the surface markers of that description. The user may not know whether the hierarchy works, whether the typography holds across use cases, whether the material choice is manufacturable, or whether the design resembles every other prompt asking for the same adjectives.

AI can generate the style before the person has learned the reasons.

The same happens in writing. A model can generate a confident argumentative voice, a lyrical passage, a consulting tone, a legal tone, a founder tone, a magazine tone. Style becomes selectable.

That raises the value of an author who knows what should not be said.

Restraint is a form of taste.

When production is costly, creators are forced to select early. Film was expensive. Printing was expensive. Studio time was expensive. Software releases were expensive. Constraints did some editorial work automatically.

Cheap generation removes the constraint.

The new discipline is self-imposed scarcity.

Do not ship everything you can make.

Do not automate every process you can automate.

Do not add every feature the model can implement.

Do not create a dashboard for every question.

Do not turn every insight into content.

Selection becomes a professional act.

This can feel like doing less at the exact moment technology makes more possible.

That is why taste has economic value. It prevents abundance from turning into complexity.

Software offers a clear example.

Coding agents reduce the cost of adding code. That should make software better because teams can address more user needs and fix more problems.

It can also make codebases worse because every local improvement adds another concept, dependency, path, configuration, and maintenance obligation. The easiest feature to build is not necessarily the cheapest feature to own.

A strong engineer uses AI to make implementation cheap and then becomes more demanding about whether implementation should exist.

The person who can say “the right answer is to delete this workflow” may create more value than the person who can generate it in ten minutes.

This principle extends to organizations.

Agentic systems make it possible to create many automated processes. A company can have an agent that summarizes meetings, another that generates tasks, another that follows up, another that builds reports, another that monitors reports, and another that writes a weekly summary of the monitoring.

The organization becomes productive at producing work about work.

Taste in operations means noticing when a capability should collapse a process rather than accelerate every step of the old process.

If an agent can answer a question directly from authoritative data, perhaps the monthly report should disappear instead of being generated faster.

If software can reconcile two systems automatically, perhaps the meeting that existed to discuss reconciliation should disappear too.

The most valuable automation may be subtractive.

This is difficult because artifacts create visible evidence of effort. A new dashboard exists. A new agent has a name. A new workflow can be demonstrated.

A removed process leaves an absence.

Good operators learn to value the absence.

Physical crafts teach this kind of taste naturally.

A clean electrical installation is not simply one that works. Routing, labeling, access, bend quality, enclosure layout, and serviceability matter because another person will encounter the work later. A well-plumbed mechanical room has a logic a maintainer can read. A good machine fixture solves the problem without creating unnecessary setups or impossible inspection.

These qualities can be specified partly, but experienced workers recognize coherence before they can list every rule.

AI vision systems may learn to recognize more of it too.

That does not reduce the human value immediately because taste shifts with the system. Once a standard of neatness or accessibility becomes easy for software to inspect, professionals can push toward higher-level questions about maintainability and design.

This is the recurring frontier again.

Taste is not a protected mystical zone.

It is judgment at the edge of explicit rules.

As rules become explicit, the edge moves.

People can train taste deliberately by comparing outcomes rather than admiring outputs.

Why did this building age well?

Why does this machine setup survive inexperienced operators?

Why does this product remain understandable after ten features were added?

Why did one contractor’s estimate turn into a profitable job while another estimate with similar numbers became a dispute?

Why did customers trust one explanation and ignore another?

These questions connect preference to consequence.

AI can make this training faster by retrieving examples and generating contrasts, but it can also corrupt the process if the person learns only from generated material.

Generated examples tend toward the model’s center.

Taste often develops by encountering excellent and terrible real work with context around it.

Visit buildings.

Use tools.

Read old code.

Inspect failed parts.

Watch customers struggle.

Walk public infrastructure.

Read contracts after the dispute, not only before.

Study what survived time.

The renaissance worker accumulates a library of reality across domains.

That library makes cross-domain taste possible.

A person who has worked in manufacturing may recognize that a software team needs stronger process control. A software engineer may recognize that a public permitting system has terrible observability. A contractor may recognize that an administrative workflow resembles a badly sequenced job site: too many handoffs, no owner, hidden rework.

The analogy is useful because the person has felt the original constraint.

This is different from asking a model for “lessons manufacturing can teach software.” The model can generate ten. The worker can tell which one actually transfers.

Taste also matters in hiring and collaboration.

When everyone can prepare polished applications, portfolios, presentations, and work samples with AI, surface quality becomes less discriminating. Interviewers need better taste about evidence of actual capability.

Does the candidate notice tradeoffs without being prompted?

Can they explain why they rejected an attractive option?

Do they understand what failed?

Can they distinguish what they personally owned from what the team or tool supplied?

Can they revise an answer when new evidence arrives?

These signals are harder to manufacture through a one-shot artifact.

They reveal process.

For individuals, this suggests an unusual career investment: cultivate strong preferences and make them accountable to outcomes.

Many people avoid preferences because preferences create the possibility of being wrong. It feels safer to apply best practices, follow the framework, or ask the model for the standard answer.

That path is becoming cheaper.

The standard answer will be available everywhere.

The person who creates value has to say, in effect, “given this particular place, customer, system, budget, and future, I think this choice is better.”

Then the world gets to judge.

That is taste under consequence.

It is especially valuable for innovators because new products cannot be fully optimized from historical data. If the exact thing already existed, it would not be new. Someone has to choose which possibility deserves the next experiment.

AI increases the search space dramatically.

It can generate product ideas, materials combinations, code architectures, business models, names, designs, and experiments. The entrepreneur becomes less constrained by the ability to produce candidates and more constrained by the ability to recognize a promising one before evidence is complete.

That is taste mixed with uncertainty.

It cannot be automated away by simply asking the same model for another opinion, because the model’s opinion comes from patterns in what already existed.

Novelty lives partly in choosing a departure from those patterns.

The person with deep domain contact can make that departure for a reason.

This is where curious innovators gain from ubiquitous intelligence.

They can search more territory.

Their advantage is not that they can search. Everyone can.

Their advantage is knowing which strange result is worth stopping for.

The next decade will contain more competence than any organization can absorb.

Models will write competent text, generate competent code, create competent imagery, summarize competent plans, and offer competent analysis. Human teams will become capable of producing far more than they should ship.

Competence becomes the beginning of the decision, not the end.

Taste is what happens after the machine has made enough plausible things that plausibility stops being scarce.

Taste also has an accounting problem.

Organizations can count production more easily than subtraction.

They can count features shipped, campaigns launched, automations created, documents generated, experiments started, dashboards published, and tickets closed. They have a harder time counting the feature that was never built because someone recognized the maintenance burden early, the contract term removed before it caused a dispute, the meeting eliminated because the information could travel directly, or the product line deliberately kept narrow so customers could still understand it.

This means abundance can create a bias toward visible addition.

AI strengthens the bias because addition becomes cheaper and faster while the organizational reward system often remains attached to visible output.

Taste fights a metric problem, not merely an aesthetic problem.

A company that wants good taste must make room for rejection.

What percentage of generated ideas should survive?

If the answer is nearly all of them, the generation step is not exploring much or the selection step is not doing much.

A healthy creative or technical process should contain discarded work.

The discarded work is not necessarily waste. It is the search cost of finding a better path.

AI makes that search cost cheaper, which should allow stronger selection standards.

Instead, organizations may lower standards because the marginal artifact feels free.

Free artifacts are rarely free once they enter a system.

A new software feature creates support burden, documentation, security surface, data states, user expectations, migration questions, and future compatibility obligations.

A new policy creates exceptions, interpretation, training, enforcement, and appeals.

A new product variant creates inventory, forecasting, marketing, and operational complexity.

A new report creates a recurring audience that may begin depending on it.

A new automation creates permissions, monitoring, failure modes, and someone who has to understand why it exists two years later.

Taste sees the carrying cost behind the creation cost.

This is one reason experienced operators can appear conservative to enthusiastic builders.

They have paid the second invoice.

The beginner sees the cost of making the thing.

The experienced person sees the cost of owning it.

AI widens that gap unless the beginner is deliberately exposed to maintenance.

This suggests a practical way to train taste: follow decisions through their afterlife.

Do not only attend the design review. Attend the postmortem.

Do not only watch the launch. Watch the support queue six months later.

Do not only inspect the new mechanical room. Return after technicians have serviced it.

Do not only read the contract at signing. Read the correspondence when the relationship is strained.

Do not only review the new policy. Watch the exception process people create around it.

Taste improves when a person learns which elegant choices age badly.

This also clarifies why taste is not synonymous with seniority.

A senior person can have entrenched preferences that once worked and no longer fit the system. They can mistake familiarity for quality. They can protect complexity they helped create.

Good taste requires renewal.

It should be able to explain why the old pattern still applies and notice when a new tool changes the tradeoff.

An engineer who always rejects generated code because it is generated has poor taste if the code is simple, well tested, and cheaper to maintain than the hand-written alternative.

A manager who automates every workflow because automation is now possible also has poor taste.

The mature judgment is conditional.

Use the new capability where it changes the economics.

Preserve the old constraint where the constraint was doing useful work.

This becomes especially important in teams because individual taste can conflict.

One designer wants coherence. One salesperson wants customization. One engineer wants simplicity. One customer-success manager wants flexibility for existing accounts. One finance leader wants lower operating cost.

No person owns “taste” for the organization.

The organization needs a way to adjudicate competing forms of quality.

That is governance through principles rather than endless voting.

Which promises are central enough that we will accept cost to keep them?

Which kinds of complexity require exceptional evidence?

Which metrics may never be optimized alone?

What should remain human even if automation is possible because human attention is part of the product?

What are we willing to be bad at in order to be excellent at something else?

These are taste questions at institutional scale.

They give models better objectives because the organization itself has a point of view.

A model cannot supply that point of view without borrowing it from patterns or instructions.

If the organization has none, AI will happily produce locally plausible additions until the system becomes a collage of competent decisions.

That may be the central danger of post-competence work.

Not incompetence.

Incoherence.

Everything works in isolation.

Nothing belongs together.

Taste is the discipline that keeps abundance from winning by accumulation.
