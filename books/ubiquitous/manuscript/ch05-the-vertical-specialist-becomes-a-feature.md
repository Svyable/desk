# The Vertical Specialist Becomes a Feature

Specialization built the modern world.

Adam Smith’s pin factory remains memorable because the observation is so hard to escape once seen: dividing work lets people become faster, tools become more specific, and production become easier to coordinate. Industrialization deepened the pattern. Medicine split into specialties and subspecialties. Engineering divided by material, system, and scale. Finance accumulated analysts for increasingly narrow instruments. Software produced specialists in languages, frameworks, databases, networks, security, testing, and infrastructure.

Specialization is not a historical mistake that artificial intelligence will correct.

Deep knowledge remains one of the ways difficult things get done.

The vulnerability appears when a specialty becomes stable enough to be specified as a service.

Give me this input and I will return that output.

Once a specialty can be described that way, it begins to look like a feature.

This has happened for a long time without AI. Payroll used to be a substantial internal clerical function. Software turned much of it into a system. Travel agents once owned access to schedules, fares, and booking infrastructure that ordinary travelers could not reach easily. Online booking exposed those capabilities directly. Typesetters converted manuscripts into print-ready pages through a distinct craft; desktop publishing absorbed large portions of the work into tools used by writers, designers, and offices.

The pattern is not that the underlying knowledge becomes worthless. It gets embedded.

Somebody still has to know how payroll rules work well enough to build and audit payroll software. Somebody still has to maintain reservation systems. Typography still exists as a discipline. The capability simply stops requiring a separate human transaction every time an ordinary case appears.

Generative AI accelerates this embedding because it can absorb domains whose interface is not a clean form.

Traditional software likes explicit rules. If income is above one threshold and below another, apply this calculation. If a purchase exceeds a limit, route it to this approver. If a field is empty, reject the submission.

Many professional tasks resist that kind of programming because the rules are written in prose, depend on context, contain exceptions, or require interpretation of messy inputs. A compliance policy can be read by a person but may be awkward to encode as thousands of branches. A support procedure can be taught through examples that are hard to reduce to a decision tree. A research process may require searching, comparing, summarizing, and deciding which source matters.

Language models make these fuzzy procedures more programmable.

That does not make them deterministic. The system can fail precisely because it is interpreting rather than executing a formal rule. But if the performance is good enough for routine cases and the cost is low enough, an organization can place the procedure inside a larger workflow and send only uncertain cases to a specialist.

The specialty becomes a callable layer.

This is already how many software developers experience AI. A model can act briefly like a database tutor, a frontend developer, a test writer, a Linux troubleshooter, a documentation editor, a regular-expression specialist, or a language translator. A strong developer still knows that each of these mini-experts can be wrong. But the developer does not need to find a colleague every time an unfamiliar syntax appears.

The same architecture is spreading beyond software.

A sales representative can call on a writing specialist for a first draft without hiring one. A manager can call on a spreadsheet analyst for a formula. A small business can call on a research assistant for a market map. A designer can call on a copywriter, and a copywriter can call on a designer. A product team can ask a model to apply a known usability heuristic before a human researcher looks at the problem.

The vertical specialist is present everywhere as a weak ghost of the profession.

Weak ghosts can still change prices.

If the embedded capability handles seventy percent of ordinary demand at zero marginal labor cost, the human specialist is left competing for the remaining thirty percent. The remaining work may be more valuable. It may also be more irregular. The specialist loses volume while gaining complexity.

This is why average occupational forecasts can conceal painful transitions.

A profession can continue to exist and even grow while its internal ladder changes. There may be fewer junior roles, more system supervisors, higher expectations per worker, and a sharper divide between people whose expertise is distinctive and people whose expertise is close to the model’s default competence.

The easiest response is to go deeper.

Sometimes that works.

If general models can handle common tax questions, become the person who understands cross-border structuring, unusual transactions, audits, or a changing regulatory niche. If basic software implementation is cheap, become excellent at distributed systems, security, performance, or a difficult technical domain. If ordinary design production becomes abundant, develop extraordinary craft in identity systems, complex interaction, or physical product design.

Depth is a rational response when the frontier remains economically large.

But depth alone can become another vertical waiting to be encoded.

A field can feel specialized from the inside while still producing repeatable outputs. The expert may spend years learning a body of rules whose application is difficult for humans because the rules are numerous, not because each case is fundamentally novel. That is exactly the kind of domain improved retrieval and model reasoning can erode quickly.

The safer form of depth is entangled depth.

Entangled expertise cannot be separated cleanly from the system around it.

A structural engineer does not merely know formulas. The engineer works with geometry, materials, codes, loads, construction methods, costs, site conditions, architects, contractors, inspectors, owners, and liability. A controls technician does not merely understand ladder logic. The technician understands sensors, motors, network behavior, safety circuits, production constraints, operator habits, and the fact that shutting a machine down for diagnosis can cost thousands of dollars an hour.

The knowledge is deep, but its value appears through relationships among domains.

This is why one vertical expertise can be more vulnerable than several connected depths.

A person with one clean specialty may be excellent at a function that software can increasingly invoke on demand. A person who can combine the specialty with adjacent systems becomes harder to reduce to a function call.

Take accounting.

Bookkeeping is already heavily software-mediated. Generative systems can classify transactions, draft explanations, reconcile discrepancies, and answer questions about reports. A person whose identity is “I know how to operate the bookkeeping process” faces pressure.

An accountant who understands tax, financing, cash conversion, inventory, contracts, pricing, and the operational reality of the client can use the same automation to spend less time recording the business and more time understanding it.

The difference is not simply seniority. A senior person can be narrow too.

The difference is whether the expertise participates in decisions outside its own output.

This can be tested with a simple thought experiment.

Imagine your specialty is exposed as an API. Someone can send it the normal inputs and receive a high-quality result. What questions still require you?

If the answer is mostly “check that the API did it right,” the role may remain necessary but compressed.

If the answer includes choosing which problem to solve, gathering missing facts, negotiating conflicting objectives, deciding whether the normal rule applies, integrating the result into a physical or organizational system, persuading other people, accepting liability, and learning from the outcome, the specialty is part of a wider loop.

Loops are more durable than outputs.

The loop matters because work creates new information after action.

A marketing analyst recommends a campaign. Customers respond. A mechanical engineer specifies a component. The component fails under a condition the model did not capture. A public engineer designs a drainage project. Residents reveal a local flooding pattern absent from the data. A machinist changes a setup. Tool wear behaves differently than expected.

The worker who remains in the loop absorbs these consequences and updates judgment.

The specialist who only produces the initial artifact can be separated from feedback. Once separated, the artifact is easier to commoditize because the work has been reduced to a snapshot.

This is one reason consultants can be vulnerable even when their knowledge is broad. A consultant may have excellent frameworks and pattern recognition but leave before the organization lives with the recommendation. AI makes generalized advisory output abundant. The consultant who owns implementation, measurement, organizational change, and revision stands on different ground than the consultant whose product is the deck.

It is also why certain field specialists can gain from AI rather than merely resist it.

An electrician who spends less time preparing estimates and more time diagnosing complex power-quality problems has moved toward entangled depth. A plumber who can combine hydronics, controls, energy efficiency, water treatment, and building science can solve problems that cross traditional trade lines. A machinist who understands CAD/CAM, metrology, automation, fixture design, and production economics can own the process rather than only the cutting operation.

The tool expands the radius around the core craft.

That is a better use of AI than attempting to defend every administrative task attached to the craft.

Many professionals make the opposite choice because routine work feels like proof of legitimacy. The lawyer drafted the document, therefore the lawyer added value. The engineer performed the calculation, therefore the engineer did the engineering. The programmer wrote the code, therefore the programmer built the software.

This confuses labor with responsibility.

A professional should be delighted when a machine performs a routine calculation correctly. The question is whether the professional can move upward into deciding what needs calculation, what assumptions govern it, and how the result affects a real system.

If not, the routine labor may have been hiding a thinner professional role than anyone wanted to admit.

There is a social dimension to this too.

Occupational identities are strong. They provide language, community, credentials, wage structures, and a sense of progression. Cross-domain work often fits badly inside them. The engineer who understands sales can be treated as less technical. The tradesperson who likes software can be pulled away from the field before the combination matures. The designer who studies economics can look unfocused to a hiring system trained to match titles with requirements.

AI may reward combinations faster than institutions update their categories.

This creates an opportunity for people willing to look slightly illegible on paper.

The renaissance worker is not someone who rejects specialties. The phrase would be empty if it meant being generally interested in things. The durable version is someone who has at least one area where reality has been allowed to punish shallow understanding, and then builds real bridges outward.

The bridge may be technical, commercial, physical, social, or institutional.

A public-works engineer who learns finance can understand why technically sensible infrastructure never gets funded. An electrician who learns data-center operations can move from installing circuits to understanding critical-power systems. A software engineer who learns manufacturing can build tools that respect the actual constraints of a shop floor. A housing developer who learns building science, permitting, finance, and construction sequencing can see why a project that works in a spreadsheet fails on a parcel.

These people are harder to replace for a simple reason.

The system cannot know which expert to call until someone understands the shape of the problem.

That meta-skill becomes more valuable as specialized capability becomes more available.

A hospital can have excellent specialists and still fail a patient because the problem crosses departments. A construction project can employ excellent trades and still miss schedule because the interfaces are poorly coordinated. A company can buy excellent software and still automate the wrong process. A government can commission excellent technical reports and still fail to build because procurement, public consent, finance, and operations do not align.

Specialists optimize pieces. Systems fail between pieces.

This does not make the generalist king. Systems also fail when someone with superficial knowledge overrules the specialist who understands a constraint deeply. The renaissance ideal becomes dangerous when breadth creates confidence without competence.

The right relationship is reciprocal.

Deep specialists need people who can integrate. Integrators need enough depth to recognize where they should stop. AI can strengthen both roles when used properly: the specialist can reach adjacent knowledge faster, while the integrator can acquire enough language to collaborate across boundaries.

It can also produce counterfeit versions of both.

The counterfeit specialist repeats model output with professional vocabulary.

The counterfeit generalist assembles model output from several domains and mistakes the synthesis for understanding.

The test is consequence again.

Can the person make a prediction before the result? Can they identify what would make the recommendation wrong? Can they defend the tradeoff to someone with deeper expertise? Can they revise when reality disagrees? Can they take responsibility for the outcome?

If yes, the machine is extending skill.

If no, the machine may be extending presentation.

The next decade will produce many professions that do not disappear but become layers inside larger harnesses. Their routine procedures will be callable. Their standard vocabulary will be common. Their familiar artifacts will be cheap.

The people inside those professions have choices.

They can defend the vertical as territory.

They can move deeper into the rare cases.

Or they can connect the vertical to a larger problem and become the person who knows when, why, and how the feature should be used.

The third path is less tidy on a résumé.

It is also where a great deal of new value is likely to collect.

There is another consequence when a specialty becomes a feature: the unit of competition changes.

The individual specialist used to compete with other specialists. Once the routine capability becomes embedded, the specialist may instead compete with a bundle. A small accounting firm no longer competes only with the firm across town; it competes with payroll software, bookkeeping automation, bank integrations, tax preparation tools, and a general model wrapped into one operating system for the small business. A freelance designer competes with templates, image generation, brand kits, publishing tools, and an AI assistant that can carry an idea from rough brief to usable assets.

The customer experiences the bundle as one capability.

That does not mean the bundle is better at everything. It means the transaction cost of assembling ordinary expertise collapses. The customer who would never have hired five specialists can now receive weak versions of all five in the same workflow.

This puts pressure on the economics of the average case.

Specialists have several possible responses. One is to sell exception quality: the case where the bundle knows enough to know that it should stop. Another is to own a relationship that generates private context the generic system does not have. Another is to embed their own expertise into products and workflows, earning from scale rather than protecting scarcity. Another is to widen into adjacent decisions so the customer buys an outcome rather than a task.

The last response is particularly important because features are easy to compare while outcomes are not.

A customer can compare two automated transcription tools by accuracy, price, or speed. It is harder to compare two people who are responsible for making a complicated public meeting actually produce a decision, or for getting a manufacturing cell back to reliable production after three interacting failures. The work contains too many local variables to reduce cleanly to one benchmark.

This is not permission to hide behind vagueness. Outcomes still need evidence. The person who claims to be an integrator but cannot define success is merely selling ambiguity at a higher rate.

The valuable integrator can usually make the system more measurable because they understand where measurement should attach.

They know which part of a sales problem is really lead quality, which part is response time, which part is product fit, and which part is a pricing decision nobody wants to revisit. They know whether machine downtime is driven by maintenance, scheduling, changeover, operator training, bad upstream material, or a design decision that made the line fragile. They know when a construction delay is a field productivity problem and when the field is waiting on a submittal, utility, inspection, payment, or unresolved design.

This is the part of renaissance work that is easy to romanticize and hard to fake.

Cross-domain curiosity becomes valuable only when it improves causal diagnosis.

Collecting vocabulary from many fields does not do that. Living through feedback does.

A developer who spends time on a factory floor begins to understand why an elegant data model can fail when operators must enter information with gloves on while a line is moving. A civil engineer who sits through budget meetings learns that an infrastructure option with the lowest lifecycle cost can still be impossible if the capital plan cannot absorb its initial expenditure. A salesperson who learns enough engineering can stop promising features whose real cost appears six months after the contract is signed.

Each person becomes less pure in professional identity and more useful to the system.

AI lowers the cost of making those excursions because the first hours in the adjacent field are no longer dominated by vocabulary. The engineer can ask how municipal bonds work before calling finance. The finance person can ask for a walkthrough of a power-distribution one-line before meeting the electrical team. The machinist can ask how a vision model is calibrated before deciding whether inspection should move in-process.

The model can get them to the doorway.

The doorway is not the room.

That distinction protects the specialist from an equally simplistic conclusion: if everyone can learn the vocabulary quickly, specialists no longer matter. The opposite can happen. Faster boundary crossing can make excellent specialists more valuable because more people become capable of discovering where the real specialist is required.

A good integrator does not eliminate expertise. They route it.

The economic role starts to resemble architecture in the broad sense of the word. An architect of a system decides where standard components are sufficient and where the project deserves a custom detail. They know which interface can be loose and which must be specified precisely. They choose when to reuse a feature and when the feature’s assumptions make it dangerous.

This is what happens after many verticals become callable.

The scarce question is no longer whether a capability exists.

It is whether this is the capability the situation needs.

The answer changes with context, sequence, incentives, maintenance, people, and physical consequence. It often requires enough knowledge of several specialties to know which one is binding without pretending to be the deepest expert in all of them.

That is why the coming premium on breadth should not be confused with a retreat from depth.

Depth is becoming modular.

The advantage moves toward people who can assemble modules without forgetting that the seams are where systems break.
