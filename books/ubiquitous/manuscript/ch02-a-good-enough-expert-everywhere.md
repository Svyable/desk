# A Good Enough Expert Everywhere

For most of history, expertise had an address.

You went to the person, the office, the guild, the library, the shop, the university, the firm, the department, or the telephone number where the knowledge lived. Even after the internet made information cheap, useful interpretation remained attached to people and institutions. Search could find a tax rule. It could not reliably tell you which part applied to your strange little business. A forum could show you a hundred ways to fix a machine. It could not look at your symptoms, ask three clarifying questions, compare the possibilities, and explain which test would distinguish them.

The expert was valuable partly because knowledge was not merely stored. It was organized around the problem in front of you.

Generative AI packages that behavior.

The package is imperfect. It can hallucinate, overgeneralize, miss local rules, repeat stale information, and speak with a fluency that exceeds its evidence. But it can also do something that static information systems could not do well: reshape knowledge around the user’s immediate context.

That is enough to change markets before the technology becomes trustworthy enough to replace the professional at the top.

Imagine a person who has never negotiated a commercial lease. The traditional path begins with ignorance of the vocabulary. Triple net. CAM. Assignment. Personal guarantee. Estoppel. Use clause. Renewal option. Build-out allowance. The first barrier is not even judgment; it is learning what the document is talking about. Search helps, but search turns the person into a temporary librarian. She has to formulate each query, choose among conflicting pages, remember definitions, and map them back onto forty pages of legal text.

An AI system can read the lease, explain unfamiliar clauses, identify terms that deserve professional review, produce a comparison table, and answer follow-up questions in plain language. It can say, in effect, this provision appears to shift this kind of risk to you; here are the questions you might ask counsel.

The system should not be mistaken for counsel. The difference matters most when law, local practice, factual context, negotiation strategy, or liability enters. But the owner who arrives at the lawyer’s office after an hour of assisted reading is not the same client who would previously have arrived with a stack of paper and the sentence, “I don’t know what any of this means.”

The professional relationship moves upward.

The lower layer of interpretation becomes less scarce. The lawyer gets pulled toward the parts where judgment, negotiation, precedent, strategy, and responsibility matter. In some cases the lawyer receives a better-prepared client. In others the client decides the matter is routine enough not to hire one at all.

This pattern is what makes “AI as an assistant” sound gentler than it is.

Assistants alter the division of labor.

A forklift assists a warehouse worker, but it also changes how many workers are needed to move a pallet. A spreadsheet assists an accountant, but it eliminated enormous quantities of manual arithmetic. A digital camera assists a photographer, but it also gave everyone else a camera and removed entire layers of processing work. The economic consequence of assistance depends on who receives the assistance and what scarcity it dissolves.

General-purpose AI is unusual because the assistant is handed to the customer too.

Professional software historically increased the productivity of the trained person. Computer-aided design made engineers and architects more productive. Bloomberg terminals made finance professionals more productive. Legal databases made lawyers more productive. The tools were expensive, specialized, difficult, or institutionally gated enough that they reinforced professional boundaries even as they accelerated work inside them.

A conversational model has a different distribution strategy. Its basic interface is ordinary language. The novice and the expert can open the same box.

That does not put them on equal footing. It removes some of the difference in how quickly they can begin.

This is the sense in which AI flattens the playing field for people who start without a marketable skill. It is not that skill ceases to matter. It is that the penalty for not yet having a skill can become smaller in the early stages of a task.

A person who cannot write code can ask for code. A person who has never written a project plan can produce one. A person who does not know how to structure a sales email can generate ten attempts and compare them. A person who has never used a command line can describe an outcome and receive instructions. A person entering an unfamiliar industry can ask the model to explain the value chain, define the acronyms, suggest primary sources, and quiz them on what they have missed.

The important word is entering.

AI reduces entry friction.

It does not guarantee mastery after entry. It may even conceal the absence of mastery for a while, which is one of its most dangerous properties. A beginner can produce work that looks advanced enough to travel farther than beginner work used to travel. The spelling is correct. The formatting is confident. The code compiles. The memo has headings. The explanation contains the expected vocabulary. Surface competence can arrive before internal understanding.

That means the novice gains reach before gaining judgment.

We have seen versions of this before. Cheap desktop publishing let people produce documents that looked professional long before they had learned typography. Spreadsheet software let managers construct elaborate financial models without understanding how small errors propagate. Website builders let anyone publish a page without becoming a designer or engineer. Each tool lowered a barrier and produced both democratization and a new category of bad work that looked much better than the bad work of the previous era.

AI extends that pattern into reasoning-like output.

This makes verification a central dividing line. The person with no prior skill can get surprisingly far when the task has quick external feedback. If the goal is to write a small script that renames a thousand files, the script either works or does not. If it breaks, the failure is visible and often reversible. If the goal is to produce three logo concepts for a neighborhood event, the user can judge whether they like them. If the goal is to translate an internal note for a bilingual colleague who can correct it, the check is nearby.

The danger rises when the output can be wrong without looking wrong.

Tax advice. Structural calculations. Medical interpretation. Safety procedures. Contract language. Security architecture. Machine setup. Electrical work. Environmental compliance. In these domains, a plausible answer can travel into the world and create a cost before the user discovers the error.

This is where experts retain an advantage that benchmark discussions often understate. Experts do not merely know more correct statements. They know which mistakes are expensive.

That knowledge changes behavior.

A competent electrician does not treat every wire as an abstract conductor. The circuit has a source, a load, a protection scheme, a physical route, code requirements, existing conditions, and a human being who may touch the result years later. A lawyer does not read a clause only for its dictionary meaning. She asks what happens in a dispute, what leverage each party has, what jurisdiction does to the language, and whether another provision quietly changes the result. A machinist does not see a dimension as a number alone. He sees the material, the tool, the fixture, the machine, the thermal condition, the tolerance stack, the measurement method, and the cost of scrapping the part.

Expertise is compressed consequence.

A good expert has accumulated examples of how reality punishes certain errors. Some of that knowledge can be written down and therefore learned by models. Some of it is already embedded in codes, standards, manuals, case law, textbooks, and procedures. As AI becomes better at retrieving and applying those materials, more expertise will become available on demand.

The remainder becomes more interesting.

There is a phrase used in machine learning and economics that deserves a life outside both fields: the distribution.

A system performs well on the kinds of examples it has seen and the kinds of examples that resemble them. Reality becomes difficult at the tails. The strange retrofit. The undocumented modification. The customer whose constraints contradict each other. The old building with three generations of work hidden behind the wall. The machine that fails only after warming up for forty minutes. The organization whose official process and actual process diverged five years ago. The legal dispute where the facts do not line up cleanly with the category.

Experts live in the tails more than job descriptions admit.

The ordinary cases create volume. The difficult cases create the reputation.

AI therefore places pressure on an old professional bargain. Many experts have historically been paid premium rates across both ordinary and difficult cases because the client could not easily separate them in advance. The routine work subsidized the maintenance of expertise for the exceptional work. Junior employees performed standardized tasks while learning enough to become senior. Firms bundled research, drafting, review, coordination, and accountability into one bill.

If software handles more ordinary cases, that bundle can separate.

The customer gets a good-enough expert everywhere for common questions. The human expert gets called later, for stranger questions, at higher stakes, with less beginner work attached.

That sounds efficient. It can also damage how experts are made.

A senior attorney did not begin with a career composed entirely of novel disputes. A senior engineer did not learn by making only decisions that could kill someone. A master technician did not begin with the intermittent failure nobody else could diagnose. Expertise grows through repetition, supervised exposure, pattern recognition, and gradually widening responsibility. If AI consumes the routine cases, we have to redesign apprenticeship rather than assume the next generation of experts will somehow appear fully formed.

The same tension shows up in the research on AI productivity. When novice customer-support workers benefit from a model that has absorbed the practices of stronger workers, the immediate result is positive. Customers get faster help. New employees become productive sooner. Workers may learn from suggestions. The organization captures more output.

But there is another interpretation worth keeping in view.

The organization has turned part of its best workers’ tacit operating method into infrastructure.

Once that happens, the distance between a new worker and an experienced worker can shrink. The experienced worker’s knowledge still matters, but some of its value has moved out of the worker and into the system.

This is expertise-leveling.

Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, and Simon Johnson use that term in their 2026 work on pro-worker AI to distinguish one type of technological change from others. A technology can make expertise more accessible without necessarily creating new tasks or making the expert more valuable. That can improve service and lower barriers while also reducing the scarcity rents attached to knowing the standard procedure.

The distinction is uncomfortable because society wants both outcomes.

We want more people to have access to competent help. We also want people to have reasons to invest years developing expertise.

Those goals are compatible only if the economy continues to create valuable problems beyond the standardized layer.

Fortunately, it usually does.

When a capability becomes cheap, people use more of it. Cheap photography produced more images, not fewer. Cheap calculation produced more calculation. Cheap software distribution produced an enormous software economy. Cheap communication produced more communication than anyone needed. Some of the new demand is noise, but some changes what is possible.

If intelligent assistance becomes cheap, people will ask questions they previously would not have paid to ask. Small organizations will attempt analyses they never commissioned. Individuals will build tools that never justified a developer. Local businesses will experiment with operations, marketing, translation, training, and customer service that previously sat below the threshold where specialist labor made sense.

This creates more activity at the edges.

A person with no formal technical background may use AI to build a prototype, discover that the prototype has customers, and then encounter a problem that requires an excellent engineer. A homeowner may use AI to understand a renovation, which may make them a more demanding and informed customer for a contractor rather than eliminate the contractor. A small manufacturer may use an AI system to analyze machine downtime, notice a pattern, and then need a controls specialist to redesign the line.

The good-enough expert everywhere can increase demand for real experts by expanding the number of people who can get far enough to discover hard problems.

That is the optimistic mechanism.

There is a less optimistic one.

Organizations can use the same systems to strip roles down to supervised exception handling. The worker receives less discretion because the procedure lives in software. Performance becomes easier to measure because the system structures the interaction. The employer hires for compliance with the workflow rather than accumulated craft. The worker becomes the human fallback around an increasingly capable machine.

This is not hypothetical in the abstract sense. It has happened repeatedly with earlier forms of software. Call centers turned parts of conversation into scripts and metrics. Warehouses turned movement into scanner-directed sequences. Enterprise systems turned many administrative judgments into fields, permissions, and workflow states. AI can make the script adaptive rather than fixed, which may improve the experience while deepening the system’s control.

The question is who owns the learning.

If the machine gives a worker an answer but the worker never learns why, the organization gets productivity and the worker may not get skill. If the machine explains its reasoning, invites verification, exposes sources, and lets the worker compare alternatives, the same interaction can accelerate learning. If the worker is allowed to explore beyond the recommendation, improvement can compound. If deviation is punished because the AI workflow is treated as policy, judgment atrophies.

This makes tool design a labor issue, not merely a software issue.

It also makes personal tool use a career choice.

A person can use AI as a vending machine for finished output. Ask. Copy. Send. Repeat.

Or a person can use it as a microscope on the skill itself. Why did you choose that structure? What assumption would reverse the answer? Show me three failure modes. Give me a case where this rule does not apply. What would an expert inspect before acting? Quiz me without giving the answer. Compare my attempt with yours. Tell me what I am not noticing.

Both modes increase short-term output.

Only one reliably increases the person.

The difference becomes especially important for people entering from outside a field. The novice has a remarkable opportunity because the first barrier is lower. But if the novice never converts assisted output into independent mental models, they remain one product change away from helplessness.

This is a deeper reason curiosity matters.

Curiosity turns access into accumulation.

A curious person does not stop at receiving the answer. They notice the new concept inside the answer and follow it. They ask what mechanism makes the rule true. They connect the explanation to something learned elsewhere. They test the boundary. They want to know what the machine is assuming.

That behavior was always useful. It becomes economically important when answers themselves are abundant.

In a world of scarce answers, the person who knows the answer has an advantage.

In a world of ubiquitous answers, the advantage moves toward the person who asks the question that reveals the next problem.

This is why the flattening effect does not create permanent equality.

It creates a new starting line.

People who had no access to expertise gain some. People with one narrow expertise may lose some scarcity. People with deep expertise can move toward the frontier where the system is less reliable. People with broad curiosity can traverse more fields and discover combinations that used to be too costly to explore.

Then differences begin accumulating again.

The novice who uses AI to avoid learning remains dependent. The novice who uses it to learn faster may become formidable.

The specialist who treats the tool as an insult protects a shrinking boundary. The specialist who helps encode the routine parts of the craft and then moves toward harder judgment may expand their reach.

The generalist who skims ten fields and understands none becomes a generator of polished nonsense. The generalist who acquires enough real depth in several places can build things no single professional category would naturally propose.

The tool flattens one landscape and exposes another.

This is easy to miss because interfaces make the first stage feel magical. Type a sentence. Receive a result. Connect a tool. Delegate a task. The distance between desire and artifact collapses.

But the artifact is not the end of work.

The artifact enters a system. Someone uses it, pays for it, relies on it, maintains it, argues with it, repairs it, or discovers that it failed. Consequences remain distributed across people, institutions, and physical things.

A good-enough expert everywhere is therefore not the end of expertise.

It is the end of one kind of scarcity.

And when a scarcity disappears, the economy starts revealing what it was hiding behind it.

One of the things it was hiding was price discrimination by confusion.

This sounds harsher than it is meant to. Many professional fees are justified by responsibility, training, insurance, judgment, and the simple fact that expertise is expensive to maintain. But some portion of the market has always existed because the buyer could not cheaply tell a routine question from a hard one. A small business owner called an accountant because she did not know whether the issue in front of her was a ten-minute classification question or the first sign of a serious tax problem. A homeowner called a contractor because he could not tell whether a crack was cosmetic or structural. A manager hired a consultant because the vocabulary of a new technology made even the first hour of orientation difficult.

A conversational model can provide triage before the expensive relationship begins.

That changes the buyer’s behavior even when the final decision remains human. The buyer can arrive with the facts organized, with obvious alternatives eliminated, with terminology understood, and with a sharper sense of what they do not know. Professional time is then spent on the portion that actually requires the professional.

Some experts will dislike this because it removes billable fog. Others will build better businesses around it.

A law firm can sell judgment rather than document explanation. An engineering firm can automate preliminary comparison while charging for design responsibility, field investigation, review, and the difficult decisions that determine whether a project survives contact with construction. A specialist physician can receive a patient who understands the broad categories well enough to describe symptoms and prior tests more precisely, while the physician concentrates on differential diagnosis, examination, treatment choice, and uncertainty that cannot be resolved from text alone.

The best experts may become easier to find because good-enough preliminary expertise can tell customers when the ordinary answer has stopped being ordinary.

That suggests a different future for credentials too.

Credentials once performed several jobs at once. They signaled that a person had learned a body of knowledge. They provided a gate into a profession. They reassured a customer who had no independent way to evaluate competence. In regulated fields they also attached legal authority and accountability to a person or firm.

AI weakens the first function before it weakens the others. It becomes easier for an uncredentialed person to retrieve and apply pieces of the knowledge. That does not automatically grant authority, insurance, fiduciary duty, licensure, or a party who can be sued when something goes wrong.

The remaining power of the credential therefore becomes easier to see.

In some occupations it will look increasingly ceremonial and markets will route around it. In others it will become more important because the world contains more competent-looking output and customers need a trustworthy way to know who is willing and legally able to stand behind a consequential decision.

The same dynamic appears inside companies. If every employee can summon a passable analyst, writer, researcher, coder, or project planner, departments lose some monopoly over their basic output. Finance no longer owns the first spreadsheet. Legal no longer owns the first reading of a contract. Engineering no longer owns the first prototype. Marketing no longer owns the first campaign concept.

But the departments may gain responsibility for standards, edge cases, system design, and review.

That creates a subtle political shift. Expertise becomes less about controlling access to a tool and more about controlling the conditions under which an output receives authority. The person who can draft a forecast is common. The person who can defend the assumptions to a board, reconcile the forecast with operational reality, and decide what the company should do when the range is wide remains scarce. The person who can generate contract language is common. The person who knows whether the company should accept the obligation and is authorized to negotiate it remains scarce.

Authority and expertise partially separate.

That separation creates opportunity for beginners because they can do more before asking permission. It also creates risk because fluent systems make it easy to cross the boundary without realizing it. A young employee can generate a persuasive analysis and circulate it beyond their level of understanding. A founder can produce a legal-looking document and assume the resemblance is protection. A homeowner can follow an electrical explanation into a panel where a mistake has immediate physical consequence.

A mature AI economy will need better markers at that boundary: not merely “AI generated,” but who checked this, against what evidence, under which standard, with what authority, and who owns the result after it leaves the screen.

That is why the good-enough expert does not merely push the human expert upward toward harder problems. It also pushes the expert sideways toward verification, provenance, and responsibility.

The scarce service becomes less often “I know the standard answer” and more often “I know when the standard answer applies, I can prove what we relied on, and I will still be here when the result has consequences.”

This is a better description of professional value than prestige ever was.

It is also harder to automate than explanation alone, because responsibility is not an output format. It is a continuing relationship between a decision and the person or institution that must live with it.

The future expert may therefore look less like a walking encyclopedia and more like a steward of consequential uncertainty.

That is not a demotion.

It is what expertise was for underneath the scarcity of information all along.
