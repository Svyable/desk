*How the World Got Addicted to the iPhone*

*Glass Face Syndrome* asks how one sheet of responsive glass became the default intermediary between people and an expanding share of ordinary life.

The book's argument is not that Apple invented distraction or that every heavy smartphone user is clinically addicted. The iPhone matters because it helped establish a cultural form: an always-carried, software-defined surface capable of becoming a camera, map, wallet, ticket, workplace, social world, entertainment system, memory archive, identity surface, and route to almost anything that might matter next.

As more useful functions moved behind the same glass, carrying the phone became increasingly rational. Once enough people carried it, institutions could assume it was present. Work expected reachability. Friends expected response. Tickets, payments, authentication, directions, photographs, school systems, and public services increasingly treated the phone as infrastructure.

That is the book's central reversal:

> **The world did not become dependent on the smartphone because the smartphone was uselessly irresistible. It became difficult to put down because it kept earning the right to be picked up — until usefulness hardened into expectation.**

“Glass Face Syndrome” is a cultural metaphor, not a medical diagnosis. The manuscript distinguishes habit, problematic smartphone use, compulsion, infrastructural dependence, and recognized behavioral disorders rather than treating every pickup or every hour of screen time as pathology.

The final section rejects nostalgia as a solution. The goal is not to return to 2006. It is to preserve navigation, accessibility, communication, cameras, payments, safety, translation, work flexibility, and portable computing while rebuilding boundaries the all-purpose device dissolved: friction where intention is fragile, rooms with local sovereignty, alternative paths when the phone fails, and technologies that know how to finish.
