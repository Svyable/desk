# Back Matter

## About the Book

*Glass Face Syndrome* is about the transformation of the smartphone from a useful object into a default intermediary between people and ordinary life.

Its argument is not that Apple invented distraction, that every heavy phone user is addicted, or that modern life would improve if everyone returned to 2006. The iPhone matters because it made a particular form of computing culturally dominant: one always-carried, software-defined sheet of glass capable of becoming a camera, map, wallet, ticket, workplace, social world, entertainment system, memory archive, identity surface, and route to almost anything that might matter next.

The book calls the resulting posture Glass Face Syndrome. The phrase is cultural, not clinical. It describes an environment in which turning toward the phone becomes an ordinary response to uncertainty, boredom, work, social expectation, memory, navigation, status, and desire — and in which institutions increasingly assume the phone will be present.

The final question is therefore not how to eliminate smartphones. It is how to keep their extraordinary capability while restoring boundaries, alternative paths, completed intentions, and the right to decide what gets attention.

## Notes and Sources

The manuscript was researched chapter by chapter using a source ledger maintained alongside the book.

Primary product history comes from dated Apple, Google, Android, Open Handset Alliance, and carrier materials where those sources can establish what a device, platform, or operating system actually introduced at a particular moment. Company announcements are not treated as independent evidence that a feature produced a social or psychological effect.

Claims about attention, memory, navigation, relationships, parenting, work, youth mental health, sleep, problematic smartphone use, measurement, and interventions are grounded in original academic studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, consensus reports, and official public-health advisories. The manuscript distinguishes laboratory experiments from field experiments, cross-sectional associations from longitudinal evidence, and causal interventions from observational findings.

Counterevidence is part of the argument. The research record does not support treating all screen exposure as one dose, all smartphone dependence as clinical addiction, or all youth technology use as a single cause of mental-health change. Effects vary by person, content, context, developmental stage, motive, business model, and the activity taking place through the device. Studies of smartphone presence and digital detox also produce mixed or conditional findings. Those limits are preserved rather than edited away.

The source ledger records titles, dates, URLs, claim summaries, and caveats. It is the canonical research record for the working draft and can be converted into publication-format endnotes during final production.

## A Note on the Phrase “Glass Face Syndrome”

“Glass Face Syndrome” is an authorial term for a cultural condition. It is not a medical diagnosis, psychiatric category, or proposed clinical syndrome.

Likewise, the subtitle’s word “addicted” is used at the cultural level to investigate how a society became behaviorally and infrastructurally dependent on a device that also contains specific activities capable of becoming compulsive or harmful for some users. Where the scientific literature uses terms such as *problematic smartphone use*, *habitual use*, *compulsive use*, *gaming disorder*, or other defined constructs, the manuscript preserves that terminology and its limits.

Nothing in this book is intended as medical or mental-health advice. Readers concerned about their own technology use, sleep, mood, anxiety, gambling, compulsive behavior, or a child’s well-being should seek advice appropriate to the actual problem rather than treating a metaphor as a diagnosis.

## Acknowledgments

This book depends on researchers willing to make a noisy subject less dramatic and more knowable: psychologists who distinguish association from causation, methodologists who compare self-report with device logs, clinicians who resist turning ordinary behavior into pathology, and reviewers who keep small or conflicting effects visible when a simpler story would travel farther.

It also depends on engineers and product teams whose work made the smartphone useful enough to create the problem worth studying. Navigation, accessibility, cameras, payments, communication, translation, emergency access, and portable computing are not footnotes to the story. They are why the phone earned its place in the hand.

The historical record is most useful when it refuses both worship and panic.

Any remaining errors of interpretation belong to the author.
