# Boredom Disappears

Boredom used to arrive uninvited and stay long enough to become a situation.

You waited in the dentist’s office.

You stood in line.

You sat in the passenger seat while the adults talked.

You lay awake before sleep.

You waited for a friend who was late and had no way to know how late.

You sat through the first half of a train ride after finishing the newspaper.

Sometimes you brought something. A book. A magazine. A puzzle. A music player. Sometimes you did not.

Then boredom was not a failure of preparation. It was part of the weather of a day.

The smartphone made that weather easier to escape.

This was one of its gifts.

Boredom is unpleasant. John Eastwood and colleagues, reviewing theories of boredom, described it as the aversive experience of wanting but being unable to engage in satisfying activity. That definition matters because nostalgia often turns boredom into a gentle meadow in which creativity grows. Actual boredom can feel restless, empty, irritable, and trapped.

A person who opens a phone because waiting is miserable is solving a real problem.

The smartphone is spectacularly good at solving it because it does not need to predict what kind of stimulation will work.

If the news is boring, there is a game.

If the game is boring, there are messages.

If nobody has written, there is video.

If video is dull, there is shopping.

If nothing works, there is search.

The infinite room becomes an anti-boredom machine.

Its success raises a strange question: what happens when a mental state becomes easy enough to abort that we rarely stay inside it long enough to learn what it would have turned into?

The answer is not simply “creativity.”

There is research suggesting that some boring tasks can improve performance on later creative tasks. In studies published by Sandi Mann and Rebekah Cadman in 2014, participants assigned to monotonous activities subsequently generated more creative responses under some conditions than control participants. The researchers discussed daydreaming as a possible pathway.

The study is interesting and modest.

It does not establish that five minutes staring at a wall will make someone a genius. It does not prove that boredom is always good. People prone to boredom can suffer, and later research has linked boredom proneness with a variety of unhealthy behaviors. More recent reviews have also found positive associations between trait boredom and problematic technology use, though association does not settle direction.

The useful idea is narrower: some unengaged intervals can open into internally generated thought if they are not immediately filled.

The smartphone changes the probability of reaching that next state.

There is a moment after external stimulation stops when the mind has not yet found another object. That moment can be uncomfortable. The phone is close enough to prevent the experiment from continuing.

This is less about boredom as virtue than about latency.

What does the mind do after thirty seconds?

After two minutes?

After ten?

We increasingly do not know because the intervention occurs at five seconds.

The phone gives external attention somewhere to go before internal attention has to improvise.

That is a historically unusual arrangement.

Human beings have always sought stimulation. We talk, hum, tap, doodle, daydream, fidget, read signs, watch people, count ceiling tiles, make games, and invent errands. There was never a pristine pre-smartphone human who calmly accepted every empty minute.

The difference is supply.

Older anti-boredom strategies often had limited content and higher activation costs. You needed to bring the book. The magazine ended. The batteries in the music player died. The stranger might not want to talk. Daydreaming was always available because the alternatives ran out.

The smartphone makes the alternatives difficult to exhaust.

This changes waiting from a condition into a cue.

Wait, therefore check.

The cue is so common that the behavior can become independent of actual boredom. The phone comes out at the first sign that a moment might become empty.

An elevator lobby is barely a wait.

A pedestrian signal may last less than a minute.

A friend takes a sip of water.

The grocery clerk turns to retrieve a receipt.

The phone appears before boredom has time to become boredom.

This is anticipatory boredom management.

We prevent the state rather than relieve it.

Prevention can alter tolerance.

If every small hunger were answered by food, mild hunger might begin to feel more urgent. If every room were perfectly climate-controlled, small temperature changes might become more noticeable. A mind accustomed to immediate stimulation may experience low-input states differently, though claims about a broad generational collapse in attention outrun evidence.

The cultural observation does not require a neurological one.

People have changed their behavior around empty intervals because an attractive alternative is always available.

Look at a line in public.

Again, the visual evidence is ambiguous. The person on the phone may be doing something important. We cannot infer damage from posture. But the collective pattern is unmistakable: waiting no longer implies looking around.

This matters socially.

Strangers used to have more opportunities to become temporarily relevant to one another because there were fewer private worlds available on demand.

The person beside you might comment on the delay.

Ask a question.

Make a joke.

Complain about the weather.

Say nothing, but become part of your awareness.

Most of these encounters were forgettable. Some were irritating. A few became stories, friendships, jobs, romances, warnings, bits of local knowledge, or simply evidence that other people existed outside one’s chosen network.

The smartphone allows us to spend public waiting time with people we already know or content already selected for us.

That is often preferable.

It also reduces accidental social exposure.

A city full of people can become a set of private tunnels through shared space.

Earbuds deepen the tunnel. Navigation directs the route. The feed fills the pause. Messaging supplies familiar company. The user can move through thousands of strangers while remaining socially somewhere else.

This is one of the paradoxes of ubiquitous connection.

The phone can make distant people present and nearby people absent.

Neither condition is inherently better. A lonely person in a hostile crowd may be grateful for distant presence. A commuter does not owe strangers conversation. Public life does not require everyone to perform sociability.

The question is what disappears when the option of immediate private escape becomes default.

One thing that may disappear is observation without purpose.

Wandering attention is often treated as the enemy of productivity. Sometimes it is. The mind can wander into anxiety, rumination, or distraction. Research has not established that mind-wandering is uniformly beneficial. But unstructured attention can also allow memories, concerns, ideas, and associations to surface without an external prompt determining the topic.

The phone is unusually good at determining the next topic.

The feed says look here.

The message says think of this person.

The headline says worry about this event.

The advertisement says desire this object.

The map says turn here.

Unstructured time with the mind is different because the next cue may come from inside.

That can be unpleasant.

Perhaps this is one reason the phone is especially compelling during anxiety. Internal thought can be harsher than external content. A feed may be easier than being alone with a worry.

Again, that use can be adaptive in the moment. Distraction is a legitimate coping strategy. A person in pain may need relief. Someone grieving may need a comedy. Someone panicking may benefit from a friend’s message. There is no virtue in maximizing exposure to distress.

The problem appears when every uncomfortable thought is treated as something to outrun.

Then the device becomes an emotional reflex.

Sad: check.

Anxious: check.

Embarrassed: check.

Lonely: check.

Angry: check.

The phone does not need to solve the emotion. It only needs to interrupt awareness of it briefly.

This can create a confusing loop because the content encountered may intensify the state that triggered the check.

An anxious person opens news for distraction and becomes more anxious.

A lonely person opens social media and encounters evidence of other people together.

A bored person opens a feed and remains under-stimulated while continuing to scroll.

The phone succeeds at occupying attention without necessarily improving mood.

Occupation and satisfaction are not the same product.

This is important to the addiction debate. People often assume repeated use demonstrates pleasure. Sometimes repeated use demonstrates that the alternative state is even less comfortable.

Boredom is particularly suited to this loop because the phone offers low-effort novelty.

A 2025 systematic review of boredom and problematic technology use found trait boredom repeatedly associated with problematic use across smartphones, social media, internet use, and gaming. A 2026 meta-analysis similarly reported a moderate positive association between trait boredom and problematic digital technology use across studies, while noting substantial heterogeneity.

These findings do not tell us that phones cause boredom or that boredom causes addiction in a simple direction. People who are more boredom-prone may use technology more. Heavy fragmented use may also alter how ordinary low-stimulation moments feel. Depression, anxiety, impulsivity, social context, and other variables may shape both.

What they do show is that boredom belongs in the mechanism rather than at the edge of it.

The phone and boredom are paired because one is an almost universal response to the other.

The pairing has commercial consequences.

A product that wins bored moments gains enormous inventory.

There are only so many lunch breaks, commutes, television ad breaks, bathroom visits, queues, and minutes before meetings in a day, but across billions of people they form a vast market of transitional attention.

The smartphone transformed dead time into monetizable time.

A bus stop can now generate advertising impressions.

A sleepless minute can become a purchase.

A bathroom visit can become a video session.

A two-minute wait can become a political argument.

The economics of the internet expanded into intervals that had never previously been easy to sell.

That may be one of the most consequential business innovations of the phone: it did not create more human time, but it made more kinds of time addressable.

The term “dead time” reveals the ideology.

Why should an unoccupied minute be dead?

Because it produces nothing measurable?

Because no information is consumed?

Because nobody can sell against it?

The smartphone era treats unclaimed attention as capacity waiting to be used.

This attitude spills into productivity culture. Listen to the podcast while walking. Answer messages in line. Watch the tutorial while cooking. Clear email during the taxi ride. Track the workout. Use the meditation app. Optimize the sleep.

Even recovery can become an assignment administered through the screen.

The phone does not merely fill boredom with entertainment.

It can fill it with self-improvement.

That is harder to resist because the use feels virtuous.

A person can become unable to take a walk without turning the walk into an input channel.

Again, there is nothing wrong with podcasts. The question is whether the person can choose silence when silence would serve them.

Choice is the recurring test.

Glass Face Syndrome is not high usage.

It is default mediation.

The phone enters before the user has decided what the empty moment needs.

Some of the most revealing experiments in changing phone habits therefore begin not with prohibition but observation.

Leave the phone in the pocket during one line.

Walk one block without audio.

Wait for the kettle without opening anything.

The first discovery may not be creativity.

It may be irritation.

That irritation is information.

The nervous system expected input.

A person can then decide whether the expectation is worth keeping.

This is different from glorifying boredom. We do not need boredom retreats. We do not need to make airports more tedious. We do not need to shame people for entertaining themselves.

The aim is to restore a missing option: unfilled time that is not experienced as a defect requiring immediate repair.

Children may need this option especially because play often begins after the first complaint that there is nothing to do.

Parents know the sequence.

“I’m bored.”

The statement sounds like a request for service.

Adults can provide a screen, activity, snack, toy, outing, or instruction. Sometimes that is exactly right. Sometimes the complaint is the threshold before the child begins making something out of what is available.

The smartphone can eliminate the threshold with industrial reliability.

An adult can do the same thing to themselves.

The difficult first minutes of a book become a check.

The difficult first minutes of writing become a check.

The silence after an argument becomes a check.

The lonely first evening in a new city becomes a check.

The mind is protected from the state in which it might have had to adapt.

Adaptation is not always good. Some situations should be escaped.

But a life with zero exposure to unchosen emptiness may lose forms of improvisation that only appear when the supplied options stop.

The smartphone did not kill boredom.

It made boredom defeatable often enough that boredom stopped setting the terms.

That sounds like progress because in many ways it is.

The cost is visible only in the moments that never happen.

The thought that would have wandered in.

The stranger who would have spoken.

The idea that would have arrived after the restlessness.

The feeling that would have had to be named.

The decision that would have emerged after doing nothing long enough to notice what we wanted.

No dataset can count these missing events cleanly.

They are counterfactuals.

That makes them easy to romanticize and easy to ignore.

The safest conclusion is smaller.

Boredom is not sacred.

Neither is its elimination free.

A culture that fills every seam teaches people that every seam needs filling.

The glass face appears before the empty moment has even introduced itself.
