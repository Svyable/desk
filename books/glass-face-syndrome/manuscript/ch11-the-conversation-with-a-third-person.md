# The Conversation With a Third Person

Two people sit at a table.

There are three parties to the conversation.

The third one is face down beside the salt.

It has said nothing. It may say nothing. Yet both people know it could become relevant at any moment.

This is one of the most important social changes created by the smartphone because it does not require active use. The device can alter a room by representing elsewhere.

Another person could write.

Another event could happen.

Another obligation could surface.

The phone is not a person, but it carries people. It is a social container, and its presence can therefore function like the presence of an absent audience.

This is why phone etiquette became so emotionally charged so quickly.

A fork on the table does not imply that someone outside the room may outrank the conversation. A watch does not contain another relationship. A phone does.

When it lights up, the hierarchy becomes explicit.

The owner glances.

The other person sees the glance.

The glance may last half a second, but attention is communicative. It says something even when the message behind it is harmless.

Maybe the owner is checking whether the babysitter wrote.

Maybe a parent is in the hospital.

Maybe the glance is involuntary.

Maybe the message is nothing.

The person across the table cannot know without asking, and asking can itself feel needy or controlling.

The ambiguity is the social cost.

Researchers gave phone-based snubbing the ungainly name “phubbing.” The term became popular because it captured a behavior that people recognized immediately: being physically with someone while attention repeatedly shifts to the phone.

Studies of romantic relationships have found associations between partner phubbing, phone-related conflict, and lower relationship satisfaction. One influential study by James Roberts and Meredith David developed a partner-phubbing scale and found that perceived phubbing was linked with conflict and lower satisfaction in their sample.

The obvious caution is that relationships are not laboratory machines. People in unhappy relationships may use phones differently. People who already feel insecure may perceive phone behavior differently. Self-report measures can capture real experience while leaving causality unresolved.

That is why a randomized field experiment is useful as a different kind of evidence.

In research published in 2018, Ryan Dwyer, Kostadin Kushlev, and Elizabeth Dunn recruited people to share meals with friends or family. Some participants kept phones on the table; others put them away. The researchers found that phone use was associated with greater distraction and reduced enjoyment of the social interaction.

The effect was not proof that every dinner must be phone-free. It did something more useful: it showed that the common intuition can survive experimental manipulation under at least some real-world conditions.

Attention changes the quality of being together.

This seems obvious enough to make research feel unnecessary. The interesting part is why the obvious behavior is so difficult to change.

People generally know that staring at a phone while another person speaks can be rude.

They do it anyway.

That is the structure of the problem.

Etiquette is competing with infrastructure.

The phone contains obligations that can be socially legitimate in another context. Ignoring the child’s message may feel irresponsible. Ignoring the work escalation may carry consequences. Ignoring the friend in distress may violate another relationship. The person at the table is not simply choosing between present human and trivial machine. They are arbitrating among humans through a machine.

The smartphone creates social concurrency.

Before mobile communication, many relationships took turns because geography enforced turns. You were with the people in the room. Others could call the landline, but doing so was a noticeable event. Now several relationships can make simultaneous claims through private channels.

Privacy intensifies the problem.

A landline ringing at dinner interrupted everyone. A mobile message may interrupt only one person, creating an asymmetry. The phone owner knows why attention shifted. The others see only that it did.

This is how a small object produces suspicion disproportionate to the action.

What is more interesting than this?

Who is more important than me?

Why did you smile at that?

Why are you hiding the screen?

The glass is opaque from the wrong side.

Even a benign message can create an information gap inside a shared moment.

Couples adapt with rules.

No phones at dinner.

No work email after a certain hour.

Tell me if you are expecting an important call.

Put the phone on the counter.

Use it to take the photograph, then put it away.

These rules are not really about devices. They are contracts about visible priority.

A phone-free meal says: during this interval, we will not force each other to compete with unknown alternatives.

The rule works partly because it removes interpretation.

Nobody has to decide whether each glance is justified.

The device is simply outside the contest.

This is why collective rules often feel easier than individual restraint. If everyone agrees the phone is away, putting it away does not signal unavailability to one another. The group creates a local norm stronger than the ambient norm of reachability.

The difficulty comes when the outside world does not honor the local norm.

A worker whose manager expects immediate replies may feel anxious through dinner.

A parent waiting for a child may need the phone audible.

A physician on call may genuinely be on call.

The room cannot always declare sovereignty.

Glass Face Syndrome is partly the loss of small sovereign spaces.

Places where only the people present could make demands have become permeable.

The same is true of conversation itself.

A phone can settle questions instantly.

Who played that character?

What year did the song come out?

Is that story true?

How far is the moon?

The answer is usually delightful. The conversation gains factual power.

It can also lose a form of play.

Before the answer, people guessed.

They argued.

They told stories around the uncertainty.

They were wrong for a while.

Being wrong for a while is an underrated social activity.

A searchable answer can collapse a conversation before the speculation becomes interesting.

This does not mean we should preserve ignorance. It means information retrieval changes the shape of talk.

The phone is a fact-checker sitting at every table.

It is also a photographer, jukebox, translator, calendar, and address book. The conversation can become richer because of it. Someone shows the old photograph. Plays the song. Finds the poem. Pulls up the map. Calls the missing friend on video.

The device can expand the room.

The problem is not expansion.

It is uncontrolled entry.

A chosen use can serve the conversation. An unchosen interruption can displace it.

This distinction matters more than a blanket rule about phones.

A family looking together at photographs on one phone may be highly connected despite high “screen time.” Four family members each scrolling separate feeds in the same room may be less so. The screen count alone cannot tell us whether technology is social glue or social solvent.

Shared attention is the variable.

The smartphone can create or fracture it.

This becomes obvious with children.

A small child does not understand the abstract category “urgent work message.” The child sees the parent’s face leave.

Researchers studying parent technology use have used the term “technoference” for technology-based interruptions in parent-child interaction. Brandon McDaniel and Jenny Radesky have reported associations among problematic parent technology use, interruptions in family interaction, parenting stress, and child behavior. Their longitudinal work is careful about bidirectionality: difficult child behavior can increase parent stress and technology use just as parent technology interference may affect interaction.

That complexity is essential.

The exhausted parent who reaches for a phone while a toddler screams is not necessarily a victim of an app. The phone may be a coping tool. A support network may live there. A few minutes of distraction may help the parent regulate enough to respond better.

The same device can interrupt caregiving and sustain the caregiver.

Moral judgment is easy when we see only the posture.

A mother looking at a phone on a playground can be texting a friend about the child’s diagnosis, ordering medicine, answering work so she can leave early tomorrow, or scrolling because she is bored. An observer cannot know.

This is why the visual culture of phone criticism often becomes shallow. Photographs of crowds looking down are treated as self-explanatory evidence that people are disconnected.

The images prove only where eyes are pointed.

Sometimes the person behind the glass is the most important person in the user’s life.

A soldier video-calling home is not choosing technology over family.

A migrant sending money through a banking app is not absent from the real world.

A teenager messaging a suicidal friend is not failing to be present.

The phone creates remote presence precisely by producing local absence.

That trade cannot be solved by declaring one side authentic.

The important question is whether the trade is chosen.

Glass Face Syndrome appears when the local absence becomes automatic, habitual, or socially imposed rather than a deliberate exchange for something of greater value.

The phone wins by default.

This is why a glance matters.

The person across the table may not resent the message itself. They resent not knowing whether the switch was chosen.

Repeated glances can make the present relationship feel provisional.

I am with you until something better arrives.

Nobody needs to say the sentence for the nervous system to hear it.

Humans are exquisitely sensitive to divided attention. Eye contact, facial response, timing, small vocal acknowledgments, and turn-taking help us know whether another person is with us. Phone use can interrupt those signals even when the user believes they are still listening.

Multitasking feels better from the inside than it looks from the outside.

The phone owner knows they heard the words.

The speaker sees a face angled toward somewhere else.

Presence has an audience.

This may be why simply explaining the phone use can reduce some of its social cost.

“I need to check whether the babysitter wrote.”

“I’m waiting for a call from the hospital.”

“I’m putting our next meeting in the calendar.”

The sentence restores shared context. The third person at the table becomes named.

The problem was partly opacity.

Older etiquette rules developed around visible technologies. Reading a newspaper during conversation meant something because everyone knew what the object was doing. Smartphone etiquette had to evolve around an object whose purpose changes every second and whose screen is often private.

We are still writing the rules.

Should phones be face down or away?

Is checking the time rude if the clock is on the phone?

Is photographing the meal part of the meal?

Is using the phone to settle an argument helpful or disruptive?

Is a video call with someone absent an interruption or an expansion of the gathering?

The answer depends on whether the tool is serving shared attention or redirecting it.

That principle is more durable than specific etiquette.

A future device may move from phone to glasses, earbuds, watches, or ambient assistants. The social problem will remain if invisible others can continuously bid for priority over visible people.

The rectangle is only the current form.

Glass Face Syndrome is the habit of giving the remote bid a privileged hearing.

The solution is not to pretend remote relationships matter less.

It is to make priority legible again.

Sometimes the person across the table should lose.

The hospital should interrupt dinner.

The child in trouble should outrank the meeting.

The emergency should break the date.

Human life requires interruption.

What changed is that ordinary systems learned to borrow the channel designed for extraordinary importance.

The result is a table with a silent third party that represents everyone else.

Every relationship outside the room waits behind the glass.

The discipline of presence is deciding, together, when they are allowed in.

Shared attention is more fragile than shared location.

That distinction became easier to see after smartphones because people could be physically co-present while participating in different social worlds with almost no visible separation. A family can occupy one couch while each member inhabits a separate feed, conversation, game, or video. Before portable networked screens, private attention usually required more obvious equipment or distance. A book, newspaper, television, stereo, or desktop computer announced the activity to everyone else.

The phone privatizes the doorway.

Earbuds can privatize it further.

This matters for coordination because social groups rely on small signals to know when interaction is available. Two people in a room once needed relatively little negotiation to begin talking. Now each may be in a private channel whose boundaries are invisible. Interrupting someone who is staring at a phone can feel oddly uncertain.

Are they reading something important?

Working?

Watching a video?

Writing to someone?

Waiting for a reply?

The device makes solitude portable inside company.

Portable solitude is useful. Teenagers sharing a house need privacy. Partners do not need to entertain one another continuously. A crowded train becomes more bearable when everyone can create a small personal world. The ability to retreat without leaving can preserve relationships as often as it strains them.

The problem comes when retreat loses a visible beginning and end.

A book can be closed and placed on the table. A phone can be locked and still contain unfinished conversation. The person returns physically while part of attention remains elsewhere, waiting for the next cue.

This is one reason the phone can produce what feels like partial presence even after it is put down.

The interruption has an afterlife.

A difficult work message is still being mentally answered.

A social post has changed the person’s mood.

A romantic reply is being anticipated.

A headline has introduced fear.

The device is no longer active, but the remote context has entered the room.

No etiquette rule can fully prevent that. A newspaper article can alter mood too. A letter can reopen a conflict. Human beings carry thoughts across contexts.

The smartphone increases the frequency with which contexts can enter one another.

Frequency changes what relationships have to negotiate.

A useful concept here is not simply attention but repair. Conversations are full of small ruptures: someone looks away, misses a sentence, misunderstands tone, becomes distracted, checks a child, remembers an obligation. Healthy interaction repairs these ruptures constantly.

Sorry, I missed that.

Say it again.

I need thirty seconds to answer this.

That was rude; put it away.

Show me what you’re looking at.

Repair restores shared reality.

The phone becomes damaging when interruptions accumulate without repair and the interrupted person must repeatedly pretend not to notice. Resentment then attaches not to any one glance but to the pattern of being demoted without acknowledgment.

This helps explain why shared phone use can feel different from private phone use even when the same screen is involved. If one partner says, “Look at this photograph,” the device becomes part of joint attention. If the partner silently turns toward the same screen to read an unrelated thread, the device competes with joint attention.

The pixels are not the variable.

The social orientation is.

Research on phubbing is beginning to reflect this distinction. The literature has repeatedly found negative associations between perceived partner phone distraction and relationship satisfaction, but newer reviews also warn that the field relies heavily on cross-sectional self-report, overlapping definitions, and measures that can pathologize ordinary technology use. That methodological caution improves the argument rather than weakening it.

We do not need to prove that every phone glance damages intimacy.

We need to understand why some glances communicate exclusion and others do not.

One clue is responsiveness.

People want evidence that a partner noticed what they said, understood the emotional weight, and can be reached in return. A phone can reduce that evidence when it captures eyes and timing. It can also increase responsiveness by giving someone access to information, translation, photographs, or distant family that enriches the conversation.

The same tool moves both directions.

This is why universal etiquette fails.

“Never use a phone at dinner” is simple enough to enforce and may be valuable for a household. It is not a complete theory of presence. A family could obey the rule and remain emotionally absent. Another family could spend an hour sharing videos, photographs, maps, and messages on one screen while having a vivid conversation.

The more durable principle is intentional shared priority.

Who or what is this moment for?

If the answer is the people here, remote claims should need a reason to enter.

If the answer is partly the person on the video call, the room can expand deliberately.

If someone is on call for an emergency, the exception can be named.

Once priority is explicit, the phone stops operating as a mysterious rival.

This becomes more important as devices become less visible. Smart glasses may allow a person to receive information without looking down. Earbuds can deliver a message while eye contact remains intact. AI assistants may participate silently in conversation through transcription or prompts.

The old visual rule—eyes off the phone—will no longer be enough.

We will need a social rule about computational participation.

Is this conversation being recorded?

Is an assistant listening?

Are messages appearing in the field of view?

Is someone else remotely present?

The smartphone era gave us an unusually visible version of a problem that may soon become invisible.

The third person at the table used to glow.

The next one may not.

That makes the lesson urgent.

Presence cannot be inferred from posture alone.

It has to be governed by trust, disclosure, and shared norms about who gets access to the moment.

The dinner table was never sacred because glass was absent.

It was sacred, when it was, because people agreed for a while about who was in the room.
