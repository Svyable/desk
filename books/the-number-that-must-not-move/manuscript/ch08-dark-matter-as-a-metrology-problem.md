# 8. Dark Matter as a Metrology Problem

Dark matter is usually introduced gravitationally. Galaxies rotate too fast. Clusters bend too much light. The cosmic microwave background wants more gravitating matter than luminous matter supplies.

But an ultralight field would not behave like a bag of invisible billiard balls. It could act as a coherent classical field over macroscopic scales. If it couples weakly to Standard Model sectors, local values of effective constants may oscillate as the field oscillates.

That converts dark matter into a frequency-modulation problem.

Suppose a scalar field couples to electromagnetism. Then alpha can acquire a tiny periodic component. Clock transitions with different alpha sensitivities acquire different periodic frequency shifts. If the field couples to electron or quark masses, other ratios move. A network of clocks can search for common spectral lines or transient signatures.

The signal model determines the experiment. A virialized ultralight field has a characteristic coherence time and linewidth set by the Galactic velocity distribution. A topological defect would look more like a transient front sweeping through geographically separated sensors. A compact clump would have another signature. One does not search for 'dark matter' in the abstract. One searches for predicted covariance.

That phrase—predicted covariance—is the hinge.

A laboratory disturbance may move several nearby clocks together. GPS or fiber-transfer faults may produce network correlations. A real field crossing Earth should produce timing relationships tied to geography and propagation assumptions. Different clock species should respond according to their sensitivity vectors, not according to which laboratory shares an air-conditioning system.

NIST and others now openly describe atomic and nuclear clocks as dark-matter sensors. The idea is no longer fringe. But maturity should make the field more demanding, not less.

Every claimed candidate must answer three questions. Did the raw ratios move? Did they move with the species pattern predicted by the coupling? Did geographically separated instruments move with the spacetime pattern predicted by the field?

A positive answer to only the first is an anomaly. Two is interesting. Three begins to look like physics.
