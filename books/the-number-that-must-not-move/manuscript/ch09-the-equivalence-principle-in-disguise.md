# 9. The Equivalence Principle in Disguise

A varying constant is rarely allowed to vary alone.

If a new scalar field changes electromagnetic binding energy, electron mass or nuclear binding, then different materials can acquire different effective couplings to that field. Composition begins to matter gravitationally. What started as spectroscopy can therefore leak into tests of the equivalence principle.

This connection is deep but model-dependent. It is too easy to say that any varying alpha automatically implies a particular free-fall violation. Bekenstein showed long ago that cancellations and coupling structure can complicate that simple inference. Modern reviews treat variation of constants, local position invariance and universality of free fall as intertwined probes rather than interchangeable ones.

The useful picture is a triangle.

One corner is clock comparison: does a frequency ratio depend on time or gravitational potential? Another is free fall: do different compositions accelerate identically? The third is cosmology: does the inferred constant differ across redshift or location? A scalar model that tries to explain a hint in one corner must survive constraints from the other two.

This is why varying-constant searches are more powerful than their niche reputation suggests. They do not merely ask whether 1/137 was once 1/136.999. They interrogate the architecture by which new fields could couple to matter without already having been seen.

Screening mechanisms, environment-dependent couplings and ultralight fields are all attempts to occupy the shrinking space between these experiments. A model may evade laboratory fifth-force tests by screening in dense environments while evolving cosmologically. It may couple mostly to photons and weakly to baryonic mass. It may oscillate too rapidly for geological probes but sit squarely in the clock band.

The correct product of an experimental program is therefore not one bound. It is an exclusion surface across couplings, masses, timescales and environments.

Physics becomes interesting when the same hypothetical field has nowhere to hide consistently.
