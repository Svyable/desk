# 1. The Ore That Remembered

The first clue did not arrive from a particle accelerator. It arrived as an accounting problem.

In 1972 technicians associated with the French nuclear industry noticed that uranium from Gabon carried too little uranium-235. Natural uranium has an isotopic signature so regular that enrichment plants treat deviation as evidence requiring explanation. The missing fraction was not missing because somebody had secretly enriched the ore. It had been burned.

At Oklo, roughly two billion years ago, groundwater, uranium concentration and the then-higher natural abundance of uranium-235 combined to produce self-sustaining fission. Multiple reaction zones have since been identified. Nature had built a reactor without pressure vessels, control rods or an operating license, run it intermittently, shut it down and buried the spent fuel inside geology.

The part physicists should find unnerving is not that natural reactors can exist. Once one does the neutronics, that is less miraculous than it sounds. The unnerving part is that a reactor is also a spectrometer.

Neutrons do not get captured by nuclei with equal enthusiasm at every energy. Some nuclei possess sharp resonances. Samarium-149 is famous here because its neutron-capture cross section near thermal energies is dominated by a resonance around a tenth of an electron volt. Shift the underlying nuclear level and the reactor's burnup changes. Two billion years later, isotopic abundances retain a distorted but recoverable record of that event.

This is where the story becomes dangerous to sloppy thought. One does not simply measure samarium today, infer an ancient resonance and divide by two billion years to obtain a changing fine-structure constant. The reactor had a temperature. It had a neutron spectrum that was not exactly Maxwellian. It evolved as fuel burned. Water moderated neutrons and boiled away. Fission products accumulated. Isotopes migrated. Nuclear theory is needed to connect a resonance-energy shift to changes in electromagnetic and strong-interaction parameters.

Every one of those complications weakens the fairy-tale version of Oklo and strengthens the scientific version. A measurement is not made rigorous by having fewer nuisance parameters. It becomes rigorous when the nuisance parameters are named, bounded and forced to compete with the signal.

Modern analyses generally find no evidence that alpha was different at Oklo. Depending on assumptions and nuclear modeling, the limits are extremely tight over geological time. The point is not the last decimal place. The point is that a rock can constrain a field theory.

A uranium deposit became critical before nervous systems existed. It then preserved a statement about whether a dimensionless coupling of electromagnetism could have changed appreciably since then. The universe did not know it was doing metrology. That may be the most useful property a natural experiment can have.
