# 6. The Dipole That Would Change Everything

The most provocative map in this subject was assembled from spectra that were never taken to make a map of fundamental physics.

The large quasar sample behind the reported fine-structure-constant dipole combined 293 absorption-system measurements from Keck/HIRES in Hawaii and VLT/UVES in Chile. The sky coverage was valuable precisely because the two observatories looked at different parts of the sky. That also created the problem. Telescope, hemisphere, observing program, calibration history and celestial direction were not cleanly independent variables.

The result was difficult to ignore. Analyses of the combined archive reported a large-scale angular pattern in alpha at roughly four-sigma significance. If real, the fine-structure constant would not merely have drifted with cosmic time. It would have depended on direction. A number written into electromagnetism would have acquired geography.

But the measurement is not made by reading alpha from a dial. It is inferred from tiny relative shifts among absorption lines with different sensitivities to alpha. A wavelength scale that stretches slightly across an echelle spectrum can therefore manufacture part of the same pattern.

That possibility stopped being hypothetical. Dedicated comparisons of HIRES and UVES against asteroid and solar-twin spectra found long-range wavelength distortions across much of both instruments' histories, as well as smaller intra-order distortions. Whitmore and Murphy found typical long-range slopes large enough to move inferred values of alpha by several parts per million. Many of the old quasar exposures lacked the coeval calibration observations needed to reconstruct those distortions later. The archive had preserved the photons better than it had preserved the experiment.

This does not turn the original result into fraud or foolishness. The observations predated the discovery of an instrumental effect important at exactly the precision the analysis was trying to reach. It does change what the old significance can mean. A statistical preference calculated under an incomplete systematic model is not four-sigma evidence against a systematic nobody had yet measured.

Newer observations make the contrast unusually clean. Purpose-built measurements, roughly thirty rather than nearly three hundred, give a weighted mean consistent with no variation. The ESPRESSO observation of the bright quasar HE 0515-4414 reported Δα/α = 1.3 ± 1.3 statistical ± 0.4 systematic parts per million. Its statistical uncertainty from one absorption system was already comparable to the ensemble precision of the older large samples, while a laser-frequency-comb calibration was designed to remove the long-range wavelength problem from the measurement.

There is an attractive temptation here to declare the case closed. That is too neat as well.

The dedicated sample is much smaller and its celestial coverage is poorer. An ESO Large Programme created specifically to improve fundamental-constant measurements devoted about forty VLT nights to the problem, yet its target list was not optimized to test the reported dipole and included no sightline toward its northern pole. Some later work has also disputed whether simple distortion models can faithfully reconstruct the complicated mix of wavelength settings used in the archival spectra. A known systematic can weaken an anomaly without automatically proving that every feature of it was caused by that systematic.

The asymmetry in the evidence nevertheless matters. The old archive cannot now acquire calibration exposures that were never taken. New spectrographs can acquire them prospectively. A serious dipole test therefore has to make telescope and sky direction cross each other instead of travel together: overlapping celestial regions from independent instruments, calibration tied to laser frequency combs or similarly traceable standards, absorber and model choices fixed before the answer is known, and deliberate observations near both claimed poles.

Then the geometry begins doing useful work. A celestial signal should remain fixed on the sky while the instruments change. A detector or wavelength-calibration distortion should follow the apparatus. Absorber chemistry and isotopic structure should follow species and environment rather than a cosmic axis. These explanations do not merely tell different stories; with the right observing program they predict different patterns in the data.

The dipole is therefore more interesting now than when it could be summarized as either a four-sigma discovery or an embarrassing calibration error. It is a case in which the first experiment cannot be rerun, because part of the experiment was never recorded. The next one can be designed so that a cosmic direction and a spectrograph direction are finally forced to part company.
