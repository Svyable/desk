# 4. The Reactor Without an Operator

Oklo is often treated as a paragraph in papers about varying constants. It deserves to be treated as an experimental apparatus.

Reaction zone RZ2, for example, can be described with uranium content, geometry, moderator fraction, neutron spectrum, operating temperature and burnup history. The reactor likely cycled as water entered, moderated the neutron population, heated and boiled, suppressing criticality until cooling allowed water to return. Even that picturesque description is an inference assembled from geological and reactor evidence rather than a surviving instrument log.

The reactor's lack of an operator is a feature. No one tuned it to produce a preferred fundamental-physics result. The isotopic archive existed before the hypothesis was fashionable. That gives Oklo an epistemic quality that modern experiments rarely have: the detector could not be optimized after seeing the anomaly.

But geology introduces another kind of researcher degrees of freedom. Which samples count as least disturbed? Which temperature proxies are trusted? How is post-reactor migration modeled? Which resonance data are adopted? How is the neutron spectrum reconstructed? A natural experiment eliminates some forms of bias and creates others.

The most useful way to think about Oklo is as a chain of physical custody.

At one end sits present-day mass spectrometry. At the other end sits a hypothetical variation in a coupling constant. Between them lie mineral transport, reactor operation, isotope production and destruction, neutron transport, capture resonances and nuclear structure. Every link should have an explicit transfer function and uncertainty.

This chain-of-custody language is borrowed from forensics because the epistemic problem is similar. An extraordinary conclusion can fail because the object changed before measurement, because the measurement was miscalibrated, or because the mapping from measurement to cause was not unique.

The result so far is conservative: Oklo strongly constrains large secular changes in alpha over roughly two billion years, with exact limits depending on modeling choices. A 2023 analysis argued that relative change over about 1.9 billion years is below the 0.01-parts-per-million scale under its assumptions. That is breathtaking if taken seriously and misleading if treated as theory-free.

Physicists should want both reactions at once.

The breathtaking part is that nuclear waste from a Precambrian accident can compete with laboratory precision in testing new fields. The misleading part is that the final bound compresses geology, neutronics and nuclear theory into one line.

The lesson is broader than Oklo. Precision is not the same thing as cleanliness. The older the detector, the more of the universe has touched it.
