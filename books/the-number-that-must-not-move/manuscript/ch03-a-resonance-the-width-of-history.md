# 3. A Resonance the Width of History

Samarium-149 makes Oklo useful because nuclear physics contains accidents.

The nucleus does not arrange its excited states for our convenience. Levels land where many-body dynamics put them. Occasionally one lands very close to the energy of a thermal neutron. Then a tiny displacement in that level can produce a large change in capture probability.

The celebrated Sm-149 resonance near 97 meV is one of those accidents. Its energy is tiny compared with the MeV scales involved in nuclear binding and Coulomb energies. That disparity creates leverage but also danger. If the resonance energy is a residual left after large contributions nearly cancel, then small changes in underlying couplings can be magnified. Yet extracting the magnification requires nuclear structure modeling that is itself imperfect.

This is the recurring pattern of the book: sensitivity is often born from cancellation, and cancellation is where theory errors become ambitious.

Damour and Dyson's 1996 reanalysis made the modern form of the Oklo argument influential by connecting isotopic data, reactor temperature uncertainty and nuclear Coulomb energies to a bound on alpha variation. Later reactor calculations improved the neutron-spectrum treatment. Petrov and collaborators modeled Oklo zone RZ2 using reactor-physics tools and found no evidence for a changed resonance, obtaining a shift range measured in tens of millielectronvolts and correspondingly stringent long-term limits on alpha drift under the adopted mapping.

But 'under the adopted mapping' belongs in the sentence.

The ancient reactor did not record alpha directly. It recorded isotopic outcomes. Those outcomes constrain effective neutron-capture cross sections. Cross sections constrain resonance placement once the spectrum and reactor state are modeled. Resonance placement constrains combinations of nuclear parameters. Only then does a model connect those parameters to alpha and possibly to strong-sector quantities.

There are at least four places to be fooled: isotope migration can alter the apparent burnup; reactor temperature alters spectral averaging; burnup changes composition and neutron flux over time; and the nuclear sensitivity coefficient may not be known as cleanly as the final alpha bound suggests.

This does not destroy Oklo. It tells us how to use it.

A good future analysis would not publish a single number labeled 'Oklo bound.' It would publish a probability distribution propagated from geology through reactor modeling through nuclear response through particle-physics interpretation. It would show which uncertainties are empirical and which are theoretical. It would include samarium, gadolinium, lutetium and other available constraints without pretending they are independent when they share reactor history.

The resonance is narrow. History is wide. The measurement lives in the conversion between them.
