# 10. The Molecule Remembers Mass

Atoms are excellent clocks because electrons are light and quantum mechanics is obliging. Molecules add another ruler because nuclei move too.

Electronic transitions, vibrational transitions and rotational transitions scale differently with the electron-to-proton mass ratio. That gives molecular spectra leverage on whether mass ratios have changed across cosmic time.

The phrase 'proton mass changed' is again too crude. The proton mass is largely QCD binding energy, not simply the sum of quark masses. A variation in a deeper scalar coupling can move electron mass, quark masses and the QCD scale in related or unrelated ways depending on the model. Molecular spectroscopy measures combinations.

Ammonia, methanol and molecular hydrogen have been used because some transitions possess enhanced sensitivity to the mass ratio. Radio observations can probe cold molecular clouds with exquisite spectral resolution. Optical and ultraviolet observations reach high redshift. Laboratory clocks and molecular spectroscopy then cover different epochs and environments.

Environment is not a nuisance here; it can be the signal.

Some scalar-field models produce density-dependent effective values. If so, comparing molecular spectra in diffuse interstellar clouds with terrestrial laboratory values tests something different from comparing two laboratory clocks over a year. A null cosmological drift does not automatically kill an environmental variation. A null laboratory drift does not automatically kill a field frozen locally but evolving cosmologically.

This is why the field needs a map rather than a leaderboard of 'best constraints.' Each measurement occupies a region of timescale, gravitational potential, density, redshift and coupling sensitivity.

The molecules remember mass because their internal motions divide the problem into different physical scales. The trick is learning to read the memory without pretending one spectral line carries a unique translation into fundamental theory.
