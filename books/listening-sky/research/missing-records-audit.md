# Missing-records audit: absence is not a motive

This is a bounded primary-record/counterevidence pass for *Listening Sky*. It addresses a dangerous evidentiary shortcut in any Roswell/secrecy narrative: treating missing government records as though the fact of their absence establishes why they disappeared.

## What GAO actually found

The July 28, 1995 General Accounting Office report, *Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico* (GAO/NSIAD-95-187), is unusually useful because it records both survival and loss.

GAO found that Roswell Army Air Field administrative records covering March 1945 through December 1949 and outgoing messages covering October 1946 through December 1949 had been destroyed. The document-disposition form did not identify who destroyed them, when they were destroyed, or the authority under which the destruction occurred.

That is a real archival gap. It is not, by itself, evidence of a Roswell-specific purge.

GAO also reported the Chief Archivist's contextual judgment: in his experience, many Air Force organizational records from the period were destroyed without a citation to the governing disposition authority. GAO checked records-control forms for other destroyed records, including RAAF outgoing messages for 1950, and said that comparison supported the archivist's view.

This comparison matters. An unexplained destruction notation is evidence that records are gone and that the surviving disposition paperwork is inadequate. A broader pattern of similarly documented destruction weakens the inference that the Roswell-era gap was uniquely engineered to erase the July 1947 event.

## What survived

The same GAO search recovered two contemporaneous records directly relevant to the event.

The combined 509th Bomb Group/RAAF history for July 1947 said the public-information office was occupied with inquiries about the reported “flying disc” and recorded that the object “turned out to be a radar tracking balloon.” The commanding officer certified the history as a complete and accurate account of RAAF activities for the month.

GAO also authenticated the July 8, 1947 FBI teletype. It records an Eighth Air Force headquarters official describing a recovered hexagonal-shaped disc suspended from a large balloon by cable, being sent to Wright Field, and resembling a high-altitude weather balloon with a radar reflector.

Neither record proves the later MOGUL reconstruction. Both are important because they prevent the archive from being described as simply blank until the Air Force wrote its retrospective account in the 1990s.

## The reporting-rule trap

GAO found that 1947 Army regulations required air-accident reports to be maintained permanently. It located four military-aircraft accidents in New Mexico during July 1947, all after July 8. But Air Force officials told GAO there was no corresponding requirement to prepare an accident report for a weather-balloon crash.

This creates a useful falsification boundary. “There is no Roswell air-accident report” has different evidentiary force under two models:

- if the recovered object was a military aircraft accident of the regulated sort, a missing permanent report is potentially significant;
- if it was an unmanned balloon mishap, absence of such a report is expected under the reporting rules GAO reconstructed.

The manuscript should not use the missing accident report as evidence for or against MOGUL without first stating which reporting regime the candidate event would have triggered.

## Strongest counterargument

Routine record destruction is not proof that no targeted destruction occurred. A Roswell-specific purge could be hidden inside a larger administrative-loss pattern, and the disposition forms' failure to state authority leaves a genuine provenance defect. Nor does the survival of two mundane contemporaneous records establish that every important record survived; selective survival is possible.

The correct conclusion is therefore narrower than either pole. The public record establishes an archival gap whose cause is unresolved. It also supplies a documented base-rate problem for interpreting that gap: comparable Air Force records were destroyed with similarly incomplete disposition citations.

Any stronger claim needs evidence of selectivity—an unusual destruction date, instruction, retention-rule violation, subject-specific gap, actor, or chain of custody—not merely the existence of missing files.

## Narrative opportunity

This can give the later “Government Explains Itself” chapter a much better scene than a generic discussion of missing records.

In 1995, congressional investigators went looking for a paper trail and discovered that some of the paper really was gone. That is exactly where a secrecy book is tempted to become careless. The missing files look like evidence of concealment because the story already contains concealment.

Then the archivist complicates the inference: this was how a great deal of Air Force paperwork from the period disappeared from the historical record.

The tension is not “cover-up versus no cover-up.” It is whether an archival absence is selective enough to carry a motive. That is a harder question and a more useful one.

## Publication holds

- Do not say Roswell records were “destroyed without authorization.” GAO said the disposition form did not properly indicate the authority; that is not the same claim.
- Do not say the missing RAAF records prove a Roswell cover-up or targeted purge.
- Do not say the records were routinely destroyed in a way that proves innocence. GAO's comparison supplies a conventional explanation, not certainty about this particular loss.
- Do not imply the 1947 record is empty. The 509th/RAAF monthly history and July 8 FBI teletype survive.
- Do not treat absence of a military-aircraft accident report as anomalous until the manuscript establishes that the candidate object would have triggered that reporting requirement.

## Extraction burden for the archival chapter

For every important missing-record claim, record:

1. exact record series and date span;
2. governing retention/disposition rule at the relevant time;
3. date and authority of destruction if known;
4. whether neighboring years/units show comparable losses;
5. whether copies should have existed at another command or agency;
6. what contemporaneous records survive from the same information flow;
7. what proposition the missing record would actually be expected to contain under each competing model.

This turns “missing records” from atmosphere into a testable archival claim.
