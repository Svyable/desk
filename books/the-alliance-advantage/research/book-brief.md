# Book Brief

## Commercial proposition

A systems book about alliances. Instead of asking whether NATO or U.S. alliances are politically strong, it asks whether they can actually move data, weapons, software, intelligence, supplies, and decisions across sovereign boundaries fast enough to matter.

## Reader promise

The reader should finish able to see alliance power inside interfaces and permissions, not only summit declarations.

## Target scale

20 chapters plus prologue. Complete draft target: 75,000–90,000 manuscript words.

## Narrative method

Open chapters with concrete coordination failures or successes: a munition that can be cross-loaded, a data feed that cannot be released, a bridge that cannot carry a vehicle, a factory waiting for demand certainty, a cloud service crossing classification boundaries, or an exercise in which several countries finally share one picture.

## AI-era relevance

AI makes data interoperability, provenance, secure compute, shared evaluation, software delivery, and machine-to-machine interfaces operational questions. The book treats AI as a stress test of alliance architecture.

## Kennedy School use

Students can design an alliance for one scenario and discover that political agreement is only the first layer. Assign teams to solve data, industrial, legal, logistics, and command seams, then compare the resulting coalition with a centralized rival.

## Central tensions

- sovereignty versus pooling
- speed versus consultation
- standardization versus innovation
- security versus sharing
- redundancy versus efficiency
- national industry versus common markets
- centralization versus resilience
