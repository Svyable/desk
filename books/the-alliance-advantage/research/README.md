# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

The Alliance Advantage tests whether the strategic value of alliances increasingly depends on converting distributed capability into usable network power through interoperability across technology, operations, logistics, industry, and political authority.

It does not assume alliances are inherently superior to centralized states. The mechanism must be demonstrated.

## Core mechanisms

1. **Conversion:** political commitments become power only through operational systems.
2. **Interoperability:** common standards at selected seams multiply the value of independently owned assets.
3. **Federation:** systems can remain sovereign while exchanging data and services through agreed rules.
4. **Industrial depth:** collective capability depends on scalable production, repair, licensing, and supply.
5. **Innovation diffusion:** alliance advantage grows when useful technology can move from one node to many without years of reintegration.
6. **Trust and permissions:** classification, identity, authority, and legal rules are technical constraints with strategic effects.

## Falsification and counter-evidence

The thesis weakens if alliance coordination costs systematically exceed the value of distributed capability, if common standards suppress innovation, or if dependence on a dominant ally produces brittle single points of failure. Preserve cases where centralized competitors field integrated systems faster.

Do not equate strategy documents with implementation. Measure exercises, deployment timelines, data sharing, production rates, repair, acquisition, and actual cross-national use.

## Chapter evidence map

Chapters 1–6 cover the network, interoperability, data, AI, intelligence, and cloud. Chapters 7–10 cover logistics, munitions, standards, and industry. Chapters 11–14 test NATO, Indo-Pacific networks, AUKUS, and innovation. Chapters 15–18 examine sovereignty costs, vulnerabilities, coalition speed, and the overall alliance advantage.

## Research discipline

Prefer alliance strategies, standards, exercise after-action material, procurement and production data, defense-ministry records, industrial filings, treaty texts, and interviews with operators and integrators. Distinguish announced interoperability from demonstrated interoperability.
