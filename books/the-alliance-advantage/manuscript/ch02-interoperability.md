# Interoperability

Two radios can be excellent radios and still fail at the one moment an alliance needs them to work together.

One may use a waveform the other cannot receive. One may be cleared for a network the other cannot enter. The operators may speak the same language and still use different procedures for identifying a target, requesting support, or reporting uncertainty. A message can arrive in time and remain unusable because the receiving system cannot parse the field in which the sender placed the information.

Nothing is broken in isolation.

The connection is broken.

That is the distinctive engineering problem of an alliance.

A centralized military can impose a common architecture. It can decide that units will buy the same equipment, use the same doctrine, accept the same security rules, and upgrade on a common schedule. Even then, integration is difficult. An alliance adds sovereignty to the technical problem. Each member has its own legislature, budget, procurement system, industrial base, classification rules, military traditions, vendors, legal constraints, political timetable, and tolerance for dependence.

The result is a force made of capable parts that do not automatically form one capability.

Interoperability is the work of closing that gap.

The word sounds narrower than it is. It is often treated as a technical property: can system A exchange data with system B? That is necessary. It is not enough.

A useful definition has several layers.

The first is physical. Can the equipment connect at all? Frequencies, plugs, power, spectrum, interfaces, ammunition dimensions, fueling systems, transport clearances, runway requirements, and network access all live here.

The second is syntactic. Can one system read what the other sends? A field called latitude must mean latitude. A message format must arrive in a structure the receiving software recognizes. Time stamps, units, identifiers, and metadata must survive the crossing.

The third is semantic. Do the two sides mean the same thing by the data they exchange? A track labeled hostile, uncertain, confirmed, priority, or degraded is not useful if the receiving organization attaches a different operational meaning to the label.

The fourth is procedural. What is someone allowed to do with the information? Can a pilot act on a target generated by an allied sensor? Can a commander redirect an allied unit? Who approves release of a classified image? What happens when the machine says yes but national rules say no?

The fifth is temporal. Can all of this happen fast enough to matter?

A system that interoperates after three days of manual conversion may be perfectly adequate for logistics planning and useless for missile defense.

The sixth is political. Will governments continue to accept the dependence created by the connection?

This last layer is easy to neglect because it is not visible in a technical diagram. It can be the most important one.

A country that relies on another ally for software updates, satellite access, cryptographic keys, spare parts, targeting data, or cloud services has not merely achieved interoperability. It has accepted a relationship of dependence. That dependence may be strategically sensible. It may also become controversial the moment national interests diverge.

Interoperability therefore cannot be designed as though sovereignty were a bug.

Sovereignty is one of the requirements.

NATO’s recent digital strategy makes this explicit in a way older conversations about common equipment often did not. The alliance describes its desired digital backbone as a federation of networks and systems, not one uniform machine. It calls for data sharing across national, organizational, operational, and security boundaries while retaining national regulation and data sovereignty. Its implementation strategy identifies digital interoperability as a separate strategic deliverable rather than assuming connection will emerge automatically from modernization.

That is a revealing institutional choice.

Modernization can make interoperability worse.

If thirty countries independently replace old systems with newer proprietary ones, the alliance can become more advanced and less connected at the same time. Each member improves locally. The seams multiply globally.

Software intensifies the problem because versions move at different speeds.

A mechanical interface can remain stable for decades. Software dependencies can change in weeks. One ally patches a vulnerability. Another delays the update because a mission system has not completed certification. A third adopts a new data schema. A fourth integrates an AI service that requires metadata the others never collected. The alliance now has systems that were interoperable last year and only partially interoperable today.

This is why NATO’s 2026 implementation strategy adopts a rolling-horizon approach rather than pretending digital transformation can be specified once and finished. The strategy extends planning through 2030 while accepting that successive iterations will have to absorb new technologies and lessons.

Interoperability is maintenance.

It is not a procurement milestone.

The same lesson appears in industry.

A standard has little strategic value if it lives only in a document repository. It becomes valuable when procurement contracts require it, vendors implement it, certification tests it, exercises expose its weaknesses, and operators know what to do when it fails.

NATO’s 2026 industry strategy accordingly pushes interoperability through the systems lifecycle. It calls for standards in contracts, greater mutual recognition of certifications, industry participation in trials and exercises, modularity, open architectures, and the adoption of useful non-NATO standards where appropriate.

The phrase “where appropriate” matters.

An alliance should not standardize everything.

That would be impossible and often undesirable. Diversity can create resilience. Different suppliers reduce dependence on one vendor. Different national approaches can generate competing ideas. A vulnerability in one architecture need not become a vulnerability everywhere. Smaller allies may adopt commercial technology faster than a central bureaucracy can approve it.

The strategic question is where commonality produces the largest network gain.

Standards are most valuable at seams.

A port does not need every allied truck to be identical. It needs to know what can be unloaded, fueled, routed, tracked, and repaired.

An intelligence network does not require every country to collect information with the same sensors. It needs common enough identity, metadata, security, and handling rules that useful information can cross the boundary.

An autonomous undersea vehicle does not have to be built by the same company in three countries. It may need a common control architecture or interface so allied operators can coordinate several vehicles in one mission.

This is why AUKUS is a useful smaller laboratory.

Australia, the United Kingdom, and the United States begin with unusually deep defense and intelligence relationships. Pillar II cooperation has focused on advanced capabilities including artificial intelligence, autonomy, electronic warfare, cyber, quantum technologies, and undersea systems. Recent work on uncrewed undersea systems has emphasized shared standards and common control approaches precisely because the countries want independently developed machines to operate as a combined system.

The interesting unit is not the vehicle.

It is the interface.

If one country can build a better sensor and another a better vehicle, interoperability allows the alliance to capture both improvements without waiting for one prime contractor to own the entire stack.

That is the upside of modularity.

The downside is integration burden.

Open architectures are often described as though openness removes complexity. It relocates complexity. Someone still has to define the interface, govern changes, test compatibility, manage security, settle disputes over intellectual property, and decide how much variation the standard can tolerate before it stops being a standard.

A successful interface becomes an institution.

It needs a change process.

This is especially true with artificial intelligence because models are probabilistic systems rather than fixed components.

Suppose several allies want to share AI-assisted targeting or intelligence workflows. Technical connection is the easiest part. The harder questions come immediately.

Are the models trained on comparable data?

Do they use the same labels?

What does confidence mean in each system?

How are model versions recorded?

Can one country inspect another country’s model output without access to the training data?

How are sensitive sources protected?

Who is responsible if one model’s output is transformed by another model before reaching the decision maker?

Can a national commander reject an allied model recommendation without losing access to the rest of the network?

Interoperability in AI therefore includes epistemology.

The allies have to understand enough about how one another’s systems know what they claim to know.

This requirement will make provenance and evaluation standards strategically important. A shared data field may eventually need to carry not only a result but the model version, source lineage, confidence convention, classification, review status, and restrictions on downstream use.

That sounds bureaucratic.

It is bureaucracy in service of speed.

A system can move quickly in a crisis only when the permissions and meanings were negotiated before the crisis.

The alternative is improvisation under pressure.

Military history contains many versions of this problem. Coalitions arrive with nominally compatible forces and discover that logistics, doctrine, communications, or command relationships are not as common as planners assumed. The lesson is often described as a need for more exercises.

Exercises help because they turn hidden incompatibility into visible failure.

A unit cannot enter a network.

A credential is rejected.

A fuel contract does not transfer.

A message arrives but the recipient does not know whether it authorizes action.

A cyber rule prevents the installation of a tool the exercise requires.

A national caveat appears only after the plan depends on the capability.

These are not embarrassing side effects of training.

They are the reason to train.

An alliance that never fails during exercises is probably testing the wrong things.

The most valuable interoperability test is not a demonstration in which engineers prepare every interface in advance. It is an operational event that introduces change, partial failure, missing data, degraded communications, and units that did not rehearse together perfectly.

Compatibility under ideal conditions is a product demo.

Interoperability under degraded conditions is a military capability.

This distinction matters for cyber resilience.

A common network can produce enormous efficiency and a common attack surface. If every ally depends on the same identity provider, cloud region, satellite service, software library, or vendor update mechanism, an adversary may be able to attack the link that created the alliance’s advantage.

The answer is not to abandon common systems.

It is to design graceful degradation.

Can national forces continue operating if the federation is partially unavailable?

Can data be routed through another ally?

Can a unit fall back to a lower-bandwidth mode?

Can credentials be verified offline?

Can humans understand what the automated system was doing well enough to continue manually for a time?

Resilience is interoperability with failure included in the specification.

There is a political equivalent.

Coalitions rarely agree perfectly. A member may withhold a capability, impose a caveat, deny overflight, limit data sharing, or refuse participation in one mission while supporting another. An architecture that assumes unanimous political participation in every function will be brittle.

The network must be able to operate with partial membership.

This is another reason federation matters.

A strong alliance is not one in which every member does everything.

It is one in which the contribution of each member can be added without forcing every other member to make the same political choice.

That design creates difficult governance questions. It can also make the coalition more durable because disagreement does not automatically destroy the whole system.

The problem resembles the internet again, but only to a point.

Internet protocols do not ask whether a packet’s destination serves national policy. Military networks do. Political authority sits inside every technical layer.

A data exchange may require a treaty.

A software module may require an export license.

A maintenance arrangement may require parliamentary approval.

A shared weapon may depend on a supplier whose government can stop delivery.

A standard can become a market advantage for firms that helped write it.

Technical architecture is political economy.

That is why interoperability deserves a chapter of its own rather than appearing as a supporting word in alliance strategy.

It is the mechanism by which promises acquire usable form.

Article 5 does not need every ally to own the same military.

It needs enough connections that the capabilities they do own can reinforce one another before the crisis is decided.

The AI age raises the stakes because the value of software, data, and models can decay quickly when they cannot cross a boundary. A brilliant national capability that takes two years to integrate into allied operations may matter less than a merely good capability that can be adopted safely across the network in two months.

Speed of diffusion becomes part of deterrence.

That changes what alliance advantage means.

The strongest alliance may not be the one with the largest combined inventory on paper. It may be the one that can turn a discovery in one node into usable capacity across many nodes without first building one centralized machine.

The test is not whether the systems are identical.

The test is whether they can arrive different and still fight, repair, decide, and adapt together.

Interoperability is what makes the difference between a coalition of inventories and a network of power.
