## Note on sources and method

The manuscript treats interoperability as an empirical problem rather than an alliance slogan. Official strategies establish institutional intent and architecture. Exercise reports establish that particular forces trained together under stated conditions. Inspector-general material, production data, and industrial reporting help test whether political commitments can be converted into usable capacity.

Those categories are not interchangeable. A strategy does not prove implementation. An exercise does not prove wartime performance. Announced factory capacity is not the same as demonstrated output. A common technical standard does not erase classification, export-control, procurement, sovereignty, or command constraints.

Where the book moves from documented systems to the broader claim that networked alliances can outperform centralized competitors, that move is analysis. The argument remains open to counterevidence: coordination costs, vendor concentration, national vetoes, divergent threat perceptions, slow procurement, and the possibility that centralized systems can integrate or decide faster.

## Source notes by part

**Network and digital architecture.** NATO digital strategy, digital-backbone and data-strategy material, revised AI strategy, and public descriptions of federated infrastructure support the discussion of common seams, data exchange, cloud, and model interoperability. These documents are treated as evidence of design and implementation priorities, not proof that every ally has reached the stated target state.

**Logistics, munitions, and industry.** U.S. inspector-general work on 155-mm ammunition, European production programs, plant-level reporting, military-mobility projects, and related public material support the book's argument that nominal capacity matters less than usable throughput. Current production and ramp-rate claims require rechecking near publication.

**Indo-Pacific networks and AUKUS.** U.S., U.K., Australian, NATO-partner, and exercise sources support discussion of multinational training, AUKUS Pillar II, shared standards, uncrewed systems, and deeper technical integration. Pillar I and Pillar II are kept distinct, and announced future capability is not treated as already fielded.

**Innovation and sovereignty.** DIANA, industry-cooperation, export-control, and licensing material support the final chapters on diffusion, permissions, dependence, and the political price of integration.
