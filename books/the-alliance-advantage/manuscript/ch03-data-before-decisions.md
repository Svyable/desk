# Data Before Decisions

Artificial intelligence makes data sound like fuel.

Collect more of it.

Feed the machine.

Get better decisions.

Alliances discover quickly that data is less like fuel than territory.

It has owners, borders, permissions, histories, dependencies, and political meaning.

A radar track may be generated by one country, enriched by another, fused with intelligence from a third, stored in infrastructure operated by a private company, processed by a model maintained in a fourth jurisdiction, and needed by a unit whose personnel hold different national clearances.

The machine sees one object.

The alliance sees several sovereign systems touching the same fact.

That is why data comes before decisions.

An alliance that wants AI-enabled command, logistics, intelligence, maintenance, planning, or warning must first decide what information can move, what can be inferred from it, who can act on the result, and what happens when permission or confidence changes.

These questions sound bureaucratic in peacetime.

In crisis they become latency, trust, and authority.

A coalition may have the sensor.

It may have the analyst.

It may have the model.

It may have the unit that can act.

If the information cannot cross the seams among them fast enough, the capability does not exist at coalition speed.

## The data path is the capability

It is tempting to inventory data assets the way militaries inventory platforms.

How many sensors?

How many feeds?

How much storage?

How many models?

Those counts can conceal the operational question.

Can an authorized user discover, obtain, understand, verify, and use the information in time for the mission?

The relevant object is therefore not the dataset.

It is the **data path**.

Collection.

Labeling.

Storage.

Discovery.

Authorization.

Transformation.

Fusion.

Delivery.

Interpretation.

Correction.

Revocation.

Each stage can become the bottleneck.

A sensor that detects a threat in seconds can still feed a coalition decision that arrives hours later because access requires manual review.

A model can process a million records instantly and still be strategically slow if nobody knows whether the output may be shared onward.

This produces a useful metric: **time from observation to trusted coalition use**.

Not time to collect.

Not model latency.

Trusted coalition use.

That is the conversion clock that matters.

## Ownership is not one question

Who owns a piece of data after it has been transformed?

The word *own* is too simple for the problem.

Several different rights can attach to the same information.

Who controls the raw record?

Who may view it?

Who may query it?

Who may retain it?

Who may combine it with other data?

Who may produce a derived assessment?

Who may share that assessment onward?

Who may use it for training?

Who may use a model trained on it after the original permission expires?

Who is responsible for correction?

Who may destroy or revoke it?

Traditional information-sharing agreements often assume a document lifecycle.

A record is created.

It receives a classification.

It is shared with named recipients.

Machine systems complicate that model.

Data is queried, transformed, embedded, summarized, joined, cached, logged, and used to condition later systems.

The information leaves traces even when the original file never moves.

This creates **derived-data rights**.

An alliance that governs only the source file will discover that the strategically useful information lives downstream.

The rule has to follow the meaning, not merely the container.

## Purpose is an executable boundary

Sharing is not binary.

An ally may permit data to support air defense but not targeting.

It may permit a partner to query the data but not retain it.

It may allow a derived alert to be shared more widely than the underlying source.

It may permit use in one theater but not another.

It may allow operational inference but prohibit model training.

It may allow training for one mission and forbid reuse for commercial development.

These distinctions matter only if systems can enforce them.

A policy buried in a memorandum does not control an API automatically.

The alliance therefore needs policy attached to data or to the service that exposes it.

Who may use it.

For what purpose.

For how long.

Under which mission.

At what classification.

With which onward-sharing restrictions.

Whether it may be retained.

Whether it may be used for training or adaptation.

Whether it may be combined with named categories of other information.

Whether a human must approve a particular downstream use.

This is **machine-readable permission**.

The goal is not to encode every diplomatic nuance into software.

The goal is to stop predictable policy questions from requiring emergency human interpretation every time a machine requests data.

The more permissions depend on manual exception, the less scalable the alliance becomes.

## Permission latency is strategic latency

A coalition can have fast networks and slow governance.

That distinction is easy to miss because technical teams measure packet latency while political systems create permission latency.

A request can traverse a continent in milliseconds and sit in an inbox for twelve hours.

Permission latency has several sources.

Unclear ownership.

Ambiguous classification.

Missing legal authority.

Unrecognized identity.

Uncertain mission purpose.

Fear of onward sharing.

No reciprocal agreement.

No named decision-maker.

The cure is not universal preauthorization.

Some information should remain tightly controlled.

The cure is **decision clarity**.

For each important category, who can say yes?

Who can say no?

What evidence is required?

How long should the decision take?

What happens after hours?

What happens if the normal authority is unreachable?

Can a narrow temporary authorization be granted while broader review continues?

Governance becomes operational when these questions have answers before the crisis.

## Data agreements are coalition constitutions

An alliance data agreement looks technical.

It actually allocates power.

It decides which actors can see which parts of reality.

It decides who can produce authoritative assessments.

It decides whose classification rules travel across the network.

It decides whether a smaller ally can challenge a larger ally’s model using its own evidence.

It decides how easy it is for new partners to join.

That makes data governance constitutional.

The analogy is useful because constitutions do not eliminate politics.

They establish procedures for recurring political conflict.

A durable data architecture should not assume allies will always agree.

It should define what happens when they do not.

Can one partner withhold a source while allowing a derived conclusion?

Can another attach a dissenting confidence label?

Can a national caveat survive fusion?

Can a contributor require revalidation before its data is used in a new mission?

Can a state withdraw future access without silently invalidating every historical product?

These are rules for disagreement inside a network.

## Identity has to carry context

Data is only useful when the system knows who is asking.

Coalitions are full of temporary relationships.

Units rotate.

Officers change jobs.

Contractors enter and leave.

Partners join one mission but not another.

Clearances differ by country.

Roles change during crisis.

The network therefore needs identity that is portable enough for coalition work and narrow enough to preserve national control.

A user may be trusted by their own government but not automatically entitled to another country’s data.

An ally may recognize the person but not the role.

A role may be valid in one command and not another.

This means identity has to carry context.

Who are you?

Who vouches for you?

What mission are you part of?

What function are you performing?

How long does the permission last?

Who can revoke it?

Which national caveats apply?

The deeper principle is **least necessary trust**.

The coalition should not require every member to trust every participant equally.

It should let partners extend enough trust for the mission while keeping other boundaries intact.

That is more realistic than demanding one universal identity regime.

## Discoverability without disclosure

An alliance can possess enormous amounts of information and still fail because nobody knows what exists.

This is a metadata problem.

What is the source?

When was it collected?

What geography does it cover?

How current is it?

What restrictions apply?

What format is it in?

How reliable has the source been historically?

Can it be queried remotely?

Who should be contacted if access is denied?

Without good metadata, users solve the problem by building private inventories.

Private inventories recreate fragmentation.

They also create hidden dependence on individuals who know where the information lives.

A mature alliance should treat discoverability as infrastructure.

The user may not be allowed to see the data.

The user may still need to know that a relevant source exists, what general category it belongs to, and whether an access route is available.

This is **discoverability without disclosure**.

It separates secrecy from invisibility.

Secrecy can be governed.

Invisibility cannot be planned around.

## Metadata is not clerical overhead

Metadata determines whether data can be trusted at speed.

Time collected.

Geographic scope.

Sensor state.

Classification.

Usage restriction.

Source lineage.

Model version.

Processing chain.

Known caveat.

Correction status.

A missing metadata field can force an analyst to stop and ask a human question.

At scale, thousands of small stops become coalition latency.

This creates a strategic principle: **standardize the questions every user must answer before trusting the data**.

Not every country needs the same internal database.

They need enough common metadata that the receiving side understands what it received.

This is a narrow waist for information sharing.

## Provenance protects against synthetic consensus

AI systems are especially vulnerable to data without lineage.

Where did the claim come from?

Which sensor?

Which analyst?

Which commercial feed?

Which partner?

Which transformation?

Which model?

Which version?

A coalition may see the same observation repeated through several systems and mistake repetition for independent confirmation.

A commercial data source can feed several national products that later appear to agree.

A model can summarize a report, another system can ingest the summary, and the original uncertainty can disappear.

Provenance protects the alliance from **synthetic consensus**.

It also protects trust among partners.

If a country is asked to act on information it cannot inspect fully, it needs enough lineage to understand why the assessment deserves weight.

The key is not that every user can see every source.

The key is that the system can distinguish independence from repetition even when details remain compartmented.

A statement such as “three independent national sources” means something different from “three products derived from the same commercial feed.”

The interface should preserve that difference.

## Provenance needs a graph, not a footnote

Linear citations are useful for documents.

Machine systems need a dependency graph.

An output can depend on hundreds of records, transformations, weights, policies, and versions.

When one element becomes suspect, the alliance should be able to ask what downstream products depend on it.

This is the **provenance graph**.

The graph supports three functions.

Trust.

Correction.

Audit.

Trust asks what supports the output now.

Correction asks what else must be revisited if one source is wrong.

Audit asks what evidence and rules existed at the moment a decision was made.

These functions become more important as systems generate outputs too quickly for humans to reconstruct the chain manually.

## Correction is a first-class capability

Bad data is inevitable.

The strategic question is whether the network can retract it.

A source is later judged unreliable.

A sensor calibration was wrong.

A commercial feed contained duplicated records.

A cyberattack corrupted a database.

An analyst mislabeled an event.

A translation changed the apparent meaning of a statement.

In a traditional document system, a correction can be issued.

In a machine system, the bad data may already have influenced cached outputs, model retrieval, automated rules, and downstream assessments.

The network needs **correction propagation**.

What products used the source?

Which partners received derivatives?

Which models retrieved or trained on it?

Which decisions were influenced?

Which confidence scores need revision?

Which alerts should be withdrawn?

A correction that cannot find its descendants is incomplete.

This suggests another coalition metric: **time to propagate a material correction through the relevant network**.

A system that can disseminate an alert in ten seconds but requires three days to retract it has asymmetric risk.

## Recall matters as much as release

Data governance usually focuses on how information is released.

Machine-speed alliances also need a recall mechanism.

A contributor may discover that a source is compromised.

A legal authority may expire.

A mistaken classification may have exposed data too widely.

A training permission may have been misunderstood.

The alliance needs a way to mark the affected information, notify users, stop new use where appropriate, and distinguish what can and cannot realistically be undone.

This is not perfect deletion.

Perfect deletion may be impossible after information has influenced human judgment or model training.

The value is in **bounded irreversibility**.

Know which uses can be stopped.

Which derivatives can be flagged.

Which models require revalidation.

Which historical decisions cannot be changed but should be annotated.

Honest governance begins by admitting that some information effects are irreversible.

## Training rights are different from inference rights

Artificial intelligence creates a specific political problem.

An ally may be willing to share data for a mission without agreeing that the same data can be retained indefinitely or used to improve a general model.

A government may permit inference inside a secure environment but prohibit copying the underlying records.

It may allow retrieval for a named operation and forbid gradient updates.

It may allow a model to run against national data while requiring the model itself to leave no local copy.

These distinctions matter because learning can outlast permission.

A partner can revoke access to a file more easily than it can revoke what a model learned from the file.

This can create strategic irreversibility and discourage sharing.

Alliances therefore need agreements written for machine use rather than human browsing.

Inference versus training.

Temporary use versus retention.

Raw access versus derived output.

Retrieval versus adaptation.

Mission-specific rights versus general rights.

These categories should be technically enforceable where feasible and explicitly governed where they are not.

## Move the model to the data

One architecture reduces some sovereignty problems: move computation to the data rather than data to the computation.

A partner can keep sensitive records inside its own environment.

An approved model or query can run there.

Only the permitted result leaves.

This is not a universal solution.

The visiting code can itself create security concerns.

The output can reveal more than intended.

Performance may be limited by local infrastructure.

Auditing can be difficult.

But the pattern is politically important because it expands the design space between “share the database” and “share nothing.”

Federation becomes practical when the alliance can exchange **capability** rather than always exchanging raw custody.

## Privacy and domestic law are alliance variables

Not all important coalition data is purely military.

Logistics systems can contain commercial records.

Medical systems contain protected information.

Border and identity systems contain personal data.

Industrial datasets can reveal firms, workers, contracts, and sensitive economic relationships.

Domestic laws can differ substantially in what may be collected, retained, or transferred.

These differences are not bugs to be erased by military urgency.

They are part of sovereign legitimacy.

A durable alliance should design around them.

Minimize collection where possible.

Separate personal identifiers from operational attributes when feasible.

Use purpose limitation.

Create audit trails.

Define retention.

Make emergency authorities explicit rather than assumed.

A coalition that treats domestic legal safeguards as peacetime annoyances may discover that its data architecture cannot survive democratic scrutiny.

Operational speed that destroys political authorization is not durable speed.

## Data minimization can improve security

The instinct of machine systems is to collect more.

More data can improve performance.

It can also increase exposure.

Every unnecessary copy becomes another place to leak.

Every retained field becomes another compliance problem.

Every central repository becomes a richer intelligence target.

Every model trained on sensitive data becomes another object that must be controlled.

This creates a security argument for **minimum necessary data**.

What is the least sensitive information that preserves the mission function?

Can a derived feature replace the raw record?

Can the query return yes/no rather than the whole database?

Can aggregation preserve operational value while reducing political risk?

Can information expire automatically when the mission ends?

Data minimization is not anti-AI.

It is a way to make AI use politically and technically survivable.

## Data poisoning is an alliance problem

An adversary does not have to penetrate every allied network.

It can corrupt a source that many partners trust.

A commercial feed.

An open-source dataset.

A compromised national system.

A supplier-maintained database.

Once the information crosses coalition boundaries, the alliance can amplify the poison through its own trust network.

This is a network paradox.

Sharing increases value and blast radius at the same time.

The defense is not to stop sharing.

It is to preserve source independence, confidence labels, anomaly detection, and the ability to quarantine suspect information without shutting down the whole coalition.

A useful architecture can say:

this source is degraded;

these products depend on it;

these independent sources remain healthy;

these missions can continue at reduced confidence.

That is graceful degradation for data trust.

## Commercial data needs sovereignty rules too

Alliances increasingly rely on commercially collected information.

That can expand coverage and reduce cost.

It also creates contractual and political dependencies.

A commercial provider may operate across many jurisdictions.

Its terms of service can change.

Its upstream data can be opaque.

Its cloud infrastructure can be located elsewhere.

Its investors or customers can create political pressure.

The alliance needs to know what strategic function the commercial feed performs and what happens if it disappears.

Can the provider guarantee continuity under crisis conditions?

Who can compel or restrict service?

Can the coalition export the historical data if the contract ends?

Can another provider substitute?

Are the data rights compatible with model training or only operational viewing?

Commercial abundance does not eliminate sovereign dependency.

It changes the form of it.

## Denial should be designed, not discovered

Allies often think about sharing in terms of permission.

They should also think about what happens when permission disappears.

A partner withdraws access.

A legal authority expires.

A network segment is compromised.

A political dispute narrows cooperation.

A provider becomes unavailable.

Can the coalition continue?

Data resilience means knowing which dependencies are critical before they fail.

A workflow that collapses because one feed disappears may be efficient but brittle.

The coalition should classify dependencies.

Mission-critical and unique.

Mission-critical but substitutable.

Useful but optional.

Experimental.

That classification should drive fallback planning and investment.

A unique critical feed deserves different governance than a convenience dataset.

## Dependency concentration creates hidden vetoes

If one country supplies the only source required for a key mission, that country holds a practical veto over the mission even if no treaty says so.

The veto may be legitimate.

The contributor may bear exceptional collection cost or risk.

The point is to make the dependency visible.

Hidden vetoes are more dangerous than explicit ones because planners assume capability exists unconditionally.

An alliance should map **data centrality**.

Which national sources sit on many critical paths?

Which commercial providers support several members at once?

Which identity service gates access to multiple domains?

Which model depends on data only one state can legally release?

Centrality should trigger conversation about reciprocity, redundancy, and political expectations.

The goal is not to eliminate leverage.

All alliances contain asymmetric contributions.

The goal is to stop asymmetry from masquerading as universal access.

## Reciprocity is not equal bytes

Not every ally contributes equally in every domain.

One may provide unique intelligence.

Another geography.

Another logistics data.

Another compute.

Another industrial information.

Another political access.

This creates bargaining questions inside the network.

A country contributing high-value information may want stronger control over use.

A country contributing less data may still be asked to assume significant operational risk based on shared assessments.

The alliance needs reciprocity that is functional rather than perfectly symmetrical.

The objective is not equal bytes.

It is a bargain in which every participant receives enough value and enough control to keep contributing.

A mature alliance can explain what each member gains from the information system without pretending all contributions are interchangeable.

## The sovereignty dividend of federation

The temptation is to solve complexity through centralization.

Put the data in one place.

Create one authoritative architecture.

Require partners to contribute.

Centralization can work in narrow domains.

It can also create three strategic problems.

Sovereignty.

Security.

Dependency.

A single repository can become a single political chokepoint and a single attractive target.

Federation is the alternative.

In a federated system, information can remain under national control while being discoverable and usable through agreed interfaces and permissions.

The difficult work shifts from moving everything into one repository to creating common rules for identity, metadata, access, provenance, audit, and revocation.

This is less elegant than centralization.

It is often more politically durable.

The **sovereignty dividend** is that members can participate deeply without surrendering every underlying asset.

That can increase willingness to share.

Federation is therefore not merely a concession to national sensitivity.

It can expand the total information available to the coalition because partners contribute more when control boundaries are credible.

## Federation creates its own coordination bill

There is a counterargument.

Federated systems can be slow, inconsistent, and hard to secure.

Different national architectures create integration cost.

Distributed policy engines can disagree.

Metadata can become stale.

Queries across several systems can fail unpredictably.

A centralized data environment may produce better performance and simpler security for selected missions.

This is true.

Federation should not become ideology.

The alliance needs a portfolio.

Centralize where common custody is politically acceptable and operationally beneficial.

Federate where sovereignty or security makes centralization brittle.

Use replicated services where continuity matters.

Use local execution where raw data should not move.

The correct architecture is mission-specific.

The strategic principle is that technical elegance should not outrun political durability.

## Emergency access needs an expiry date

Crises create pressure for broad access.

Open the feed.

Waive the restriction.

Share everything.

Emergency access can be necessary.

It can also become permanent through inertia.

A mature system should attach an expiry mechanism to extraordinary permissions.

Who granted the exception?

For which mission?

For how long?

What data was actually used?

What derivatives were created?

What happens when the authority expires?

Can a narrower permission replace it?

This prevents emergency governance from quietly becoming the new normal.

It also makes allies more willing to grant exceptional access because they know the exception has a defined end.

Temporary trust is easier to authorize than ambiguous permanent surrender.

## Data escrow can separate preparation from release

Another useful pattern is pre-positioned escrow.

A country may not want a partner to access sensitive data in peacetime.

It can nevertheless prepare the technical and legal route by which access would be released under agreed conditions.

The data remains controlled.

Identity is prevalidated.

Interfaces are tested.

Release triggers are defined.

The crisis does not begin with integration from scratch.

This stores time without surrendering peacetime control.

Escrow can apply to datasets, model access, decryption capability, or specific derived products.

The broader principle is powerful: **pre-negotiate the path even when you do not pre-authorize the use**.

That is how alliances preserve discretion without accepting avoidable latency.

## Exercises should test denial and correction, not just sharing

A declaration of information sharing is not evidence of information sharing.

The network has to test the actual path.

The user logs in.

The identity is recognized.

The dataset is discovered.

The policy engine checks the request.

The data is queried.

The provenance survives.

The output is marked correctly.

The product can be passed to the next authorized user.

The audit trail records what happened.

Then the exercise should break something.

Deny the request.

Remove a feed.

Expire a credential.

Disconnect a partner.

Corrupt a record.

Change the classification.

Withdraw training permission.

Issue a material correction.

Revoke an emergency authorization.

See whether the mission still functions and whether failure is visible.

A network that is tested only when every permission succeeds has not tested governance.

It has tested connectivity.

## Data-readiness metrics

The alliance needs measures that reward usable governance rather than data accumulation.

**Time to discover:** how long before an authorized user knows a relevant source exists?

**Time to authorize:** how long from request to a valid decision?

**Time to trusted use:** how long from observation to coalition-ready use?

**Correction latency:** how long to propagate a material correction?

**Revocation latency:** how quickly can compromised or expired access be removed?

**Fallback coverage:** what fraction of mission-critical data dependencies have tested alternatives?

**Provenance completeness:** how much of the decision-relevant chain can be reconstructed?

**Dependency concentration:** how many critical workflows depend on a single source, provider, identity system, or jurisdiction?

**Permission-exception rate:** how often does the system require manual workaround?

These metrics make the hidden bureaucracy visible as operational performance.

They also create falsifiable improvement targets.

## The data debt problem

Every shortcut creates future work.

A manually copied spreadsheet.

An undocumented feed.

A one-off permission.

A local translation table.

A temporary classification workaround.

A model trained on data whose rights were never fully specified.

During a crisis, these shortcuts may be rational.

If they persist, the alliance accumulates **data debt**.

Data debt is the set of unresolved governance and integration assumptions hidden inside working systems.

It resembles technical debt in software.

The system functions until change exposes the shortcuts.

A partner leaves.

A vendor updates.

A legal review occurs.

A source is compromised.

Then the coalition discovers that nobody knows why the workflow was allowed in the first place.

Data debt should be recorded and retired deliberately after exercises and crises.

Otherwise emergency improvisation becomes invisible architecture.

## AI should reveal the policy graph

Artificial intelligence can help manage data complexity.

It can inspect metadata, trace lineage, detect duplicate sourcing, identify unusual access patterns, map dependencies, and help users discover relevant information.

It can also make governance disappear behind convenience.

A natural-language assistant may retrieve information from several restricted systems and return one fluent answer.

The user sees the answer.

The policy graph is hidden.

That is dangerous.

A coalition AI should be able to show, at the level appropriate to the user, what permissions shaped the result.

Which sources were unavailable?

Which caveats affected the answer?

Which data was current?

Which inferences came from restricted sources that cannot be shared onward?

Which parts are model synthesis rather than direct observation?

The machine should make the governance boundary more legible, not less.

Otherwise convenience becomes policy laundering.

## What would make the thesis false?

The data-before-decisions thesis should weaken if coalition performance proves largely insensitive to governance latency and data fragmentation.

If centralized architectures consistently outperform federated ones without creating meaningful sovereignty, security, or dependency costs, the case for federation would be overstated.

If partners willingly grant broad standing rights for training, retention, and onward use without reducing future sharing, the emphasis on derived-data rights may be too cautious.

If provenance rarely changes strategic judgments in practice, the proposed investment in lineage may exceed its value.

If emergency manual workarounds repeatedly deliver reliable coalition performance, machine-readable permissions may be less important than argued.

And if data abundance rather than data path quality best predicts operational advantage, the chapter has located the bottleneck incorrectly.

These are empirical questions.

The chapter should not treat governance as inherently valuable.

Governance is valuable only when it converts distributed information into trusted usable capability faster than its own friction slows the coalition.

## Data before decisions means politics before automation

AI does not eliminate the politics of data.

It makes the politics executable.

The alliance advantage begins before the model runs.

It begins with whether partners have built a data path that survives sovereignty, secrecy, legal difference, model training, correction, denial, and crisis.

The strongest coalition is not the one that centralizes every record.

It is the one that knows which meanings can cross which borders, at what speed, under whose authority, with what provenance, and how the answer changes when trust changes.

Data is not valuable because it exists.

It becomes alliance power when the network can turn it into a trusted shared decision without pretending that sovereignty disappeared along the way.
