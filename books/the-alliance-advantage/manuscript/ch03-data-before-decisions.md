# Data Before Decisions

A decision made without the data that existed elsewhere in the alliance is not only a mistake.

It is a network failure with a human face.

Somewhere a sensor already saw the track. Somewhere a logistics database already knew the magazine was low. Somewhere an ally had already labelled the image, scored the model’s confidence, or recorded that a runway could not take the aircraft weight. The decision-maker acted inside a local picture because the wider picture could not cross a boundary in time.

This is the ordinary condition of coalitions, and the AI age makes it more consequential rather than less.

Models do not abolish the data problem. They intensify it. A model trained on one nation’s labelled set can misread another nation’s environment. A fusion algorithm that never receives an allied feed will produce confident local nonsense. An autonomous cueing system that cannot ingest releasable metadata will either refuse to act or act on a thinner world than the alliance actually possesses.

Data before decisions is therefore not a slogan about being analytical.

It is a claim about sequence and architecture. If the alliance cannot move, label, discover, authorize, and protect data at the seams, later investments in decision aids become decorations on national silos.

NATO’s 2026 digital strategy states the premise directly: data is a corporate resource and an enduring strategic asset that should be integrated into political and military decision-making. The implementation strategy describes an Alliance Data Sharing Ecosystem covering domains such as geospatial, space operations, and intelligence and ISR, under a governance framework that respects data sovereignty and national regulations. Those sentences are easy to applaud and hard to operationalize.

Sovereignty is not a footnote.

It is the reason federation exists.

Allies will not dump every sensor feed into a common lake. They will share under rules. The quality of those rules—labels, identity, access control, audit, purpose limitation, and releasability—determines whether the ecosystem is a living system or a museum of unread memoranda of understanding.

A piece of data without machine-readable classification, provenance, confidence, and handling caveats arrives as a human problem. A person must interpret markings, request exceptions, and decide whether the receiving system is allowed to store it. That process can be correct and still be too slow for missile defense, for maritime strike coordination, or for allocating scarce interceptors. Standardized metadata tagging and attribute-based access are not aesthetic preferences. They are the difference between a feed that can be routed automatically and a feed that waits for a duty officer.

Federated identity sits beside labelling.

If users, services, and devices cannot be recognized across national boundaries under agreed credentials, every connection becomes a bespoke trust exercise. Federated Identity, Credential and Access Management is the unglamorous name for a strategic capability: the ability to know who or what is requesting data, under what authority, for what mission, and with what residual rights. Without it, Zero Trust remains a poster. With it, Zero Trust can become a way to share more rather than less, because access can be continuous, least-privilege, and revocable.

Discovery is the next neglected layer.

Data that cannot be found might as well not exist for the decision that needed it. National systems often optimize storage and protection. They rarely optimize cross-national discoverability under policy constraints. A catalog that reveals only what a requester is entitled to see is more useful than a warehouse that contains everything and exposes nothing. Alliance data spaces, in the sense used in NATO’s digital strategy notes, are attempts to build that middle path: decentralized infrastructures with common governance so diverse actors can share without surrendering custody of the underlying stores.

A state that retains control of its repositories while permitting policy-compliant queries has not abandoned sovereignty. It has encoded sovereignty into technical mediation. That encoding can fail. Policies can be too tight for the mission or too loose for domestic law. Interfaces can leak. Adversaries can target the brokers. The design still beats the alternative of pretending that political authority can be suspended for the convenience of a common operating picture.

Tempo exposes the cost of failure more clearly than architecture diagrams do.

In a slow staff process, missing data produces another meeting. In a high-intensity fight, missing data produces empty magazines, late intercepts, or friendly confusion. The same is true in political consultation. Leaders asked to authorize reinforcement, release weapons, or make public statements need intelligence and logistics facts that are current enough to be honest. An alliance that deliberates on stale national extracts is performing ritual rather than strategy.

Ukraine again made the data-consumption relationship visible, though from a different angle than digital strategy papers usually emphasize.

Consumption rates, loss reports, repair status, and stockpile levels became strategic variables. Governments discovered that their peacetime visibility into industrial and logistical reality was thinner than their visibility into platforms. Data about factories, energetics, shipping, and warehouse contents turned out to be as operationally relevant as data about the front. An alliance that treats only battlefield ISR as “operational data” will mis-prioritize. Industrial and logistics telemetry belong in the same conversation if decisions about production and transfer are to be made in time.

Commercial data complicates the picture further.

Satellites, AIS tracks, open-source reporting, financial flows, and social media can create situational awareness that outpaces classified systems in some dimensions while remaining unreliable in others. Allies differ in how they ingest commercial feeds, how they verify them, and how they mark them for military use. A coalition that cannot agree on the status of a commercial image will argue about the image while the event moves. The solution is not to worship open source or to dismiss it. It is to treat provenance and confidence as first-class data, so decision-makers can see what kind of world they are looking at.

AI changes the economics of labelling and the politics of sharing at the same time.

Models need volume, diversity, and ground truth. No single ally owns all of the environments that matter. Weather, terrain, electromagnetic conditions, urban patterns, and adversary tactics vary. Training only on national data risks national overfitting. Sharing raw training sets, however, can reveal collection methods, gaps, and sources. The workable compromises tend to involve shared feature stores with strict access, synthetic data generation under agreed rules, federated learning where gradients move instead of raw records, and carefully curated evaluation sets that allow models to be tested across allied conditions without exposing everything used to train them.

None of those compromises are free of risk.

Federated learning can still leak information. Synthetic data can encode biases. Evaluation sets can be gamed. The point is not purity. The point is that “we cannot share” and “we must pool everything” are both inadequate answers. Alliance advantage in the model age depends on intermediate institutions that make partial sharing safe enough to be routine.

Routine is the decisive word.

Exceptional releases during crises are better than nothing. They are worse than practiced pipelines. If every useful transfer requires a novel legal interpretation, the alliance will discover its true data latency under fire. Exercises that force releasability decisions, cross-domain transfers, and degraded-network sharing are therefore not communications drills alone. They are decision-architecture drills.

Cross-domain solutions—technical bridges between classification levels—illustrate the stubbornness of the problem.

An ally may be willing to share a secret product with another ally and still be unable to move it into the operational system where the shooter lives. Guards, filters, and human review exist for good reasons. They also create queues. Designing those queues for wartime volume is part of treating data as a strategic asset rather than as a library problem.

Edge computing adds another constraint.

Not all decisions can wait for a central cloud. Tactical units in contested environments may need local inference on locally available data, with intermittent synchronization when links recover. Federation then includes the edge: what subsets of models, what caches of maps, what identity tokens, and what offline policies travel with the force. An alliance that designs only for peacetime enterprise networks will field edge systems that cannot talk when they meet.

Human-machine collaboration, emphasized in NATO’s digital vision language, depends on this substrate.

A human cannot usefully collaborate with a machine that is starved of allied context or flooded with uncleared noise. Trust in the machine is partly trust in the data path behind it. Operators who have been burned by late or contradictory feeds will ignore decision aids regardless of model accuracy metrics published in a lab.

There is a temptation to treat data strategy as a headquarters specialty.

That temptation should be resisted. Data quality begins at the sensor, the warehouse scanner, the maintenance log, and the after-action report. If units are not resourced and trained to label, correct, and protect data, the ecosystem above them will polish garbage. Digital literacy, in the sense used in NATO’s people-and-culture sections, is not only about using software. It is about understanding that a missing metadata field can strand a later decision.

Adversaries understand the same terrain.

They will target the seams: poison data, impersonate identities, overload discovery services, exploit cross-domain bridges, and amplify disagreements about releasability to slow coalition action. Resilience therefore includes diversity of sources, independent verification paths, rapid revocation, and the ability to operate on reduced data without paralysis. A decision architecture that collapses when the richest feed disappears is not an alliance advantage. It is a shared vulnerability.

What would falsify the claim that data-before-decisions is a central alliance mechanism?

If allies with poor data integration still consistently outperform better-connected coalitions because of superior mass, geography, or political will, then data architecture is secondary. If common data ecosystems systematically leak in ways that outweigh their operational value, then tighter national silos may be rational. If labelling and access systems become so cumbersome that operators bypass them, then the governance layer is defeating itself. Those counter-possibilities should be kept in view. The argument here is not that data solves strategy. It is that without usable data movement, many other alliance investments cannot compound.

The practical agenda that follows is narrower than a dream of total transparency and tougher than a press release.

Agree which data domains must be discoverable under federation for priority missions. Fund labelling and identity as operational infrastructure. Measure release latency in exercises as a performance metric, not as an anecdote. Build industrial and logistics data into the same ecosystem that carries ISR. Create safe intermediate methods for model-relevant sharing. Design for edge degradation. Audit for adversary manipulation. And accept that sovereignty will remain encoded in the system—then make that encoding fast enough to matter.

Decisions will still be human, political, and uncertain.

They do not need to be ignorant of what the network already knew.

Consider a concrete sequence that recurs in planning rooms.

A maritime patrol aircraft detects a contact. The track is entered into a national system with fields that make sense to that navy’s software. An allied ship could use the track if it arrived with compatible identity, time, uncertainty, and classification tags. Instead, a liaison officer paraphrases the contact over a voice circuit. The paraphrase is late and incomplete. Both units are professional. The data path was not.

Multiply that sequence across air defense, logistics, cyber indicators, and space cueing, and the alliance accumulates a tax on every decision. The tax is paid in time, ambiguity, and duplicated effort. AI systems that sit on top of those paths will automate the tax unless the paths are redesigned.

Redesign begins with deciding which decisions are latency-critical.

Missile warning, air defense engagement, maritime strike coordination, and certain cyber defensive actions cannot wait for human re-keying between national systems. Political consultation, campaign planning, and industrial surge decisions can tolerate more latency but still fail if the underlying inventories are invisible. A serious data agenda ranks decisions by time sensitivity and builds the shortest compliant path for the top of that list first. Trying to boil the ocean of all possible shares is how programs stall.

Governance has to match that ranking.

Committees that meet quarterly cannot adjudicate releasability for a minutes-scale kill chain. Authority must be pre-delegated into policy that machines can enforce and humans can audit. That is uncomfortable for legal cultures built around case-by-case review. It is also the only way federation scales. The alternative is a permanent backlog of uncleared packets and a force that learns to ignore the official path.

National regulations will continue to differ.

Some allies restrict biometric data more tightly than others. Some treat commercial satellite imagery as operationally sensitive. Some bind defense data to parliamentary oversight mechanisms that have no exact counterpart elsewhere. Federation does not erase those differences. It maps them into attributes so that a request from a German logistics officer, a Polish air defender, and a Canadian intelligence analyst can be evaluated without inventing a new treaty each Tuesday.

The United States will remain a disproportionate producer and consumer of allied data for the foreseeable future. That fact creates both opportunity and distortion. Opportunity, because American sensors, clouds, and analytic capacity can thicken the network quickly. Distortion, because architectures optimized for U.S. classification practice can freeze out allies who need releasable products rather than system-high feeds. Designing for the most restrictive common practice can make the ecosystem safe and empty. Designing only for the richest bilateral channels can leave multilateral operations blind.

A healthier pattern is layered releasability.

Produce data once with rich provenance. Derive products at multiple releasability levels. Prefer automatic downgrade paths where policy allows. Measure how often operators are forced to choose between speed and compliance. If that choice is frequent, the labels are wrong or the authorities are misplaced.

Industrial data deserves a special institutional home.

Factory output, energetics availability, shipping status, and maintenance backlogs are often held by ministries of economy, companies, or acquisition agencies rather than by operational commands. In a long war those numbers decide strategy. An alliance data ecosystem that stops at the headquarters door will under-serve the munition and logistics chapters of this book. Connecting industrial telemetry to operational planning, under commercial confidentiality and security rules, is part of treating data as a strategic asset rather than as a staff curiosity.

There is also the problem of competing truths.

Two allies can field honest sensors and still disagree about a track’s identity, a battle-damage assessment, or a cyber attribution. Data sharing does not automatically produce agreement. It produces a shared basis for disagreement that can be adjudicated faster. Common schemas and confidence fields make that adjudication possible. Without them, each capital argues from its own extract and suspects the other of politics. With them, the argument can at least locate whether the conflict is in the sensor, the model, the label, or the policy.

Training audiences should feel this friction before war does.

Synthetic environments and federated mission networks can rehearse data denial, poisoned feeds, and partial sharing. If every exercise assumes clean, complete, timely allied data, forces will train for a world that will not be offered. Degraded data literacy—knowing how to decide with less—is as important as rich data integration.

Smaller allies face a distinct version of the problem.

They may lack the analytic depth to exploit a large shared lake, yet they often hold geographically unique sensors or local knowledge that larger allies need. Reciprocity matters. If smaller members only ever send and rarely receive usable products, political support for sharing erodes. The ecosystem has to return value in forms that smaller forces can actually use: tailored warnings, logistics visibility, and decision aids sized for their command systems.

Metrics should follow the same honesty.

Count not only petabytes stored or connections built, but decision latency from detection to authorized action across at least one multinational path. Count the percentage of priority mission threads that can run on releasable data without human re-entry. Count how often industrial and logistics queries return current answers. Count revocation time when a credential is compromised. Vanity metrics about digital transformation will otherwise crowd out the few numbers that reveal whether data is arriving before decisions.

Culture sits underneath the metrics.

A force that treats data hygiene as clerks’ work will not sustain an ecosystem. A force that treats every field as a potential lifeline will still make mistakes, but fewer of them will be self-inflicted. Leaders who ask for a picture without asking how old it is, who contributed to it, and what could not be shared are training their staffs to perform confidence rather than to deliver truth under constraint.

The chapter’s title reverses a common institutional habit.

Institutions often decide first—on force packages, on transfers, on public lines—and then commission the data that might have changed the decision. In domestic politics that habit is familiar. In alliance operations it is dangerous. The network’s advantage begins earlier: arrange the world so that what is already known somewhere can be known, lawfully and quickly, where the decision will be made.

If that arrangement holds, models, clouds, and command systems have something to work with.

If it does not, the alliance will keep buying decision superiority as a product and receiving decision theater as a service.

A final distinction helps keep the argument honest.

Data abundance is not the same as decision advantage. An alliance can drown operators in feeds while still withholding the one field that would make a track actionable. Selective scarcity—moving the right attributes under the right authority—beats indiscriminate volume. That is why labelling, identity, and purpose limitation belong in the same sentence as sharing. The goal is not maximal exposure. The goal is timely, lawful availability of the facts that change choices.

If allies can build that habit, later chapters on models, intelligence, and cloud become extensions of a working premise rather than attempts to invent one under fire.

In the end, the phrase data before decisions is a discipline about humility. It asks leaders to notice what the network already holds before they spend authority. It asks architects to build paths that make that noticing lawful and fast. It asks alliances to stop confusing the ownership of sensors with the availability of facts. Ownership can remain national. Availability, under rule, is what turns ownership into collective judgment. Without that habit, later investments in models and clouds will automate local blindness at greater speed.

The sequence can be stated without romance. Label. Identify. Discover. Authorize. Move. Protect. Audit. Decide. Every skipped verb becomes a delay or a blind spot. Every verb performed only inside one nation becomes a local virtue and a coalition vice. Data before decisions is the insistence that the verbs come early enough that authority is spent on a world the alliance actually jointly perceives, within the limits law allows.

One more concrete failure mode closes the argument.

A coalition air tasking order built on incomplete magazine data will assign sorties that cannot be armed. Everyone in the room may be acting in good faith on the extracts they were allowed to see. The missing field was sitting in another nation’s inventory system behind a policy that no one exercised until the order was already signed. Data before decisions is the insistence that such fields be reachable by design for the missions the alliance claims it can fly. Without that insistence, decision superiority remains a phrase that flatters local screens.
