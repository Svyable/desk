# The Sovereignty Cost

Interoperability feels free when the other system adopts your standard.

It feels different when you adopt theirs.

Every alliance network imposes a sovereignty cost.

Common communications require agreed frequencies and security rules. Shared logistics require common procedures. Joint procurement constrains future choices. Data sharing creates obligations. Cloud integration can expose national operations to foreign vendors or foreign law.

These costs are real.

Ignoring them makes alliances brittle because resentment accumulates inside the technical architecture.

The strategic question is not how to eliminate sovereignty costs.

It is how to price them fairly.

A government will accept dependence when the network provides more capability, resilience, or influence than national autonomy would provide alone.

The bargain fails when dependence becomes asymmetric.

If one ally can cut off critical software, munitions, intelligence, or compute while remaining largely insulated from retaliation, the relationship begins to look hierarchical.

Reciprocity reduces that fear.

So does exit capacity.

Paradoxically, an alliance can become stronger when members know they retain some ability to operate independently. Sovereign fallback systems make participation less politically threatening.

This is the logic of reversible integration.

Connect deeply enough to gain network effects, but avoid designing the network so that political disagreement becomes national paralysis.

The cost is duplication.

Duplication is often criticized as inefficiency.

Some of it is the price of voluntary cooperation.

Empires can command integration.

Alliances must make it worth choosing.
