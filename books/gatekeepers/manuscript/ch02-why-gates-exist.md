# Why Gates Exist

Before Frances Oldham Kelsey became famous for stopping a drug, more than a hundred Americans died because an earlier system had almost no gate to stop one.

In 1937 a Tennessee pharmaceutical company wanted to sell sulfanilamide in liquid form. Sulfanilamide itself had become an important treatment for certain bacterial infections. The new formulation was different. To dissolve the drug, the company used diethylene glycol, a toxic solvent. According to the FDA's historical account, the company's control laboratory checked the preparation for appearance, flavor, and fragrance. It did not establish that the new liquid was safe before shipping it around the country.

The product was called Elixir Sulfanilamide. It killed 107 people, many of them children.

The law at the time did not require a manufacturer to prove a new drug safe before selling it. Federal law could reach adulteration and misbranding, but there was no general premarket safety gate comparable to the one Americans now take for granted. The disaster helped break a legislative stalemate. In 1938 Franklin Roosevelt signed the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, which required new drugs to be shown safe before marketing and expanded federal authority over drugs, food, cosmetics, and devices.

A gate had been built where the deaths showed one was missing.

Twenty-two years later, Kelsey arrived at the Food and Drug Administration as a medical officer. One of the first new drug applications assigned to her concerned thalidomide, a sedative already sold in many countries. Its American sponsor wanted to market it under the name Kevadon.

The application did not convince her.

The company continued pressing for approval and continued sending material intended to establish the drug's safety. Kelsey kept asking for scientifically reliable evidence. The FDA's historical record describes her refusal plainly: the safety data were insufficient. By late 1961 researchers in Germany and Australia had connected thalidomide exposure to severe birth defects. The drug had already produced a catastrophe abroad. It was never approved for marketing in the United States.

Kelsey's act became one of the great stories of American regulation because the outcome makes the value of refusal unusually easy to see. The important event was something that did not happen. There was no mass U.S. market for the drug before its risks became known. No factory fire provided the image. No collapsed bridge marked the day. The success was an absence created by a person with authority to keep a gate closed.

That is an awkward kind of value.

When a gate works, the people stopped by it feel the gate directly. The people protected by it may never know they were protected. A rejected applicant receives the letter. A prevented accident has no passengers. A blocked fraudulent payment never appears on the victim's statement. A contaminated product that fails inspection never reaches the shelf. The inconvenience is visible; the counterfactual benefit is not.

This asymmetry helps explain why gatekeeping is so easy to criticize and so dangerous to discuss carelessly. Bad gates are abundant. Arbitrary gates are real. Incumbents use regulation to protect themselves. Platforms write self-serving rules. Institutions impose paperwork that survives long after its purpose has vanished. But none of that changes the reason societies keep inventing gates.

Sometimes the person asking to cross knows more than the person who would bear the risk of letting them through.

A pharmaceutical company knows more about its drug than a patient standing at a pharmacy counter. A builder knows more about the hidden work inside a wall than the future occupant. A bank customer initiating an unusual transfer knows whether it is legitimate, while the bank sees only traces and patterns. A software developer knows what a program is intended to do; the person installing it cannot easily inspect every line of code, every dependency, every network request, and every future update. A surgeon's competence cannot be inferred from a pleasant conversation in a waiting room.

The difficulty is not simply ignorance. It is uneven ignorance.

George Akerlof made this problem famous in economics with a used-car market. In his 1970 paper on the market for lemons, he examined what can happen when sellers know more about the quality of a product than buyers do. If buyers cannot reliably tell a good car from a bad one, they will be reluctant to pay a price that assumes high quality. Owners of genuinely good cars then have less reason to sell at the depressed price. As good cars leave the market, the average quality gets worse, which gives buyers even more reason to distrust what remains.

A market can unravel because people are rationally suspicious.

This is important for a book about gatekeepers because the opposite of gatekeeping is not always freedom. Sometimes it is a market in which nobody can trust anybody.

A warranty is a response to that problem. So is a certification, an inspection, a professional license, an audit, a building code, a safety standard, a reputation system, an escrow account, a return policy, and a trusted marketplace willing to remove sellers who repeatedly cheat customers. These institutions differ enormously in legitimacy and effectiveness. Some are private promises. Some are public law. Some are standards written by technical bodies. Some are commercial rules. What they share is an attempt to make a claim about quality credible when ordinary inspection is not enough.

The gate does not merely keep people out. It can make strangers willing to transact with those who get in.

That second function is often neglected.

Imagine a marketplace in which every buyer had to personally verify the electrical safety of a charger, audit the factory that made it, inspect the seller's financial history, test for counterfeit components, and determine whether the product would damage the device it was supposed to power. The market would technically be open. It would also be exhausting.

Trust can be centralized because verification is expensive.

When a competent institution does the checking once, many other people can rely on the result. That is one reason gates proliferate as societies become more specialized. The more we depend on work we cannot personally evaluate, the more we need mechanisms for deciding whom and what to trust.

A nineteenth-century farmer might have known the person who repaired a roof, sold a horse, or milled grain. A modern patient usually cannot personally evaluate the manufacturing controls behind an injectable medicine. A traveler boarding an airliner does not inspect the maintenance log, certify the pilots, test the fuel, evaluate the air traffic system, and verify every replacement part. Modern life is possible partly because those judgments have been delegated into institutions.

The delegation is easy to mistake for mere bureaucracy because it usually arrives in bureaucratic form.

A stamp. A license. An approval number. A passed inspection. A valid certificate. A green check beside an identity. A signed release. An accepted specification.

Behind the symbol is a promise that somebody checked something you could not reasonably check yourself.

The promise may be weak. It may be corrupted. It may be outdated. The institution may inspect badly. The credential may measure the wrong competence. The marketplace may tolerate sellers that make it money. The certifier may become dependent on the firms it certifies. The symbol can turn into theater.

But a counterfeit promise of verification is evidence that verification is valuable. Nobody counterfeits a credential that confers no trust.

Safety and hidden quality explain only part of the gatekeeping world. Another class of gates appears even when everyone agrees on the quality of what is asking to pass.

There is simply not enough room.

An airport runway is a useful place to see this. A runway can handle only so many arrivals and departures in a period of time. Aircraft require separation. Weather changes capacity. Taxiways, gates, airspace, runway configuration, and operating procedures impose additional constraints. If airlines schedule more flights than the airport can reliably process, the result is not infinite aviation. It is delay.

At the most congested U.S. airports, the Federal Aviation Administration uses slots and schedule controls to constrain planned operations. The FAA defines a slot as authorization to take off or land at a specified airport during a specified period. John F. Kennedy International, LaGuardia, and Ronald Reagan Washington National are the principal U.S. Level 3 or equivalent slot-controlled airports. At Reagan National, scheduled operations during controlled hours are limited by regulation.

The slot is a gate created by arithmetic.

Suppose every airline were free to schedule as many flights as it wished at the same attractive hour. That sounds competitive until the runway queue forms. One airline's new departure consumes capacity needed by others. Passengers do not receive the timetable they were promised because the timetable described more use of the shared system than the system could deliver.

The gate exists because a shared resource becomes less useful when access is not coordinated.

This pattern appears everywhere once you see it. A concert hall has a fire-code capacity. A radio spectrum band cannot host unlimited transmitters using the same frequencies and power without interference. A road can absorb only so many cars before congestion changes the value of entering it. A hospital has a finite number of operating rooms, specialists, scanners, and beds. A computing service has finite capacity even if its users encounter that limit only as rate limits or queues. A courtroom has finite hearing time. A university course has finite seats. A wilderness permit may limit visitors because unlimited access would damage the place everyone came to enjoy.

Scarcity does not automatically tell us who deserves access. It creates the need for a rule.

First come, first served is a rule. Auctioning access is a rule. Need is a rule. Lottery is a rule. Seniority is a rule. Price is a rule. Professional triage is a rule. Political priority is a rule. A reservation system is a rule.

The argument begins after scarcity, not before it.

Who should receive the scarce slot? Should an airline that has long held runway access keep it? Should new entrants get protected opportunities? Should emergency needs override ordinary allocation? Should unused rights expire? Should they be tradable? Can a large incumbent accumulate enough of them to keep competitors out?

Those are gatekeeping questions, but they are not evidence that the gate itself is unnecessary. The runway cannot negotiate with ideology. It still has finite capacity.

This is a recurring mistake in debates about institutional access. We identify an unfair allocation mechanism and leap to the conclusion that allocation is unnecessary. Sometimes the real choice is among gates.

The same is true of standards.

Standards are a quiet form of permission. A screw that meets the specification fits. A device that implements the protocol can communicate. A measurement traceable to a common reference can be compared. A fire hose with the right coupling can attach to the hydrant. A payment message in the expected format can travel through the network. A shipping container built to standard dimensions can move among ships, cranes, trains, and trucks designed by different companies in different countries.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology describes technical standards as part of the machinery that allows products to interoperate, markets to expand, technologies to diffuse, and unsafe or substandard products to be excluded. NIST also makes the opposite point: standards can be poorly designed, raise barriers, constrain innovation, and entrench inferior technologies.

That tension is exactly right.

A shared system needs boundaries. The question is whether the boundary remains attached to the reason it was created.

Interoperability provides an especially useful case because openness itself often depends on discipline. Two devices cannot interoperate if each party insists on complete freedom to define voltage, timing, message format, dimensions, or identifiers however it likes. Cooperation requires participants to surrender some design freedom in exchange for compatibility with everyone else who follows the rule.

The gate is the specification.

It says: if you want to join this system, your contribution must look enough like this.

A good standard enlarges the world on the other side of the gate. A manufacturer that conforms to a common interface may reach more customers. Customers can switch among suppliers. Components become replaceable. Knowledge transfers. The discipline of admission creates freedom after admission.

This is very different from a gate that exists mainly to collect rent, yet the surface can look similar. Both say that certain conditions must be met before entry. Both may require testing, documentation, fees, and delay. Both can punish nonconformity.

Purpose alone does not settle which is which. Incumbents can describe exclusion as safety. Regulators can preserve obsolete requirements long after technology changes. A platform can invoke security for a rule that also protects revenue. A professional association can describe a training requirement as consumer protection even if its main effect is to reduce competition. An institution can sincerely believe that every inherited control is necessary because nobody still working there remembers life before it.

The existence of a legitimate problem does not validate every gate built in its name.

That is why Kelsey's story is useful but dangerous if told too simply.

She was right to refuse thalidomide. It does not follow that a regulator is right whenever it refuses. A safety gate can prevent disaster and also delay beneficial products. Evidence requirements can protect patients while increasing development time and cost. A system that makes false approvals very rare may generate more false rejections. Risk cannot be eliminated; it can be moved, made visible, hidden, or assigned to someone else.

Premarket drug review concentrates one kind of risk before release. A permissive system shifts more uncertainty onto patients and clinicians after release. Neither side of that trade can be described honestly with one word such as freedom or safety.

Kelsey's decision also benefited from a feature of good gates that is easy to overlook: the gatekeeper had institutional permission to resist pressure.

A reviewer who is formally authorized to demand evidence can say no in circumstances where an ordinary customer cannot. A building inspector can force correction where the future tenant would have no way to inspect hidden work. A bank's anti-fraud system can stop a transaction before a thief turns an irreversible transfer into somebody else's problem. Air traffic rules can force separation even when every airline would prefer to use the runway immediately.

The gate can protect the people who are not present at the decision.

This may be the most important reason gates exist.

Many transactions impose consequences on third parties. A builder and contractor may both save money by ignoring a fire rule, but future occupants bear the risk. A lender and borrower may willingly accept a structure whose failure later threatens depositors or a broader financial system. A company and customer may both enjoy a cheap product whose waste is dumped downstream. A developer may want fast distribution while the security cost of malicious software falls across an ecosystem.

If the people making the immediate deal capture the upside while others inherit the downside, voluntary agreement does not settle the social question.

A gate can be a place where absent interests are represented.

This is also where gatekeeping becomes political. Who counts as the absent party? How much risk may the gatekeeper impose on present applicants to protect hypothetical future victims? How certain must the harm be? Who chose the gatekeeper? What evidence can change the rule? Can the institution become so focused on avoiding blame that it stops taking useful risks?

Organizations that control gates are exposed to an unusual psychology. A harmful approval often has a name and a date. The file shows who signed it. The rejected good idea is harder to count because it may never exist.

Imagine two regulatory errors. In the first, an agency approves a dangerous product that harms a thousand people. In the second, the agency rejects or delays a useful product that would have helped a thousand people. The first produces victims who can be photographed, investigated, and connected to the decision. The second produces missing benefits scattered across a counterfactual future.

Institutions learn from the events they can see.

Over time a gate designed to manage risk can become biased toward the kind of error that is least embarrassing to the gatekeeper rather than least harmful to society. The safe bureaucratic action may be to demand one more document. The cost of that demand is usually paid outside the office.

This is how necessary gates become bad gates without anyone deciding to become a villain.

The process can run further. Once a gate exists, people organize around it. Consultants learn how to cross it. Schools sell preparation for it. Lawyers specialize in it. Incumbents master its paperwork. Vendors build software around its requirements. The gatekeeper receives budget, status, data, and authority. Applicants alter their behavior in anticipation of its criteria. A temporary control acquires a constituency.

Eventually the institution can become better at reproducing the gate than explaining the problem the gate was built to solve.

That is the moment when the question should change.

Not: do we like gatekeepers?

Ask instead what failure this gate was created to prevent.

Could a buyer not observe quality? Could an unsafe action impose harm on strangers? Was a shared resource congested? Did many independent systems need a common interface? Was fraud cheaper than verification? Was the consequence difficult to reverse after the fact?

Then ask whether the gate still addresses that failure at acceptable cost.

A gate created because inspection was impossible may become obsolete when reliable testing becomes cheap. A gate created for local scarcity may persist after capacity expands. A credential once strongly related to competence may become detached from the work. A technical standard may lock a system to yesterday's architecture. A platform rule built for security may become a commercial weapon. A review process may accumulate layers because every past failure added a requirement and no past success ever removed one.

Good gatekeeping therefore requires a capacity that bureaucracies do not naturally develop: the ability to abolish one's own reason for existing.

The most legitimate gate is not the one that never opens. It is the one that can explain both why it stops passage and why passage is worth enabling when the conditions are met.

That second obligation matters. Safety agencies exist so useful products can be trusted, not merely so dangerous products can be blocked. Airport scheduling exists so more people can travel reliably through scarce infrastructure, not so slots can become trophies. Standards exist so independent products can work together, not so committees can multiply documents. Professional screening exists so people can trust competence, not so practitioners can inherit protected markets.

A well-designed gate serves the system on both sides of it.

This is the paradox. The best gatekeepers are often builders of access.

They turn uncertainty into enough trust for strangers to cooperate. They convert scarcity into an allocation people can plan around. They make incompatible parts interoperable. They carry the interests of people who would otherwise be absent from the transaction. They stop actions whose harm would be difficult to repair afterward.

And because doing those things requires the power to say no, the institution acquires something that can be used for other purposes.

Frances Kelsey needed authority to keep thalidomide off the market. The authority was valuable because the company could not simply ignore her and sell the drug anyway. A runway slot matters because an airline cannot substitute its own opinion about capacity. A safety certification matters because customers and institutions treat failure to obtain it as consequential. A standard coordinates only when enough participants accept its discipline.

A gate that cannot constrain anyone is not much of a gate.

The same power that makes the gate useful is therefore the power that makes it dangerous.

This is where the book has to go next. Once society gives an institution the ability to decide who may pass, the institution possesses more than a procedure. It possesses leverage over whatever people need on the other side.

The question is no longer only why the gate exists.

It is what permission becomes once somebody controls it.
