# The Right to Exit

A door is not an exit if everything you care about is bolted to the floor.

This is the weakness in many claims about choice.

“You can always leave.”

The sentence sounds decisive.

Leave the platform.

Leave the bank.

Leave the employer.

Leave the software vendor.

Leave the marketplace.

Leave the cloud.

Leave the social network.

Leave the country.

Sometimes this is true in the useful sense.

Sometimes it is true only grammatically.

The account can be closed.

The accumulated life cannot be moved.

This is why exit deserves to be treated as a gatekeeping institution of its own.

A gatekeeper's power depends not only on the right to say yes or no at entry.

It depends on what happens after dependence forms.

The more value a person builds inside a system, the more expensive leaving can become.

Photos accumulate.

Messages accumulate.

Reputation accumulates.

Reviews accumulate.

Followers accumulate.

Files accumulate.

Workflows accumulate.

Models are trained.

APIs are integrated.

Employees learn the system.

Customers learn the interface.

Partners build around it.

The gate is no longer only at the entrance.

It surrounds the investment.

This is lock-in.

Lock-in is often discussed as though it were suspicious by definition.

It is not.

A good product can create switching costs because people sensibly invest in it.

A company that learns a tool deeply will incur cost if it changes tools.

A musician who builds a catalog on one service cannot make the years spent building an audience costless.

A family that organizes photographs in one system cannot teleport its memories into another structure without work.

A business that builds infrastructure around one cloud provider will use provider-specific capabilities because those capabilities create value.

This is ordinary economic gravity.

The problem begins when the gatekeeper can increase the cost of leaving without increasing the value of staying.

That is the difference between **earned dependence** and **manufactured dependence**.

Earned dependence comes from the product being useful.

Manufactured dependence comes from making alternatives artificially difficult to use.

The distinction is rarely perfect.

A proprietary feature can be both genuinely useful and hard to migrate away from.

A tightly integrated ecosystem can create real convenience and real lock-in at the same time.

The purpose of an exit analysis is not to punish integration.

It is to ask whether the person can take a reasonable share of what they created when they leave.

The first layer is data.

Data portability sounds like a complete answer.

It is not.

The OECD has described data portability as a tool that can reduce switching costs and lock-in and support competition and innovation across digital services. That is an important principle.

But the word portability hides several different levels.

The first is **exportability**.

Can the user obtain the data at all?

The second is **syntactic portability**.

Can another system technically parse the file?

The third is **semantic portability**.

Does the receiving system understand what the fields mean?

The fourth is **functional portability**.

Can the user actually continue doing the thing they were doing before?

These are different achievements.

A ZIP file full of JSON is an export.

It may or may not be an exit.

Suppose a social network lets you download your posts, photographs, and profile information.

That is valuable.

But your social graph may not transfer cleanly.

Your followers may not come with you.

Your reputation may not travel.

Your recommendations may not travel.

The relationships represented by the data may depend on other people's accounts and permissions.

The destination may not understand the network's internal concepts.

You have your archive.

You do not have your life in motion.

This is why export is not portability.

And portability is not interoperability.

Interoperability means systems can work together sufficiently that leaving does not require abandoning every connection at once.

Email is the canonical example.

You can change email providers and still send messages to people who did not change with you.

The network survives provider exit because the protocol is larger than the provider.

Telephone number portability offers another useful example.

The phone number represents identity inside a communications network. If changing carriers required changing the number every time, switching would be much more expensive because every personal and commercial relationship would need to be updated.

Portability separates identity from supplier.

This is a powerful design pattern.

What else can be separated from the supplier?

A domain name can move between hosting providers.

A payment account can sometimes preserve customer-facing identity while underlying processors change.

A business can move software workloads if interfaces and data formats are sufficiently portable.

A professional license may travel through reciprocity or compact arrangements.

A reputation system could theoretically allow verified history to travel across marketplaces.

A social graph could theoretically be interoperable across services.

Each separation weakens the gatekeeper's ownership of the relationship.

This is why portability often looks like a technical issue but acts like a competition issue.

The OECD's work makes this connection explicitly: data portability can reduce switching costs and lock-in, lower entry barriers, and increase data flows across services.

The European Union's Digital Markets Act follows the same general logic for designated gatekeepers by imposing obligations around data portability, access, and interoperability in certain core platform services.

The goal is not to make leaving free.

The goal is to prevent control of the exit from becoming an independent source of market power.

We can think of exit cost as a stack.

**Data cost.** What information must be moved?

**Format cost.** Can the destination understand it?

**Integration cost.** What systems must be rebuilt?

**Identity cost.** What names, addresses, handles, numbers, or credentials change?

**Relationship cost.** Which customers, followers, contacts, suppliers, or counterparties do not travel?

**Reputation cost.** Which reviews, ratings, history, rankings, or trust signals reset to zero?

**Learning cost.** How much human knowledge is specific to the old system?

**Contract cost.** What fees, terms, minimums, or notice periods make departure expensive?

**Coordination cost.** How many other people must change with you before leaving becomes useful?

**Risk cost.** What can break during the transition?

The total is the real exit price.

This suggests a metric: **exit debt**.

Exit debt is the accumulated future work required to leave a system.

Like financial debt, it may be worth taking on.

A company may rationally choose a proprietary database because it is excellent, even knowing migration will be hard.

A user may choose an integrated ecosystem because the convenience is worth the dependency.

The problem is not debt.

It is unpriced debt.

People often choose systems based on entry cost and current utility while exit cost remains invisible.

The product is easy to adopt.

The leaving price is discovered later.

This asymmetry is useful to gatekeepers.

Entry is where competition is visible.

Exit is where dependency is harvested.

That does not mean every long-term contract or proprietary architecture is predatory.

It means exit terms deserve to be considered at purchase time.

A mature procurement process should ask not only whether a vendor can meet today's requirements.

It should ask how the organization leaves.

What can be exported?

In what format?

How long will export take?

What does deletion mean?

What APIs are stable?

What egress fees apply?

What proprietary services will need replacement?

Can data be transferred incrementally?

Can both systems run in parallel during migration?

Does the vendor assist transition?

What happens if the vendor itself fails?

These are exit requirements.

They belong in architecture, not merely legal boilerplate.

Cloud computing makes the point vivid.

Cloud services create enormous value precisely because providers offer integrated infrastructure, managed databases, analytics, identity, storage, networking, security, and higher-level services. Using these capabilities deeply can make systems cheaper and better.

It can also make migration difficult.

The more a business relies on provider-specific services, the more it may need to redesign when switching.

Data egress charges can add another cost, though providers and regulators have increasingly scrutinized or changed such fees in some markets.

The OECD has highlighted interoperability, portability, data access, and switching barriers as important competition concerns in cloud and AI markets, especially where downstream firms become structurally dependent on compute, proprietary data, or APIs.

Again, dependence is not proof of abuse.

But dependence changes bargaining.

A customer deciding whether to accept a price increase behaves differently when migration takes two days than when migration takes two years.

The gatekeeper does not need to threaten.

Architecture negotiates on its behalf.

This gives us another concept: **credible exit**.

A credible exit is not merely legally permitted.

It is sufficiently possible that the gatekeeper must believe the user might actually do it.

Credible exit disciplines power even when few people leave.

A worker's ability to quit affects employment bargaining.

A merchant's ability to change processors affects payment negotiations.

A developer's ability to distribute elsewhere affects platform policy.

A company's ability to move clouds affects contract pricing.

A customer's ability to port a phone number affects carrier behavior.

A country's ability to trade through another route affects dependence on a port or canal.

Options matter before exercise.

This is the economic force behind exit.

The gatekeeper's strongest position is not when everyone chooses to stay.

It is when everyone must stay.

That is why theoretical alternatives should be treated skeptically.

“There are competitors” does not settle whether exit is credible.

The relevant question is whether switching preserves enough of the accumulated value to make the competitor a real substitute.

A second social network with no one you know is not equivalent merely because it has the same features.

A cloud provider that lacks the managed services your system depends on is not equivalent merely because it rents compute.

A bank that cannot support your payment flows is not equivalent merely because it accepts deposits.

A marketplace with no buyers is not equivalent merely because sellers can list products.

Substitution depends on the thing the user is actually buying.

Sometimes the product is not software.

It is the network.

Sometimes it is not storage.

It is the ecosystem of integrations.

Sometimes it is not a credential.

It is the recognition of the credential.

Sometimes it is not a payment account.

It is access to customers' preferred payment methods.

Exit analysis therefore has to identify the **carried value**.

What is the user trying to preserve?

Data?

Identity?

Audience?

Reputation?

Workflows?

History?

Counterparties?

Rights?

Once that is clear, the difference between ceremonial and meaningful portability becomes easier to see.

This is also why interoperability can be more powerful than portability.

Portability assumes a move.

Interoperability allows coexistence.

If two messaging systems can communicate, a user can switch gradually.

If two cloud environments can exchange data and workloads, a company can multi-home.

If two payment systems can interoperate, merchants and customers do not have to coordinate a simultaneous migration.

If identity is federated, users can authenticate across systems without recreating credentials everywhere.

Interoperability lowers the coordination cost of exit.

That matters in networked systems because coordination is often the real lock.

Everyone would switch if everyone switched.

Nobody switches because nobody else switches.

Interoperability breaks this loop.

But interoperability also has costs.

Common protocols can constrain product design.

Security boundaries become more complicated.

Lowest-common-denominator standards can slow innovation.

Malicious actors can exploit open interfaces.

Compliance costs can disproportionately burden smaller firms.

Mandated interoperability can freeze architecture around today's assumptions.

The answer is not maximum interoperability everywhere.

The answer is to identify which layers need openness for credible exit and which layers benefit from differentiation.

Email interoperability does not require every email client to look identical.

Number portability does not require every carrier to use the same pricing plan.

Open file formats do not require every application to have the same features.

A useful standard preserves continuity at the boundary while allowing competition above it.

This is the same design move we have seen throughout the book.

Separate the protective or coordinating function from control over the entire channel.

Exit also raises privacy questions.

Making data easy to move can make data easy to steal.

A portability right without strong authentication can become an exfiltration feature.

Transferring social graphs can expose other people's information.

Exporting corporate data can violate contractual or regulatory restrictions.

Automated transfer APIs can create new attack surfaces.

So portability must authenticate the person, scope the data, respect third-party rights, log transfers, and sometimes delay or challenge suspicious requests.

The right to exit should not become the attacker's right to take everything.

Security and exit are not opposites.

They are design constraints that must be solved together.

The same tension appears in deletion.

Leaving often includes the desire to remove data from the old system.

But deletion is not always absolute.

Financial institutions may have retention duties.

Businesses may need records for disputes, fraud, taxes, safety, or legal obligations.

Backups may persist for operational periods.

Shared communications may belong partly to other participants.

A mature exit process should therefore distinguish active use, retention, archival copies, legal obligations, and final deletion rather than promising magical disappearance.

Clarity matters more than slogans.

Exit also has an emotional dimension.

People build identity inside systems.

A username can become a reputation.

A creator's archive becomes biography.

A community becomes social belonging.

A professional account becomes a career surface.

The cost of leaving is therefore not always reducible to dollars or bytes.

A platform can become a place.

This is why “just leave” can be such an unserious response to gatekeeper power.

It assumes the relationship is replaceable because the contract is terminable.

Human systems are stickier than contracts.

The same is true of geography.

A citizen may legally leave a jurisdiction while family, language, property, profession, immigration rights, community, and history make exit enormously expensive.

Political theorists have long distinguished voice and exit because the ability to leave can discipline institutions but is not equally available to everyone.

This book does not need to turn that distinction into a complete political theory.

It needs one practical lesson:

Exit is a control on gatekeeper power only when exit is credible for the people actually governed.

The burden of switching is therefore part of governance.

A gatekeeper that says “you can leave” while deliberately destroying portability is not offering meaningful choice.

A regulator that says “competition exists” while ignoring network effects and switching costs may be measuring firms rather than options.

A procurement team that chooses a vendor without an exit plan may be purchasing future dependency.

A user who builds an entire life inside a closed system without backups is accumulating private exit debt.

Everyone has a role.

This leads to a design discipline: **exit rehearsal**.

Do not wait for crisis to discover whether leaving works.

Export the data.

Restore it somewhere else.

Rotate the provider.

Move a representative workload.

Test account closure.

Verify deletion behavior.

Port a phone number.

Run a disaster-recovery migration.

Revoke a credential.

Replace an integration.

A fire exit is tested before the fire.

Digital exits should be too.

Exit rehearsal turns contractual promises into operational knowledge.

It also reveals hidden dependencies.

The export is missing metadata.

The destination cannot import attachments.

The new service interprets timestamps differently.

The identity provider was secretly central to ten other systems.

The old vendor owns the encryption keys.

A crucial integration has no equivalent.

The migration window is longer than the business can tolerate.

These facts are useful before a dispute.

They are bargaining information.

This gives us another metric: **time to viable exit**.

Not time to close the account.

Time until the user can perform the important function elsewhere at acceptable quality and risk.

A company can terminate a cloud contract today and still need eighteen months to move.

A creator can delete an account in seconds and need years to rebuild an audience.

A merchant can close a processor account immediately and lose sales until another processor finishes underwriting.

A worker can resign today and need months to replace income, health coverage, or immigration sponsorship.

The time to viable exit reveals the practical duration of dependence.

A second metric is **value preserved on exit**.

What fraction of accumulated value survives the move?

Raw files may survive while comments vanish.

Contacts may survive while relationships vanish.

Customer records may survive while payment tokens cannot move.

Code may survive while proprietary infrastructure does not.

Credentials may survive while recognition does not.

Reputation may survive only as screenshots.

A high-quality exit preserves more than the minimum legal artifact.

It preserves continuity where continuity can reasonably travel.

This principle should not be pushed beyond feasibility.

A platform cannot force followers to join another service.

A marketplace cannot force buyers to trust a seller elsewhere.

A country cannot export citizenship rights into another jurisdiction.

Some value is inherently system-specific.

The gatekeeper should not be required to recreate the world outside its gate.

But it should not unnecessarily hold hostage the parts that can travel.

That is the boundary.

The right to exit is not a right to frictionless cloning.

It is a right against unnecessary captivity.

This distinction becomes particularly important for AI agents.

Agents will accumulate state.

Preferences.

Memories.

Tool permissions.

Work histories.

Contacts.

Embeddings.

Custom instructions.

Learned workflows.

Reputation.

Delegated authority.

The longer an agent works for a person or organization, the more valuable this state becomes.

If that state is bound to one provider, switching agents may mean losing the agent's practical knowledge of the user.

The gatekeeper of the future may not control your files.

It may control your accumulated machine relationship.

This raises questions that current portability debates only partially capture.

Can memories be exported?

In what form?

Can permissions be transferred safely?

Should an agent's learned preferences move to another provider?

How do we separate user-owned history from provider-owned model weights or proprietary inference?

Can another agent understand the exported state?

Can provenance survive transfer?

Can revoked information remain revoked after migration?

Can a user migrate without leaking secrets embedded in long-term context?

The technical answers are still evolving.

The governance principle is already visible.

A person should not become trapped merely because a system became helpful.

Helpfulness should not compound into captivity.

This is the agent-era version of earned versus manufactured dependence.

A provider may deserve loyalty because its agent is excellent.

It should not need to make departure destructive.

A credible agent exit might therefore require some combination of structured memory export, permission manifests, tool inventories, provenance records, user-visible state, standard schemas, and clear separation between portable user context and non-portable proprietary capability.

This will not be easy.

Interoperability among agents can create privacy and security risks.

Standardized memory formats can expose sensitive data.

Transferring permissions can accidentally duplicate authority.

A departing agent may contain secrets that should not move.

A receiving agent may misinterpret context.

Again, the answer is not “port everything.”

It is to define portable boundaries deliberately rather than letting lock-in emerge accidentally.

A good exit system should answer five questions.

**What can leave?**

**In what form?**

**What cannot leave, and why?**

**What breaks when it leaves?**

**How long until the user is viable elsewhere?**

These questions transform exit from rhetoric into engineering.

The chapter began with a door.

A real exit is more than an opening in the wall.

It is a path that carries enough of the traveler to make departure possible.

Appeal says: change the gate's answer.

Bypass says: find another route.

Exit says: stop depending on this gatekeeper.

All three limit power differently.

Appeal creates correction.

Bypass creates resilience.

Exit creates discipline.

A gatekeeper that can survive all three deserves more trust because its users remain by choice rather than captivity.

The strongest system is not the one nobody can leave.

It is the one people can leave and often decide not to.

The next chapter turns from users leaving gates to agents approaching them on our behalf.

Once software can negotiate permissions continuously, the number of gate interactions explodes.

The gatekeeper will no longer always meet a person.

It will meet a delegate.
