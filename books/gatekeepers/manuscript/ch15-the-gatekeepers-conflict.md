# The Gatekeeper's Conflict

The most dangerous gatekeeper is not necessarily the strictest one.

It is the one who can profit from the answer.

Imagine a market with a single entrance. The owner of the entrance checks every merchant before allowing them inside. The inspection has a legitimate purpose. Counterfeit goods should be stopped. Dangerous products should be stopped. Fraudulent sellers should be stopped.

Then the owner opens a shop of his own.

Nothing about the inspection function has changed on paper.

Everything about it has changed in structure.

The person deciding whether you may enter is now also deciding whether you may compete with him.

This is the gatekeeper's conflict.

It appears whenever an institution performs two roles that pull in different directions: referee and player, auditor and consultant, marketplace and merchant, platform and application, certifier and seller, recommender and competitor, lender and adviser, reviewer and beneficiary.

The problem is not that people with conflicts always cheat.

That would be too simple.

The problem is that the institution has acquired a reason to prefer one legitimate-looking answer over another.

A conflict is an incentive before it is a scandal.

This distinction matters because gatekeeping debates often begin too late. We wait for proof of misconduct. We ask whether a particular ranking was manipulated, whether a particular competitor was excluded, whether a particular auditor softened a finding, whether a particular procurement officer favored a friend.

Those questions matter.

But a well-designed institution asks an earlier question:

**What happens when the gatekeeper benefits from one side of the gate?**

The securities world has wrestled with this problem for generations.

Public-company auditors are gatekeepers to the capital markets because investors rely on them to examine financial statements produced by management. Yet the company being audited hires and pays the auditor.

The arrangement is economically awkward.

The customer buys scrutiny of itself.

The Securities and Exchange Commission therefore treats auditor independence not as a matter of good intentions but as a structural requirement. Its framework asks whether relationships create mutual or conflicting interests, place the auditor in the position of auditing its own work, turn the auditor into management, or make the auditor an advocate for the client.

Notice the logic.

The rule does not wait for a dishonest audit.

It asks whether the arrangement threatens objective and impartial judgment.

This is a profound idea because it separates **integrity** from **independence**.

A person can be honest and still be badly positioned.

A company can employ decent people and still create incentives that lean in one direction.

A gate can produce biased pressure without producing a villain.

Institutions should therefore be designed for ordinary humans, not imaginary saints.

The same problem appears in digital platforms.

A platform can create extraordinary value by bringing buyers and sellers together, developers and users together, travelers and hotels together, viewers and creators together. It reduces search costs, supplies identity and payment infrastructure, enforces rules, and makes transactions possible among strangers.

Then the platform enters the market it governs.

It sells a product beside the sellers it ranks.

It offers an app beside the developers whose distribution it controls.

It provides a service beside the businesses whose visibility depends on its search results.

Again, dual roles are not automatically corrupt.

Vertical integration can improve products.

A platform operator may understand its infrastructure better than anyone and therefore build excellent complementary services. Integration can reduce transaction costs. It can make hardware and software work together. It can eliminate double marginalization. It can create a coherent user experience.

The existence of a conflict does not tell us the outcome.

It tells us where to look.

The U.S. Department of Justice's merger guidance makes this point directly for multi-sided platforms. A platform operator that is also a participant on the platform may have an incentive to advantage its own products over competing participants. The concern is not merely ownership. It is control over the environment in which competition occurs.

A merchant normally competes by improving price, quality, service, or reputation.

A merchant who owns the market's map has another option.

It can redraw the map.

This is why self-preferencing has become such a central concept in digital regulation.

In July 2026, the European Commission found Google in breach of the Digital Markets Act for giving preferential treatment to its own services in Google Search and separately for restrictions affecting developers' ability to steer users toward alternative purchasing channels through Google Play. The Commission imposed fines and ordered the non-compliance to end.

The legal details belong to the particular cases.

The institutional principle is broader.

A gatekeeper may be able to compete in two ways at once.

It can compete **inside** the rules.

And it can compete **through** the rules.

Those are not the same thing.

A hotel competes inside a travel marketplace when it changes its room price.

The marketplace competes through the rules when it changes which hotels appear first.

An app competes inside a mobile ecosystem when it improves its software.

The operating-system owner competes through the rules when it changes default placement, permissions, technical access, or distribution terms.

A seller competes inside a marketplace by improving its product.

The marketplace operator competes through the rules when it changes eligibility, search placement, fulfillment requirements, data access, or fees.

Rule-making power is a second competitive instrument.

This does not make every rule suspect.

The gatekeeper still has to govern.

Fraud must be controlled. Malware must be stopped. Search results must be ranked. Technical interfaces must evolve. Privacy protections must change. New products require new rules. A platform forbidden to alter anything because it competes somewhere in the ecosystem would eventually become unusable.

So the design problem cannot be solved by telling gatekeepers to stop governing.

It must be solved by separating legitimate governance from interested governance.

One useful test is **symmetry**.

Does the gatekeeper's own product face the same rule?

If a marketplace requires sellers to meet delivery standards, does its own retail operation meet comparable standards?

If an app platform requires developers to use a particular safety mechanism, do first-party apps operate under an equivalent constraint?

If a search service claims that ranking is based on relevance and quality, are affiliated services evaluated under the same criteria?

Symmetry is not always possible.

The platform operator may have technical responsibilities third parties do not. First-party software may legitimately need deeper system access. A marketplace may bear liabilities ordinary sellers do not.

But asymmetry should have a reason.

That reason should be inspectable.

Otherwise the gatekeeper can convert structural privilege into a thousand tiny exceptions.

A second test is **separation of evidence from interest**.

The people or systems deciding whether a participant passes the gate should not rely solely on information produced by the business unit that benefits from exclusion.

This principle is familiar from science, accounting, journalism, medicine, and courts.

Independent evidence does not eliminate judgment.

It reduces the ability of interested judgment to manufacture its own facts.

Suppose a platform's retail division says a third-party product creates a safety risk.

That may be true.

But the claim should be evaluated through a process whose success is not measured by the retail division's revenue.

The metric matters because organizations follow metrics even when nobody issues an explicit order.

A gatekeeper does not need a secret memo saying “hurt competitors.”

It may only need a review team whose promotions depend on goals correlated with the gatekeeper's commercial interests.

The conflict can travel through the org chart without ever becoming a sentence.

This is why independence is partly a budget question.

Who pays the reviewer?

Who sets the reviewer's goals?

Who can overrule the reviewer?

Who controls the data?

Who benefits when the reviewer says no?

Who suffers when the reviewer says yes?

The answers reveal more than the ethics policy.

The auditor example makes this especially clear.

Auditors are paid by the firms they audit because someone has to pay them and the alternative arrangements have their own problems. The response has not been to pretend the economic relationship disappears. The response has been to surround it with independence rules, audit committees, professional obligations, disclosure, inspection, and restrictions on relationships and services that can compromise objectivity.

A conflict can be governed without being denied.

This is a more mature posture than the common institutional defense: “Trust us, our teams are separate.”

Separation is useful only if it changes incentives, information flows, authority, or review.

Two teams with different Slack channels are not independent if the same executive can change both decisions to improve the same quarterly number.

A wall drawn on an org chart is not a wall.

Good separation has consequences.

The gatekeeping function may have a protected mandate.

Its leadership may report through a different chain.

Its performance metrics may exclude commercial outcomes that create bias.

Its decisions may be logged and reviewable.

Exceptions may require written reasons.

The regulated party may have an appeal path outside the interested business unit.

Material rule changes may require notice and evidence.

The gatekeeper's own products may be subject to comparable documentation.

These mechanisms do not make people pure.

They make influence more expensive and more visible.

That is often the practical purpose of governance.

The goal is not to remove temptation from human affairs.

It is to stop temptation from becoming invisible infrastructure.

Conflicts also arise when the gatekeeper sells relief from the gate.

Consider a platform that controls visibility and also sells advertising.

Organic ranking and paid placement can coexist perfectly well. Advertising finances enormous amounts of useful infrastructure.

But the combination creates a delicate boundary.

If ordinary visibility is degraded in ways that make payment increasingly necessary, the gatekeeper can profit from scarcity it helps create.

The toll collector can influence traffic.

Again, motive is difficult to prove and often beside the point.

The design question is whether paid and unpaid systems are clearly distinguished, whether ranking rules are coherent, whether participants can understand what money buys, and whether the platform has incentives to make the unpaid path artificially bad.

The same structure appears in expedited review.

A government agency, certification body, or platform may charge for faster processing because priority service consumes resources.

That can be reasonable.

But if the ordinary queue becomes unusably slow, the premium lane stops looking like a service and starts looking like ransom.

The gatekeeper profits from the pain of its own gate.

This is a recurring pattern:

- create or control a bottleneck;
- offer a paid route around the bottleneck;
- call the payment a convenience.

Sometimes it really is a convenience.

Sometimes it is monetized dependency.

The distinction depends on the baseline.

Would the ordinary gate still be reasonably usable without the paid option?

This is why service-level expectations matter in conflicted systems.

A gatekeeper should not be able to justify a premium lane by neglecting the free one.

Conflict also changes the meaning of information.

Platforms often see more than the participants they govern.

A marketplace can observe search queries, conversion rates, prices, returns, customer complaints, and emerging demand across thousands of sellers. An app platform can observe categories of software gaining traction. A payment intermediary can see transaction patterns. A cloud provider can see resource demand.

Aggregated data can improve the system.

It can also reveal where to compete.

This creates an informational conflict.

The participant must expose information to use the gate.

The gatekeeper can potentially use that information to become a participant.

The merchant teaches the marketplace where the opportunity is.

The developer teaches the platform what users want.

The supplier teaches the buyer what a new category is worth.

Then the gatekeeper may enter with superior information about the field.

The problem is not learning.

All institutions learn from activity.

The problem is **purpose drift**.

Information collected to operate the gate can migrate into a competitive weapon.

A good governance system therefore asks what data was collected for, who may use it, whether it is aggregated, whether sensitive participant-level information is firewalled, and whether the participant had a reasonable expectation that operational data would become competitive intelligence.

This is another place where formal consent can be misleading.

A seller may technically agree to broad terms because refusing means leaving the marketplace.

The existence of a checkbox does not erase dependency.

Gatekeeper conflicts are especially difficult when users benefit from integration.

Suppose a search engine places its own map directly in the results.

That may be extremely convenient.

The user gets an answer without another click.

A rule that mechanically forbade every first-party integration could make the product worse.

The challenge is that the same design can improve the user experience and disadvantage a rival.

Both can be true.

This is why conflict analysis should not reduce to identifying who was harmed.

A competitor's pain is not proof of bad governance.

Competition is supposed to hurt competitors.

The stronger question is whether the gatekeeper used a power unavailable through ordinary competition.

Did it win because its product was better?

Or because it controlled admission, ranking, defaults, data, interoperability, or the rules under which rivals had to operate?

Even this distinction can blur.

Control of the platform may be part of why the integrated product is better.

The operating system can make its own features work smoothly because they are built together.

The marketplace can integrate logistics because it owns the transaction flow.

A bank can price a product better because it sees account history.

The gatekeeper's advantage may be simultaneously efficient and exclusionary.

Institutional design therefore needs proportional remedies rather than moral labels.

Sometimes disclosure is enough.

Sometimes equal-access rules are appropriate.

Sometimes interoperability is the answer.

Sometimes data-use restrictions are necessary.

Sometimes independent review is necessary.

Sometimes structural separation is warranted.

Sometimes the integration should remain because its benefits exceed the competitive risk.

The remedy should match the mechanism of conflict.

This principle matters because structural separation is powerful medicine.

Breaking a function apart can reduce conflicts but also destroy coordination benefits. Requiring every platform layer to be owned by a different company could create fragmented products, duplicated costs, security seams, and slower innovation.

Conversely, relying only on promises of neutrality can leave the conflicted actor with every lever intact.

The correct question is not “integration or separation?”

It is “which powers must be separated for this gate to remain trustworthy?”

Auditing provides a useful analogy again.

Society does not prohibit audit firms from earning money.

It restricts relationships and activities that compromise the audit function.

The point is not asceticism.

The point is that the gatekeeper's economic model must leave room for an answer the payer dislikes.

That sentence generalizes.

A trustworthy gate must be able to produce an answer against the gatekeeper's immediate interest.

If every incentive points toward one answer, formal discretion is not enough.

This gives us another diagnostic: the **expensive no**.

Can the gatekeeper say no to itself?

Can the app-review team reject a first-party application?

Can the marketplace compliance function penalize the platform's own retail operation?

Can the auditor lose a lucrative client rather than bless questionable accounting?

Can the safety team delay a launch favored by executives?

Can the procurement function reject the CEO's preferred vendor?

Can the credit committee decline a deal championed by the revenue team?

Institutions often advertise independence by describing routine decisions.

The expensive no tests it.

Independence becomes real when exercising it costs someone powerful something they wanted.

This is also why exception logs matter.

Many organizations have good written rules and informal override cultures.

The official gate says one thing.

A senior person sends a message.

The gate moves.

Some exceptions are necessary. Rules cannot anticipate every case. A system without exception authority can become brittle and absurd.

But exceptions should leave fossils.

Who requested the override?

What rule was overridden?

Why?

For how long?

Who approved it?

What happened afterward?

An exception without memory becomes precedent without accountability.

A conflicted gatekeeper especially needs memory because selective flexibility is one of the easiest ways to favor allies while claiming neutral rules.

The rule remains equal.

The exceptions are not.

This is where transparency must become statistical rather than anecdotal.

Publishing one policy is useful.

Publishing aggregate patterns can be more revealing.

How often are applications rejected?

How long do reviews take?

How often are decisions reversed on appeal?

Do affiliated products receive different treatment?

How often are exceptions granted, and to whom?

Do similarly situated participants experience similar outcomes?

No single statistic proves fairness.

Patterns expose where to investigate.

The same principle applies to regulators themselves.

A regulator can become conflicted through political incentives, industry capture, revolving-door employment, budget dependence, or institutional self-protection. Public status does not abolish incentives.

Neither does nonprofit status.

Neither does a mission statement.

Every gatekeeper has interests.

The question is whether those interests are aligned with the gate's purpose and constrained when they are not.

This leads to a useful taxonomy.

A gatekeeper can have at least five kinds of conflict.

**Commercial conflict:** the gatekeeper earns more when one participant wins or loses.

**Competitive conflict:** the gatekeeper competes with the participant it governs.

**Informational conflict:** the gatekeeper can reuse privileged information obtained through the gate.

**Institutional conflict:** the gatekeeper's reputation or authority depends on not admitting its own past mistakes.

**Personal conflict:** an individual decision-maker has relationships, compensation, or interests tied to the outcome.

These conflicts require different controls.

Disclosure may help with a personal conflict.

A data firewall may address an informational conflict.

Non-discrimination rules may address a competitive conflict.

Independent appeal may address institutional self-protection.

Compensation design may reduce commercial pressure.

One generic “conflict of interest policy” is rarely enough.

The deeper principle is to follow the incentive to the decision.

Ask what the gatekeeper wants.

Then ask whether the gatekeeper controls a lever that can produce it.

Then ask whether anyone can see the lever move.

This method is more useful than asking whether the institution is good or bad.

Good institutions have conflicts.

Good people have conflicts.

The purpose of governance is not to discover purity.

It is to make conflicted power legible and bounded.

The European Union's Digital Markets Act is one contemporary attempt to do this for the largest digital platforms. It identifies designated gatekeepers and imposes obligations intended to preserve fairness and contestability in core platform services. Among the concerns are self-preferencing, steering restrictions, interoperability, and access conditions.

The law is significant partly because it treats gatekeeper status as a structural condition rather than waiting for every disputed practice to be litigated from zero.

That approach has costs.

Bright-line obligations can be overinclusive. Technology changes quickly. Rules written for one platform architecture can fit another badly. Compliance can itself favor large incumbents that can afford armies of lawyers and engineers.

Gatekeeper regulation can become a gate.

This book should not forget its own argument when discussing its favorite subject.

Every remedy creates another decision point.

Every oversight body becomes capable of delay, error, capture, and overreach.

The question is never whether to have gatekeepers.

It is how to govern gates all the way down.

That includes the people who police conflicts.

A competition regulator can make a bad call.

An independent reviewer can become complacent.

A compliance team can protect itself by rejecting anything unusual.

A board committee can become ceremonial.

Separation reduces some failure modes and creates others.

This is why the best conflict controls are plural.

No single mechanism should carry the entire burden.

Use incentives.

Use separation.

Use disclosure.

Use audit trails.

Use appeals.

Use competition.

Use portability.

Use external oversight where consequences justify it.

Most importantly, preserve the possibility of exit.

A gatekeeper's conflict becomes most dangerous when the governed party cannot leave.

If a seller dislikes one marketplace's rules and can move customers elsewhere cheaply, the platform's conflict is disciplined by competition.

If an app developer can distribute through another channel without losing most users, the platform's discretion is bounded.

If a company can change auditors easily, commercial pressure weakens.

If a merchant can change payment rails, exclusion loses some force.

If every practical path leads back to the same gatekeeper, conflict becomes governance.

This connects the chapter to what comes next.

When a gate makes a mistake—or merely makes a decision the subject believes is mistaken—appeal becomes the test of whether authority can correct itself.

But appeals are not free.

They consume time, expertise, money, attention, and often emotional endurance.

A gate can technically permit challenge while making challenge practically impossible.

That is the next form of power.

For now, the rule is simpler.

Do not ask only whether the gatekeeper is trustworthy.

Ask what the gatekeeper gains from your answer.

Then design the gate so that trust is not the only thing standing between permission and profit.
