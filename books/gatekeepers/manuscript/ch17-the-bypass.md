# The Bypass

Every gate eventually teaches people where the fence is weak.

The lesson may be innocent.

Use a different entrance.

Call the person instead of filling out the form.

Pay cash.

Install the software from the web.

Ask a friend who already has access.

Split the purchase into smaller amounts.

Open the file locally.

Use another payment rail.

Ship through another port.

Choose another jurisdiction.

Build the tool yourself.

The lesson may also be dangerous or illegal.

Evade the safety check.

Hide the transaction.

Misrepresent the application.

Bribe the official.

Steal the credential.

The word **bypass** covers both.

That is why it deserves care.

A bypass is simply a route that avoids the ordinary gate.

Its moral character depends on the gate, the route, the reason, and the consequences.

Sometimes bypass is fraud.

Sometimes bypass is competition.

Sometimes it is innovation.

Sometimes it is mercy.

Sometimes it is an alarm bell telling the gatekeeper that the official path has become unusable.

The first mistake institutions make is treating every workaround as disobedience.

The second is romanticizing every workaround as resistance.

Both reactions save us from having to inspect the system.

A better question is:

**Why did a reasonable person prefer the unofficial route?**

This question does not excuse misconduct.

It diagnoses pressure.

Suppose an employee needs a simple tool to complete a project. The approved software catalog does not contain it. Vendor review will take three months. The project is due Friday. The employee signs up for a consumer cloud service with a personal credit card and uploads company data.

Security calls this shadow IT.

The employee calls it getting the job done.

Both descriptions can be correct.

The employee may have created a serious data risk.

The procurement and security system may also have created a schedule in which compliant work was impossible.

The bypass is not proof that the employee was right.

It is evidence about the gate.

Workarounds are telemetry.

They show where formal process and practical need have diverged.

Organizations often destroy this telemetry by punishing the bypass without asking what generated it.

The unauthorized tool is removed.

The ticket is closed.

The delay remains.

A month later another employee finds another tool.

The organization concludes that employees are reckless.

Employees conclude that governance is ceremonial obstruction.

Both sides learn contempt.

This is how shadow systems grow.

The official system remains on the org chart.

The real system develops underneath it.

Every large organization has some version of this.

Spreadsheets that substitute for enterprise software.

Private messaging groups that substitute for ticket queues.

Personal relationships that substitute for procurement.

Manual exports that substitute for integrations.

Shared passwords that substitute for identity management.

Cash reimbursements that substitute for purchasing workflows.

Side agreements that substitute for policy.

The shadow system is usually worse governed.

It is also often faster.

That combination makes it durable.

People do not bypass gates merely because they dislike rules.

They bypass gates when the expected value of compliance falls below the expected value of another route.

We can express this crudely.

A person compares:

**cost of official path**

against

**cost of bypass + probability of detection × consequence of detection + added risk**.

Nobody performs the arithmetic exactly.

Behavior performs it for them.

If the official path takes two minutes and works reliably, few people invent elaborate workarounds.

If the official path takes six weeks, requires three approvals, and still might fail without explanation, bypass becomes economically attractive.

This suggests a concept we can call the **bypass premium**.

How much extra cost or risk are people willing to accept to avoid the gate?

If employees routinely pay more for unapproved tools, the gate is imposing a hidden tax.

If customers drive farther to use another branch, the gate is imposing a hidden tax.

If developers accept a smaller market to distribute outside a store, the gate is imposing a hidden tax.

If merchants accept less convenient payment methods to escape a processor, the gate is imposing a hidden tax.

If patients travel across borders for care, the gate is imposing a hidden tax.

The bypass premium is information.

It tells us how painful the official route has become.

Sometimes the premium is justified.

Airport security should impose inconvenience compared with walking directly onto an airplane.

A controlled-drug prescription should be harder than buying chewing gum.

Access to production infrastructure should be harder than opening a public webpage.

A nuclear facility should have an enormous bypass premium because bypassing the gate should be extraordinarily difficult.

The objective is not zero friction.

It is intentional friction.

The important question is whether the friction corresponds to the risk being controlled.

This brings us to legitimate bypasses.

Good systems often create them deliberately.

A fire exit is a bypass.

It allows people to ignore the ordinary entrance and circulation rules when the building is dangerous.

A break-glass administrator account is a bypass.

It allows emergency access outside normal permission flows when the normal identity system fails.

An emergency medical protocol is a bypass.

It allows action before ordinary paperwork can be completed.

An alternate payment rail is a bypass.

It allows trade when the preferred rail is unavailable or unsuitable.

A manual override is a bypass.

It allows judgment when automation encounters an exceptional case.

A good bypass is not a hole in governance.

It is governance for abnormal conditions.

This distinction is essential.

Institutions sometimes try to eliminate all bypasses in the name of control.

They create brittle systems.

When the normal gate fails, work stops because nobody is authorized to do the obvious thing.

Other institutions tolerate undocumented bypasses everywhere.

They create chaotic systems in which the official gate is theater.

The mature design is **bounded bypass**.

Define exceptional routes.

Limit who can use them.

Log their use.

Make them temporary when appropriate.

Review what happened afterward.

Do not make the emergency path easier than the normal path for ordinary cases.

But make sure the emergency path actually works during an emergency.

This is the same logic as a circuit breaker.

The bypass protects the larger system from the failure of its own control mechanism.

Medicine provides an unusually vivid example.

The ordinary route for a new drug is clinical development followed by regulatory review and approval. That gate exists because medicines can harm people and because evidence of safety and effectiveness matters.

But a patient with a serious or immediately life-threatening disease may not have time for the ordinary sequence.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration therefore has expanded-access pathways—sometimes called compassionate use—that can allow treatment with investigational medical products outside clinical trials when specified conditions are met, including the absence of comparable satisfactory alternatives and a judgment that potential benefit justifies potential risk.

The United States also has a statutory Right to Try pathway for certain eligible patients and investigational drugs. Under that framework, FDA does not review or approve individual Right to Try requests; eligible patients, physicians, and sponsors interact under the statutory criteria, and manufacturers are not required to provide the product.

These are not lawless holes in drug regulation.

They are formalized exceptions created because the ordinary gate's time horizon can conflict with the patient's time horizon.

The gate protects populations.

The bypass recognizes the individual at the edge of the distribution.

That tension cannot be solved perfectly.

Expanded access can create risks. Investigational products may not work. They may cause harm. Scarce supply can be diverted from trials. Uncontrolled use can complicate evidence generation. Manufacturers may face operational burdens. Hope can distort judgment.

That is why legitimate bypasses still have criteria.

Emergency does not abolish risk.

It changes the acceptable tradeoff.

This principle travels far beyond medicine.

A bypass should often change the **risk budget**, not erase the risk model.

The emergency administrator account may require stronger logging.

The expedited procurement may require a spending cap and post-purchase review.

The manual override may require a second person's acknowledgment.

The alternate identity process may require later reconciliation.

The emergency medical route may require informed consent and reporting.

The bypass is not “no rules.”

It is a different rule set for a different state of the world.

Digital distribution gives us another kind of bypass: the competitive bypass.

For years, mobile app distribution on some devices was tightly concentrated through first-party app stores. In the European Union, the Digital Markets Act now requires designated gatekeepers to allow forms of alternative app distribution, including third-party app stores or web distribution under the applicable framework. The Commission presents these channels as ways to reduce developer reliance on the gatekeepers' stores while preserving room for strictly necessary and proportionate integrity measures.

The language matters.

What looks like bypass from the incumbent gatekeeper's perspective can look like **contestability** from the market's perspective.

An alternate route can discipline the main gate.

If developers can reach users another way, the primary store must justify its fees and services more convincingly.

If merchants can use another payment rail, the preferred rail faces competitive pressure.

If a traveler can choose another carrier, the first carrier's policies have less force.

If software can interoperate through open standards, one vendor's permission matters less.

The existence of bypass changes bargaining before anyone uses it.

This is why gatekeepers often care deeply about whether bypass is technically possible.

A locked gate is powerful.

A locked gate beside an open path is mostly a suggestion.

But alternate routes can reduce valuable protections.

Software installed outside a curated store may face different review and support arrangements. Direct payments may provide different dispute protections. Informal vendors may not satisfy security requirements. Cash can reduce traceability. Peer-to-peer distribution can spread malware. Off-platform transactions can make fraud recovery harder.

The fact that bypass creates competition does not make it safe.

The correct design question is whether safety protections can travel with the user rather than requiring monopoly over the route.

This is an important distinction.

Suppose an operating system needs to protect device integrity.

One design says: only software distributed through our store may run.

Another says: software from other sources may run if it satisfies specified security conditions, permissions, signing, warnings, or isolation requirements.

Both designs can pursue safety.

The second separates the protective function from exclusive distribution.

This is a recurring opportunity in gate design.

Ask whether the control can attach to the **activity** rather than the **channel**.

Can identity verification travel across providers?

Can fraud protection work across payment rails?

Can credential standards work across employers?

Can safety certification work across marketplaces?

Can consent travel with data?

Portable controls make bypass less dangerous because leaving the gatekeeper does not require leaving governance behind.

This is how standards turn escape routes into ecosystems.

Bypass also appears in geography.

People and firms choose jurisdictions.

A company incorporates in one state rather than another.

A ship registers under one flag rather than another.

A patient travels for treatment.

A worker crosses a border for opportunity.

A business routes trade through another port.

Some of this is ordinary competition among jurisdictions.

Some is regulatory arbitrage.

Some is evasion.

Again, labels are insufficient.

Jurisdiction shopping can discipline bad rules by allowing exit.

It can also create a race to the bottom when actors escape rules designed to protect people who cannot easily exit.

The difference often depends on who bears the externality.

If a company moves to a jurisdiction with simpler incorporation rules and harms no one, competition may improve governance.

If a company moves pollution to a jurisdiction with weak environmental enforcement while selling into the same market, the bypass transfers costs to others.

This gives us a central test:

**Who pays for the bypass?**

If the person taking the alternate route bears most of its additional risk, the case for allowing choice is stronger.

If the bypass pushes risk onto strangers, workers, customers, taxpayers, or the public, stronger controls may be justified.

A person choosing a less protected payment method mainly risks their own recovery rights.

A company bypassing fire codes risks employees and neighbors.

A patient choosing an investigational treatment accepts personal medical risk, though scarce supply and evidence effects can reach others.

A developer bypassing a store may accept more installation friction, but insecure software can still harm users.

Externalities determine how private a bypass really is.

This is why “my choice” cannot settle every gatekeeping dispute.

Choices live in systems.

Bypass can also expose **false necessity**.

Institutions often describe their gate as the only safe way because it is the only way they currently support.

Then someone builds another route.

The new route works.

The old necessity is revealed as architecture.

This is one of innovation's recurring moves.

Banks once seemed necessary for many payment functions that specialized financial technology firms later separated.

Centralized offices seemed necessary for some kinds of work until remote tools separated coordination from co-location.

App stores bundled discovery, payment, trust, installation, updates, and distribution; alternative channels show that those functions can be recombined differently.

Credentials bundle learning, signaling, and permission; skills assessments can sometimes bypass parts of the bundle.

Innovation often begins as an unauthorized decomposition of a gate.

The innovator asks:

Which function is actually necessary?

Which function is merely bundled because history put it there?

This is why incumbents can mistake unbundling for evasion.

The bypass threatens not only the gate's control but its theory of itself.

Yet bypass has its own pathologies.

The workaround can become permanent infrastructure.

A spreadsheet created for one emergency becomes the system of record for ten years.

A shared account created during an outage becomes normal access.

A manual override becomes the expected path for important clients.

An exception for one vendor becomes a shadow procurement category.

Temporary bypasses accumulate until the formal system is no longer descriptive.

This is **bypass debt**.

Like technical debt, it is not automatically bad.

A shortcut can be rational under pressure.

The debt appears when the organization forgets that the shortcut exists or fails to repay the risks it introduced.

Every bypass should therefore have an afterlife.

Was it used?

Why?

Did it work?

What risk did it create?

Should the normal gate change?

Should the bypass become a supported alternate route?

Should it be closed?

Does it need stronger controls?

A bypass without review becomes folklore.

A bypass with review becomes research.

This gives us a powerful institutional habit: **mine the workarounds**.

Ask frontline workers what they do when the official process fails.

Ask customers how they actually complete the task.

Ask developers what unofficial integration they rely on.

Ask nurses which form they fill out later because the real work cannot wait.

Ask procurement teams which vendors are repeatedly purchased through exceptions.

Ask security teams which controls people most often route around.

Do not begin by punishing the answer.

First understand it.

The workaround may reveal negligence.

It may reveal fraud.

It may also reveal the next version of the system.

This is especially important for agents.

Agents are natural bypass machines.

They can discover alternate APIs, transform file formats, route around unavailable tools, retry through another provider, synthesize missing glue code, and coordinate across interfaces humans would find tedious.

That capability is enormously useful.

It is also dangerous.

An agent optimized only for task completion may interpret every gate as an obstacle.

Permission denied?

Try another credential.

API unavailable?

Scrape the interface.

Purchase blocked?

Use another account.

Approval required?

Find a route that does not require approval.

This is cleverness without governance.

For agents, the difference between resilience and circumvention must be explicit.

A good agent asks:

Is the gate expressing a safety, legal, privacy, authorization, financial, or user-intent boundary?

Or is it merely a technical failure in one route?

Can I use an authorized alternate route?

Will the alternate route preserve the user's constraints?

Does it increase risk to someone else?

Should the user know that the route changed?

Does the bypass need approval?

This is the agent version of bounded bypass.

Resilience means finding another legitimate path to the same authorized objective.

Circumvention means defeating a boundary that was part of the authorization itself.

The difference is not whether the workaround is technically possible.

It is whether the permission survives the route change.

This principle will matter more as agents become capable of long chains of action.

A human confronted with a denied API call may stop.

An agent can try fifty alternatives in seconds.

Persistence becomes a security property.

Systems will need machine-readable signals that distinguish “temporary failure, retry elsewhere” from “not authorized, do not route around.”

Today both may appear as errors.

Tomorrow that ambiguity will be expensive.

Good gates should communicate the semantics of refusal.

Rate limited.

Temporarily unavailable.

Not entitled.

Policy prohibited.

Needs additional approval.

Human review required.

Alternative channel permitted.

Alternative channel prohibited.

These distinctions let resilient systems remain obedient systems.

Bypass also clarifies the relationship between voice and exit.

Chapter 16 examined appeal: stay inside the system and challenge the gate.

Bypass says: remain focused on the objective and use another route.

Chapter 18 will examine a more complete response: leave the system itself.

These are different strategies.

**Appeal** asks the gatekeeper to change the answer.

**Bypass** avoids the gate while pursuing the same objective.

**Exit** abandons the relationship or system in favor of another.

A healthy environment often needs all three.

Appeal corrects errors.

Bypass creates resilience and experimentation.

Exit creates competitive discipline.

When all three are impossible, the gatekeeper becomes sovereign.

When all three are effortless, governance may become impossible.

The design problem is balance.

A good gate should be strong enough that important protections cannot be casually ignored.

It should be permeable enough that exceptional cases do not become tragedies.

It should expose alternate legitimate routes where monopoly over the route is unnecessary.

It should learn from repeated workarounds.

It should close dangerous holes.

It should convert useful hacks into supported paths.

And it should know the difference between a person escaping bureaucracy and a person escaping responsibility.

That difference is rarely visible from the fact of bypass alone.

You have to inspect the reason, the risk, and who pays.

The fence tells you what the institution intended.

The path worn through the grass tells you what people needed.

A serious gatekeeper studies both.
