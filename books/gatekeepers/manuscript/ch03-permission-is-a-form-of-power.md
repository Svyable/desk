# Permission Is a Form of Power

In the 1870s, a farmer in Iowa could own the grain in his wagon and still discover that ownership was not the most important fact about it.

The grain had to move.

By then Chicago had become the great transfer point between the agricultural interior of the United States and markets farther east. Grain arrived by rail and water, entered enormous elevators, was weighed and stored in bulk, and then left again by vessel or railroad. The elevator was more than a building with bins. It was a mechanical joint in a continental system.

If your wheat had to pass through that joint, the person controlling the elevator possessed a kind of power that was not captured by the deed to your farm.

This became the background to *Munn v. Illinois*, decided by the U.S. Supreme Court in 1877. Illinois had adopted rules for public grain warehouses, including licensing requirements and maximum charges. Munn & Scott, operators of Chicago's North-western Elevator, challenged the state's authority after being convicted for operating without the required license and charging more than the statutory maximum.

The owners had an intuitively strong argument. They had leased the ground, built the elevator with private capital, and conducted a private business. Why should the state be allowed to tell them what they could charge for using their own property?

The Court answered by looking past the title to the position the property occupied in commerce.

The factual record described fourteen Chicago warehouses adapted to the grain-elevator business, owned by roughly thirty people but controlled by nine business firms. Rates had been established jointly from year to year. Vast quantities of grain from western states had to pass through these facilities on their way toward eastern markets. The Court accepted the description of the elevators as something close to a virtual monopoly and noted that the businesses stood at a gateway through which commerce passed.

That language matters more here than the later doctrinal career of the case. American constitutional law did not freeze in 1877, and no sensible argument about today's platforms, railroads, utilities, or warehouses can simply paste the holding of *Munn* onto a modern dispute. The useful point is older and more durable than the exact doctrine.

Private ownership and public dependence can occupy the same piece of ground.

An owner may possess a facility while thousands of other people possess practical reasons they cannot easily avoid using it. The legal title says who owns the gate. The economic structure says who must pass through it.

Those are different questions.

The nineteenth century generated many examples because industrialization created enormous gains from connecting systems. Rail lines, bridges, ports, warehouses, telegraph networks, and exchanges made trade faster and cheaper. The same infrastructure that enlarged markets also created narrow points inside them. If ten routes converge on one bridge, whoever controls the bridge does not need to own the ten routes to affect all of them.

St. Louis made the geometry almost theatrical.

The Mississippi River divided railroad systems approaching the city from the east and west. Entering St. Louis and connecting traffic across the river depended on bridges, ferries, tracks, yards, and terminal facilities shaped by the city's topography. Over time, a group of major railroads consolidated control over the principal terminal infrastructure through the Terminal Railroad Association of St. Louis.

By the time the arrangement reached the Supreme Court in 1912, the physical facts did much of the argument. The Court described a situation in which the association and its proprietary or tenant lines controlled the critical bridges, ferries, and terminal routes. Because of the geography, it was practically impossible for another railroad to enter or pass through St. Louis in commercially useful fashion without relying on facilities the association controlled.

The member railroads did not own every shipment in the country. They did not own every locomotive. They did not own all the factories in St. Louis. They controlled something smaller and, in the relevant moment, more consequential: the transition between outside and inside.

The Supreme Court held that the combination unlawfully restrained interstate commerce and required a remedy that allowed nonmember railroads to use the terminal facilities on equal terms.

The case has often been discussed as an ancestor of what later became known as essential-facilities reasoning in antitrust. That doctrine has its own contested history. Again, the useful lesson for this book is more basic.

A bottleneck converts permission into bargaining power.

Suppose I own a factory and you own the only bridge capable of carrying my finished product to the port. My property rights remain real. I can decide what to manufacture, whom to employ, whether to sell the factory, and what color to paint it. Yet every commercial plan that requires the port contains an unstated condition: you must let my trucks cross.

That condition changes our relationship.

If there are five comparable bridges, your refusal is annoying. If there is one, your refusal can determine whether my factory functions. The bridge owner's power therefore comes not simply from ownership but from the absence of an adequate alternative.

This is why a gatekeeper can be powerful while owning surprisingly little of the value on either side of the gate.

A payment network need not own the merchant's inventory or the customer's bank balance. An app store need not own the developer's code or the user's phone. A professional licensing board need not employ the worker or purchase the service. An identity provider need not own the user's account at the destination. A cloud platform need not own the customer's business. The gatekeeper's leverage comes from controlling a passage that other people have already built plans around.

The word *permission* can make this sound bureaucratic. It is better to think in terms of dependencies.

A dependency is any condition your plan needs but does not control.

Some dependencies are natural. Crops need rain. Ships need sufficient water. Human bodies need oxygen. Others are technological. Software depends on an operating system, a power grid, a network connection, a domain name system, a certificate chain, perhaps a cloud service. Still others are institutional. A business may depend on a bank account, insurance, a permit, a professional credential, a wholesaler, a marketplace, a distributor, a landlord, or a regulator.

When a dependency has many substitutes, it is background competition. When the substitutes narrow, it begins to resemble a gate.

The practical power of a gate therefore lives in the counterfactual: what happens if the answer is no?

That question is more revealing than market share alone.

A small organization can have extraordinary power over a narrow population if the consequences of its refusal are severe. A professional board in one state may be tiny compared with a multinational company, but to a person whose lawful occupation depends on its license, the board can dominate a specific transition. A building inspector may have no interest in owning a developer's property, yet withholding approval can stop occupancy. A university registrar may control no student's future career, yet a missing transcript can block the next institution from recognizing completed work.

Conversely, a very large company can control a service that is easy to replace and therefore possess less gatekeeping power than its size suggests.

Scale matters. Substitutability matters more than rhetoric about scale alone.

The same is true of price.

We usually think of economic power as the ability to charge more. Gatekeeping power can be exercised through money, but it can also be exercised through conditions.

Use this payment method.

Provide this data.

Accept this dispute process.

Use our identity system.

Buy the approved component.

Do not communicate with the customer in that way.

Route the transaction through this interface.

Submit to this audit.

Wait.

The gatekeeper's most valuable demand may not be a toll at all. It may be control over the architecture around the transaction.

This is one reason gatekeeping relationships often expand. Once a party can condition access on compliance, it can attach new requirements to the original permission. A rule that began as a safety requirement can acquire reporting requirements. A marketplace admission rule can grow into pricing, fulfillment, advertising, data, and dispute rules. A procurement qualification can expand from proof of competence into cybersecurity questionnaires, insurance minimums, diversity reporting, financial disclosures, contractual clauses, audit rights, and software integrations.

Some additions are sensible. Complex systems learn. New risks appear. Fraudsters adapt. Cybersecurity requirements that would have seemed exotic thirty years ago are ordinary now because the networked surface of organizations changed.

But permission creates a convenient place to collect obligations.

If an institution already controls the door, every other institutional concern is tempted to stand beside it.

This can produce what might be called policy accretion without requiring any grand design. One department wants a certification added. Another wants a disclosure. Legal wants an indemnity clause. Security wants a scan. Finance wants a credit check. Compliance wants another attestation. Each requirement has a reason in isolation. The applicant experiences the sum.

The gate becomes a stack.

For a large incumbent, the stack may be irritating but manageable. It has lawyers, compliance staff, established systems, and years of familiarity with the process. For a small entrant, the same stack can function as a moat even if no single rule was written to exclude competition.

This is where permission power begins to shape markets indirectly.

A gatekeeper does not need to choose winners explicitly if the cost of crossing is easier for some participants to bear than others.

Consider a certification that costs $100,000 to obtain and maintain. If it is genuinely necessary to prevent a million-dollar safety failure, the expense may be justified. But the burden is not experienced equally. A company with $10 billion in revenue treats the certification as overhead. A startup with $300,000 in capital may treat it as a decision about whether to exist.

The rule can be neutral in language and unequal in consequence.

The same pattern appears with time. A six-month approval period affects a company with eighteen months of cash differently from one with permanent financing. A requirement to maintain lawyers in several jurisdictions favors firms already operating at scale. A demand for years of historical data is trivially easier for an incumbent than a new entrant. A performance bond may be prudent and still advantage companies with stronger balance sheets.

Permission power is therefore often visible in the shape of the survivors.

Who repeatedly makes it through the gate? Who never applies? Which business models disappear before anyone issues a formal rejection? Which entrants choose to sell themselves to incumbents because independent passage is too expensive? Which professionals move to another state rather than repeat a licensing process? Which customers remain with an unsatisfactory service because their accumulated identity, history, data, reputation, or social graph cannot travel?

A gate can structure behavior long before it closes.

This anticipatory effect distinguishes permission from a simple transaction. People begin designing for the gatekeeper.

A developer studies platform review guidelines before writing the feature. A bank configures its systems around supervisory expectations. A pharmaceutical company designs trials around the evidentiary standard it expects regulators to apply. A contractor buys insurance because procurement will demand it. A student chooses courses that transfer. A merchant modifies descriptions to avoid moderation triggers. A worker invests years in the education required by a licensing regime.

This is not necessarily bad. Many of the best gates work precisely because people conform before reaching them. Building codes matter because architects design with them in mind. Aviation rules work because operators organize around them. Financial-capital requirements change balance sheets before a bank approaches insolvency.

The gate's influence is strongest when nobody needs to say no.

Power often works through expected permission.

This also complicates measurement. If a platform rejects one percent of submitted applications, does that mean the gate is weak? Not if another twenty percent of potential applicants never submit because they know their product violates the rules. If a regulator formally denies almost nothing, is it permissive? Not necessarily if companies abandon projects after preliminary meetings or redesign them to meet expectations.

Counting refusals captures only the visible edge.

A mature analysis of gatekeeping has to look upstream at adaptation.

The distinction between ownership and permission also changes how we think about competition.

Two companies may compete fiercely in the market behind a gate while depending on the same gatekeeper. Ten merchants can undercut one another while all relying on one marketplace. Hundreds of software firms can compete while depending on the same operating systems, distribution channels, certificate authorities, or cloud interfaces. Thousands of exporters can compete while using the same ports and customs systems.

Competition downstream does not guarantee competition at the gate.

This was the concern embedded in the St. Louis terminal dispute. Railroads could compete across vast distances, yet if meaningful entrance to a central city depended on common terminal facilities controlled by a subset of those same competitors, the chokepoint could shape the competition occurring elsewhere.

The arrangement was also efficient in important ways. Duplicating bridges, tracks, switching yards, and terminal facilities is expensive. Shared infrastructure can reduce waste and improve coordination. A single integrated terminal may be better engineering than a thicket of redundant terminals.

This creates a recurring tension in network industries: integration can make the system better and the gatekeeper stronger at the same time.

Breaking the infrastructure into many competing copies may sacrifice efficiency. Leaving it unified may create leverage. One answer, used repeatedly across legal history, is to separate ownership from the right to arbitrary exclusion. The owner can retain the asset while access is governed by duties of reasonable, nondiscriminatory, or equal treatment.

That idea appears in different forms around common carriers, utilities, communications networks, transportation systems, and modern debates over digital infrastructure. The details matter, and the law has changed repeatedly. But the intuition is stable: when a privately controlled facility becomes a route that others cannot practically avoid, society becomes interested not only in who owns it but in the terms on which others may pass.

*Munn* carried that intuition in one direction. The Court described property used in a way that affected the public as subject to forms of public regulation. The dissent warned that such reasoning could expand government power dangerously far. That disagreement should not be tidied away. If every useful private business is declared public merely because people depend on it, almost nothing remains meaningfully private. If no dependency ever matters because ownership is private, bottleneck owners can convert infrastructure into private sovereignty.

The line is difficult because the underlying reality changes.

A ferry that is the only crossing today may face a competing bridge tomorrow. A telephone network that once looked like a natural monopoly may face wireless alternatives. A dominant software distribution channel may become less important if regulation, technology, or user behavior enables other routes. A standard may become so widely adopted that switching away is expensive even though nobody legally requires its use.

Gatekeeping power is not a permanent property of an institution. It emerges from a relationship among architecture, alternatives, rules, and dependence.

That makes it both harder and more useful to study.

We should resist the temptation to identify a permanent caste called gatekeepers and then assign motives to everyone inside it. The same organization can be a gatekeeper in one relationship and an applicant in another. A major retailer may control access to consumers while depending on payment networks, landlords, logistics providers, customs authorities, cloud systems, and labor markets. A bank can deny customers access to payment services while itself depending on central-bank infrastructure, clearing systems, regulators, data providers, and correspondent banks.

Power is layered.

A person may encounter the bank as a gatekeeper while the bank encounters a regulator as its gatekeeper and the regulator depends on legislators for statutory authority. A software company may control an API while depending on a cloud provider whose data center depends on a utility whose new transmission line depends on a permitting authority.

The world is not divided into people with keys and people waiting outside. Most of us carry some keys and wait at other doors.

What matters is the local transition.

Who can refuse whom? What does refusal cost? How many alternative routes exist? Can the rule be changed? Can the applicant leave with accumulated value intact? Does the gatekeeper compete with the applicant? Is the gatekeeper compensated for allowing passage, for denying it, or simply for occupying the position? What other conditions can be attached to the yes?

Those questions reveal permission power without requiring a theory of villainy.

They also explain why gatekeepers attract politics.

A price can be bargained over. A gate decides whether bargaining begins.

A competitor can lower its price, improve its product, advertise, borrow money, recruit talent, and work harder. None of those actions solves a blocked transition if the blocking party controls the only workable route. The ordinary tools of competition operate poorly when the problem is access to competition itself.

This is why entrance rules become so consequential. They do not merely influence the terms of a market; they help determine who is present to make offers.

The grain elevator owners in Chicago were not powerful because wheat was unimportant. They were powerful because wheat was important and passage was concentrated. The Terminal Railroad Association did not need to own the industries of St. Louis. Its leverage came from the fact that trains had to cross physical geography through a small set of facilities.

Modern gates often hide their geography inside software, contracts, standards, and identity systems, but the structure is familiar.

There is still a river.

There is still a bridge.

There is still somebody deciding the terms of crossing.

The next question is what happens when the gatekeeper realizes how much that position is worth.
