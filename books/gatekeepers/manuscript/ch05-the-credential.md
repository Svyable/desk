# The Credential

A credential is a judgment that learned how to travel.

The person who interviews you does not know how you performed in every classroom. The hospital administrator granting privileges did not attend medical school with you. A customer hiring an electrician cannot inspect years of apprenticeship from the kitchen table. A state trooper who sees a driver's license does not administer a road test beside the highway.

Some other institution already did the checking.

The document, number, badge, license, degree, certificate, score, or digital token carries that earlier judgment into the next decision.

This is a remarkable compression technology.

A complicated human history becomes a small claim: qualified, licensed, certified, graduated, cleared, verified.

The claim saves time because the next gatekeeper does not have to begin from zero. It also creates power because the person who controls the credential can influence every later gate that trusts it.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics makes a useful distinction between two kinds of occupational credentials. A certification is issued by a nongovernmental body and indicates that a person has particular knowledge or skill. A license is awarded by a government agency and conveys legal authority to work in an occupation.

The difference is enormous.

A certification says, roughly, someone examined this person and is willing to vouch for something about them.

A license can say, without this permission you may not legally do the work.

In the United States, these are not edge cases. In 2025, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, 21.6 percent of employed people had a license and another 2.5 percent had a certification but no license. Among professional and related occupations the licensed share was substantially higher.

That does not mean one-fifth of American jobs are identical gatekeeping arrangements. A physician's license, a commercial driver's license, a real-estate license, and a cosmetology license regulate very different risks. Lumping them together would destroy the very judgment the system is supposed to improve.

What they share is the portable yes.

A credential exists because the next person does not know enough.

Hiring is an obvious example. Employers face uncertainty about applicants. Resumes contain claims. Interviews are short. Work samples reveal only part of ability. Past employers may say little. The cost of a bad hire can be significant, while the applicant naturally knows more about their own history than the employer does.

Michael Spence built one of the classic economic models of this problem in his 1973 paper on job-market signaling. The paper explored how education can function as a signal in a labor market where employers make hiring decisions under uncertainty. A signal can carry information even when the signal itself is not identical to the productive quality the employer ultimately wants.

This point is often flattened into the claim that education is merely signaling. That is not what we need here. Education can build knowledge, discipline, networks, technical ability, cultural fluency, and many other forms of human capital. The useful insight is narrower: when quality is hard to observe directly, institutions start relying on observable proxies that are correlated with the qualities they care about.

Credentials are organized proxies.

The danger begins when the proxy becomes easier to inspect than the underlying competence.

An employer can verify a degree in seconds. Evaluating whether someone can actually perform an ambiguous job may take months. A licensing board can count completed training hours. Measuring judgment under unusual conditions is harder. A certification can require a standardized exam. Measuring whether somebody will behave ethically when incentives become uncomfortable is much harder.

Institutions naturally gravitate toward what can be documented.

The result can be a subtle reversal. The credential begins as evidence about competence. Eventually competence is inferred from possession of the credential so automatically that people without the credential are not examined at all.

The signal becomes the gate.

This can be entirely appropriate. Nobody wants an airline to hire a person as captain because the applicant gives an unusually persuasive interview about being good with airplanes. In high-risk fields, proof of training, tested competence, supervised practice, and continuing requirements are forms of protection for people who cannot evaluate the professional themselves.

The patient under anesthesia is in no position to audit the surgeon.

The problem is deciding how much credential is enough.

Occupational licensing offers the cleanest version because the government itself sets conditions for entry. The policy case can be strong where consumers cannot evaluate quality before purchase and mistakes create serious harm. A person may not know that electrical work was unsafe until the wall catches fire. Poor medical treatment can cause irreversible injury. Structural engineering failures can kill people who never chose the engineer.

Licensing can make competence legible and create an enforcement mechanism after admission. A license can be suspended or revoked. Continuing education can be required. Complaints can attach to a recognized practitioner. A consumer has an institution to contact.

But the same system can also protect incumbents.

The Federal Trade Commission has spent years examining this tension. Its economic-liberty work describes licensing as potentially useful for public safety and consumer protection while also emphasizing that entry requirements can reduce worker mobility, raise costs, reduce output, and restrict consumer choice when they extend beyond what the underlying risk justifies.

This is not a paradox. It is the normal anatomy of a gate.

A requirement can produce benefits and exclusion simultaneously.

Suppose an occupation requires two thousand hours of training. Perhaps the work genuinely requires that preparation. Perhaps five hundred well-designed hours would produce the same safety. Perhaps the requirement covers tasks most practitioners never perform. Perhaps experienced workers arriving from another state must repeat training because the local board does not recognize their old license.

The number two thousand tells us almost nothing by itself.

The question is what failure those hours prevent.

Gatekeeping becomes vulnerable when inputs are used as substitutes for outcomes. Hours are easy to count. Years of education are easy to count. Course names are easy to list. Fees are easy to collect. Competence is messier.

This is how credential systems can accumulate barnacles.

One training requirement is added after an incident. Another appears because technology changed. A professional association wants a new module. A legislature adds an ethics course. A board requires a particular examination. Nobody removes material whose value has faded. The credential becomes longer, more expensive, and more socially prestigious partly because it is longer and more expensive.

Cost itself can become mistaken for rigor.

That mistake has consequences because applicants do not start with equal resources.

A year of unpaid training costs more to a person supporting a family than to someone whose family supports them. An exam offered in a distant city has travel costs. A degree requirement carries tuition and the opportunity cost of years outside full-time work. A complicated licensing transfer is harder on military spouses and other people who move frequently. A required internship may be a minor inconvenience in a wealthy household and an impossible luxury in another.

A gate can therefore select for the ability to complete the gate as well as for the ability to do the work.

Sometimes those are correlated. Perseverance is useful. Studying difficult material may reveal commitment. Completing a demanding apprenticeship may actually be part of becoming competent.

Sometimes the correlation is accidental.

If the job requires diagnosing engines, an applicant's ability to navigate university financial aid is not the skill the customer needs. If a nurse has practiced safely for fifteen years, the social value of making that nurse repeat educational steps after moving across a state line deserves scrutiny. If a talented programmer can demonstrate working software, a degree requirement may exclude useful ability because the employer prefers a familiar filter.

Credentials simplify selection by turning a continuous world into categories.

Has degree.

Does not have degree.

Licensed.

Unlicensed.

Board certified.

Not board certified.

Cleared.

Not cleared.

The categories are powerful precisely because they travel cheaply. A thousand gatekeepers can use the same binary distinction without understanding how it was created.

This portability can produce credential inflation.

Imagine that employers use a bachelor's degree as a convenient screen for a job that does not intrinsically require four years of university study. Applicants notice. More applicants obtain degrees. The degree becomes common among candidates. Employers can now require it with less fear of shrinking the pool too far. The credential that once distinguished applicants becomes a baseline admission ticket.

People respond by seeking the next signal: a master's degree, specialized certificate, selective internship, named fellowship, prestigious employer.

The staircase rises.

Again, none of this proves that the education lacks substantive value. The gatekeeping issue is that an individually rational signal can become a collectively expensive prerequisite.

Everybody stands taller and nobody sees farther over the crowd.

Credential systems can also create concentrated authority over language.

If one institution defines what counts as qualified, it influences how the occupation describes itself. The syllabus becomes a boundary around legitimate knowledge. The exam blueprint shapes study. The approved degree list influences universities. Continuing-education rules create markets for courses. Scope-of-practice rules decide which tasks belong to which profession.

The credential does not merely recognize a field. It can help produce the field.

This has benefits. Common vocabulary and minimum standards make professions coherent. Shared training helps people collaborate. Ethical rules can establish duties that go beyond ordinary commercial contracts. Professional discipline can protect the public from practitioners who repeatedly violate them.

It also creates a familiar conflict: incumbents may participate in writing the rules newcomers must satisfy.

Expertise makes that participation necessary. Who better to know what safe electrical work requires than experienced electricians? Who understands medical competence better than clinicians? A licensing regime designed with no practitioners would be foolish.

But expertise and self-interest can occupy the same person.

A board dominated by incumbents may honestly believe a requirement protects quality while also benefiting from reduced competition. The motives do not need to be corrupt for the incentive to exist.

That makes outside review important. A gate that controls entry should periodically have to explain why each major condition is tied to the risk the gate is meant to manage.

The burden should be especially high when the condition is expensive, slow, geographically restrictive, or unrelated to observed performance.

There are alternatives to full licensing.

Certification can provide information without making unauthorized practice illegal. Registration can create accountability without extensive entry barriers. Bonding and insurance can shift the cost of some failures. Inspections can focus on the work product rather than the worker's entire career. Apprenticeship can demonstrate competence in practice. Skills tests can allow experienced outsiders to prove ability directly. Disclosure rules can let consumers know what training a provider has.

Each alternative solves a different portion of the trust problem.

A good system chooses the least restrictive gate that still handles the real risk.

That principle sounds tidy until real work arrives. Some failures are too dangerous to discover through consumer complaints. Some skills cannot be measured in one exam. Insurance does not reverse death or severe injury. Customers cannot reliably evaluate complex professional claims. Reputation systems are vulnerable to manipulation and often mature only after harm occurs.

There is no universal substitute for licensing.

The right question is proportionality.

How hard is it for a consumer to evaluate competence independently? How serious is the harm from a bad provider? How reversible is that harm? Can poor quality be caught through inspection? Is there a less restrictive way to provide trustworthy information? Does the credential actually predict the performance it claims to certify? Can experienced people from other jurisdictions demonstrate equivalent competence without repeating irrelevant steps?

These questions turn credential policy back toward evidence.

They also reveal something more general about gatekeeping.

Every credential system contains at least two gates.

The obvious one decides who receives the credential.

The second decides where the credential will be trusted.

A university can issue a degree, but employers decide how much weight to give it. A certification body can create a credential, but customers decide whether it matters. One state can license a professional while another state decides whether to recognize the license. A security-clearance process can approve a person for one context while another institution demands its own review.

The value of a credential therefore depends on a network of downstream acceptance.

This is why prestigious credentials can become self-reinforcing. Employers trust them because strong applicants seek them; strong applicants seek them because employers trust them. The institution at the center acquires power from the network's belief.

The credential becomes a small currency.

Like money, it works because other people expect others to recognize it.

That analogy points toward the digital future. Credentials are moving from paper into databases, identity wallets, cryptographic certificates, reputation systems, verified profiles, risk scores, and machine-readable claims. Software agents will increasingly present credentials to other software before a human knows a gate was encountered.

A service may ask whether an agent is authorized to spend up to a limit, whether a merchant is verified, whether a person is old enough for a transaction, whether a device satisfies a security standard, whether an employee has completed required training, or whether an organization holds adequate insurance.

Machine-readable credentials can make legitimate passage dramatically faster.

They can also make exclusion instantaneous.

A human clerk confronted by an unusual situation can ask questions. A software gate consuming a credential may simply receive true, false, missing, expired, or not recognized.

The design problem becomes whether people can challenge the credential itself and whether alternative evidence can ever be considered.

A good credential should make trusted competence portable without making one historical path mandatory forever.

That is harder than printing a certificate.

The strongest credential systems know what claim they are making. They distinguish identity from competence, competence from legal authority, and legal authority from performance. They expire claims that genuinely grow stale. They preserve records needed for accountability. They recognize equivalent evidence where equivalence can be shown. They do not demand a surgeon's proof for a haircut or a haircut's proof for surgery.

Most important, they remember that the document is evidence about the person, not the person themselves.

Credentials fail when the proxy becomes sacred.

The next gate in this book takes that logic and makes it numerical. Instead of a degree or license standing in for a history, a score does.

The applicant may never know exactly how the number was made.

But the number can still decide whether money moves.
