# Prior Authorization

The doctor can recommend the treatment and still not be the last person whose yes matters.

That is the peculiar position created by prior authorization.

A clinician examines the patient, reaches a judgment, orders an MRI, a rehabilitation stay, a procedure, a piece of equipment, or another service. The patient may have insurance that covers the general category of care. Yet before the service proceeds, the payer may require another question to be answered: does this particular request satisfy the conditions under which we will pay for it?

The second question is not absurd.

Insurance is a promise to pay under rules. Those rules have to distinguish covered care from excluded care, medically necessary care from care that does not meet coverage criteria, ordinary choices from unusually expensive ones, and sometimes one treatment from another. Health-care spending is not a bottomless resource. Unnecessary procedures can harm patients as well as budgets. Fraud exists. Practice varies. New treatments arrive faster than many institutions can evaluate them. A payer that never reviews utilization would not be neutral. It would be making a decision too: every covered request passes regardless of evidence, duplication, cost, or appropriateness.

Prior authorization is one way of placing a gate before payment and, in practice, often before care.

That last phrase is where the difficulty begins.

The payer may describe the formal decision as whether a service is covered. The patient experiences the decision as whether the service happens now.

Money and medicine become joined at the gate.

This makes prior authorization a particularly revealing case because nearly every mechanism in this book appears inside it at once. There is a credential: the clinician's recommendation and the medical record. There is a rule: coverage criteria. There is a queue: the request must be reviewed. There is an information problem: the payer sees a claim for care but not the patient in the room. There is a conflict over error: approving unnecessary treatment has costs, while denying necessary treatment has costs of a different kind. There is an appeal. There is often software. There may be a human reviewer somewhere behind it.

And there is time.

In medicine, time can change the meaning of permission.

A prior authorization granted after a routine delay may be annoying but harmless in one case. In another, delay can prolong pain, postpone rehabilitation, extend a hospital stay, interrupt a treatment course, or force a patient and clinician to choose a different path. The same seven days do not have the same medical price for every request.

This is why the word *authorization* can be misleading if heard as a simple yes-or-no switch. A medical gate controls timing, information, and sometimes the practical range of treatment options as well as formal coverage.

The strongest criticism of a gate like this should begin by admitting why it exists.

Health systems need utilization management because medical decisions combine expertise, uncertainty, financial incentives, imperfect evidence, and enormous variation in cost. A test can be overused. An expensive therapy can be ordered where a cheaper alternative is equally effective. Procedures can continue out of habit after evidence changes. Providers can disagree. Patients can reasonably prefer options that an insurance contract never promised to finance.

Some refusals are not failures. They are the gate working.

The same is true of delay when delay reflects necessary review. A complicated case may require a physician with relevant expertise to examine records that cannot be evaluated safely by a simple rule. A request missing information may genuinely need more information. A system that promises instant approval for every complex case may simply be moving judgment somewhere less visible.

The serious question is not whether prior authorization should ever say no.

It is whether the gate can distinguish useful scrutiny from administrative friction well enough to justify standing between recommendation and care.

The Office of Inspector General at the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services gave that question unusually concrete form in 2022.

Its investigators examined denied prior authorization and payment requests from Medicare Advantage organizations. Medicare Advantage plans receive payment to provide Medicare benefits through private plans operating under federal rules. They can use utilization-management tools, including prior authorization, within those rules.

The OIG wanted to know whether some denials involved care that would have satisfied Medicare coverage requirements.

Among the prior authorization requests that Medicare Advantage organizations denied in the study, the OIG estimated that 13 percent met Medicare coverage rules.

The denominator matters.

The finding does not say that 13 percent of all prior authorization requests were wrongly denied. It says that within denied prior authorization requests, the OIG estimated a share met the Medicare coverage rules that applied to the services. That is a narrower claim and an important one.

Even in that narrower form, it exposes the central risk of the gate.

A denial can be procedurally valid inside a plan's machinery and still fail the coverage standard the system is supposed to implement.

The OIG identified two recurring reasons.

In some cases, Medicare Advantage organizations applied clinical criteria that were not contained in Medicare's coverage rules. The OIG gave the example of requiring an x-ray before approving more advanced imaging. Physician reviewers concluded that some requests denied under such additional criteria were medically necessary and met Medicare coverage rules.

In other cases, plans said the record did not contain enough documentation to support approval, while the OIG's reviewers concluded that the existing medical records were sufficient.

This is gatekeeping in its purest administrative form.

The dispute is not necessarily over whether health care should have standards. It is over which standard controls and whether the evidence already satisfies it.

For the patient, those distinctions are invisible until something goes wrong.

A physician may believe the documentation is complete. The payer may interpret it differently. The patient receives a message that the service is not authorized. The next action becomes another piece of work: call, resubmit, send more records, request a peer review, appeal, wait.

The OIG found that some denials were eventually reversed when beneficiaries or providers appealed or disputed them, or when the plans identified their own errors.

Reversal is better than permanence.

It does not make the original delay disappear.

An appeal can prove that a gate is correctable and also prove that the gate imposed a cost before correction. Both facts can be true.

This is a recurring mistake in institutional analysis. We treat the existence of an appeal as though it neutralizes the first decision. In reality, an appeal is a second transaction. It consumes time, expertise, persistence, and administrative capacity. A provider's office has to know the denial is contestable, understand why it occurred, assemble what is needed, track the case, and continue while caring for other patients.

The patient has to remain in the process long enough for the system to repair itself.

That is why explanation matters.

A denial that says only no sends the applicant back to the beginning. A specific reason turns the next action into something more directed. Missing imaging report. Coverage criterion not met. Requested service not included. Additional clinical information required.

The reason can still be wrong. It can still rest on a bad criterion. But a named reason is at least an object that can be examined.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services has moved policy in this direction.

A 2024 interoperability and prior authorization rule requires certain affected payers to provide a specific reason for prior authorization denials. Beginning primarily in 2026, those payers are also subject to decision timeframes: within seventy-two hours for expedited requests and seven calendar days for standard requests for covered medical items and services under the rule's scope.

Those numbers are not a declaration that every medically important request can safely wait seventy-two hours or seven days. The expedited category exists precisely because urgency changes the clock. Nor do the deadlines mean every request should consume the entire allowed period. They are outer process requirements for the affected programs, not clinical targets.

Still, the existence of a clock changes accountability.

Without a deadline, delay can be treated as administrative weather. With a deadline, the system has to classify the request, count time, and explain a failure to decide.

CMS also requires affected payers to publish aggregated prior authorization metrics. The current requirements include measures such as the share approved, denied, approved after appeal, and the average time between submission and decision.

A queue that once existed mainly inside the payer becomes partially visible from outside.

Metrics will not solve the problem by themselves. A plan can improve average decision time while unusual cases worsen. Approval rates cannot tell us whether the approved care was necessary or the denied care was inappropriate. A high reversal rate might reflect a poor initial decision process, a strong appeal process, unusual case mix, or some combination.

But measurement changes what can be asked.

How often does the gate say no? How often does no become yes? How long does each answer take?

Once those numbers exist publicly, prior authorization becomes easier to compare as an operating system rather than an anecdotal annoyance.

Medicare Advantage rules also show another way to discipline a gate: limit what the gate is for.

CMS has clarified that coordinated care plans may use prior authorization to confirm diagnoses or other medical criteria and to ensure that an item or service is medically necessary. The agency also established requirements aimed at aligning coverage decisions with Traditional Medicare criteria, constraining the use of internal criteria, and making certain internal coverage criteria publicly accessible.

The underlying idea is simple but important.

A gate should not acquire purposes merely because it already controls the door.

If prior authorization is justified as a tool for medical necessity and coverage, then the criteria should remain attached to medical necessity and coverage. Otherwise the gate becomes a convenient place to insert every institutional preference.

CMS also created continuity protections. When a Medicare Advantage enrollee who is already in an active course of treatment switches to a new plan, the rules require a minimum ninety-day transition period during which the new plan generally may not require prior authorization for that active course. An approved prior authorization for a course of treatment must remain valid for as long as medically reasonable and necessary under applicable criteria, considering the patient's history and treating provider's recommendation.

This is gate design through memory.

The system recognizes that a person already moving through care should not repeatedly return to the starting line merely because an administrative boundary changed.

That principle reaches far beyond health insurance.

Many bad gates forget immediately.

A person proves identity to one department and proves it again to the next. A vendor passes security review and begins again after a reorganization. A licensed professional moves jurisdictions and repeats evidence already accepted elsewhere. A customer resolves a dispute and has to explain the entire history to the next support agent.

Every forgotten approval creates another gate.

Continuity is therefore a form of access.

Prior authorization also exposes one of the most consequential questions in gate design: who is qualified to make the second judgment?

The treating clinician knows the patient. The payer has coverage rules, broader utilization data, and financial responsibility. A reviewer may know the general medical evidence but never have examined the patient. A software rule may recognize a coded diagnosis but not an unusual clinical context.

None of these views is complete.

The temptation is to resolve the tension by declaring one actor sovereign. Doctors should decide everything because they practice medicine. Payers should decide everything because they finance the contract. Algorithms should decide routine cases because they are consistent. Patients should decide because the body is theirs.

Each claim captures a truth and ignores another one.

The physician's recommendation is medically privileged but not automatically a statement about what an insurance contract covers. The insurer's coverage authority does not make its reviewer better informed about a patient than the treating clinician. The patient's preference matters profoundly but does not establish medical effectiveness or contractual coverage. Automation can apply rules consistently and still fail on atypical cases.

Good gatekeeping distributes judgment instead of pretending one institution possesses all of it.

This is why peer review and appeal matter when clinical disagreement is genuine. It is why coverage criteria should be knowable in advance. It is why evidence from the treating clinician should have a route into the decision. It is why a reviewer needs authority to depart from a crude automated outcome when the case warrants it.

And it is why the payer remains responsible for the decision even when software participated.

The movement toward electronic prior authorization can reduce a great deal of pointless work.

CMS is requiring affected payers to implement a prior authorization application programming interface using standardized health-data technology. The aim is to let providers determine requirements, send requests and supporting information, and receive decisions electronically instead of navigating a patchwork of portals, faxes, phone calls, and proprietary workflows.

This is real progress if it works.

Much of gatekeeping friction is not substantive judgment at all. It is incompatible forms, repeated data entry, missing status, and uncertainty about what the gate requires. Software is exceptionally good at making structured information move.

But digitizing a gate and improving a gate are different achievements.

A bad criterion can be applied electronically.

A weak denial can arrive faster.

An unnecessary request for documentation can be converted into a perfect API call.

The CMS rule itself implicitly recognizes this. It does not require every prior authorization decision to be made in real time. CMS notes that some requests may be resolved quickly while others still require clinical review and evaluation. Automation is a tool for the process, not a substitute for the medical judgment the process is supposed to support.

This is the difference between transaction friction and decision friction.

Transaction friction is the fax that never arrives, the portal that requires another login, the information already in the record but not in the right field, the phone call to learn whether the request is pending.

Decision friction is the genuine question: does this patient, under these rules and this evidence, meet the criterion for this service?

Technology should attack the first aggressively.

It should be much more careful about pretending the second has disappeared.

The distinction will become increasingly important as artificial intelligence enters utilization management. Models can summarize records, identify likely missing information, compare a request with policies, surface similar cases, and automate straightforward approvals. Those uses could save clinicians and payers enormous amounts of clerical labor.

The dangerous version is an institution that cannot explain why a model denied care, cannot identify which criterion was decisive, or treats a statistical pattern as a replacement for the rules governing coverage.

The credit chapter gave us the principle: complexity does not remove the duty to give reasons.

Medicine adds another one: speed does not remove the duty to use the right standard.

There is a deeper asymmetry here that should not be exaggerated.

It is tempting to say that a mistaken approval merely wastes money while a mistaken denial harms a patient. That is too easy. Unnecessary care can harm patients directly. An avoidable procedure carries medical risk. Overuse can consume scarce capacity and expose people to downstream interventions they never needed. A false yes in medicine can be dangerous.

But the errors still have different pathways.

The person harmed by an unnecessary approval may be harmed by receiving care that should not have occurred. The person harmed by an improper denial may be harmed because medically necessary care did not occur or did not occur in time. A legitimate gate has to care about both.

The best design therefore is not maximally permissive or maximally strict.

It is selective.

Scrutiny should be concentrated where the potential clinical, financial, or evidentiary value of review is worth the burden it imposes. Routine requests that almost always meet clear criteria are poor candidates for elaborate human queues. Unusual, high-risk, or genuinely ambiguous cases may deserve deeper review.

This sounds like common sense. Implementing it is hard because utilization patterns change, medical evidence changes, prices change, and people respond to rules. A category that once needed scrutiny may become routine. A new expensive therapy may suddenly justify attention. Providers learn which documentation satisfies a payer. Payers learn where overuse is concentrated.

A gate has to be maintained like software.

Rules that once served a purpose should not become permanent because nobody has authority to delete them.

The Medicare Advantage utilization-management committees required by CMS are an institutional attempt to create that maintenance function. Plans using utilization management have to review those policies and their relationship to Medicare coverage rules. Later requirements added a health-equity lens to that review, including analysis of whether prior authorization policies have disproportionate effects on certain groups of enrollees.

Again, the mechanism is not magic. A committee can become ceremonial. A public report can become unreadable compliance material. But the design principle is sound: a gate should have an owner responsible not merely for operating it but for reevaluating whether it still deserves to exist in its current form.

Prior authorization is not one gate.

It is a population of gates attached to thousands of services, diagnoses, plans, criteria, providers, and patients. Some may prevent waste and inappropriate treatment. Some may be so predictable that automation should make them nearly invisible. Some may impose more cost than the review saves. Some may catch real safety issues. Some may be built on criteria that should change.

The useful argument is not a slogan about the whole category.

It is a demand that every consequential gate be able to explain its job.

What decision is this authorization meant to improve?

What evidence justifies requiring it?

How often does review change the outcome?

How long does the decision take?

What happens when the system is wrong?

Can the patient and clinician understand the reason?

Does an appeal arrive while the care is still useful?

Is the same evidence being demanded repeatedly?

Could the administrative work be removed without removing the substantive safeguard?

Those questions make the gate inspectable.

And inspectability matters because the patient cannot simply walk around the payer's decision while keeping the same insurance arrangement. The gate is embedded in a contract, a network, and a financing system that took years to build.

The next chapter moves to a different kind of boundary entirely.

There, the institution does not merely decide whether it will finance movement.

It decides whether the person may cross the line at all.
