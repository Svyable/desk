# Chapter 17 evidence notes — The Bypass

## Chapter claim

A bypass is a route around the ordinary gate. It is not inherently good or bad: it can be fraud, competition, innovation, emergency resilience, regulatory arbitrage, or a diagnostic signal that the official route is mispriced. Good institutions distinguish authorized alternate paths from circumvention and learn from repeated workarounds.

## Primary sources used

### U.S. Food and Drug Administration — expanded access and Right to Try

- FDA, *Expanded Access* and patient/physician guidance.
- FDA, *Right to Try*.
- FDA, *Expanded Access for Medical Devices*.

Claim boundary: expanded access and Right to Try are used as examples of formal alternate pathways when the ordinary approval/trial route does not fit a serious or immediately life-threatening patient's time horizon. They are not described as equivalent systems. Under Right to Try, FDA states that it does not review or approve individual requests, eligible criteria apply, informed consent is required, and manufacturers are not obligated to provide a product. Expanded access generally involves FDA review and additional actors/criteria.

Sources:
- https://www.fda.gov/news-events/public-health-focus/expanded-access
- https://www.fda.gov/patients/learn-about-expanded-access-and-other-treatment-options/right-try
- https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/investigational-device-exemption-ide/expanded-access-medical-devices

### European Commission — alternative app distribution and interoperability under the DMA

- European Commission DMA developer portal, *App distribution*.
- European Commission DMA developer portal, *Interoperability*.

Claim boundary: the chapter treats alternative distribution as a competitive bypass to a formerly more concentrated distribution gate in the EU. It preserves the Commission's stated allowance for strictly necessary and proportionate measures protecting OS/hardware/software integrity and does not claim alternative distribution is risk-free.

Sources:
- https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/developer-portal/app-distribution_en
- https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/developer-portal/interoperability_en

## Counterevidence and constraints preserved

- Bypasses can defeat legitimate safety, security, fraud, privacy, financial, and public-interest controls.
- Alternate channels can reduce protections such as curation, dispute resolution, traceability, or centralized review.
- Regulatory and jurisdictional arbitrage can shift costs onto third parties rather than merely increasing private choice.
- Emergency and compassionate pathways still need criteria, informed consent, logging, or other safeguards.
- Temporary workarounds can harden into ungoverned permanent infrastructure.
- A gate's inconvenience can be intentional and justified when it corresponds to the consequence being controlled.

## Design principles developed

1. **Workarounds are telemetry.** Repeated bypass reveals a mismatch between official process and practical need.
2. **Bypass premium.** Measure the extra cost or risk people willingly accept to avoid the gate.
3. **Bounded bypass.** Exceptional routes should have scope, authority, logging, expiry, and post-use review where appropriate.
4. **Change the risk budget, not erase the risk model.** Emergency paths can accept different tradeoffs while preserving safeguards.
5. **Attach controls to activity where possible.** Safety need not always require monopoly over the channel.
6. **Ask who pays for the bypass.** Externalized risk weakens the case for unrestricted circumvention.
7. **Bypass debt.** Temporary shortcuts become liabilities when they persist without review or governance.
8. **Mine the workarounds.** Useful hacks can reveal how the normal gate should evolve.
9. **Resilience is not circumvention.** For agents, an authorized alternate route is different from defeating a permission boundary.
10. **Refusal needs semantics.** Systems should distinguish unavailable, rate-limited, unentitled, prohibited, review-required, and alternate-route-permitted states.

## Forward link

Chapter 18 should distinguish bypass from exit. Appeal asks the gatekeeper to reconsider; bypass pursues the objective through another route; exit leaves the relationship or system. The next chapter should examine portability, switching costs, data export, interoperability, lock-in, credible alternatives, and why a theoretical exit is not meaningful when the cost of leaving destroys what the user built inside.
