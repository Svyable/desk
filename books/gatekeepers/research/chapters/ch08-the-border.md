# Chapter 8 evidence notes — The Border

## Core claim

Borders are layered permission systems rather than single lines. Citizenship, passports, visas, carrier checks, admission decisions, customs rules, and protection obligations can each control a different transition. Because territorial admission can be difficult or impossible to route around, border gates concentrate unusually high consequences in identity, evidence, timing, discretion, and appeal.

## Sources

### U.S. Department of State — About Visas: The Basics

- https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/visa-information-resources/frequently-asked-questions/about-basics.html
- State Department guidance says a U.S. visa does not guarantee entry.
- A visa permits a foreign citizen to travel to a U.S. port of entry and request admission; CBP has authority to permit or deny admission.
- This distinction grounds the chapter's opening claim that a visa can function as permission to ask permission.

### U.S. Department of State — What the Visa Expiration Date Means

- https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/visa-information-resources/visa-expiration-date.html
- Distinguishes visa validity from the period of authorized stay.
- Useful for showing that permission is layered across issuance, travel, admission, and duration.

### OHCHR — International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, Article 12

- https://www.ohchr.org/Documents/Publications/CoreTreatiesen.pdf
- Article 12 protects freedom to leave any country, including one's own, subject to specified lawful restrictions.
- It also provides that no one shall be arbitrarily deprived of the right to enter his or her own country.
- These rights do not amount to a general right to enter any foreign state of choice.

### UNHCR — Access to territory and non-refoulement

- https://emergency.unhcr.org/protection/legal-framework/access-territory-and-non-refoulement
- UNHCR describes non-refoulement as prohibiting return to territories where specified serious threats apply.
- It treats the principle as relevant at borders and in circumstances where a state exercises jurisdiction or effective control.
- The source supports the chapter's architectural claim that a protection-sensitive border cannot be designed as a pure document/no-document binary.

### UNHCR — Non-Refoulement, Executive Committee Conclusion No. 6

- https://www.unhcr.org/publications/non-refoulement
- Reaffirms the importance of non-refoulement at the border as well as within a state's territory.

## Boundaries and counterevidence

- The chapter does not argue for open or closed borders as a political program. It analyzes the operating structure of territorial permission.
- A visa's failure to guarantee admission is not evidence that visa issuance is meaningless; issuance and border admission answer related but distinct legal questions.
- States have legitimate security, customs, public-health, migration-control, and sovereignty interests at borders.
- Redundant checks can improve security and fraud detection; duplication is not automatically waste.
- Faster border processing is not always better if it removes necessary scrutiny. The chapter argues for separating routine cases from exceptional cases, not eliminating inspection.
- Human discretion can correct rigid systems and can also introduce inconsistency or bias. Automation can improve consistency while scaling bad rules.
- Non-refoulement and asylum protections have precise legal scopes and should not be generalized into a universal right of admission.
- The chapter uses U.S. visa practice as a concrete example while keeping the broader argument jurisdiction-neutral.

## Design implications under test

1. Treat the border as a chain of permissions rather than one yes/no event.
2. Reuse verified information where legally and operationally appropriate rather than repeatedly rebuilding identity and status.
3. Do not make routine cases pay the full cost of exceptional cases.
4. Preserve deep review paths for genuinely unusual or high-risk cases.
5. Keep data inputs proportional to the actual decision rather than collecting everything because it is available.
6. Provide meaningful explanation where a consequential rejection or delay can be corrected.
7. Treat irreversibility as a design variable: where later correction cannot make a person whole, front-load stronger safeguards.
8. Map distributed responsibility across carriers, consulates, border agencies, databases, and automated systems so that failures do not disappear between institutions.
