# The Signal That Wouldn't Work

In March 1980, somebody in the Pentagon wanted to know where an American hostage was.

The surviving report is not mystical. That is the first useful thing about it. It has the bureaucratic complexion of almost everything the government does when it wants the impossible to become a procedure. A date. A session number. A statement of purpose. A protocol. A warning about classification. Pages for the viewer’s impressions. Pages for drawings. The extraordinary proposition is forced into ordinary paperwork.

The purpose of the session, the report says, was to provide information relevant to the hostage situation at the United States Embassy compound in Tehran. The viewer was asked to locate and describe the surroundings of an identified person. The impressions were not presented as finished intelligence. The document calls them raw intelligence data and places responsibility for interpretation and use on the requester.

That sentence is more interesting than the psychic part.

Imagine, for a moment, that there is something here. Not telepathy. Not astral travel. Not a theory of consciousness leaking through spacetime. Strip the vocabulary down until the claim becomes almost offensively modest: under some conditions, a person produces descriptions that correspond to information they should not have acquired through the sensory channels the experimenters know about.

If that happened once, you would suspect luck. If it happened repeatedly, you would suspect leakage, judging artifacts, selection, fraud, memory, bad statistics, or some combination. If careful experiments kept producing a residual after those attacks, you would have a scientific problem.

You would not yet have an intelligence capability.

That distinction sounds pedantic until somebody is missing in Tehran.

An intelligence officer cannot task a p-value. A commander cannot send a confidence interval to a building. The hostage is either in the room or he is not. The submarine is either under this patch of ocean or somewhere else. The hidden installation either has the feature described or does not. Operational intelligence has to survive contact with specificity, time pressure, alternatives, false positives, incomplete targets, adversary deception, and the ugly fact that a decision must often be made before anybody tells you the answer.

This is where the history of remote viewing becomes much stranger than the version in popular culture.

The easy story is that the American intelligence community once went crazy, hired psychics, stared at goats, discovered nothing, and eventually came to its senses. The opposite easy story is that the government secretly proved psychic functioning, used it operationally for decades, and then buried the result. Both stories are wonderfully efficient. Neither requires much paperwork.

The paperwork is worse.

There really was a government program. There really were classified sessions. There really were operational requests, not merely laboratory games. There were also sessions that went badly. One 1982 training report says, with admirable economy, that post-session analysis showed “very little correlation.” It points the reader toward another report on the same target. This is not how a mythology normally preserves itself. Somewhere in the archive, alongside the material that believers have spent decades extracting for spectacular correspondences, sits an official form saying: this one did not work very well.

There were researchers who believed the experiments had produced evidence of an anomalous information channel. There were critics who thought the methods and inferential chain did not justify that conclusion. By the time the program returned to CIA for review in the mid-1990s, the question had acquired a peculiar shape. The public institutional summary is not that every result had been random. CIA’s later account says the independent review found enough accurate experiences to defy randomness while also concluding that the phenomenon was too unreliable, inconsistent, and sporadic to be useful for intelligence purposes.

Read that again without choosing a side.

Something can beat chance and still fail.

That possibility is the subject of this book.

We are accustomed to arranging extraordinary claims on a single line. False at one end. True at the other. Move the slider far enough toward true and the implication seems automatic: if remote viewing is real, remote viewers should be useful. If they are not useful, remote viewing must not be real. The structure is seductive because it collapses an engineering problem into a metaphysical referendum.

Real systems do not work like that.

A sensor can detect a signal too weakly to guide action. A medical test can have measurable sensitivity and still become dangerous when deployed against a population with a low base rate. A financial indicator can correlate with an outcome and still lose money after costs, latency, and regime change. A radar can register energy without identifying the object that reflected it. A person can give you information that looks impressive once the target is known and nearly useless while the target remains hidden.

The difference between detection and decision is where weak signals go to die.

Remote viewing makes that difference unusually visible because the output is language and drawing rather than a voltage on an instrument. Language is generous. A viewer can report height, metal, movement, water, enclosure, heat, a feeling of industrial activity, a shape that narrows, a bright surface, something underground. Once the target is revealed, the human mind is excellent at finding the pieces that fit. It is also excellent at forgetting the pieces that do not.

This does not mean every apparent correspondence is retrospective fantasy. It means the judging system is part of the instrument.

That point is easy to miss. In a conventional detector, we think of the apparatus as the thing sitting on the bench. But if the output requires a person to decide which of several possible targets best matches a free-form description, the judge belongs inside the measurement chain. So does the target pool. So does the method by which targets were selected. So does the information given to the viewer. So does feedback. So does the rule for deciding what counts as a match. So does the choice of which sessions become anecdotes twenty years later.

The viewer may be the strangest component, but the viewer is not the whole experiment.

This is why the declassified session reports matter even when they are frustrating. They let us see an institution trying to turn ambiguity into procedure. A 1981 report for session 836 describes the impressions as raw intelligence and explicitly says they had not been subjected to intermediate analysis, evaluation, or collation. The requester owns interpretation. That sounds like sensible caution. It also exposes the central operational problem.

If the product is sufficiently ambiguous that its meaning has to be reconstructed by a customer who may possess contextual knowledge the viewer does not, where exactly does the information enter the system?

Suppose the viewer writes “large angular structure, metallic, open space, vertical elements.” Suppose the target is a radar installation. A customer who knows the candidate set may see a useful correspondence. Suppose the target is an industrial plant. The same words still have somewhere to go. Suppose it is a bridge, shipyard, solar facility, launch complex, radio telescope, construction site, refinery, or airport. Free description has a vast surface area for partial contact.

Now reverse the test. Before revealing the target, ask the description to discriminate among those possibilities.

That is a different problem.

Intelligence is full of information that becomes obvious after the event. Before Pearl Harbor, before September 11, before an invasion, before a coup, before a missile launch, the record can contain fragments that look painfully clear in retrospect. The failure is not necessarily that nobody possessed a relevant fragment. It may be that the fragment could not be distinguished from the surrounding mass of fragments until the world supplied the answer key.

Remote viewing belongs in that larger family of epistemic traps whether or not the anomalous effect survives.

That is why I do not want to begin with Uri Geller, spoon bending, Mars, submarines, goats, or any of the other cultural magnets that have accumulated around psychic espionage. They pull the argument toward spectacle before we have built the measuring device.

Begin instead with the hostage report.

A person is missing. A government wants to know where he is. Somebody sits in a room and describes impressions. The report moves through a classified system. There is no need to ridicule anyone in that chain. The people involved were facing a genuine problem with high stakes and poor information. If an unconventional method had even a small chance of producing useful information at low cost, testing it was not self-evidently irrational.

This matters because government interest is routinely misread as government validation.

States investigate low-probability possibilities all the time. They do it because the cost of being surprised can be enormous. An intelligence service does not need to believe an adversary has a capability before asking whether the adversary might have it. A research contract therefore proves expenditure, concern, curiosity, bureaucratic momentum, or some mixture. It does not prove the underlying phenomenon.

The same mistake runs in the other direction. The fact that a program sounds ridiculous does not prove the investigators found nothing interesting. Institutions sometimes fund strange work because strange work occasionally matters. The history of technology contains enough absurd-sounding successes that ridicule is a poor experimental control.

So we need a harder standard than belief.

The first question is whether the experimental output contains information not explained by the ordinary channels and methodological weaknesses we can identify. That is a scientific question.

The second is whether the effect, if present, is stable across investigators, subjects, laboratories, targets, scoring methods, and time. That is a replication question.

The third is whether anyone knows enough about the conditions of success and failure to task the effect prospectively. That is an engineering question.

The fourth is whether the output improves decisions relative to existing sources and baselines. That is an intelligence question.

The fifth is whether the improvement is worth the cost, false positives, distraction, security burden, and opportunity cost. That is an institutional question.

These questions can have different answers.

That is the part that both believers and skeptics are tempted to erase.

A believer can point to a statistically significant body of experiments and slide, almost without noticing, from “the null model is under pressure” to “humans can perceive distant targets” to “the government possessed a psychic intelligence capability.” Those are three different claims. Each additional step adds requirements the previous step does not satisfy.

A skeptic can make the reverse compression. If the operational program did not produce sufficiently reliable intelligence, the skeptic may treat that as proof that no experimental anomaly ever existed. That also skips a step. A weak, unstable, poorly understood effect can be scientifically real and operationally useless. Physics is full of effects that are real only under conditions too delicate for a battlefield. Biology is full of signals that can be measured statistically but cannot diagnose an individual. Economics is full of predictive relationships that evaporate when traded.

The world does not owe us a binary result.

In fact, the most uncomfortable possibility is a channel that is neither nothing nor enough.

Call it a noisy channel for now. Not because Claude Shannon solved remote viewing in advance, and not because information theory can be waved over an anomaly to make it respectable. The phrase is useful because it forces us to ask what information, if any, gets from target to output, at what rate, with what error structure, and through which transformations. If a viewer produces ten descriptors and two are unexpectedly target-relevant, that may be scientifically interesting. If an analyst cannot know which two until after feedback, the operational value may be close to zero.

The location of uncertainty matters.

This is the mistake hidden inside the phrase “psychic spying.” Spying is not the acquisition of an interesting impression. Spying is a system. It has tasking, collection, processing, exploitation, dissemination, and use. Information that cannot move through those stages without becoming dependent on the answer it is supposed to provide is not finished intelligence.

The declassified files occasionally reveal that problem in plain language. The session report hands interpretation to the requester. Fine. But the requester is exactly where contextual knowledge lives. If that context helps select the correspondences that matter, then a successful-looking final product may be partly a property of the combined viewer-analyst system rather than the viewer alone. That may still be useful. Intelligence systems routinely combine weak sources. But then the combined system has to be tested as a system.

Did it change a search area before conventional information arrived? Did it rank candidate locations better than chance or analyst baseline? Did it produce enough false locations to consume resources? Were successes recorded prospectively? Were failures retained with equal care? Could an independent analyst, blinded to the target and outcome, extract the same actionable conclusion?

Those questions are less cinematic than a drawing that resembles a secret facility.

They are also the questions that decide whether the drawing matters.

There is another reason to stay with the boring paperwork. Classification distorts memory.

A public scientific controversy leaves journals, replications, criticism, correspondence, conference fights, failed experiments, and eventually textbooks. A classified research program leaves a different ecology. Some records survive. Some are redacted. Some were never retained. Operational anecdotes circulate among people who cannot show their work. Declassification happens unevenly. Later participants remember the cases that mattered to them. Journalists and enthusiasts select the vivid material. Skeptics select the embarrassing material. The archive becomes a machine for producing asymmetric certainty.

This does not mean “the real evidence is still classified.” That sentence is too convenient. Missing evidence cannot be used as positive evidence. Classification can explain why a record is incomplete; it cannot supply the missing result.

But neither can the surviving public corpus be treated automatically as a random sample.

The right response is not to give up. It is to label the joints.

What was known before the session? Who knew it? What could leak? How was the target chosen? What exactly did the viewer produce, and when? What was changed after feedback? Who judged the result? Against what alternatives? How were misses recorded? What did the customer do differently because of the information? What independent evidence later verified the claim? Which part of that chain is documented, which is testimony, which is disputed, and which is our own inference?

Do that long enough and the supernatural haze starts to clear.

Not necessarily because the phenomenon disappears. Because the claim becomes specific enough to fail.

That is progress.

A serious investigation of anomalous cognition should be trying to make the claim easier to kill. If remote viewing is merely leakage, target-set artifacts, flexible judging, selective memory, fraud, sensory cueing, or statistical malpractice, stronger protocols should squeeze the effect toward zero. If a residual persists, stronger protocols should narrow the places where an ordinary explanation can hide. Either result is better than a century of anecdotes.

The government program gives us something laboratory parapsychology alone cannot: the collision between an anomalous claim and an institution that ultimately needs answers, not effects.

That collision is brutal.

A laboratory can ask whether descriptions are matched to targets above chance across many trials. An operations officer may need to know which warehouse contains a hostage tonight. The laboratory can average. The officer has one hostage. The laboratory can tolerate a weak effect if the sample is large enough. The officer cannot deploy a hundred rescue teams so the signal can emerge statistically.

This is not an argument that only large effects are real. It is an argument that usefulness has a denominator.

Later in this book we will have to reconstruct the 1995 fight carefully, because it is too often summarized as a scoreboard: Utts said yes, Hyman said no. That caricature wastes the case. The interesting question is how two experienced evaluators could look at overlapping material and disagree about what had been established while the sponsoring institution could still reach a practical decision about the program.

We will also have to go back into the operational files, including the failures. Especially the failures. A hit tells you that something coincided. A miss can tell you which part of the supposed machine is unstable. Training records, target pools, cueing procedures, feedback classes, and mundane administrative forms may teach us more about the claim than the famous sessions do.

And we will have to resist the oldest temptation in this subject: to make government secrecy do evidentiary work it cannot do.

The fact that a report was secret does not make its contents true. The fact that officers took a claim seriously does not validate the claim. The fact that a program lasted for years does not establish efficacy. Bureaucracies can continue weak programs. They can also continue cheap hedges against uncertain threats. They can become attached to unusual tools. They can collect anomalies without understanding them. They can terminate programs that contain a real effect because the effect is not useful enough.

All of those possibilities remain open at the beginning.

That is where they should remain.

The hostage report is still sitting there, decades later, stripped of its urgency by declassification. The viewer’s impressions are now public artifacts. We know the larger history in a way the people in the room did not. We know that the American hostages would eventually come home. We know the remote-viewing program would acquire new names, sponsors, defenders, critics, and eventually a public archive large enough to support almost any preexisting opinion if sampled carelessly.

What we do not get to do is send our hindsight backward.

At the moment of collection, the answer was hidden.

That is the only moment that counts.
