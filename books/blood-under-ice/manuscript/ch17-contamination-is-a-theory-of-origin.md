# Contamination Is a Theory of Origin

Contamination is usually treated as laboratory hygiene: keep foreign material out of the sample.

For extraordinary biological claims it is something deeper.

It is an alternative theory of origin.

If a sequence appears in a clean-access Antarctic brine sample, one explanation is that the organism lived there. Another is that the sequence entered through drilling fluid, equipment, reagents, clothing, air, transport or laboratory processing. Controls are not bureaucratic accessories. They are competing-world detectors.

Blood Falls is unusually useful because the contamination argument did not remain hypothetical. The IceMole clean-access campaign turned it into something that could be measured.

The probe was not described as "sterile" and then waved through by adjective. Its exterior bioburden was tracked during field cleaning. In the later planetary-protection analysis, the cleaned IceMole surface reached microbial loads comparable to most clean rooms and below those measured in the ice surrounding the sampling target.

That is already a better statement than *uncontaminated*.

It names a comparison.

The sampling sequence also matters. During the November 2014 Blood Falls operation, melt generated during drilling was continuously pumped out. Conductivity was monitored as the probe advanced. A sharp conductivity increase indicated penetration into the pressurized brine conduit when the probe had reached about **15.2 meters below the surface** along a **16.6-meter melt path**. After penetration, the team pumped through roughly **two tubing volumes** before collecting the englacial brine.

That flushing step is not procedural trivia.

The tubing itself is a possible transport route. Fluid touching the line before the target is reached can carry surface or borehole material. If the first liquid out of the tube is treated as pristine reservoir water, the sample inherits the history of the access path. Flushing converts that history from an unexamined assumption into a controlled transition.

The campaign ultimately collected on the order of **100 to 150 liters** of brine for microbial and geochemical work. That volume creates opportunities for replication and chemistry. It does not make the sample magically free of foreign DNA.

The strongest contamination result is smaller and more interesting.

The team identified potential microbial contaminants during cleaning and then asked whether those same taxa appeared in the final englacial sample. Very few did. In the published validation, cleaning-associated potential contaminants represented only about **0.063 percent of the final 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequences**.

That number should not be used as a universal contamination rate for Blood Falls.

It is assay-specific, campaign-specific and taxon-identification-dependent. It also does not prove that every contaminant was recognized. A contaminating organism absent from the cleaning reference set can still evade that comparison. Reagent contamination can enter later. Low-biomass sequence workflows can amplify tiny backgrounds. The right interpretation is narrower:

**the clean-access team measured a known contamination channel and found little carry-through from that channel into the final sample.**

That is evidence.

It is also a model for how extraordinary biological claims should be argued.

Instead of asking whether contamination was "possible," enumerate routes.

Probe exterior.

Melt channel.

Sampling tube.

Pump.

Collection vessel.

Field tent.

Extraction kit.

PCR reagents.

Index hopping.

Reference-database error.

Human handling.

Then attach controls to routes.

This is what turns contamination from a skeptical incantation into a competing causal model.

The same logic governs meteorite biology, ancient DNA, deep-biosphere drilling and future ocean-world missions. The more astonishing the biological claim, the more aggressively mundane transport routes must be modeled.

Blood Falls sits in an environment where tiny biomass and unusual chemistry magnify this problem. A trace contaminant can look important because the indigenous signal is weak. Conversely, hyperconservative contamination filtering can erase genuine low-abundance organisms.

There is no universal abundance threshold that resolves the tradeoff.

A taxon present at 0.01 percent could be contamination.

It could also be a real rare member of the community.

Abundance alone cannot decide origin.

What helps is provenance: blank controls, equipment controls, reagent controls, replicate sampling, spatial gradients, molecular damage patterns when relevant, independent laboratories and predictions made before the desired organism appears.

The 2026 micro-eukaryote work around the Taylor Glacier terminus illustrates the same problem at a different scale. Those samples were collected from the surface/mixing system under sterile DNA/RNA-free procedures, not from one perfectly isolated ancient reservoir. The biological inference therefore depends on geography and comparison: brine-influenced sites versus surrounding moraine and other Dry Valleys habitats, composition rather than mere detection, and whether the assemblage fits a relict marine history better than modern transport.

That distinction matters because *marine lineage detected near Blood Falls* can be rewritten too easily as *ancient marine organism recovered from a sealed subglacial lake*.

Those are not the same claim.

One is a molecular and biogeographic observation in a mixing zone.

The other specifies source reservoir, isolation history and transport.

The gap between them is exactly where contamination, mixing and provenance live.

Planetary science raises the stakes further.

Suppose a future Europa melt probe returns a microbial-looking sequence. The discovery claim will not depend only on whether the instrument was clean at launch. It will depend on whether Earth organisms survived cruise, whether the probe carried them through the ice, whether sampling hardware transported them between depth horizons, whether reagents contributed sequences, and whether the signal repeats in independently sampled material.

Planetary protection is therefore not separate from life detection.

It is part of the inference engine.

A sterile-looking instrument is not enough. A chain of custody must be able to explain how the signal could have traveled from every known terrestrial reservoir—and then show why those routes fit the data worse than indigenous origin.

This creates an important asymmetry.

Contamination does not have to explain the whole sample to matter. One contaminant lineage can invalidate one spectacular taxonomic claim while leaving the rest of the community real. Conversely, finding a small measurable contaminant fraction does not invalidate all biology in the sample.

The inference must be organism by organism, route by route.

This is why the IceMole result is more scientifically useful than the word *clean*.

A residual contamination estimate can be challenged.

A control taxon can be tracked.

A tubing flush can be repeated.

A field blank can disagree.

A second lab can test the same prediction.

"Pristine" cannot be falsified nearly as well.

Contamination is not the enemy of discovery.

Unmodeled contamination is.

And the strongest biosignature is not the one found in the cleanest-sounding place.

It is the one whose alternative routes of origin have been measured badly enough to lose.
