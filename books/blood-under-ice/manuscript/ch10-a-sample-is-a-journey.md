# A Sample Is a Journey

Scientists often speak of a sample as if location alone defines it: a Blood Falls sample, a Martian sample, a plume sample. But every sample has a biography.

Brine under pressure moves toward a fracture. Temperature changes. Salts precipitate or redissolve. Iron oxidizes. Gas equilibria shift. Cells experience stress. The material reaches a collection bottle, then a freezer, an aircraft, a laboratory, a preparation bench and an instrument. Each stage can preserve some features and erase others.

The problem is especially acute for biosignatures. A molecule useful as evidence of life can be destroyed by oxidation, generated abiotically during processing, introduced by contamination or concentrated from trace abundance into apparent significance. A living cell can die while preserving DNA. Extracellular DNA can survive after the source organism disappears. Mineral surfaces can protect organics or catalyze their destruction.

Blood Falls lets researchers compare end-member brine with transformed surface deposits and surrounding environments. That comparison turns transport into data rather than nuisance.

For planetary missions, the same mindset is essential. The correct question is not simply what did the instrument detect? It is what sequence of physical and chemical events could have produced the detected state from the original environment. A biosignature is not a thing. It is an argument with a chain of custody.
