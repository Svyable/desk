# What Counts as a Biosignature

A biosignature is often described as a substance, structure or pattern indicating life. In practice it is a competition between explanations.

Methane can be biological or geological. Organic molecules can be synthesized abiotically. Isotopic fractionation can be suggestive without being unique. Cell-like shapes can be mineral. Pigments can degrade. DNA is decisive for terrestrial life in a clean terrestrial sample and nearly useless if contamination cannot be excluded.

Blood Falls is a good training ground because researchers already know life is present somewhere in the system, yet individual measurements remain ambiguous. Iron minerals are not proof of microbial iron cycling. Sulfur species are not proof of a particular pathway. A gene sequence does not prove current metabolic activity.

The strongest biosignatures are therefore compound arguments: morphology plus chemistry plus isotopes plus environmental context plus exclusion of plausible abiotic routes, ideally with replication across independent methods.

Alien life, if it exists, will not owe us familiar DNA. The habit of building convergent cases on Earth is preparation for a situation where no single measurement can be granted privileged status.
