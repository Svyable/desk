Trust, Truth, and Value in the Age of Infinite Intelligence.

For most of history, fabricating convincing evidence — a signature, a photograph, a familiar voice — was expensive relative to producing the real thing, and the institutions that run modern life (audits, credentials, safety marks, editorial trust) were built on that cost gap rather than on any special honesty in human affairs. Generative AI has narrowed or reversed that gap across video, audio, and text at the same moment several of those older institutions were already showing structural fatigue for reasons that had nothing to do with artificial intelligence.

The book argues that the resulting scarcity is not content, which has never been more abundant, but verifiable authenticity: proof of where a piece of media came from, and proof that a given actor online is a real, distinct human being. It traces that argument from a $25 million deepfake video-call fraud in Hong Kong, through the deep history of body doubles, forged Vermeers, and seals and audits, through Theranos, Wirecard, and psychology's replication crisis, into the economics of costly signaling, and out into the content-provenance standards, biometric personhood credentials, and identity-verification businesses now being built in response — without losing sight of the exclusion, insurance, legal, and labor questions that infrastructure raises in turn.
