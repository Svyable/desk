# The Watermark's Limits

In 2024, researchers turned watermarking into a sport. The NeurIPS competition *Erasing the Invisible* gave teams thousands of AI-generated images carrying invisible watermarks and asked them to remove the marks without visibly damaging the pictures. By the time the results were written up, the best teams were removing watermarks from at least eighty-nine percent of images on average across the leading submissions; the winning beige-box attack reported a 95.7 percent removal rate with little visible degradation. The important point was not that one clever attack had defeated one careless implementation. It was that several modern watermarking systems could be stress-tested by outsiders, under realistic uncertainty about the underlying scheme, and fail often enough to make presence or absence a weak basis for certainty.

That result belonged to a longer argument. In 2023, University of Maryland researchers had shown that low-perturbation image watermarks could be attacked with diffusion purification: add noise, reconstruct the image with a generative model, and wash away enough of the hidden signal while preserving much of what a person sees. They also showed the reverse problem. An attacker could sometimes make an ordinary image look watermarked to a detector. This matters because a watermark is useful only if two errors stay acceptably rare at once: missing marked material and accusing unmarked material of being marked. A system that is merely hard to erase but easy to spoof has not solved the trust problem. It has moved it.

Text watermarks have the same basic difficulty in a different medium. One influential scheme proposed by John Kirchenbauer and his coauthors biases a language model toward a pseudorandom subset of tokens, leaving a statistical pattern across enough generated text for a detector to find. The appeal is obvious. The prose need not contain a visible label, and no single word gives the game away. But language is unusually easy to transform while preserving meaning. Paraphrase it. Translate it and translate it back. Let a person rewrite a few sentences. The detector is being asked to recognize origin through a channel whose ordinary use consists of changing the surface form. Later research has therefore treated watermark robustness less as a solved labeling problem than as an adversarial tradeoff: how much transformation can the mark survive before the content itself must be changed enough that the mark becomes conspicuous or the output less useful?

None of this makes watermarking pointless. It changes what the claim can reasonably be. A good watermark can raise the cost of laundering synthetic material, help a platform identify untouched model output, support copyright or provenance workflows, and make casual deception less convenient. Security rarely requires an unpickable lock. But the strongest version of the public promise — that invisible marking will let a viewer reliably decide whether a disputed image, recording, or passage came from a generative system — asks more of the signal than current evidence supports. An adversary does not need to defeat the system forever. They need one transformation that survives the detector while preserving whatever makes the artifact persuasive.

Content Credentials are a different mechanism and deserve a different criticism. Earlier versions of this debate often described a C2PA credential as if it were simply metadata attached to a file and therefore doomed the moment somebody took a screenshot or a platform recompressed the image. That is now too crude. The current C2PA architecture binds a signed manifest to an asset cryptographically. A manifest can be embedded in the file, but the standard also supports external manifests and what it calls soft bindings: fingerprints or invisible watermarks that can help match a changed rendition back to provenance stored elsewhere. C2PA calls a credential using such recovery mechanisms a durable Content Credential. Version 2.4, published in April 2026, goes further by adding repository receipts and continuing the standard's effort to make provenance recoverable rather than merely attached.

The distinction is important because ordinary redistribution can still sever the obvious path to provenance without proving that provenance is gone forever. A screenshot creates a new asset and breaks the original hard binding. Re-encoding, metadata removal, or editing in software that does not preserve C2PA data can remove an embedded manifest. C2PA's own security guidance treats manifest stripping as a real threat. But the standard also explicitly anticipates that problem: if a manifest survives in a repository and a usable soft binding still matches the derivative, provenance may be rediscovered. Whether that works depends on what was deployed, how aggressively the content changed, and whether the relevant repository and resolver are available. "The credential was stripped" is therefore not the same claim as "this asset can no longer be linked to provenance."

That produces a less dramatic but more useful rule: **presence can be evidence; absence is ambiguous.** A valid credential can tell you something about the file in front of you, the signer, and the recorded chain of creation or editing. A missing credential might mean the asset never had one. It might mean the credential was stripped. It might mean the copy passed through a system that did not preserve it. It might mean a durable binding exists but the viewer is not using a resolver capable of finding it. Treating an uncredentialed image as "probably fake" would create exactly the kind of implied-truth problem that labeling systems are supposed to reduce: marked material acquires scrutiny while everything outside the system quietly inherits undeserved innocence or suspicion.

Adoption also makes static claims about fragility age quickly. In September 2025, Google announced that Pixel 10 cameras would attach Content Credentials to JPEG captures and that Google Photos would validate and display credentials for supported images. The interesting part is not the brand name. It is the change in architecture. Once capture devices, editors, operating systems, platforms, and repositories begin preserving or resolving provenance by default, the cost of maintaining a chain can fall sharply. That is the strongest counterargument to the bleak version of this chapter. A provenance standard does not need to be physically inseparable from every copy if enough of the surrounding ecosystem learns how to preserve, recover, and display it.

The harder limitation survives all of those improvements because it has nothing to do with metadata. Imagine a credentialed camera photographing a staged scene. The camera can sign the image at capture. Every later edit can be recorded. The manifest can validate perfectly. The photograph can still mislead. A person may have posed the subject, chosen an angle that excludes inconvenient context, waited for an unrepresentative instant, or attached a false caption afterward. Nothing in cryptographic provenance can tell us whether the world in front of the lens was arranged honestly. A valid chain of custody authenticates a history of an artifact. It does not authenticate the proposition a person wants the artifact to prove.

That boundary is not a defect in C2PA so much as a reminder of what provenance is for. The system can help answer questions that were becoming dangerously hard: Who or what signed this? Has this file changed since that signing? What edits were declared? Can this derivative be linked back to an earlier version? Those are valuable questions. They are not the same as: Is the caption fair? Is the scene representative? Was the source coerced? Is the claim true? Verification infrastructure becomes dangerous when a useful answer to the first set is allowed to masquerade as an answer to the second.

The economic question is therefore not whether watermarking or provenance can make fabrication impossible. They cannot. It is whether they can make some forms of deception more expensive, make authentic originals easier to defend, and give institutions better evidence when a dispute arrives. A publisher holding a signed original has something a reposting account with a stripped copy does not. A camera that can establish capture provenance gives a fact-checker more to work with than a JPEG that appeared from nowhere. A platform that can resolve durable credentials may recover history that an ordinary file viewer misses. Each of those gains is real even though none settles the underlying truth of the content.

The useful standard is calibrated trust. Watermarks are signals whose robustness has to be tested against adversaries, not assumed from the fact that they are invisible. Content Credentials are provenance records whose value depends on signing, preservation, recovery, trust lists, and the behavior of the surrounding ecosystem. Both can raise the cost of lying. Neither abolishes the need to investigate what the artifact is being used to claim.

That is a less satisfying ending than a permanent authenticity stamp. It is also closer to how trust has always worked. Seals were forged. Signatures were copied. Audits failed. Currency was redesigned after counterfeiters learned the old design. The answer was rarely to abandon verification because it could be defeated, or to mistake a successful check for proof of everything that mattered. It was to build systems in which evidence accumulated, failure left traces, and no single signal had to carry more epistemic weight than it could bear.
