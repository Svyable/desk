# The Watermark's Limits

In October 2023, computer science researchers at the University of Maryland published a study asking a deliberately unglamorous question about the watermarking schemes described in the previous chapter: how hard is it, actually, for an ordinary adversary to add a fake watermark to a real image, or strip a real watermark from a fake one? Their answer, reported widely at the time, was considerably less reassuring than the marketing language around most watermarking products implied. Low-perturbation watermarks — the kind embedded so faintly that they remain invisible to a human viewer, which is precisely the design goal every commercial system aims for — turned out to be vulnerable to a class of attack called diffusion purification, in which an adversary runs the watermarked image through a diffusion model's noising-and-denoising process, effectively regenerating the image from a noisy version of itself and washing out the embedded signal along with it, while leaving the visible content largely intact. The technique does not require breaking any cryptography or reverse-engineering a specific company's proprietary watermarking algorithm. It requires access to the kind of generative model the watermark was trying to flag content as coming from in the first place — a circularity that has proven difficult for the field to escape.

Subsequent research has only sharpened the picture. A widely publicized adversarial challenge run alongside the 2024 NeurIPS conference — one of the field's most prominent annual research gatherings — invited teams to compete specifically at defeating state-of-the-art image watermarks under varying levels of information about how the watermark had been embedded. The winning submission achieved watermark removal rates above ninety-five percent while leaving the underlying image's visual quality essentially unchanged to a human viewer, a result that would have been merely interesting as an academic exercise if it did not directly undercut the practical promise made by every watermarking scheme examined in the previous chapter: that a viewer, a platform, or a court could rely on the presence or absence of a watermark as reasonably durable evidence of an image's origin. Separate research published around the same time, evaluating detectors built around a family of watermarking techniques called TreeRing, found that a comparatively simple adversarial perturbation — a small, deliberately crafted distortion invisible to the eye but specifically engineered to confuse the detector — could collapse the detector's accuracy from a level useful enough to act on down to close to the reliability of a coin flip.

Text carries its own version of this problem, distinct from images in ways worth examining on their own terms. John Kirchenbauer and his coauthors at the University of Maryland published an influential 2023 scheme for watermarking language-model output that works by subtly biasing which words a model is statistically inclined to choose next: the vocabulary is silently split into "green" and "red" token lists determined by a pseudorandom function seeded by the preceding word, and the model is nudged, imperceptibly to a human reader, toward selecting from the green list more often than chance alone would predict, producing a statistical signature detectable by an algorithm even though no individual sentence looks unusual to a person reading it. The scheme is elegant, and it works reasonably well against unmodified output. It is considerably less robust against an adversary willing to do what any student or professional writer routinely does anyway: paraphrase the text, run it through a translation and back-translation cycle, or simply rewrite a few sentences by hand. Kirchenbauer's own follow-up research, along with a wider body of subsequent work, found that such paraphrasing attacks can substantially degrade a watermark's detectability, and a separate strand of theoretical research has gone further, arguing that strong, tamper-proof watermarking of generative model output may be fundamentally impossible to guarantee against a sufficiently motivated adversary, rather than merely difficult with current techniques — an unusually stark claim for a body of applied computer science research to make about its own subject, and one that, if it holds up, would mean the watermarking arms race examined throughout this chapter has no finish line even in principle, only a series of temporary advantages that shift back and forth as each side's techniques improve.

Content credentials, the C2PA-style manifests examined in the previous chapter, face a different and in some ways more mundane vulnerability, one that requires no adversarial machine learning expertise at all. Because a content credential is a separate record attached alongside an image or video file rather than woven irreversibly into the pixels themselves, almost any ordinary act of sharing an image can strip it away entirely, whether or not anyone involved intends deception. Taking a screenshot of an image discards its metadata by construction, since a screenshot is a new file capturing only what appeared on the screen, with no mechanism to carry forward an underlying manifest it never had access to. Uploading an image to most major social media platforms triggers automatic recompression that frequently strips embedded metadata as an incidental side effect of the platform's own file-size optimization, with no malicious intent required from anyone in the chain. Converting a file between formats using tools not specifically built to preserve C2PA manifests routinely drops them as well. The practical result is a genuinely uncomfortable irony: the content most likely to need verifiable provenance — an image or video that has gone viral, been reposted across a dozen platforms, been screenshotted and reshared thousands of times — is precisely the content most likely to have lost whatever provenance record it once carried somewhere in that chain, while the original, still-credentialed file sits untouched and unseen in whatever archive first produced it.

The economics of this arms race are also worth stating plainly, because they cut against the intuitive assumption that better-funded defenders should eventually win a technical contest against attackers. Watermark removal research of the kind surveyed above is typically conducted by academic teams with modest compute budgets, explicitly to demonstrate a vulnerability rather than to build a commercial product; the NeurIPS "Erasing the Invisible" challenge examined earlier drew its winning submission from researchers working with far less computational infrastructure than the major AI labs that built the watermarking schemes being attacked. That asymmetry matters because it means watermark defeat is not, in practice, a contest reserved for equally well-resourced state or corporate adversaries; it is closer to a contest any well-informed graduate student can meaningfully compete in and sometimes win, using techniques published openly in the same academic venues the watermarking schemes themselves were originally introduced in. A defense that can be defeated by a comparatively modest academic effort is not a defense that will reliably hold against a well-funded state intelligence service or organized fraud operation with considerably deeper technical resources at its disposal, a point this book's chapters on state-linked influence operations and the North Korean IT worker scheme make directly relevant rather than hypothetical.

A staged but entirely undoctored photograph makes this limitation concrete rather than theoretical. A photojournalist could, in principle, arrange a scene to create a misleading impression — asking a subject to pose in a particular way, choosing a specific camera angle that omits relevant context, timing a shot to catch an unrepresentative moment — using only a completely unmodified camera, with every C2PA credential intact and cryptographically valid from the moment of capture onward. The resulting image would pass every technical check this chapter and the previous one describe: a legitimate manifest, an unbroken chain of custody, no detectable watermark tampering, because no digital manipulation occurred at all. The deception, if there is one, happened in front of the lens rather than inside the file, a category of misrepresentation content-provenance infrastructure was never built to catch and, by its own internal logic, cannot catch, because the technology's entire claim is about what happened to a file after capture, not about what happened in the world the capture was pointed at.

There is a further limitation worth stating plainly, because it cuts against an intuitive but mistaken assumption many casual observers bring to this technology: a valid content credential does not certify that an image's content is authentic in the sense of depicting something that actually happened. It certifies only that the credential's own chain of custody — which software touched the file, in what order, starting from what claimed origin — is intact and cryptographically consistent. A photograph staged to create a false impression, manipulated within the same software the credential system is designed to log, can carry a perfectly valid, uninterrupted C2PA manifest showing exactly what was done to it, without that manifest saying anything at all about whether the underlying scene was genuine, posed, or staged for a specific rhetorical effect. The credential answers "what happened to this file after it was created," a genuinely useful and previously unavailable piece of information. It does not answer, and was never designed to answer, the older and harder question that has always mattered most: does this content fairly represent the world it claims to depict?

None of this means the infrastructure examined in the previous chapter is worthless, and it would be a mistake to read this chapter as an argument for abandoning it. A watermark that can be removed by a sophisticated adversary running a diffusion-purification attack still meaningfully raises the cost and technical sophistication required to strip it, compared to no watermark at all — and, in keeping with this book's recurring economic argument, raising the cost of an attack, even without eliminating the possibility of a successful one, changes who bothers to mount it and how often. A casual bad actor sharing a fabricated recording through an ordinary social media repost is stopped, or at least meaningfully slowed, by a watermark's fragility to screenshotting in exactly the same way a casual shoplifter is stopped by a locked display case that a determined professional thief could still defeat. The Getty Images litigation examined in the previous chapter, and the growing adoption of content credentials by major news organizations, both depend on a somewhat narrower and more defensible claim than "this technology stops fabrication": the claim that a credentialed original, held by its publisher and checkable against any copy whose provenance is later disputed, gives a court, a fact-checker, or a skeptical reader somewhere authoritative to point, even if the credential attached to a widely circulated copy has long since been stripped away in transit.

What this chapter's evidence does argue for, more modestly, is calibrated expectations about what "proof of work for reality" actually accomplishes, and against the kind of overselling that has characterized much of the public conversation about watermarking and provenance since these tools first launched. A watermark is a costly signal in the sense described two chapters earlier only to the extent that defeating it actually costs something meaningful relative to the value of the fabrication it would otherwise flag — and the research surveyed in this chapter suggests that, for at least the current generation of low-perturbation, imperceptible watermarks, that cost is measured in a graduate student's afternoon rather than in the kind of specialized expertise and infrastructure this book's earlier chapters describe forgers historically needing to fake a signature or a bond rating convincingly. The arms race between watermarking and watermark removal is still young, and it would be premature to declare either side permanently ahead; publishers of watermarking schemes continue to respond to each published attack with more robust successors, in the same iterative pattern that has characterized essentially every security technology this book has examined, from wax seals to engraved currency to cryptographic signatures. What the current state of that race does establish, with reasonable confidence, is that no one should treat the presence of a watermark or a content credential today as durable proof of anything, in the way a well-designed audit or a UL mark could once be trusted to remain durable for years or decades without renewed scrutiny. The infrastructure examined in this book's chapters on provenance is best understood not as a finished lock but as the opening move in a contest that has not yet been decided, and that may never fully resolve in either side's permanent favor.
