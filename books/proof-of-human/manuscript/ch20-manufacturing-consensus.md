# Manufacturing Consensus

More than seventy countries, home to over half the world's population, held national elections in 2024 — a scheduling coincidence significant enough that election researchers and journalists routinely described the year as democracy's largest single test in history, with something on the order of four billion people eligible to cast a ballot somewhere across the calendar year. That concentration of electoral activity, arriving at precisely the moment generative AI tools had become cheap, fluent, and widely available, made 2024 an unusually revealing natural experiment in how state and non-state actors alike would attempt to use the technology examined throughout this book to influence a genuinely global wave of democratic choices, rather than a single isolated vote.

In May 2024, OpenAI published an unusual document for a company more accustomed to talking about model capabilities than intelligence operations: a threat report describing five distinct, covert influence campaigns that had been using its tools and that the company had subsequently banned from its platform. The report was notable partly for its content and partly for the fact that it existed at all — the first time a major AI developer had published a systematic account of state-linked actors attempting to weaponize its own products for propaganda, rather than leaving that kind of disclosure entirely to government intelligence agencies and independent researchers.

It is worth being precise about what OpenAI's report actually established versus what it merely raised as a concern, because the distinction matters for how much confidence this chapter's account deserves. OpenAI's own methodology, as described in its report, identified the networks primarily through behavioral signals in how the accounts used its own products — patterns of usage, prompt content, and account clustering internal to OpenAI's own platform — rather than through independent verification of the accounts' actual downstream influence on public opinion once their content reached other platforms. This means the report is strong evidence that these networks used OpenAI's tools in the ways described, and reasonably strong evidence about the networks' scale and organization, but comparatively weaker evidence about how much the resulting content actually moved anyone's beliefs once it left OpenAI's own platform and entered the wider information ecosystem this book's other chapters describe. That gap between documented use and documented effect recurs across nearly every influence-operation case this book has examined, and it is worth naming here explicitly rather than letting the scale of the operations described imply a scale of proven impact this evidence does not fully support.

The largest and most developed of the operations OpenAI described was Doppelganger, a Russian network the U.S. Treasury Department has separately linked to the Kremlin, known for spoofing the web addresses and visual branding of legitimate news organizations to spread content undermining Western support for Ukraine. According to OpenAI's account, the network had been using its tools for distinctly unglamorous, industrial-scale tasks: generating comments in multiple languages to post across social media platforms, translating Russian-language articles into English and French, and converting website content into ready-to-post Facebook updates — the same generative-text capability examined earlier in this book's chapter on when machines learned to lie well, deployed here not to fabricate a single dramatic fake but to multiply the reach of an existing propaganda apparatus at a scale and linguistic range no human translation team could match affordably.

The financial scale of state-linked influence operations, though harder to pin down precisely than the corporate fraud figures examined earlier in this book, appears to dwarf even the more expensive individual scams this book has traced. Meta's own public disclosures about the Spamouflage takedown described a network that had persisted and adapted across years of repeated enforcement action, spending resources on infrastructure, account maintenance, and content production across dozens of platforms simultaneously in a way no individual fraudster or even a well-funded criminal syndicate could sustain, precisely because a state budget, unlike a criminal enterprise's, does not need the operation to be profitable on its own terms; the payoff is measured in influence and discord rather than direct financial return, which removes one of the ordinary economic constraints — the operation has to eventually pay for itself — that limits how long a purely profit-motivated fraud can persist once its returns start to shrink.

A second network, called Spamouflage and linked by researchers to actors operating on behalf of Chinese state interests, showed a different and in some ways more mundane pattern of misuse. Rather than generating propaganda content directly, the accounts OpenAI identified had used its tools to debug code for a website targeting Chinese dissidents abroad, to analyze the performance of the network's own social media posts, and to research current events and public sentiment — the unglamorous back-office work of running an influence operation, rather than its most visible output. Meta, disclosing its own findings around the same period, described Spamouflage as the largest covert influence operation the company had ever taken down, spanning a wide array of platforms and forums and linked by Meta's own investigators to Chinese law enforcement. The overlap between OpenAI's and Meta's independent findings on the same network is itself instructive: two different companies, examining two different layers of the same operation — the text-generation layer and the distribution layer — arrived at converging conclusions about a single, sophisticated, state-linked actor operating across the seams between them.

What both networks illustrate, more than any single fabricated image or cloned voice could, is that the most consequential use of generative AI in information warfare may not be deepfakes in the dramatic sense this book's opening chapters describe — a fabricated video call, a cloned presidential voice — but something considerably less cinematic: the industrialization of ordinary-looking content at a volume and linguistic range that used to require a much larger human staff. A propaganda operation that once needed dozens of fluent speakers to translate, adapt, and localize its messaging across several target languages can now accomplish the same task with a handful of operators and a language model, redirecting the labor savings toward volume, personalization, or simply toward staying one step ahead of platform detection efforts. This is the same unit-economics argument that runs through this entire book, applied here not to a single fraudulent transaction or a single fabricated recording but to an entire propaganda apparatus: the marginal cost of producing another comment, another translated article, another repackaged post has fallen toward zero, and an operation constrained for decades by the cost of skilled human labor can now scale its output largely without that constraint.

The detection and disruption of both networks also illustrates something about where the current balance of power in this contest actually sits, at least for now. OpenAI and Meta caught these operations not primarily because the AI-generated content itself was detectably synthetic — machine-translated text and AI-assisted code debugging do not carry an obvious tell the way an early deepfake video's flickering jawline once did — but because the accounts using these tools exhibited the same behavioral patterns that have always characterized coordinated inauthentic activity: clusters of accounts posting in synchronized bursts, content originating from a small number of underlying operators despite appearing to come from many independent voices, and engagement patterns inconsistent with organic human activity. In other words, the most effective current defense against AI-powered influence operations is not a tool that inspects a single piece of content and declares it synthetic, in the manner of the watermarks and deepfake detectors examined in earlier chapters, but the older, network-level analysis that has been used to catch coordinated propaganda campaigns since long before generative AI existed, merely applied at a scale commensurate with the new threat. The content-level verification infrastructure this book has spent several chapters examining — watermarks, content credentials, personhood credentials — turns out to matter less here than the platform-level behavioral analysis that both OpenAI and Meta relied on, a reminder that no single layer of the verification stack this book describes is sufficient on its own, and that the network is sometimes a more honest witness than the individual piece of content moving through it.

Microsoft's own threat intelligence unit, tracking the run-up to the 2024 American election independently of OpenAI's and Meta's disclosures, documented a broader and more geopolitically varied picture than the Doppelganger-and-Spamouflage account alone suggests. Microsoft's researchers found Russian-linked operators shifting their messaging over the course of the campaign, moving from attacks on one candidate to attacks on another as the race's frontrunner changed, while separately tracking Iranian cyber-enabled influence operations that combined hacking, leaked material, and generative-AI-assisted fake news sites in an effort researchers assessed leaned toward damaging the opposing party's chances, and Chinese-linked accounts that appeared less focused on favoring either American candidate than on identifying and amplifying domestic social divisions likely to weaken public trust regardless of who won. That three-way, multi-directional picture — different state actors pursuing different, sometimes opposing electoral preferences using a shared toolkit of generative AI capabilities — complicates any simple narrative in which a single foreign adversary is trying to install a single preferred outcome. It suggests instead that generative AI has become a generic capability multiple, often rival, state intelligence services now reach for by default, each pursuing its own narrower interests, in the same way this book's earlier chapters describe generative tools becoming a generic capability for corporate fraud, personal grudges, and school-level cruelty alike, regardless of who is doing the reaching.

The 2024 election cycle also demonstrated a subtler point about influence operations that the industrial-scale content-generation framing of this chapter can obscure: volume alone does not guarantee effectiveness. Independent researchers who studied Doppelganger's and Spamouflage's actual measured engagement — genuine shares, comments, and click-throughs from real human users, as distinct from the network's own automated amplification — found relatively modest audience reach for the sheer scale of content produced, suggesting a persistent gap between an operation's productive capacity and its actual persuasive impact. This complicates any simple narrative in which cheap AI-generated propaganda straightforwardly translates into proportional influence over public opinion; producing content at industrial scale is not the same achievement as an actual industrial-scale audience choosing to believe or share it, and the sheer existence of a large, well-funded influence operation does not by itself prove the operation succeeded at its stated goal, a distinction this book's chapters on pink slime and manufactured consensus alike should not blur in the retelling.

The United States has no direct federal equivalent to the Digital Services Act's systemic-risk reporting requirement, a regulatory asymmetry worth noting because it means the two governments' own citizens are, in effect, protected by different tiers of mandatory disclosure depending on which side of the Atlantic a given influence operation happens to target most heavily. American platforms operating in the European Union must comply with the DSA's requirements for their European users regardless of any domestic American statute, which has produced the somewhat unusual situation of major American technology companies building compliance infrastructure specifically to satisfy European regulatory demands that have no equivalent American counterpart, then in some cases voluntarily extending elements of that same infrastructure to their American operations rather than maintaining two entirely separate systems. Whether that voluntary extension proves durable, or whether it depends on a European regulatory stick that American users cannot themselves independently invoke, is a live and unresolved question this book's overall skepticism about relying on any single company's discretion would counsel taking seriously rather than assuming will simply continue by default.

European regulators have moved to make this kind of detection and disclosure a legal obligation rather than a voluntary courtesy from whichever company happens to notice misuse of its own products first. The European Union's Digital Services Act became directly enforceable across member states in February 2024, and among its provisions is a requirement that the very largest online platforms — those reaching more than forty-five million users in the European Union — routinely assess and report on systemic risks their services pose around elections and the spread of disinformation, with vetted outside researchers granted access to platform data specifically to study influence operations rather than depending entirely on the platforms' own internal threat-intelligence teams to self-report. The European Commission formally folded its previously voluntary Code of Practice on Disinformation into the Digital Services Act's binding framework in mid-2024, converting what had been a set of commitments platforms could choose to sign onto into an enforceable regulatory obligation. This is precisely the structural fix this book's earlier concern about self-reporting was gesturing toward: an outside body with legal authority to compel disclosure and grant independent researchers data access, rather than relying indefinitely on OpenAI's and Meta's own discretion about what to publish and when, even when that discretion has so far produced genuinely useful public disclosures.

There is also a less comfortable implication buried in both companies' decision to disclose these findings publicly, one this book's chapter on verification businesses raised in a different context: OpenAI and Meta are, in this instance, functioning simultaneously as the platforms whose tools were misused and as the institutions positioned to reveal and adjudicate that misuse, with no fully independent outside auditor standing between the two roles. Both companies deserve credit for choosing transparency over silence, a choice that was not legally compelled and that exposed their own products' vulnerabilities to public scrutiny. But the structural position is worth naming plainly: a company investigating and publicly characterizing misuse of its own tools is not equivalent to an outside body doing the same work, and the history examined earlier in this book — Arthur Andersen auditing Enron, Prager Metis auditing FTX — offers ample reason to want a genuinely independent check on any single entity's account of its own platform's failures, even when that entity's current account happens to be accurate and useful. Influence operations research remains, for now, a domain where the tech companies whose products are being weaponized are also the primary source of information about how that weaponization is occurring, an arrangement that has produced real value so far and that this book's broader argument suggests should not be allowed to remain the only check in place indefinitely.
