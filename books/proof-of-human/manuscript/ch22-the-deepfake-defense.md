# The Deepfake Defense

Every institution examined earlier in this book eventually built some formal or informal process for authenticating a claim before acting on it — an auditor's engagement letter, a journal's peer-review workflow, a bank's identity-verification pipeline. Courts are, in one sense, the oldest and most formalized version of exactly that same institutional function, built around rules of evidence refined over centuries specifically to decide what a factfinder may treat as reliably established. That a system this mature and this heavily theorized is now visibly struggling with the same underlying problem this book's opening chapters describe in corporate boardrooms and school hallways is a useful corrective to any temptation to treat this book's earlier institutional failures as products of particular carelessness or incompetence. Courts are, by design, among the most careful, procedurally deliberate institutions this book examines, and even they have not yet settled on a stable answer.

American courts have operated for more than a century under a legal standard for authenticating evidence that assumes something this book has spent its previous chapters arguing is no longer reliably true. Federal Rule of Evidence 901 requires only that a party offering a piece of evidence — a photograph, a recording, a video — produce enough supporting information for a reasonable person to conclude the item is what it is claimed to be: testimony from a witness who was present, comparison with a known authentic sample, or simply distinctive characteristics that make the item's origin plausible. That bar was set, and has remained largely unchanged, in an era when fabricating a convincing photograph or recording required real skill, real time, and real motive — precisely the differential-cost assumption this book has traced from wax seals through UL marks to financial audits, and precisely the assumption generative AI has spent its opening chapters undermining.

The legal profession has been unusually candid about the resulting anxiety, which shows up in two distinct forms that mirror the two-sided problem this book identified in its very first chapter. The first is the straightforward fear that a genuinely fabricated video or recording could be admitted as real evidence, swaying a jury or a judge with something that never happened. The second, and in some ways more consequential fear, is the liar's dividend problem in its purest legal form: a party facing damaging, entirely genuine video or audio evidence can simply assert that the recording might be an AI-generated deepfake, forcing the opposing side to bear the cost, delay, and technical burden of proving a negative — that a piece of evidence is not synthetic — in a legal system that has no settled, efficient procedure for resolving that specific question. Legal scholars examining the issue have noted that Rule 901's traditional bar, "enough evidence to support a finding" of authenticity, was calibrated for a world where fabrication was rare and difficult; applied unchanged to a world where a deepfake defense can be raised cheaply and often, the same low bar that once let genuine evidence in efficiently now risks becoming a tool for delay and obstruction, deployable by anyone regardless of whether the underlying evidence was actually fabricated.

The federal judiciary's own Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules took up this exact question directly, considering two competing proposals. One would have amended Rule 901 itself, creating a specialized authentication procedure specifically for evidence a party plausibly alleges might be an AI-generated deepfake, shifting some of the burden of proof onto the party raising the challenge rather than leaving it entirely on the party offering the evidence. The other, which drew more support within the committee, proposed an entirely new rule — numbered 707 — that would treat machine-generated evidence, including forensic AI-detection tools themselves, under something closer to the reliability standards courts already apply to expert witness testimony, requiring a foundation showing that a detection method is scientifically sound rather than accepting a detection tool's output on faith merely because it came from software rather than a person. After extended deliberation, the committee ultimately concluded that a formal rule change was not yet necessary, reasoning that existing procedures — judicial discretion, expert testimony, cross-examination — remained adequate given how comparatively few deepfake authentication disputes had actually reached American courtrooms by the time of its review, a conclusion that reads either as prudent restraint or as a bet that the problem will not accelerate as quickly as this book's opening chapters suggest it might.

That restraint has not stopped the deepfake defense from surfacing in real litigation well before any formal rule was written to handle it. Defense attorneys in criminal cases involving video or audio evidence have begun raising the possibility of AI fabrication as a matter of course, sometimes with a credible basis for concern and sometimes, according to judges and prosecutors who have written about the trend, as a tactical maneuver aimed at introducing reasonable doubt regardless of the evidence's actual provenance — precisely the liar's dividend Chesney and Citron warned about in the abstract, now showing up as an actual line of argument in actual courtrooms. Family court proceedings, where recorded conversations and voicemails are frequently introduced as evidence of a parent's fitness or a spouse's conduct, have seen a similar pattern emerge, with one legal commentary specifically flagging deepfake authentication disputes as an emerging complication in contested estate and custody litigation, where the stakes are high, the parties are frequently unsophisticated about the underlying technology, and the cost of commissioning genuine forensic analysis — the kind Hany Farid provided pro bono in the Pikesville case — is often beyond what an individual litigant in a family court matter can afford.

This asymmetry in who can actually afford real forensic verification echoes a concern raised earlier in this book's examination of the DEFIANCE Act and the broader legal response to nonconsensual synthetic imagery: a legal remedy that exists on paper is not equally accessible to everyone it is meant to protect. A large corporation defending against a deepfake-enabled fraud, or a well-funded criminal prosecution with access to FBI forensic resources, can typically commission the kind of expert analysis that resolved the Pikesville case within days once it was undertaken seriously. A private individual in a family court dispute, or a criminal defendant relying on a public defender's office without a dedicated digital forensics budget, faces a much harder and more expensive road to the same kind of certainty — which means the practical burden of the deepfake authentication problem, like so much else this book has examined, is likely to fall unevenly, granting the loudest benefit of the doubt to whichever party in a dispute can afford to buy it.

A civil case, Mendones v. Cushman & Wakefield, is frequently cited by legal commentators as one of the first documented instances of a party actually submitting a suspected deepfake as ostensibly genuine evidence in American litigation, rather than merely raising the possibility of fabrication as a defensive tactic. The dispute itself involved workplace allegations rather than anything as dramatic as the cases examined elsewhere in this book, but its significance lies in what it previewed procedurally: opposing counsel and, eventually, the court itself had to grapple in real time with how to evaluate a piece of submitted evidence whose authenticity was seriously contested on technical grounds neither side's legal training had prepared them to assess unassisted, a scenario legal commentary on the case treated as a bellwether for exactly the wave of authentication disputes the Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules would spend the following two years debating.

Two cases from 2024 and 2025 illustrate the courtroom problem from opposite directions, and both are worth walking through in some detail because they show how quickly this became a live courtroom issue rather than a hypothetical one law professors debate in journals. In a Maricopa County, Arizona courtroom in 2025, the family of Chris Pelkey, an Army veteran killed in a 2021 road-rage shooting, asked the presiding judge for permission to play an AI-generated video of Pelkey delivering his own victim impact statement at the sentencing of his killer, Gabriel Horcasitas, who had already been convicted of manslaughter. Pelkey's sister had spent roughly two years writing the script in her brother's voice, and the resulting video — an AI likeness of a dead man, speaking words his family believed captured what he would have wanted to say, including, notably, an appeal for leniency toward the man who killed him — was played for the court with the judge's approval. The judge nevertheless handed down the maximum sentence available, a detail that matters because it shows a court could absorb an unusually emotionally manipulative use of synthetic media, from a completely sympathetic and transparently disclosed source, without that media determining the outcome. Nothing about the Pelkey video was hidden or deceptive; the court knew exactly what it was watching and why, which distinguishes it sharply from every deceptive fabrication examined elsewhere in this book, while still raising an open question about how differently the same technique might land in a courtroom where its use was not so clearly disclosed, sympathetic, or emotionally aligned with the outcome a judge was already inclined to reach.

The second case shows the deception running the opposite direction. In a 2025 contract dispute before a New York state appellate court, a 74-year-old pro se litigant named Jerome Dewald submitted a video of an AI-generated avatar to help present his own legal argument, without disclosing to the court beforehand that the person speaking was synthetic. The panel of judges grew visibly confused mid-hearing, one of them stopping the presentation to ask directly who was actually speaking; when it became clear the "attorney" on screen was an AI construct Dewald had built himself, the presiding judge sharply reprimanded him for failing to disclose the fabrication in advance, treating the omission itself, rather than the underlying technology, as the offense. Dewald later said he had intended the avatar merely as a presentation aid, akin to a slide deck with narration, and had not anticipated that appearing without disclosure would read to the court as an attempt at deception. Whatever his intent, the incident became one of the first publicly documented instances of a litigant appearing before a U.S. appellate court by way of an undisclosed synthetic likeness, and it crystallized, in a single embarrassing courtroom exchange, the exact anxiety the Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules had been debating in the abstract: judges, it turns out, do not currently have a reliable, instantaneous way to tell whether the person addressing them in a submitted video is real, and the current remedy — an embarrassed judge stopping the proceeding to ask — does not scale to a legal system that processes millions of filings a year.

Both the Pelkey and Dewald cases share a feature worth naming directly: in neither instance did the presiding judge have any independent technical means of detecting the synthetic media in front of them without a party volunteering the information first. Judge Lang approved the Pelkey video because the family disclosed exactly what it was and why they wanted to present it; the appellate panel in Dewald's case caught the fabrication only because a judge happened to notice something behaviorally off about the presentation and asked a direct question mid-hearing, not because any court-provided tool flagged the video as synthetic before it played. This is the same detection gap this book's earlier chapters describe playing out in school districts, corporate finance departments, and newsrooms: the institutions encountering synthetic media for the first time are, almost without exception, relying on an individual's attentiveness and willingness to ask an awkward question, rather than on any systematic verification infrastructure built into their standard process, because that infrastructure either does not yet exist in a court-ready form or has not yet been adopted by the specific institution encountering the problem for the first time.

What courts are grappling with, in slower and more procedurally careful language than this book has used, is the same core problem this book's opening chapters identified through the Arup fraud, the Pikesville recording, and the New Hampshire robocall: a legal system built on the assumption that authentic-seeming evidence is probably authentic, because fabrication used to be expensive, now has to operate in an environment where that assumption no longer reliably holds, without yet having settled on a replacement standard that is both rigorous enough to catch genuine fabrications and efficient enough not to let every case become a referendum on whether AI could theoretically have produced the evidence at hand. The content-provenance and personhood-verification infrastructure examined earlier in this book offers one path toward a partial answer — a credentialed recording, timestamped and cryptographically signed at the point of capture, would give a court considerably more to work with than an unmarked file of uncertain origin — but as the chapter on watermarking's limits made clear, that infrastructure remains partial, defeatable, and unevenly adopted, which means the courts, like the rest of the institutions this book has examined, are likely to spend years operating in the uncomfortable gap between the old assumption's collapse and any new standard's full arrival.
