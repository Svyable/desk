# The Anchor Who Isn't

China's state news agency Xinhua introduced the world's first AI news presenter in 2018, a digital figure named Qiu Hao whose voice and appearance were modeled directly on a real Xinhua broadcaster of the same name, built in partnership with the search engine company Sogou. A few months later, Xinhua unveiled a female counterpart, Xin Xiaomeng, extending the same underlying technology to a second anchor persona. The pitch was explicitly about labor economics rather than journalistic innovation: an AI presenter could deliver the news around the clock, in multiple languages, without salary, sleep, or the ordinary limitations of a human broadcaster's schedule, at a marginal cost per additional hour of programming approaching zero once the initial model was built.

Kuwait News, the Gulf region's first English-language daily outlet, followed a similar path in April 2023 with its own AI-generated presenter, Fedha, though with a notably different design philosophy than Xinhua's approach of directly modeling a real employee. Fedha was built as a wholly synthetic persona rather than a digital replica of an existing human broadcaster, deliberately designed, according to the outlet's deputy editor-in-chief, with blonde hair and light brown eyes intended to reflect Kuwait's diverse population rather than any single real person's likeness. The distinction between Xinhua's approach and Kuwait News's matters for exactly the reasons this book's chapter on the SAG-AFTRA agreement examined at length: a digital replica modeled on a specific real broadcaster raises the consent and compensation questions that chapter explored in detail, while a wholly synthetic, unaffiliated persona like Fedha sidesteps that particular problem by never claiming to represent any actual human journalist's face, voice, or likeness in the first place.

Personal accountability of exactly this kind has historically functioned as one of journalism's quieter but sturdier verification mechanisms, worth naming explicitly because an AI anchor removes it so cleanly. A human broadcaster who knowingly reads a fabricated or badly sourced story risks a real, personal, career-ending consequence: public humiliation, termination, an inability to find comparable work elsewhere in the industry. That risk gives a human anchor, and the producers and editors standing behind them, a personal stake in the material being accurate that extends beyond the outlet's institutional reputation alone. An AI anchor has no career to protect, no professional reputation that outlasts any single broadcast, and no personal consequence to weigh against the convenience of reading whatever script it is handed, which means the accountability this book's earlier chapters describe institutions relying on has to be relocated entirely to the human editors and engineers standing behind the persona, with none of it attaching to the recognizable face actually delivering the news.

Kuwait News's decision to build Fedha as a wholly invented composite, rather than modeling her on any single real employee, is worth reading as a deliberate risk-management choice as much as a design preference. A persona with no real-world referent cannot be accused of misrepresenting a specific living journalist's views, cannot resign, cannot be caught in a personal scandal that damages the brand built around her face, and cannot demand a renegotiated contract the way a human anchor eventually might. She is, in the vocabulary this book's economics chapter uses throughout, a trust signal deliberately engineered to be cheap to produce and impossible to hold personally accountable in the way a human anchor's own reputation and career can be held accountable when a broadcast gets something wrong — a tradeoff that solves one problem this book has raised repeatedly (the cost and fragility of human labor) while quietly discarding a mechanism (personal, reputational accountability) that has done real disciplining work in journalism for generations, largely invisibly, until a persona arrives that was never exposed to it in the first place.

A Los Angeles startup called Channel 1 pushed the same underlying idea a step further in late 2023, proposing not merely an AI-generated anchor but an entire AI-generated news network built around one. Rather than reporting original stories, Channel 1's model draws on human-reported content from what it describes as trusted news sources, then has large language models summarize and rewrite that material into scripts, which human editors review before a photorealistic virtual presenter delivers them on camera — anchors the company says are modeled on and compensated real people who licensed their likenesses for the purpose, addressing directly the consent question this book's chapter on the SAG-AFTRA agreement examined in a different context. Channel 1's stated long-term ambition goes further still: a personalized news experience in which each viewer's feed, and potentially the anchor delivering it, could be tailored to their individual interests, and in which AI-generated video could illustrate events for which no camera happened to be present to capture the actual footage — a feature that, whatever its intended documentary use, sits uncomfortably close to the fabrication-for-persuasion problem this entire book has spent its chapters describing.

Both approaches nonetheless converge on the same underlying question this book has asked of every trust mechanism it has examined: what, specifically, was a familiar news anchor's face and voice actually verifying, before any of this technology existed, and does an AI presenter erode that verification even when the underlying reported facts are accurate? A human news anchor's presence has never really certified that a given story is true; anchors read scripts prepared by reporters and editors, and their personal credibility has always been a proxy for the editorial process behind them rather than a direct guarantee of factual accuracy. But that proxy has done real work regardless. Viewers built parasocial trust in specific broadcasters over years of consistent, recognizable appearances, an accumulated relationship not unlike the "embodied knowledge" this book's closing chapter argues remains difficult for synthetic media to counterfeit in personal relationships — except here, the relationship was always one-directional and, it turns out, considerably easier to replicate than a genuine friendship, since a viewer's trust in a familiar anchor's face depended on consistent appearance and vocal cadence rather than the kind of two-way, unpredictable interaction a companion chatbot still struggles to sustain convincingly.

The economics behind why a state broadcaster, a Gulf media outlet, and a Los Angeles startup all reached for the same underlying technology within a few years of each other are not mysterious once placed against this book's recurring cost argument. A human news anchor is one of the more expensive line items in a broadcast's production budget relative to their screen time: years of training, an agent, a salary commensurate with the trust their face has built with a returning audience, benefits, contract negotiations, and the simple human limitation of needing rest, vacation, and eventual retirement. An AI presenter, once built, can be deployed continuously across every language a text script can be translated into, updated instantly if a company wants to change its house style, and never needs a new contract negotiated or a replacement found when it eventually wants to retire. Xinhua's explicit framing around round-the-clock, multilingual availability names this economic logic directly rather than dressing it up as journalistic innovation, and it is worth taking that framing at face value: the primary value proposition of an AI anchor, for the outlets adopting the technology honestly, is cost and scale, not any claim that a synthetic presenter reports, edits, or verifies the news more rigorously than a human one would.

An AI-generated presenter can, at least in principle, offer perfect consistency: the same face, the same voice, the same measured delivery, night after night, without the illness, fatigue, scheduling conflicts, or eventual retirement that limit a human broadcaster's tenure. Whether that consistency constitutes a strength or a fresh vulnerability is precisely the split this book's argument about differential cost would predict. A consistent, always-available anchor persona is valuable to a news organization for exactly the reason a UL mark or a Good Housekeeping seal was valuable to a manufacturer: it is a stable, recognizable signal a returning viewer can build trust around over time. But an AI-generated persona is also, by construction, far cheaper to produce than a real broadcaster's decades of on-camera experience, credentialing, and editorial judgment — and a persona that can be built cheaply by a legitimate outlet like Kuwait News for entirely transparent reasons can, in principle, be built just as cheaply and far less transparently by exactly the kind of pink slime operation examined earlier in this book, wearing the visual trappings of a trustworthy news anchor without any of the editorial infrastructure that trustworthiness was originally meant to signal.

A related but distinct failure, less deliberate than a disinformation network's synthetic anchor but arguably more revealing about ordinary newsroom incentives, played out at the technology outlet CNET beginning in November 2022, before most of the public had heard of ChatGPT at all. CNET quietly began publishing financial explainer articles — pieces on topics like how compound interest works — written by an AI tool under the byline "CNET Money Staff," disclosing the automation only in fine print a reader had to hover over the byline to discover. The arrangement went unnoticed publicly for two months, until the technology outlet Futurism reported on the practice in January 2023 and found factual errors in the AI-generated pieces. CNET's own subsequent internal review, conducted after the external criticism rather than before publication, found meaningful inaccuracies in fifty-three of the seventy-seven AI-written articles — more than two-thirds of the entire batch — including one explainer that told readers a $10,000 deposit earning three percent annual interest would grow to $10,300 in interest alone, rather than $10,300 in total balance, an error basic enough that a first-year finance student should have caught it, published under a byline designed to look like an ordinary staff credit rather than a machine's unreviewed output. CNET paused the practice and issued public corrections, but the episode is a small, self-inflicted preview of the pink slime dynamic examined elsewhere in this book: a mainstream, reputable outlet, under competitive pressure to produce content cheaply, quietly adopted exactly the kind of low-cost, low-oversight production model this book's other chapters describe disinformation networks and content farms using deliberately, and got caught making the same kind of avoidable errors.

Channel 1's stated compensation model for the real broadcasters whose likenesses it licenses is worth reading against the SAG-AFTRA framework this book's earlier chapter examined at length, because it represents an early, live test of that framework's actual bargaining dynamics outside the unionized film and television and video game industries that negotiated it directly. A startup building an entire network around licensed digital anchors has an obvious commercial incentive to normalize a licensing arrangement that looks generous compared to nothing, while still costing it far less than the ongoing salary, benefits, and creative control a traditional on-air employment relationship with a human anchor would require. Whether the specific compensation Channel 1 or comparable ventures offer their licensed likenesses ends up resembling the quantified, real-time-generation-premium protections the video game performers' union fought a year-long strike to establish, or something closer to the thinner "clear and conspicuous consent" language critics flagged as too easily satisfied in the original film and television contract, will depend on exactly the bargaining leverage question this book's chapter on digital replicas raised: consent given by a broadcaster with real alternatives and real negotiating power looks very different, in practice, from consent given by someone with neither.

This is not a hypothetical extension of the problem. Independent researchers tracking synthetic media have documented AI-generated news-anchor personas used specifically by disinformation networks, including operations linked to the same state-aligned influence campaigns examined in this book's chapter on manufacturing consensus, presenting fabricated or slanted news reports through a synthetic anchor persona designed to borrow the visual authority of a professional news broadcast without any of the editorial accountability a real news organization, however imperfect, still nominally provides. The anchor's face was never the thing actually verifying a broadcast's accuracy. It was always standing in for an entire apparatus of reporters, editors, fact-checkers, and institutional accountability that took decades to build and that a synthetic persona, whether deployed honestly by Kuwait News or dishonestly by an anonymous disinformation network, can now borrow the appearance of without necessarily bringing any of the substance along with it.
