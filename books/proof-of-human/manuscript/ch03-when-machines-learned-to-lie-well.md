# When Machines Learned to Lie Well

In 2014, a graduate student named Ian Goodfellow described an idea to colleagues at a party in Montreal and, according to the story he later told, went home and coded the first version that same night. The idea was to pit two neural networks against each other: a generator that tried to produce fake images convincing enough to pass as real, and a discriminator that tried to catch the fakes. Each network improved by trying to defeat the other, in a loop that pushed both toward a strange equilibrium where the generator's output became difficult even for its dedicated adversary to distinguish from the genuine article. Goodfellow called the architecture a generative adversarial network, and the paper he and his coauthors published that year turned out to be one of the more consequential documents in the recent history of applied mathematics, not because anyone at the time could produce a passable fake photograph of a real person, but because the underlying trick — train a forger and a detector against each other, and let the forger win — scaled far better than anyone in that room likely expected.

It took a few more years for the trick to reach faces. The word "deepfake" entered public circulation in late 2017, coined, more or less, by an anonymous Reddit user posting synthetic pornographic videos that swapped celebrities' faces onto other people's bodies, using techniques descended from Goodfellow's original architecture. The early examples were often crude enough to be spotted by attentive viewers: mismatched lighting, unnatural blinking, a faint shimmer at the jawline where the swapped face met the original footage. Researchers and journalists treated the flaws as reassuring, in the way early smoke is reassuring compared to what a fire eventually becomes. The technology did not stay crude. Within a few years, models trained on ordinary consumer hardware could produce face swaps convincing enough to fool casual viewing, and the compute and expertise required kept falling while the output kept improving, the same trajectory that has characterized nearly every consequential machine learning technique since.

Voice followed a similar arc, on a faster timeline, and it is voice — not video — that has done the most concrete financial damage so far, largely because a convincing fake voice requires far less raw data than a convincing fake face, and a phone call offers far fewer visual cues for a listener to double-check against. By the early 2020s, companies including ElevenLabs had built commercial tools that could clone a person's voice from a short sample. Public reporting has described the system producing a recognizable clone from roughly thirty seconds of clean audio, with quality that degrades below that threshold but does not disappear; some accounts of the underlying models describe even three seconds of speech yielding a clone accurate enough to fool a casual listener a large share of the time. Within weeks of ElevenLabs opening its voice-cloning tool more broadly in early 2023, users on forums including 4chan began using it to clone celebrities' voices for harassment and pranks, and the company subsequently added a verification step requiring speakers to record specific consent language before a voice could be cloned — a safeguard that blocks casual misuse without doing much to stop a determined bad actor working around it.

The Federal Trade Commission issued a consumer warning in 2023 describing exactly the scenario that safeguard was meant to prevent: criminals cloning a family member's voice from audio pulled off social media or a old voicemail, then calling an elderly relative in apparent distress, asking for money to be wired immediately for an emergency that does not exist. This is an old scam wearing new clothes — the so-called grandparent scam has worked for years using nothing more sophisticated than a stranger claiming, over a bad phone connection, to be someone's grandchild in trouble — but the new clothes fit unnervingly well. A voice that sounds correct removes the single biggest reason a target might pause before wiring money. Reported losses from fraud targeting older Americans have climbed sharply across this period; the Federal Trade Commission has reported billions of dollars in fraud losses among older adults in recent years, and law enforcement agencies including the FBI have specifically flagged AI voice cloning as an accelerant behind a documented rise in these calls since 2023, even though clean national statistics that isolate the AI-specific share of a much larger fraud category remain difficult to produce with precision.

What is worth noticing across the deepfake video timeline and the voice-cloning timeline is not any single dramatic leap but the shape of the curve. Each technique started as a research curiosity capable of fooling almost no one who looked closely, then became a research curiosity capable of fooling most casual observers, then became a consumer product cheap enough and easy enough to use that the limiting factor was no longer technical skill but merely the decision to try. That last transition is the one that matters most for a book about trust, because it is the transition that moves a technology from something institutions can treat as a rare, containable threat to something they have to assume is happening constantly, to anyone, at any time.

Text generation tracked a version of the same curve, arriving slightly earlier in public consciousness and reaching fluency slightly faster, because language models could be trained on vastly more available data than either faces or voices. By the time large language models were producing fluent, plausible prose on demand, the internet already carried a meaningful and rapidly growing volume of machine-generated text, embedded in product reviews, social media posts, and — more quietly — academic submissions, comment sections, and the kind of low-stakes local news content that had never attracted much scrutiny in the first place. None of this required a villain twirling a mustache. Much of it was generated for reasons as mundane as filling a content quota cheaply, gaming a search algorithm, or padding an astroturfed comment section to make a fringe opinion look like consensus. The fabrication did not need to be flawless to work. It needed only to be cheap enough, and plausible enough, to overwhelm the attention anyone had available to check it.

That is the real inflection point this chapter has been circling. Forgery is ancient. What generative AI changed was not the existence of fabrication but its unit economics. A skilled human forger, cloning a signature or faking a photograph, was constrained by time, talent, and the practical limits of doing the work by hand — constraints that kept the supply of convincing fakes low relative to the supply of genuine material, which is exactly the asymmetry the institutions in the previous chapter were built to exploit. Generative models removed the constraint. A single operator with commercially available tools can now produce a synthetic video call, a cloned voice, or a fluent wall of text at a volume and price that no forger's guild, however skilled, could have matched a decade earlier. The Arup fraud from the opening chapter did not require a criminal mastermind who happened to also be a gifted mimic. It required someone willing to feed a company's own publicly available meeting footage into tools that were, by 2024, available to more or less anyone.

The institutions described in the previous chapter — seals, audits, safety marks, signatures — were built on the assumption that fabrication would remain expensive relative to verification. That assumption held, with periodic strain, for most of the twentieth century. It no longer holds evenly across the kinds of evidence people rely on every day: a familiar face on a screen, a familiar voice on the phone, a document that looks the way documents are supposed to look. The remaining chapters take that reversal as a given and ask a harder question. Even before generative AI arrived to make fabrication cheap, how many of the institutions built to guarantee trust had already quietly stopped doing the job they were built for?
