# The Verification Economy

Somewhere in the last decade, checking whether someone is who they claim to be turned from a compliance cost that businesses tolerated into a product category investors actively fund. The identity verification market — the software and services that banks, exchanges, gig platforms, landlords, and dating apps use to confirm a new user's government ID matches their face and their claimed name matches a real, living person — has been estimated at somewhere near fourteen to fifteen billion dollars in 2025, with multiple market research firms projecting growth toward forty billion dollars or more by the early 2030s, driven by a compound annual growth rate research firms have placed in the low-to-mid teens. Those figures should be read as industry estimates rather than settled fact, since market-sizing reports vary considerably in methodology and often come from firms with an interest in describing the market they cover as large and growing. But the direction is not seriously in dispute, and it is corroborated by concrete transactions: Entrust, a longtime player in digital security, completed its acquisition of the identity verification company Onfido in April 2024, in a deal reported at roughly six hundred fifty million dollars, folding one of the sector's most established biometric verification providers into a larger security conglomerate. Jumio, another major player, has built out coverage supporting thousands of identification document types across more than two hundred countries and territories, a scale of infrastructure that would have made little commercial sense before the volume of remote, document-based identity checks reached its current level. Persona, a newer entrant, has continued expanding into adjacent use cases, including verification tools aimed at confirming that job candidates are who their resumes claim, a corner of hiring fraud that has become newly urgent as AI-generated resumes, AI-assisted interview coaching, and even fully synthetic candidates attempting remote interviews have started showing up in corporate hiring pipelines.

The businesses behind these numbers are, in the vocabulary of earlier chapters, selling differential cost as a service. A company that wants to open an account, hire an employee, or process a payment without exposing itself to fraud can either build its own verification apparatus in-house — cameras, document scanners, liveness detection, fraud databases, compliance staff trained on constantly shifting regulations — or it can pay a specialist to do it, the same logic that led insurers in 1893 to fund an independent electrical testing laboratory rather than each separately evaluate the wiring at every building they insured. The specialists survive by getting better, faster, and cheaper at exactly the kind of verification that individual companies would find prohibitively expensive to replicate in-house, and by staying ahead, at least most of the time, of the fraud techniques that would otherwise erode the value of their check.

This is a genuinely disruptive dynamic in the sense Clayton Christensen described in his account of industries upended not by incumbents doing their existing job worse, but by new entrants solving an adjacent, previously underserved problem well enough that the problem becomes central rather than peripheral. Notarization, background checks, and reference calls were, for most of their history, minor friction in much larger transactions — a stamp needed to close a mortgage, a phone call a hiring manager made somewhat perfunctorily before extending an offer. As the volume of remote, high-stakes interactions has grown, and as the cost of faking a credential, a reference, or a face-to-face impression has fallen, verification itself has become the transaction's central bottleneck rather than an afterthought bolted onto it, and companies built specifically to solve that bottleneck have grown accordingly.

The Getty Images lawsuit against Stability AI, discussed in the previous chapter for its ironic watermark angle, is worth revisiting here as a business case study rather than a legal one, because it captures an industry watching its underlying product get commoditized in real time and responding by leaning harder into the one asset a generative model cannot manufacture from scratch: verified, licensed provenance. A stock photography library's core commercial offering was never really the images themselves — a sufficiently motivated customer could always find or create a similar image elsewhere — it was the licensed, indemnified certainty that a particular image could be used commercially without risking a copyright claim from an unknown original photographer. Generative image models threaten that business less by producing better pictures than by making pictures themselves nearly free, which pushes competition onto exactly the dimension incumbents in the trust business are best positioned to win: not "can we make an image" but "can we guarantee, with contractual and legal backing, that this specific image is safe to use." Getty's own commercial strategy since the lawsuit has leaned into training and licensing arrangements built around exactly that guarantee, rather than trying to out-compete generative models on raw image production.

A similar reorientation is visible, in earlier and messier form, across journalism, where outlets including the Associated Press, Reuters, and the New York Times have adopted C2PA-based content credentials for at least some of their photography and video, embedding a verifiable chain of custody into images at the point of capture and through the editing process. The commercial logic is not subtle: as the cost of producing a plausible-looking fake news photograph has fallen toward zero, a news organization's ability to say, credibly and verifiably, "this image came from our photographer, at this place, at this time, unaltered except in these documented ways," becomes a differentiator worth the engineering investment, in an industry that has spent two decades watching most of its previous differentiators erode under pressure from free, algorithmically distributed content.

None of this guarantees that verification businesses will remain trustworthy simply because trust happens to be their product, a mistake worth flagging explicitly given how the earlier chapter on Wirecard and Arthur Andersen played out. A credential-issuing company, a biometric verification vendor, or a content-provenance coalition is itself an institution subject to exactly the incentive problems that hollowed out ratings agencies before 2008 and auditors before Enron: if the fees flow from the entity being verified rather than from the party relying on the verification, or if a dominant vendor becomes too large and too interwoven with its customers to credibly reject a lucrative client's fraudulent claims, the same slow drift from adversarial checking to ceremonial approval can recur inside the verification industry itself, just as it did inside the industries verification was built to police. A UL mark stayed credible for a century in large part because Underwriters Laboratories built its business model, from the outset, around insurers and the public rather than around manufacturers seeking an easy stamp of approval; identity verification companies, content provenance coalitions, and personhood-credential providers will face a version of the same structural test, likely sooner than most of their current marketing materials acknowledge.

The broader pattern across every business discussed in this chapter is that verification has stopped being overhead and started being infrastructure, in something close to the literal sense the word implies: a layer other businesses build on top of, that most of their customers will never think about directly, provided it keeps working. That is precisely the fate of every successful trust mechanism this book has examined, from wax seals to safety marks to financial audits — quiet, background, and largely invisible right up until the moment it fails. The verification economy's current growth reflects how much of that infrastructure had to be rebuilt, essentially from scratch, once the assumptions the older infrastructure depended on stopped holding. What it cannot yet tell us is whether the new infrastructure will age any better than the old.
