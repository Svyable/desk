# The Seal and the Signature

In 1893, a twenty-four-year-old electrical engineer named William Henry Merrill was hired by fire insurance underwriters to inspect the wiring at the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, the fair that introduced much of the country to alternating current, the Ferris wheel, and an entire midway of electrified spectacle. Electricity was still new enough that nobody was quite sure how to keep an exposition full of wooden pavilions from burning down around it, and insurers, who would be the ones paying for the fire, had a direct financial reason to want an answer. Merrill's inspection went well enough that the underwriters who had funded it saw the shape of a permanent business. With two employees and $350 worth of equipment, he set up a small testing laboratory above a Chicago fire patrol station and ran his first formal test on March 24, 1894. That laboratory became Underwriters Laboratories, and its founding logic has barely changed in the century and a third since: someone who is not the manufacturer and not the customer examines a product against a known standard, and puts a mark on it that says, in effect, this has been checked.

The UL mark is easy to overlook because it has succeeded so completely at becoming boring. Nobody stands in a hardware store agonizing over whether a power strip's safety claims are trustworthy; they glance for the mark, or they do not glance at all, having long since outsourced the glancing to habit. That is what a mature trust mechanism looks like from the inside. It disappears into the product. The interesting work happened decades earlier, when insurers, manufacturers, and the public had to agree that a small circular stamp from an independent lab was worth more than a manufacturer's own assurance that its wiring wouldn't start a fire.

The same logic, arrived at independently and for a different set of failures, produced the modern audit. Merchants and rulers had used forms of double-entry bookkeeping for centuries before anyone thought to formalize an outside check on it, and for a long time a company's own ledger was taken more or less at its word, particularly if the company's owners were also its managers. That stopped being tenable as corporations grew large enough that the people risking capital — shareholders scattered across a country or an ocean — had no way to personally verify what the people running the business told them. Railway booms in nineteenth-century Britain, financed by exactly this kind of distant, dispersed capital, produced enough inflated accounts and outright swindles that an independent audit function grew up around the industry almost by necessity: someone had to certify the numbers to people who could not otherwise check them. By the twentieth century, publicly traded companies in most developed economies were required to have their financial statements examined by an outside firm before anyone would responsibly lend to them, insure them, or buy their stock.

An audit, like a UL mark, is a costly signal wearing the disguise of a routine formality. The company being audited pays for its own scrutiny, submits its records to people it does not control, and accepts a report that could say something the company does not want said. The value of the whole arrangement depends entirely on the auditor having no reason to lie on the company's behalf. That dependency is a structural weakness as much as a structural strength, and it is one this book will return to, because the moment an auditor's incentives quietly shift — when the firm doing the checking is also collecting large consulting fees from the firm it checks — the seal keeps looking the same on the outside while meaning less and less on the inside.

Seals, in fact, are the oldest version of this idea, predating both accountants and electricians by thousands of years. A wax seal pressed with a signet ring did not prevent a letter from being opened and reread; it made tampering detectable, because breaking a seal and remaking an identical one required either the original ring or enough skill in forgery to be unusual and risky. Guild marks on medieval goods worked on a related principle: a stamp on a piece of silver or a bolt of cloth signaled that the item met the standards of an organization with its own reputation to protect, and a guild had every incentive to punish a member who cut corners and dragged the mark's credibility down with him. Merchant marks, hallmarks on precious metals, and later brand names all did versions of the same job — compressing a costly, hard-to-observe process (careful craftsmanship, honest metal content, safe manufacturing) into a small, cheap-to-check symbol that a stranger could rely on without personally inspecting the underlying work.

What all of these mechanisms share is that they are not actually claims about truth. They are claims about cost. A seal is credible because breaking and remaking it plausibly costs more than whatever a forger would gain. An audit is credible, when it works, because an accounting firm's own reputation and legal exposure make lying for a client more expensive than losing that client. A safety mark is credible because an independent lab's entire business depends on manufacturers believing its tests are real. Strip away the cost of faking the signal, and the signal stops signaling anything.

Some of history's most damaging financial frauds have been, in this light, less about the absence of institutions than about a moment when the cost of faking a signal collapsed faster than anyone updated the systems built around it. The Good Housekeeping Seal, the FDA's approval process, Consumer Reports' testing labs, credit ratings from agencies like Moody's and Standard & Poor's — each was built to solve the same underlying problem that faced Merrill's insurers in 1893: a buyer cannot personally verify a seller's claims, so somebody has to build an institution whose entire reason for existing is verification, and that institution has to remain more expensive to corrupt than to satisfy honestly. When that balance breaks — when a ratings agency starts getting paid by the very banks whose mortgage bonds it is supposed to be grading, as happened in the years before 2008 — the seal survives even after the substance behind it has hollowed out, and the public usually finds out only after the collapse.

There is a reason this chapter has spent so much time in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries before this book ever mentions a neural network. Every one of these mechanisms — the wax seal, the guild mark, the safety label, the signed audit — was built to solve a version of the same problem generative AI has now made dramatically more acute: verification is expensive, fabrication is comparatively cheap, and civilization runs on institutions that exist specifically to widen that gap rather than let it close. For most of these institutions' histories, the gap held because faking a signature, a stamp, a photograph, or a familiar voice took real skill, real time, and a level of individual criminal effort that did not scale well. A forger could fake one signature convincingly. Faking a million of them, cheaply, in real time, tailored to whoever happened to be listening, was never something a lone forger — or even a well-funded counterfeiting operation — could do.

That is precisely what changed. The chapters ahead trace how the cost of fabrication fell, and what happens to a civilization's trust infrastructure when the thing it was built to make expensive becomes, for the first time, nearly free.
