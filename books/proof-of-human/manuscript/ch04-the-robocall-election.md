# The Robocall Election

Two days before New Hampshire's presidential primary in January 2024, thousands of Democratic voters' phones rang with a recorded message. The voice, warm and familiar, belonged to President Joe Biden. It urged listeners to save their vote for November rather than "waste" it in the primary — a message calibrated to sound like insider political advice rather than obvious interference, discouraging turnout without ever quite telling anyone to break the law. The caller ID had been spoofed to display the number of a local political operative uninvolved in the scheme, adding a second layer of misdirection to a call that was, in its content, already a fabrication.

Biden had said none of it. The voice was synthetic, produced using ElevenLabs' voice-cloning software at the direction of Steve Kramer, a political consultant working, without his client's knowledge, against the presidential campaign of Congressman Dean Phillips, whom Kramer had been separately retained to help. By Kramer's own later account, he hired a magician in New Orleans with a sideline in vocal impressions to help generate the script and voice, ran it through the cloning tool, and arranged for a mass robocalling operation — coordinated with a Louisiana-based robocalling company and its affiliated firm, both controlled by a single operator — to blast the recording out across the state on the eve of the vote.

The scheme did not stay hidden for long. Voters and local officials flagged the calls almost immediately, and the New Hampshire Attorney General's office opened an investigation within days, working alongside the Federal Communications Commission. Kramer eventually admitted his role publicly rather than waiting to be conclusively identified, a decision he framed, when confronted, as an attempt to draw attention to the vulnerability rather than an act of straightforward deception — an argument that convinced almost no one in a position to judge him. The FCC proposed a $6 million fine against him in 2024 under the Truth in Caller ID Act for the number-spoofing alone, and New Hampshire prosecutors separately charged him with felony voter suppression and misdemeanor candidate impersonation across four counties. A jury in the state ultimately found him not guilty on the criminal charges in 2025, even as the civil telecommunications fine and a separate settlement with the robocalling company and a voting-rights group proceeded on their own tracks — a reminder that a defendant's culpability under one legal framework, built around specific statutory elements like caller ID fraud, does not automatically translate into culpability under a different one built around different elements, like the state's voter suppression statute.

Whatever the final legal accounting, the operational lesson of the New Hampshire robocall does not depend on the criminal verdict. A single consultant, working largely alone, used a consumer-facing voice-cloning product and an off-the-shelf robocalling contractor to impersonate a sitting American president in a live presidential primary, at a cost that was almost certainly a rounding error next to the scale of the disruption it caused. No hostile foreign intelligence service was required. No unusual technical sophistication was required. What was required was a plausible pretext, a willingness to break clearly established telecommunications law, and a synthetic voice good enough to be mistaken, over a phone line, for the real thing — which, by January 2024, off-the-shelf tools could already reliably provide.

Slovakia's parliamentary election, held a few months earlier in September 2023, offers a useful point of comparison precisely because the mechanism was so similar and the political stakes arguably even higher. Two days before Slovaks went to the polls, an audio recording began circulating on social media purporting to capture Michal Šimečka, the liberal candidate whose Progressive Slovakia party had been leading in pre-election polling, discussing with a journalist named Monika Tódová how to rig the vote using ballots cast by the country's Roma minority. Researchers and forensic reviewers who examined the clip concluded it had been synthetically generated rather than recorded. Its timing was almost surgical: Slovak law imposes a mandatory pre-election media silence period, a rule dating to an era when the chief threat to fair coverage was thought to be last-minute broadcast propaganda rather than an anonymously posted audio file, and that silence period meant broadcasters were legally constrained from publicly and immediately debunking the recording in the window when it would have mattered most.

Tens of thousands of people heard the clip before the election closed. Robert Fico, running against a candidate who had led him in the polls, won the election and went on to form a government considerably friendlier to Moscow's interests than his opponent would have been — an outcome that arrived alongside a Russian intelligence press release, issued in the same narrow window, alleging unrelated American interference in the Slovak vote, a coincidence researchers have treated as suggestive of coordinated disinformation strategy rather than proof of it. Academic reviewers who have studied the Slovak case closely, including a widely cited treatment in the Harvard Kennedy School's Misinformation Review, have been careful to note that no clean causal chain links the deepfake to Fico's victory; Slovak politics in 2023 carried plenty of other currents capable of producing the same result on their own, and disentangling one audio clip's marginal effect from an election's full churn of turnout, messaging, and voter sentiment is not something after-the-fact analysis can do with confidence. What the Slovak case demonstrates cleanly, regardless of its ultimate electoral impact, is the same operational point as the New Hampshire robocall: a piece of synthetic audio, released at the right moment against the right legal and informational backdrop, can outrun every institutional mechanism built to correct it.

India's 2024 general election, the largest democratic exercise in human history by sheer number of eligible voters, produced a version of election-season synthetic media that inverted the New Hampshire and Slovak playbooks entirely: rather than fabricating a living politician saying something damaging, several campaigns used AI to resurrect politicians who were already dead. M. Karunanidhi, a towering figure in Tamil Nadu politics who died in 2018, appeared via AI-generated video and voice synthesis at multiple party events in the months before the vote, "congratulating" his political successors and endorsing the current leadership in an eight-minute address delivered in his unmistakable style — dark sunglasses, white shirt, yellow shawl — produced by an AI media firm called Muonium and welcomed enthusiastically by the party he had led for decades. Unlike the Slovak and New Hampshire cases, the Karunanidhi recreations were not secret, deceptive fabrications meant to be mistaken for a genuine leaked recording; they were openly commissioned, publicly staged campaign tools, presented to audiences who understood perfectly well that the man had been dead for years and were nonetheless meant to feel his continued endorsement of his party's current direction. That distinction matters, and it complicates the liar's dividend framework this chapter has otherwise relied on: nobody was deceived about whether Karunanidhi was really alive, yet the emotional and political work the fabricated video performed — borrowing a beloved, deceased leader's remaining authority to bless a living successor — depended entirely on synthetic media's capacity to simulate a specific, recognizable human being convincingly enough to feel emotionally authentic, even without any claim to factual authenticity at all. India's 2024 election saw AI deployed this way dozens of times across multiple parties and languages, suggesting that the more immediately consequential political use of this technology, at least in some democracies, may turn out to be this kind of open, sanctioned emotional manipulation rather than the covert deception this chapter's other two cases represent.

Put the two initial cases side by side and a pattern comes into focus that neither one shows completely on its own. New Hampshire demonstrates the mechanism working against the machinery of an election directly — turnout, ballot behavior, the operational conduct of a primary — using a synthetic voice of a specific, singular, maximally recognizable figure. Slovakia demonstrates the mechanism working against the machinery of a campaign's reputation, using a synthetic conversation rather than a synthetic public address, timed against a specific legal vulnerability (the silence period) that existed for reasons entirely unrelated to AI and had simply never been updated for a world where a fabricated recording could appear from nowhere in the final forty-eight hours. Each attack exploited a different seam in a different country's electoral apparatus, and each seam had been sitting there, unnoticed and unpatched, because nobody who designed the underlying rule — the primary voting calendar, the media silence period — had been building for an adversary who could manufacture a plausible falsehood cheaply, on demand, in a specific victim's own voice.

Kramer's own explanation for his conduct, offered publicly after his role became known, deserves scrutiny precisely because it previews an excuse this book's later chapters find recurring in less overtly political contexts. Kramer characterized the robocall as an attempt to demonstrate a genuine vulnerability in American election security, a public service performed through unauthorized and illegal means rather than through any legitimate disclosure channel. That framing — I broke the rule to prove the rule needed breaking — is structurally identical to the justification offered by penetration testers and so-called ethical hackers in the cybersecurity world, with one crucial difference this book's chapters on institutional accountability would flag immediately: a legitimate security researcher discloses a vulnerability to the affected party and gives them a chance to fix it before any public demonstration, rather than deploying the exploit against tens of thousands of unwitting voters two days before an actual election with no advance warning to anyone responsible for the system being tested. Kramer's framing asked the public to accept the demonstration's stated public-interest purpose while ignoring that its actual execution looked, in every practical respect, identical to the malicious version of the same attack it claimed to be warning against.

The not-guilty verdict Kramer ultimately received on the state criminal charges is worth examining closely, because it illustrates a gap between moral and legal culpability that recurs throughout this book whenever new fabrication technology outpaces the specific statutes written to police an older category of harm. New Hampshire's voter-suppression and candidate-impersonation statutes were drafted with a paradigm case in mind — a human being directly, knowingly misrepresenting a candidate's actual words or actively suppressing a specific voter's actual ballot access — and prosecutors evidently struggled to fit Kramer's conduct cleanly into elements written before anyone anticipated an AI-generated robocall spoofing a president's voice at scale. The jury's decision does not mean Kramer's actions caused no harm, and it did not spare him the federal fine or the civil settlement that followed from other legal theories better suited to what he had actually done. It means the specific criminal statute prosecutors reached for, built for an earlier generation of election dirty tricks, did not map cleanly onto a fabrication this new, a legislative gap several states have since moved to close with statutes written specifically to name AI-generated political communications as their own distinct offense, rather than trying to prosecute them under laws written for a world where impersonating a candidate required an actual human being to do the impersonating.

The financial mechanics behind the New Hampshire scheme are worth dwelling on for a moment, because they show how little capital an attack on a democratic election actually required compared to the scale of disruption it caused. Kramer's own later accounts of the operation describe a modest budget: a consumer voice-cloning subscription costing a few dollars, a robocalling contractor's standard commercial rate for blasting out tens of thousands of calls, and a magician's fee for helping produce the initial script and vocal performance used to seed the clone. The entire scheme, by these accounts, cost less than most local political campaigns spend on yard signs, yet it generated national news coverage, a formal FCC enforcement action, felony criminal charges across four counties, and a lasting change to how the Federal Communications Commission interprets its own robocall regulations. No comparable disruption of a presidential primary was achievable for that price before consumer-grade voice cloning existed; the entire apparatus of paid political advertising, direct mail, and field operations that ordinarily costs a campaign millions of dollars to influence a primary at the margins was, in this one instance, functionally short-circuited by a fabrication that cost a small fraction of what an ordinary attack ad would have cost to produce and air.

Election officials and platforms have not been passive in response. Following the New Hampshire incident, the FCC clarified that AI-generated voices in robocalls fall under the same Telephone Consumer Protection Act restrictions that already govern robocalls generally, closing off one narrow avenue for prosecutors and regulators going forward. Several states moved to pass or strengthen their own laws specifically addressing synthetic media in political advertising and campaign communications in the two years following both incidents, generally requiring some form of disclosure when a political message uses AI-generated audio, image, or video of a real person. Whether disclosure requirements meaningfully deter someone willing to spoof a caller ID and impersonate a sitting president in the first place is a separate question from whether they are worth having; a determined bad actor operating with Kramer's apparent indifference to established telecommunications law is unlikely to be stopped by a disclosure requirement he could simply ignore, though the requirement may still matter for the much larger number of campaigns and consultants operating closer to the legal line who would prefer not to risk the exposure.

What both cases share, and what the rest of this book keeps circling back to, is a mismatch of timescales. A fabricated recording can be produced in minutes and distributed in seconds, at a marginal cost near zero. Confirming that it is fabricated — through forensic audio analysis, through law enforcement investigation, through a coordinated public response from the impersonated candidate's own campaign — takes hours or days even when every institution involved moves as quickly as it possibly can. An election held on a fixed date does not wait for that gap to close. Neither, in the Pikesville case from the previous chapter, does a school board meeting, a community's judgment of its principal, or a media cycle that has already moved on to the next controversy by the time a forensic report is finished. The asymmetry between how fast a lie can be manufactured and how fast the truth can be established is, at bottom, the same asymmetry this entire book is about — only here it is measured not in dollars stolen, but in votes cast under a false premise that arrived too late, or too quietly, to correct.
