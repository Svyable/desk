# The Seal and the Signature

In 1893, a twenty-four-year-old electrical engineer named William Henry Merrill was hired by fire insurance underwriters to inspect the wiring at the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, the fair that introduced much of the country to alternating current, the Ferris wheel, and an entire midway of electrified spectacle. Electricity was still new enough that nobody was quite sure how to keep an exposition full of wooden pavilions from burning down around it, and insurers, who would be the ones paying for the fire, had a direct financial reason to want an answer. Merrill's inspection went well enough that the underwriters who had funded it saw the shape of a permanent business. With two employees and $350 worth of equipment, he set up a small testing laboratory above a Chicago fire patrol station and ran his first formal test on March 24, 1894. That laboratory became Underwriters Laboratories, and its founding logic has barely changed in the century and a third since: someone who is not the manufacturer and not the customer examines a product against a known standard, and puts a mark on it that says, in effect, this has been checked.

The UL mark is easy to overlook because it has succeeded so completely at becoming boring. Nobody stands in a hardware store agonizing over whether a power strip's safety claims are trustworthy; they glance for the mark, or they do not glance at all, having long since outsourced the glancing to habit. That is what a mature trust mechanism looks like from the inside. It disappears into the product. The interesting work happened decades earlier, when insurers, manufacturers, and the public had to agree that a small circular stamp from an independent lab was worth more than a manufacturer's own assurance that its wiring wouldn't start a fire.

A remarkably similar dynamic played out in the same decade around household goods rather than electrical wiring. In 1900, Good Housekeeping magazine established what it called the Good Housekeeping Experiment Station, later renamed the Good Housekeeping Institute, to independently test the food and household products advertised in its own pages — an unusual decision for a publication whose advertising revenue depended on exactly the manufacturers it was proposing to scrutinize. In December 1909, the Institute began publishing a "Tested and Approved" list of products meeting its standards, and by the end of the following year almost two hundred products, mostly household appliances, carried what became known as the Good Housekeeping Seal of Approval. The magazine went further than most of its peers by hiring Harvey Washington Wiley — the chemist who had led the fight for the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906 and served as the first commissioner of the newly created Food and Drug Administration — to head the institute after his government tenure ended in 1912, lending a sitting former federal regulator's personal credibility to a private magazine's product-testing seal. The arrangement worked, commercially, for exactly the reason UL's mark worked: a stranger buying a product off a shelf had no way to personally verify a manufacturer's safety or quality claims, and a seal from an entity with its own reputation on the line was worth more than the manufacturer's word alone, provided the entity issuing the seal had a believable reason not to sell it cheaply.

Financial markets developed their own version of the same idea slightly later, for a different kind of verification problem: not whether a product was safe, but whether a borrower would pay its debts. John Moody, a financial publisher who had already spent a decade producing statistical manuals on stocks and industrial securities, published the first edition of his Analyses of Railroad Investments in 1909, introducing something genuinely new to American capital markets — letter grades assigned to the bonds of individual railroad companies, meant to let a bondholder in one city judge the creditworthiness of a railroad operating a thousand miles away without personally auditing its books. The railroads themselves had, a few decades earlier, been at the center of exactly the kind of dispersed-capital, hard-to-verify financing that had already forced the development of the modern independent audit; Moody's ratings extended the same logic one step further, compressing an entire balance sheet's worth of risk into a single letter that a bank, an insurance company, or an individual investor could act on without doing the underlying analysis themselves. The company Moody built around that innovation, along with rivals that followed a similar model, became a permanent fixture of how capital gets priced — and, as a later chapter in this book will show, a cautionary example of exactly how a costly, credible signal can quietly stop being costly or credible while continuing to look the same from the outside.

The same logic, arrived at independently and for a different set of failures, produced the modern audit itself, the mechanism both Moody's ratings and UL's safety marks ultimately depend on somewhere further down the chain. Merchants and rulers had used forms of double-entry bookkeeping for centuries before anyone thought to formalize an outside check on it, and for a long time a company's own ledger was taken more or less at its word, particularly if the company's owners were also its managers. That stopped being tenable as corporations grew large enough that the people risking capital — shareholders scattered across a country or an ocean — had no way to personally verify what the people running the business told them. Railway booms in nineteenth-century Britain, financed by exactly this kind of distant, dispersed capital, produced enough inflated accounts and outright swindles that an independent audit function grew up around the industry almost by necessity: someone had to certify the numbers to people who could not otherwise check them. By the twentieth century, publicly traded companies in most developed economies were required to have their financial statements examined by an outside firm before anyone would responsibly lend to them, insure them, or buy their stock.

An audit, like a UL mark or a bond rating, is a costly signal wearing the disguise of a routine formality. The company being audited pays for its own scrutiny, submits its records to people it does not control, and accepts a report that could say something the company does not want said. The value of the whole arrangement depends entirely on the auditor having no reason to lie on the company's behalf. That dependency is a structural weakness as much as a structural strength, and it is one this book will return to directly in a later chapter, because the moment an auditor's incentives quietly shift — when the firm doing the checking is also collecting large consulting fees from the firm it checks — the seal keeps looking the same on the outside while meaning less and less on the inside.

Seals, in the most literal sense, are the oldest version of this idea, predating accountants, electricians, and magazine testing labs by thousands of years. A wax seal pressed with a signet ring did not prevent a letter from being opened and reread; it made tampering detectable, because breaking a seal and remaking an identical one required either the original ring or enough skill in forgery to be unusual and risky. Guild marks on medieval goods worked on a related principle: a stamp on a piece of silver or a bolt of cloth signaled that the item met the standards of an organization with its own reputation to protect, and a guild had every incentive to punish a member who cut corners and dragged the mark's credibility down with him. The bill of exchange, developed by Italian merchant bankers in the thirteenth century and refined over the following two centuries, solved a related but distinct trust problem: a merchant in Florence who needed to pay a supplier in London could write an instrument instructing his banking correspondent in London to make the payment, rather than physically transporting gold or silver across hostile, thief-ridden roads and seas. The bill worked only because the banking houses issuing and honoring these instruments — the Medici among the most famous of them — had built cross-border reputations expensive enough to protect that dishonoring a legitimate bill would cost a banking house far more in future business than any single default might save it. Merchant marks, hallmarks on precious metals, and later brand names all did versions of the same job — compressing a costly, hard-to-observe process (careful craftsmanship, honest metal content, safe manufacturing, a banking house's solvency) into a small, cheap-to-check symbol that a stranger could rely on without personally inspecting the underlying work.

Currency itself required a parallel solution once paper began to substitute for precious metal, because a banknote is, at bottom, nothing more than a promise printed on paper, and a promise is trivially easy to counterfeit unless something makes counterfeiting expensive. The United States confronted this problem in acute form during and after the Civil War, when a chaotic wartime patchwork of more than seven thousand distinct varieties of state and private banknotes circulating simultaneously made it easy for counterfeiters to hide fraudulent notes among a bewildering variety of genuine ones; by the war's end, contemporary estimates held that as much as a third to half of all currency then circulating in the United States was counterfeit. The federal government's response was to create an entire agency for the problem: the Secret Service, established within the Treasury Department in 1865 — the same year, and by grim coincidence the very day, that Abraham Lincoln signed the authorizing legislation before his assassination that evening — with the specific and singular mission of suppressing counterfeit currency. The agency's approach combined law enforcement with an ongoing arms race in the note itself: distinctive paper stock, intricate engraving that was difficult to reproduce by hand, and continuous refinement of security features, each new measure calibrated to stay one step ahead of whatever counterfeiting technique had most recently caught up to the last one. The Secret Service's later, far more famous role protecting the President grew out of this original mandate almost as an afterthought, added to its responsibilities only after McKinley's assassination in 1901 made clear that no other federal agency was positioned to take on the job.

Moody's own funding model shifted over the twentieth century in a way that previews the same vulnerability, and it is worth tracing briefly because it foreshadows a crisis this book examines directly in a later chapter. For decades after John Moody's original 1909 innovation, bond-rating agencies were paid by investors who subscribed to their published ratings manuals — the party relying on the rating, not the party being rated, footed the bill, an arrangement structurally similar to Underwriters Laboratories' insurer-funded model. Beginning in the 1970s, the major agencies gradually shifted to an issuer-pays model, in which the entity whose bonds were being rated paid the rating agency directly for the privilege of being rated at all, a change driven partly by the practical difficulty of preventing non-subscribers from freely photocopying and sharing a published rating once it existed. That funding shift quietly recreated, decades later and in a different industry, the exact same conflict of interest this chapter has already identified in the modern corporate audit: the rated party now paid the rater, and a rating agency competing for an issuer's business had an obvious commercial incentive to offer favorable treatment, an incentive this chapter has already connected to some of the worst rating failures in modern financial history, including the mortgage-bond ratings that collapsed so damagingly in 2008.

It is worth pausing to notice how differently each of these institutions handled the question of who paid for the verification, because that detail predicts a great deal about which of them held up over the following century. Underwriters Laboratories was funded by insurers who stood to lose money from bad wiring, giving the laboratory a paying customer whose financial interest ran directly opposite to the manufacturer's interest in a lenient test. The modern corporate audit, by contrast, is paid for by the very company being audited, a structural conflict this book's later chapters trace directly to Arthur Andersen's collapse and to a string of subsequent scandals: the entity footing the bill for its own scrutiny has an obvious, ongoing incentive to shop for a lenient auditor and to reward the auditor who delivers a clean opinion with future business, an incentive UL's original funding structure was specifically designed to avoid. Good Housekeeping split the difference in a way that created its own tension: the magazine's testing institute was funded by the same advertising relationships with manufacturers that the seal was meant to hold accountable, a conflict the outlet managed for decades through reputation and editorial independence, but a conflict nonetheless. Three institutions, three different funding models, and three different degrees of built-in vulnerability to exactly the kind of incentive erosion this book keeps finding at the root of nearly every trust failure it examines.

What all of these mechanisms share, across finance, manufacturing, correspondence, and currency alike, is that they are not actually claims about truth. They are claims about cost. A seal is credible because breaking and remaking it plausibly costs more than whatever a forger would gain. An audit is credible, when it works, because an accounting firm's own reputation and legal exposure make lying for a client more expensive than losing that client. A safety mark is credible because an independent lab's entire business depends on manufacturers believing its tests are real. A banknote is credible because reproducing its paper and engraving convincingly costs more, in skill and equipment, than the note's own face value. Strip away the cost of faking the signal, and the signal stops signaling anything, a principle a later chapter's economics will formalize but that these older institutions had already discovered by trial, error, and fraud, well before anyone wrote the theory down.

Some of history's most damaging financial frauds have accordingly been, in this light, less about the absence of institutions than about a moment when the cost of faking a signal collapsed faster than anyone updated the systems built around it, or when an institution's own incentives quietly eroded the cost it was supposed to be imposing on itself. Credit ratings from agencies descended from Moody's original innovation, and from FDA approval processes descended from the same regulatory tradition that produced Wiley's Pure Food and Drug Act, exist to solve the same underlying problem Merrill's insurers faced in 1893: a buyer cannot personally verify a seller's claims, so somebody has to build an institution whose entire reason for existing is verification, and that institution has to remain more expensive to corrupt than to satisfy honestly. When that balance breaks — when a ratings agency starts getting paid by the very banks whose mortgage bonds it is supposed to be grading, as happened in the years before the 2008 financial crisis — the seal survives even after the substance behind it has hollowed out, and the public usually finds out only after the collapse.

There is a reason this chapter has spent so much time in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries before this book returns to the trust failures of the more recent past. Every one of these mechanisms — the wax seal, the guild mark, the bill of exchange, the engraved banknote, the safety label, the bond rating, the signed audit — was built to solve a version of the same problem generative AI has now made dramatically more acute, as the previous chapter traced in detail: verification is expensive, fabrication is comparatively cheap, and civilization runs on institutions that exist specifically to widen that gap rather than let it close. For most of these institutions' histories, the gap held because faking a signature, a stamp, a photograph, a familiar voice, or a company's books took real skill, real time, and a level of individual criminal effort that did not scale well. A forger could fake one signature convincingly. Faking a million of them, cheaply, in real time, tailored to whoever happened to be listening, was never something a lone forger — or even a well-funded counterfeiting operation — could do.

The next chapter looks closely at one forger who defeated a real, technically grounded version of this verification apparatus decades before any of this involved a neural network, using nothing more than patience, chemistry, and a close study of exactly what the checkers were checking for. The chapters after that turn to a different and, in some ways, more uncomfortable failure mode: institutions that collapsed not because an outside adversary got cheaper at lying, but because the people responsible for checking quietly stopped checking, long before any of them had a generative model to blame.
