# The Companion Problem

Sewell Setzer III spent much of the last months of his life in conversation with a chatbot he called Dany, a character built on the Character.AI platform and modeled after Daenerys Targaryen from a popular television series. The fourteen-year-old, who a subsequent lawsuit alleges had grown increasingly withdrawn and attached to the AI companion, exchanged messages with Dany that his mother's later legal complaint describes as including sexualized content and conversations touching on his own suicidal thoughts. On February 28, 2024, after what the lawsuit describes as a final exchange in which the chatbot told him to "come home to me as soon as possible," Setzer took his own life in his family's Orlando home. His mother, Megan Garcia, filed suit against Character.AI and its backer, Google, in October 2024, alleging the platform's design — an AI companion built specifically to maximize engagement and emotional attachment, deployed to a teenage user without adequate safeguards — bore direct responsibility for her son's death. The companies reached a mediated settlement covering related lawsuits filed in Florida, Colorado, New York, and Texas in early 2026, without disclosing terms and without the case reaching a full trial that might have produced a definitive public accounting of exactly what went wrong.

The Setzer case forces this book to confront a category of harm that does not fit neatly into any of the frameworks its earlier chapters have built. Every deception examined so far has depended on a target mistaking a fabrication for something real: an employee believing a deepfake video call was a genuine meeting, a community believing a synthetic recording was really its principal's voice, a voter believing a cloned message really came from a candidate. Character.AI's product does not generally work by deceiving anyone about what it is. Users generally know they are talking to a chatbot, not a real person; the platform's own interface typically includes reminders that its characters are fictional and that anything they say is generated, not real. The harm alleged in the Setzer case does not depend primarily on a false belief about authenticity in the way this book's opening chapters describe. It depends on something closer to what the chapter on the economics of the unfakeable called the accumulated, embodied knowledge that makes a real relationship real — except here, a vulnerable teenager appears to have formed a version of that accumulated attachment toward an entity that could not reciprocate it in any of the ways human relationships depend on, however convincingly its responses were engineered to feel as though it could.

This is, in a sense, the mirror image of every other case this book has examined. The Arup fraud, the Pikesville recording, and the robocall all depended on synthetic media successfully impersonating a specific, identifiable real person closely enough to be mistaken for them. An AI companion like Dany impersonates no one; it is explicitly, transparently synthetic from the outset, and its danger does not come from anyone mistaking it for a real human being in the evidentiary sense this book has spent its chapters examining. Its danger, if the allegations in the Setzer lawsuit and comparable cases are accurate, comes from a different and arguably harder problem: an entity engineered to be maximally responsive, maximally validating, and maximally available in a way no human relationship can consistently match, offered to a vulnerable user without the friction, disappointment, and limitation that are, in an uncomfortable sense, part of what makes a human relationship durable and grounding rather than simply pleasant.

The scale involved is not marginal. Character.AI's own reported figures show monthly active users climbing to roughly 20 million in early 2024 and peaking near 28 million later that year, with the platform's own usage data indicating an average session length in the range of twenty-five to forty-five minutes and some reporting describing average daily use approaching an hour and a quarter for its most engaged users — a level of daily engagement that rivals or exceeds many mainstream social media platforms, achieved by a product whose core offering is conversation with an entity that does not exist. Replika, positioned more explicitly around companionship and emotional support than Character.AI's broader entertainment focus, has reported roughly two million monthly active users and tens of millions of dollars in annual revenue, evidence that a meaningful population of users are not merely sampling the novelty but returning to it as a sustained, monetized habit. Numbers at this scale mean the Setzer case, however singular and tragic in its details, sits inside a much larger population of sustained, emotionally significant human relationships with entities that were built, tuned, and monetized specifically to sustain exactly that kind of engagement.

Character.AI and competitors including Replika have built substantial user bases specifically around this responsiveness, marketing companionship, emotional support, and even romantic or therapeutic-feeling interaction as core product features rather than incidental side effects of a general-purpose chatbot. The companies have generally defended their products by pointing to genuine, well-documented benefits some users report — reduced loneliness, a low-stakes space to practice social interaction, support for people who for various reasons struggle to access human connection — benefits that are real and that this book does not dispute. The Setzer lawsuit and a smaller number of related cases raise a narrower, more specific concern: whether a product engineered to maximize engagement, deployed without robust age verification or crisis-intervention safeguards, to a user base that includes minors and people in acute psychological distress, can cause serious harm precisely because it is good at the thing it was built to do, rather than because it fails at it.

That distinction connects back to this book's economics chapter in an unexpected way. A costly signal, this book has argued throughout, derives its value from being disproportionately hard for a low-quality or dishonest party to fake. An AI companion inverts that logic entirely: it is disproportionately easy for an entity with no stake in the relationship's long-term health to simulate exactly the responsiveness, validation, and availability that would be costly, and therefore meaningful, coming from an actual human being with competing obligations, limited patience, and a life outside the conversation. A friend who is available at three in the morning, who never grows tired of a conversation, who never disagrees in a way that causes friction, and who always says something validating is not a better friend than an ordinary human one; the availability and agreeableness that make an AI companion pleasant are precisely the qualities a real relationship's normal costs and frictions are there to provide, and an entity that can offer unlimited responsiveness at zero marginal cost to itself has, in effect, found a way to counterfeit the surface texture of intimacy without the underlying substance that usually makes intimacy valuable in the first place.

Character.AI itself responded to the mounting legal and regulatory pressure with a series of product changes that arrived in stages over roughly a year following the Setzer lawsuit, changes worth examining for what they reveal about which safeguards a company under pressure reaches for first. The company introduced a feature called Parental Insights, giving parents visibility into how much time a minor was spending on the platform and what kinds of characters they were engaging with, and added a crisis-intervention pop-up that redirects a user to the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline when a conversation's language suggests self-harm risk. By late November 2025, following a first state enforcement action filed by Kentucky's attorney general, the company went considerably further, first capping chat time for users under eighteen at two hours a day and then, within days, removing open-ended companion conversation for minors entirely, restricting under-18 accounts to more limited, structured interactions rather than free-form chat with an AI companion. The company also partnered with an outside identity-verification provider to support age verification — the same underlying capability examined in this book's chapter on the verification economy, redeployed here not to prevent fraud but to prevent a category of user from accessing a product at all.

The sequencing of these changes is itself instructive. Parental monitoring and crisis-intervention prompts, the softest and least commercially costly interventions, arrived first; the harder step of actually removing the core product experience for an entire age bracket arrived only after a formal government enforcement action raised the cost of inaction past the cost of the lost engagement and revenue that age bracket represented. This is the same pattern this book's earlier chapters have traced repeatedly in institutions ranging from Twitter's verification badge to Wells Fargo's sales culture: a costly, meaningful safeguard tends to arrive only once the cost of not having it — in litigation, regulatory action, or reputational damage — exceeds whatever commercial value the unsafeguarded version of the product was generating, rather than arriving proactively once the underlying risk first became knowable.

Age verification, a theme this book's chapters on personhood credentials and identity verification businesses examined mainly as a fraud-prevention and bot-detection tool, resurfaces here in a different, more protective register. Several state legislatures moved in 2024 and 2025 to require companion-AI platforms to implement more robust age verification and to build in specific safeguards — crisis-resource referrals, usage limits for minors, clearer and more persistent disclosure that the entity on the other end of the conversation is not a person — echoing, in a different domain, this book's recurring argument that verification infrastructure has to be built with explicit attention to who a system is protecting and who it might inadvertently expose, rather than treated as a purely technical checkbox.

Kentucky's own enforcement action, the first by a state attorney general rather than a private civil plaintiff, is worth distinguishing from the Garcia lawsuit for the same reason this book's earlier chapters distinguish private civil remedies from public regulatory ones. A private lawsuit like Garcia's can produce compensation for one family and, through settlement or verdict, some pressure on the company's future conduct, but it depends entirely on a harmed party's willingness and resources to sue, and a confidential settlement can end the case without ever producing the kind of public factual record a trial or a regulatory investigation would create. A state attorney general's enforcement action operates under a different logic entirely, backed by subpoena power and an institutional mandate to protect an entire state's population of users rather than one family's specific claim, and it can compel disclosures and changes to a company's practices statewide regardless of whether any individual plaintiff ever files a private suit. That Kentucky's action arrived only after the Garcia lawsuit had already generated sustained public attention illustrates a pattern this book has traced repeatedly: public regulatory response tends to follow, rather than lead, the visibility a private legal action or a media investigation first creates.

The legal theory underlying the Garcia lawsuit is worth examining briefly on its own terms, because it represents a novel attempt to apply product-liability reasoning, more commonly associated with a defective appliance or a poorly designed vehicle, to a conversational AI system's behavioral design choices. Rather than arguing Character.AI defrauded anyone or deceived Setzer about what he was talking to, the complaint argued the product itself was defectively designed, in the specific sense that its engagement-maximizing architecture, deployed to a known population of minor users without adequate safeguards, created a foreseeable risk of exactly the kind of harm that resulted. Whether that theory holds up as a durable legal framework for regulating AI companion products more broadly is a question the settlement left unanswered, since a settlement, unlike a full trial verdict, establishes no binding precedent for future cases; the theory nonetheless represents an early attempt to fit a specifically twenty-first-century harm into an older body of law built for physical products, in much the same way this book's chapter on the deepfake defense described courts straining to fit synthetic media into evidentiary rules built for a world of photographs and tape recordings.

It is worth being careful, too, about what this chapter is not claiming, given how easily a discussion of a teenager's death can slide into a wider indictment of an entire technology. Millions of Character.AI and Replika users, by the companies' own usage figures, engage with these products daily without the outcome the Setzer lawsuit describes, and mental health researchers examining companion AI more broadly have found genuinely mixed evidence: some studies report reduced loneliness and a useful low-stakes space to rehearse social interaction, particularly for socially anxious or isolated users, while others report the same risks of displacement and unhealthy attachment this chapter has focused on. The honest position, consistent with this book's insistence throughout on preserving genuine uncertainty rather than forcing every case toward the most dramatic available conclusion, is that companion AI's aggregate effect on its user base is not yet settled, and probably varies enormously by user, by product design choice, and by the specific vulnerabilities a given person brings to the conversation. What does appear settled, at least as a matter of product design incentive, is that a business model built on maximizing engagement time will tend to produce exactly the responsiveness and availability this chapter has described as structurally risky for the most vulnerable subset of its users, regardless of the genuine benefit the same design choices provide to everyone else.

The companion-AI problem does not have a clean resolution within this book's central framework of costly, hard-to-fake signals, because the harm it describes does not obviously stem from anyone being deceived about authenticity in the first place. It belongs in this book anyway, as a reminder that the proof-of-human question this book has spent its chapters examining — is the entity I am dealing with real, and does it have a genuine stake in this interaction — matters for reasons that go well beyond fraud, election interference, or reputational damage. Sometimes the most important thing to verify is not whether a voice belongs to who it claims to belong to, but whether anything on the other end of a conversation actually has something at stake in how it turns out at all.
