# Eating Itself

Every generative AI model examined in this book was trained on a snapshot of human-produced text, images, audio, and video, scraped from a version of the internet that existed before that same technology began flooding the internet with its own output at scale. That fact was, for the first few years of the generative AI boom, a background detail rather than a central concern. It has since become one of the more consequential open questions in the entire field, because the internet those future models will need to train on is no longer the internet of 2019. It is an internet increasingly populated by the pink slime news sites, the AI-generated comments, the synthetic academic papers, and the machine-translated propaganda examined throughout this book's earlier chapters — content produced by earlier generations of the very models that need fresh, high-quality data to keep improving.

The intuition behind model collapse had already been circulating for years in a cruder, more conspiratorial form before any peer-reviewed research formalized it. The so-called dead internet theory originated on fringe forums in the mid-2010s and crystallized into something like a manifesto in a 2021 post on a small message board, arguing that the internet had effectively "died" around 2016 and that most of what appeared to be human activity online since then was actually bots, algorithmically generated content, and engagement-optimized automation talking past one another with no real person reading along. The theory, as originally stated, was considerably more sweeping and less evidenced than anything this book's chapters on bad-bot traffic and pink slime journalism have documented, and much of its early framing bordered on genuine conspiracy thinking, imagining a coordinated, deliberate effort to depopulate the internet of humans rather than the more mundane economic incentives this book has traced instead. But the underlying suspicion — that a rising share of what looks like organic online activity is neither organic nor, in a meaningful sense, activity at all — turned out to anticipate a real and measurable phenomenon by several years, arriving at roughly the right diagnosis through internet folklore before Shumailov's team arrived at a version of the same concern through formal machine learning research.

Ilia Shumailov and a team of collaborators formalized the resulting risk in an influential paper published in Nature in 2024, describing a phenomenon they termed model collapse: when a generative model is trained, directly or indirectly, on data substantially produced by earlier generative models rather than by humans, the resulting model's outputs tend to narrow and degrade across successive generations, losing the tails of the original data's diversity first and, if the recursive training continues long enough, eventually collapsing toward a kind of averaged, repetitive sameness that resembles none of the rich variation in the original human-produced training set. The mechanism is not exotic; it resembles what happens when a photograph is photocopied, then the photocopy is photocopied again, and again, each generation losing a little more fidelity to the original until the accumulated degradation becomes obvious even to a casual observer, except that the "photocopying" here happens inside a statistical model whose errors compound in ways that are harder to visually inspect than a blurry scan.

The practical stakes of this research follow directly from the pink slime and paper-mill chapters examined earlier in this book. If a meaningful and growing share of the freely available text on the internet is now machine-generated — the local news copy, the search-engine-optimized product reviews, the comment-section filler, the propaganda translations — then any company training a new generative model on a fresh scrape of the internet is, whether it intends to or not, increasingly training on its predecessors' own output rather than on the rich, unfiltered human record those earlier models were themselves trained on. Some AI researchers have taken to calling this dynamic, somewhat informally, a slow contamination of the shared data commons the entire field depends on — a resource that, unlike a fishery or a forest, cannot simply be left alone to regenerate, because the pre-2020 internet snapshot that made the first generation of large language models possible is not a renewable resource; it was a specific historical artifact, produced by a specific density of human-only online writing that may never recur in the same form now that so much of the internet's ongoing output is itself AI-assisted or AI-generated outright.

This is a genuinely different category of risk than anything else examined in this book, because it does not primarily threaten an individual, an institution, or an election. It threatens the raw material every subsequent generation of the technology examined throughout this book depends on. Every chapter of this book has assumed, implicitly, that generative AI capabilities will simply continue to improve, making fabrication cheaper and more convincing over time in the way this book's economics chapter describes as an ongoing collapse in the cost of faking a costly signal. Model collapse research complicates that assumption in an unexpected direction: if training data quality degrades as machine-generated content increasingly pollutes the shared corpus future models learn from, the capability curve this book has treated as an inexorable one-way ratchet could plateau, or even partially reverse, for reasons that have nothing to do with any deliberate policy intervention and everything to do with the technology's own success at flooding its future training data with lower-quality copies of itself.

The major AI labs' response to this risk has itself become a distinct commercial and technical strategy worth naming, because it inverts the usual relationship between synthetic and human-generated content this book's other chapters have described. Rather than treating all synthetic data as contamination to be filtered out, several leading developers have begun deliberately generating carefully curated synthetic training data themselves, produced under controlled conditions specifically designed to preserve diversity and correctness rather than compound errors — a practice some researchers distinguish sharply from the uncontrolled, ambient synthetic content examined throughout this book's chapters on pink slime and disinformation, on the theory that synthetic data generated deliberately, checked against known-correct answers, and mixed carefully with human-verified sources behaves differently than synthetic data scraped indiscriminately from an internet that no longer reliably labels its own origins. Whether that distinction holds up as cleanly in practice as it does in a research paper's methodology section is an open question this book cannot resolve, but the distinction itself is instructive: it suggests that the relevant variable determining whether synthetic data helps or harms a future model is not simply whether the data is synthetic, but whether its provenance, curation, and verification were handled with the same kind of costly, deliberate care this book's chapters on content credentials and identity verification describe institutions applying to human-generated content — provenance, in other words, mattering just as much for a model's training diet as it matters for an individual viewer trying to judge a single photograph or video.

Subsequent research has complicated the initial, more alarming framing of the Shumailov paper, in ways consistent with this book's recurring insistence on preserving genuine uncertainty rather than forcing every finding toward the most dramatic available conclusion. Several follow-up papers have argued that "model collapse" in its starkest form depends on specific, somewhat artificial experimental conditions — full replacement of human data with synthetic data across many successive training generations, with no fresh human data mixed back in at any point — that may not accurately describe how real AI labs actually build their training pipelines in practice. Major AI developers have publicly described active efforts to filter, detect, and deliberately exclude machine-generated content from their training datasets, and to seek out curated, verified-human sources specifically to guard against the contamination this research describes, treating data provenance as a competitive and technical priority in a way that echoes, at the level of an entire industry's raw material supply chain, the same content-provenance argument this book's earlier chapters applied to individual photographs and videos. Whether those filtering efforts can keep pace with the sheer volume of synthetic content now being produced is an open empirical question this book cannot resolve, but it is worth noting explicitly rather than skating past: the alarming version of model collapse assumes AI developers do nothing to address a problem they are, in fact, already documented to be actively working against.

There is an obvious economic incentive problem lurking underneath this proposed second use case, worth naming rather than leaving implicit. A company racing to train the largest, most capable model as quickly as possible has every short-term incentive to scrape as much data as it can find, filtered or not, since filtering rigorously for verified-human provenance means discarding some usable-looking training data and accepting a smaller, slower-growing dataset than a rival willing to skip the filtering step entirely. This is the same collective-action problem this book's chapters on financial auditing and academic peer review describe in a different guise: a safeguard that benefits the entire field's long-term data quality can still lose out, company by company, to the short-term competitive pressure to move faster and scrape more broadly than whichever rival is filtering more conservatively. Whether the major labs' current, largely voluntary curation efforts can hold up against that competitive pressure over the following decade, rather than eroding the way this book's chapter on ratings-agency and audit incentives describes similar safeguards eroding under commercial pressure, is precisely the kind of question this book's evidence would counsel treating with skepticism rather than assumed confidence.

Content provenance infrastructure of the kind examined in this book's chapters on watermarking and C2PA manifests takes on an unexpected second life in this context, worth spelling out because it is a use case none of that infrastructure's original designers had primarily in mind. A watermark or a content credential built to help an individual reader distinguish a real photograph from a synthetic one could, in principle, be repurposed by a model developer's own data-collection pipeline to filter incoming training data, discarding content that carries a synthetic-origin marker and preferentially retaining content that can be verified as human-produced. This use case requires the same infrastructure this book has already examined to work at a scale and reliability it does not currently achieve — universal adoption across generation tools, robustness against the removal techniques this book's chapter on watermarking's limits documents, and a training pipeline sophisticated enough to actually check for and act on the signal rather than ingesting whatever text and images it happens to scrape. Model collapse research gives that infrastructure a second reason to exist beyond protecting individual readers from individual fakes: protecting the shared training commons every future model depends on from being slowly diluted by the outputs of the models that came before it.

There is a further wrinkle worth drawing out, because it connects model collapse directly back to the pink slime chapter's argument about journalism rather than leaving it as a purely technical, abstracted concern about future model quality. If AI-generated content increasingly displaces human-written local news, human-written product reviews, and human-generated social media commentary in the raw material available for future models to train on, the effect is not merely that future models get statistically blander or more repetitive in some generic sense. It is that the specific kind of content most likely to be replaced by cheap synthetic substitutes — precisely the pink slime local news, the templated product review, the astroturfed comment examined in this book's earlier chapters — is also disproportionately the kind of content that used to encode genuinely local, specific, hard-to-fake human knowledge: a real reporter's knowledge of which city council member actually said what at an actual meeting, a real customer's actual experience with a specific product's actual defects, a real community's own specific concerns voiced in its own words. A future model trained increasingly on synthetic approximations of that content, generated by earlier models that were themselves several steps removed from the original human sources, risks losing not just statistical diversity in some abstract sense but the specific, granular, ground-truth texture of human experience that made earlier training data valuable in the first place — the same kind of loss this book's closing chapter argues no synthetic system can easily manufacture from scratch, because it was never built from a formula to begin with.

What model collapse research adds to this book's broader argument is a reminder that the differential-cost logic this book has applied to individual acts of verification — is this video real, is this voice genuine, is this person who they claim to be — scales up to something like an ecosystem-level version of the same question: is the shared pool of human-generated knowledge and expression that trains every future model still growing, or is it being diluted by the model's own prior output faster than genuine human contribution can replenish it? Content provenance infrastructure, the watermarking and credentialing systems examined earlier in this book, take on a second, less obvious purpose in this light, beyond helping an individual reader distinguish a real photograph from a fake one: they are also, potentially, one of the few practical tools available for helping a future model's training pipeline distinguish genuine human contribution from synthetic content generated by an earlier version of itself or a competitor, a use case none of those systems' original designers had fully in mind when they were built primarily to solve the individual-verification problems this book's earlier chapters describe.
