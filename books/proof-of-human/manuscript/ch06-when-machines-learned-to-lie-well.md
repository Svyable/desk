# When Machines Learned to Lie Well

In 2014, a graduate student named Ian Goodfellow described an idea to colleagues at a party in Montreal and, according to the story he later told, went home and coded the first version that same night. The idea was to pit two neural networks against each other: a generator that tried to produce fake images convincing enough to pass as real, and a discriminator that tried to catch the fakes. Each network improved by trying to defeat the other, in a loop that pushed both toward a strange equilibrium where the generator's output became difficult even for its dedicated adversary to distinguish from the genuine article. Goodfellow called the architecture a generative adversarial network, and the paper he and his coauthors published that year turned out to be one of the more consequential documents in the recent history of applied mathematics, not because anyone at the time could produce a passable fake photograph of a real person, but because the underlying trick — train a forger and a detector against each other, and let the forger win — scaled far better than anyone in that room likely expected.

It took a few more years for the trick to reach faces convincingly. Early GAN-generated images, circa 2014 to 2016, tended toward the uncanny: blurred backgrounds, asymmetric features, textures that looked more like wet clay than skin. Researchers at Nvidia made the decisive leap in output quality with a family of architectures called StyleGAN, published in 2018 and refined through several subsequent versions, which could generate photorealistic human faces of people who had never existed, controllable along axes like age, expression, and pose. A companion website, built to demonstrate the technology's fluency, simply refreshed to a new invented face each time a visitor reloaded the page — a small, almost playful demonstration that nonetheless made a serious point: a machine could now manufacture an infinite supply of convincing human faces belonging to nobody, on demand, for free.

The word "deepfake" itself entered public circulation slightly earlier, in late 2017, coined, more or less, by an anonymous Reddit user posting synthetic pornographic videos that swapped celebrities' faces onto other people's bodies, using techniques descended from Goodfellow's original architecture. The early examples were often crude enough to be spotted by attentive viewers: mismatched lighting, unnatural blinking, a faint shimmer at the jawline where the swapped face met the original footage. Researchers and journalists treated the flaws as reassuring, in the way early smoke is reassuring compared to what a fire eventually becomes. The technology did not stay crude. Within a few years, models trained on ordinary consumer hardware could produce face swaps convincing enough to fool casual viewing, and the compute and expertise required kept falling while the output kept improving, the same trajectory that has characterized nearly every consequential machine learning technique since.

Image generation took a further, distinct leap forward with the arrival of diffusion models — a different mathematical approach that starts with random noise and iteratively refines it into a coherent image, guided by a text description — which by 2022 had produced consumer-facing tools like OpenAI's DALL-E 2, Stability AI's Stable Diffusion, and Midjourney, each capable of generating an image from a plain-language prompt in seconds. These tools were built for entirely legitimate creative purposes — illustration, concept art, marketing material — and the overwhelming majority of their use has remained exactly that. But a technology built for one purpose rarely stays confined to it once it becomes cheap and widely available, and by 2023 the same diffusion architectures were being adapted, sometimes by the original commercial vendors and sometimes by open-source communities working from leaked or openly released model weights, into tools specifically optimized for realistic human faces, bodies, and voices, stripped of the safety filters the mainstream commercial products had built in.

Voice followed a similar arc, on a faster practical timeline, and it is voice — not video — that has done the most concrete financial and reputational damage documented so far, for reasons this book's earlier chapters have already illustrated directly: a convincing fake voice requires far less raw data than a convincing fake face, and a phone call or an audio clip offers far fewer cues for a listener to double-check against than a video does. By the early 2020s, companies including ElevenLabs had built commercial tools that could clone a person's voice from a short sample. Public reporting has described the system producing a recognizable clone from roughly thirty seconds of clean audio, with quality that degrades below that threshold but does not disappear; some accounts of the underlying models describe even three seconds of speech yielding a clone accurate enough to fool a casual listener a large share of the time. Within weeks of ElevenLabs opening its voice-cloning tool more broadly in early 2023, users on forums including 4chan began using it to clone celebrities' voices for harassment and pranks, and the company subsequently added a verification step requiring speakers to record specific consent language before a voice could be cloned — a safeguard that blocks casual misuse without doing much to stop a determined bad actor working around it, as the Pikesville and New Hampshire cases examined a couple of chapters earlier both demonstrate in their different ways.

The Federal Trade Commission issued a consumer warning in 2023 describing exactly the scenario that safeguard was meant to prevent: criminals cloning a family member's voice from audio pulled off social media or an old voicemail, then calling an elderly relative in apparent distress, asking for money to be wired immediately for an emergency that does not exist. This is an old scam wearing new clothes — the so-called grandparent scam has worked for years using nothing more sophisticated than a stranger claiming, over a bad phone connection, to be someone's grandchild in trouble — but the new clothes fit unnervingly well. A voice that sounds correct removes the single biggest reason a target might pause before wiring money. Reported losses from fraud targeting older Americans have climbed sharply across this period; the Federal Trade Commission has reported billions of dollars in fraud losses among older adults in recent years, and law enforcement agencies including the FBI have specifically flagged AI voice cloning as an accelerant behind a documented rise in these calls since 2023, even though clean national statistics that isolate the AI-specific share of a much larger fraud category remain difficult to produce with precision.

Open-source model releases accelerated this dynamic in a way proprietary, closed commercial products alone would not have. When a well-resourced lab releases a generative model's weights publicly, rather than keeping the model accessible only through its own metered, monitored commercial interface, the safety filters and usage restrictions that company built into its own consumer product can often be removed or bypassed by anyone with the technical skill to modify the underlying model directly, since the restrictions typically live in a wrapper around the model rather than in the model's own core capabilities. This is precisely the pattern this book's chapters on deepfake video and voice cloning describe: mainstream commercial tools with real safety guardrails coexisting, often within the same technical lineage, with modified or unrestricted versions circulating in smaller, less visible communities specifically because they strip out exactly the protections the original developer built in. A single well-resourced lab's decision to open its weights, made for entirely legitimate reasons of research transparency or competitive strategy, can therefore have consequences for the wider fabrication ecosystem examined throughout this book that extend well past that lab's own intended use case or its own terms of service.

The compute and cost curve behind all three modalities tells the same story from a different angle. Training a state-of-the-art generative model from scratch remains expensive, requiring specialized hardware and engineering talent well beyond an individual's reach; this is why the handful of companies capable of training frontier models remain few. But the marginal cost of using an already-trained model to produce one additional fake — one more cloned voice clip, one more synthetic face, one more fabricated image — collapsed toward the cost of ordinary cloud computing, a few cents or fractions of a cent per output, orders of magnitude below what the same output would have cost a human forger, artist, or voice impersonator to produce by hand. This is the specific economic mechanism behind every case examined in this book's opening chapters: the expensive part of generative AI is building the factory, not running it, and once a small number of companies had built the factories, the marginal fabrication became available to anyone with an internet connection at a price low enough to be functionally irrelevant to the decision of whether to commit the fraud at all.

What is worth noticing across the deepfake video timeline, the diffusion-model image timeline, and the voice-cloning timeline is not any single dramatic leap but the shape of the curve, which recurs with almost tedious regularity across every modality. Each technique started as a research curiosity capable of fooling almost no one who looked closely, then became a research curiosity capable of fooling most casual observers, then became a consumer product cheap enough and easy enough to use that the limiting factor was no longer technical skill but merely the decision to try. That last transition is the one that matters most for a book about trust, because it is the transition that moves a technology from something institutions can treat as a rare, containable threat to something they have to assume is happening constantly, to anyone, at any time — a school athletic director with a grievance, a political consultant willing to break telecommunications law, a stranger with no relationship to the person being harmed at all.

Text generation tracked a version of the same curve, arriving slightly earlier in public consciousness and reaching fluency slightly faster, because language models could be trained on vastly more available data than either faces or voices. By the time large language models were producing fluent, plausible prose on demand, the internet already carried a meaningful and rapidly growing volume of machine-generated text, embedded in product reviews, social media posts, and — more quietly — academic submissions, comment sections, and the kind of low-stakes local news content that had never attracted much scrutiny in the first place. None of this required a villain twirling a mustache. Much of it was generated for reasons as mundane as filling a content quota cheaply, gaming a search algorithm, or padding an astroturfed comment section to make a fringe opinion look like consensus. The fabrication did not need to be flawless to work. It needed only to be cheap enough, and plausible enough, to overwhelm the attention anyone had available to check it. Later chapters in this book — on AI-generated local news sites, and on state-sponsored influence operations — trace what happens when that dynamic operates at industrial rather than individual scale.

Video generation reached its own inflection point somewhat later than image, audio, and text, for reasons that are more computational than conceptual: a video is, in effect, a very large number of images that must remain coherent with one another across time, which multiplies the difficulty of the underlying generation problem substantially. Tools capable of producing short, coherent video clips from a text prompt, without any recorded footage as a starting point, reached rough commercial viability only in the mid-2020s, several years behind their image-generation counterparts. That lag is worth noting because it means the video-based frauds examined earlier in this book — the Arup call, the attempt on WPP — still relied on a hybrid technique: real recorded footage and audio of real people, manipulated and recombined, rather than video generated from nothing but a text description. As pure text-to-video generation continues to close that gap, the raw material constraint that has so far limited fully synthetic video fraud — the need for enough authentic footage of a target to build a convincing model of their face and voice in the first place — is likely to loosen considerably, extending this book's opening chapters' scenarios to targets who have far less publicly available footage than a corporate executive or a sitting president.

That is the real inflection point this chapter has been circling. Forgery is ancient. What generative AI changed was not the existence of fabrication but its unit economics. A skilled human forger, cloning a signature or faking a photograph, was constrained by time, talent, and the practical limits of doing the work by hand — constraints that kept the supply of convincing fakes low relative to the supply of genuine material, an asymmetry that an entire apparatus of historical institutions, examined in the next chapter, was built specifically to exploit. Generative models removed the constraint. A single operator with commercially available tools can now produce a synthetic video call, a cloned voice, a fabricated image, or a fluent wall of text at a volume and price that no forger's guild, however skilled, could have matched a decade earlier. None of the cases examined in the opening chapters of this book — the Arup fraud, the attempt on WPP, the Pikesville recording, the New Hampshire robocall, the images of Taylor Swift — required a criminal mastermind who happened to also be a gifted mimic, painter, or sound engineer. Each required only someone willing to feed publicly available material into tools that were, by the early-to-mid 2020s, available to more or less anyone with an internet connection and, in most cases, no payment at all.

The chapters that follow this one turn from the technology itself to the older question of how societies built mechanisms to guarantee trust before any of this existed, and how well those mechanisms have actually held up, including in domains — scientific publishing, financial auditing, corporate governance — that have never involved a single deepfake and have still, on their own, produced spectacular and instructive failures of verification. The reversal in fabrication's economics described in this chapter did not create the trust problem this book is about. It arrived at a moment when a great deal of the trust infrastructure built across the preceding two centuries was already under more strain than most people realized, and it removed one of the few advantages that infrastructure had left: the assumption that convincing fakes required unusual skill, unusual time, or unusual motive.
