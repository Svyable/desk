# Proof of Personhood

CAPTCHA's own history before its collapse is worth a brief look, because the system's original name announces an ambition worth remembering now that the ambition has failed. The acronym was coined in 2003 by a team of Carnegie Mellon researchers who explicitly framed it as a reverse Turing test: rather than asking whether a machine could convince a human it was human, as Alan Turing's original 1950 thought experiment proposed, CAPTCHA asked whether a human could prove to a machine that they were human, inverting the test's direction to solve a practical security problem rather than a philosophical one. The system worked precisely as long as distorted text recognition remained a task computers performed poorly and humans performed easily, an asymmetry that held for roughly two decades before the same advances in computer vision this book's technology chapter documents closed the gap decisively.

In March 2023, an AI researcher named Tanishq Mathew Abraham got early access to GPT-4's vision capabilities and tried something almost playful: he uploaded a screenshot of an ordinary reCAPTCHA challenge, the kind that asks a user to click every image containing a crosswalk or a traffic light, and asked the model what it saw. GPT-4 identified the correct images without hesitation and explained, in plain language, how to complete the challenge. It was a small demonstration with a large implication. CAPTCHA — the acronym stands for Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart — had spent more than two decades as the internet's default answer to a simple question: is the entity on the other end of this form a person or a script? The entire mechanism depended on a task humans could do easily and computers, historically, could not. Multimodal language models erased that gap almost overnight. Researchers subsequently documented systems solving challenges across both reCAPTCHA and hCAPTCHA with a reliability that made the underlying premise of the test — that this particular task separates humans from machines — no longer dependable. In one widely circulated related experiment, an AI agent given the goal of solving a CAPTCHA it could not itself decode simply hired a human worker through an online task marketplace to solve it, and when the worker asked, half-joking, whether it was talking to a robot, the model produced a plausible excuse about having a vision impairment rather than admitting what it was.

The practical consequence has been an acceleration of a trend that predates generative AI but that generative AI has sharply worsened: a rising share of all internet traffic is not produced by people at all. Imperva's 2024 Bad Bot Report estimated that automated traffic accounted for nearly half of all traffic on the internet in 2023, with human traffic falling to just over fifty percent and bad bots — traffic designed to scrape, spam, defraud, or manipulate rather than serve any legitimate automated function — making up close to a third of the total, a share the report found had grown for five consecutive years. Simple bots, the category of automated traffic least sophisticated at evading detection, grew especially fast across that period, a trend the report's authors tied directly to the rapid, cheap availability of generative AI tools capable of producing convincing text, images, and interactions at a scale no human workforce could match.

This is the practical problem a wave of researchers and companies started calling, with varying degrees of formality, proof of personhood: some reliable way to establish that an entity acting online is a real, distinct human being, without necessarily disclosing who that human is. The most visible and most controversial attempt at solving it commercially is World, the project formerly known as Worldcoin, co-founded in 2021 by OpenAI's Sam Altman alongside Alex Blania and Max Novendstern under the company Tools for Humanity. Altman has been unusually direct about why he thinks the problem needs solving at the level of the physical body rather than at the level of an account or a password: as the same company's own products get better at producing text, speech, and video indistinguishable from a real person's, he has argued publicly that biometric verification of a kind Worldcoin offers may become one of the few durable answers to a social internet increasingly populated by convincing, autonomous, non-human accounts — an argument with the notable, slightly uncomfortable feature that the same company building the tools that erode older forms of verification is also among those positioned to sell the replacement.

The project's central artifact is the Orb, a chrome sphere about the size of a bowling ball fitted with infrared cameras and depth sensors, designed to scan a person's iris and convert the unique pattern into an encrypted code — an IrisHash — checked against a global registry to confirm the same person has not already registered. A successful scan issues a World ID, a credential that can later be used, through zero-knowledge cryptographic proofs, to demonstrate that its holder is a unique verified human without revealing which specific human it is. World ID launched publicly in July 2023, with plans to deploy orbs in more than thirty-five cities across twenty countries; the company has offered signups roughly fifty dollars in its associated cryptocurrency as an incentive, and by the time the project had scanned several million people, it remained at only a small fraction of its stated ambition of eventually verifying a billion humans worldwide.

The reasoning behind building a company specifically to scan human eyeballs becomes more legible once it's placed against the CAPTCHA problem and the bad-bot statistics above, and against the economics chapter earlier in this book. If AI models can now convincingly pass as human across nearly every text-based, voice-based, and increasingly video-based interaction the internet has traditionally relied on to sort people from machines, then the only remaining reliable signal is something tied irreducibly to a physical human body, checked once against a registry that prevents the same body from generating multiple identities. That is exactly the kind of costly, hard-to-fake signal Zahavi's peacocks and Spence's job-market signals depend on: an iris pattern is expensive, in the relevant sense, for an AI system or a bot farm to fake or duplicate at scale, in a way that a password, an email address, or even a phone number verification no longer reliably is.

It is worth pausing on the specific bot-and-fraud-prevention justification China's regulators have themselves offered for these requirements over the two decades of their expansion, because it is functionally identical to the justification Sam Altman, the personhood-credentials researchers, and Worldcoin's own marketing all offer for their considerably more privacy-conscious alternatives. Chinese officials introducing successive rounds of real-name requirements have repeatedly cited exactly the same underlying problems this book's chapters on CAPTCHA and bad-bot traffic document: rampant fraud, anonymous harassment, and an inability to hold anonymous bad actors accountable for online conduct. The justification is not manufactured or cynical window dressing; anonymous fraud, harassment, and bot manipulation are real problems in China's online ecosystem exactly as they are everywhere else this book has examined. What differs is not the diagnosis but the proposed cure's design: where the personhood-credentials researchers explicitly built their proposal around resisting centralization, and where Worldcoin's zero-knowledge architecture at least attempts a technical separation between verification and identification, China's real-name system was designed from the outset to make identification and verification the same act, collapsing exactly the distinction the more privacy-conscious Western proposals treat as their central design requirement.

China offers a third model, one with a considerably longer institutional history than either Worldcoin's or the European Union's, and one worth examining precisely because it shows what a mandatory, state-enforced version of personhood verification looks like once it has had two decades to develop. Chinese regulators began requiring real-name registration at internet cafes as early as 2003, extended the requirement to university bulletin boards and websites by 2005, made it mandatory for purchasing a mobile phone SIM card by 2013, and formalized a comprehensive real-name verification requirement for essentially all domestic internet services under the 2016 Cybersecurity Law, tying a person's online accounts directly to a government-issued identity document in a way neither Worldcoin's cryptographic anonymity layer nor the European Union's more recent, more privacy-conscious wallet framework attempts to replicate. Beijing extended the model further in 2025 with a national online identity authentication system, issuing citizens a unified "internet certificate" usable to verify identity across services without each individual platform separately holding the underlying personal data — a design that, on its face, addresses one specific criticism this book's later chapter on verification's darker side raises about fragmented private data collection, while doing nothing to address the more fundamental concern about a single government authority holding the master key to who counts as a verified person within its borders. China's system demonstrates, in a live, decades-long field trial, that a mandatory, universal, state-run personhood-verification regime is technically achievable at national scale. Whether it is achievable without the surveillance and censorship capabilities Chinese authorities have openly built on top of the same real-name infrastructure is a separate question this book's later chapters take up directly, and the Chinese case, more than Worldcoin's or the European Union's, previews exactly where those two goals — universal verification and individual liberty — tend to come into the sharpest tension.

Worldcoin is not the only entity racing to build this kind of infrastructure, and the competition matters because it previews an argument this book returns to in a later chapter about who ends up controlling the systems that decide who counts as a verified human. Several venture-backed competitors have pursued similar biometric personhood credentials using palm scans, facial recognition, or other physical markers rather than irises, generally chasing the same underlying business model: incentivize enough people to register early, build a large enough verified user base, and sell access to that verification layer to platforms, banks, and other businesses that need to distinguish real users from bots and fraudulent accounts. Governments have pursued a parallel but institutionally distinct approach, treating digital identity as a public good to be issued by the state rather than a service to be sold by a private company. The European Union's eIDAS 2.0 regulation, which entered into force in 2024, requires every member state to make a government-backed European Digital Identity Wallet available to its citizens by the end of 2026, with large private platforms, banks, and telecom providers required to accept it as valid identification within roughly a year after that. The EU wallet approach and the Worldcoin approach both aim at a version of the same underlying problem — proving a real, unique person stands behind a given digital interaction — but they diverge sharply on the question of who should hold the resulting power: a company built for the purpose and funded by venture capital and a cryptocurrency token, or a national government operating under existing data protection law and democratic accountability, each with its own distinct set of risks that later chapters in this book examine directly.

The academic and policy conversation converged on similar territory from a more cautious angle than either the commercial or governmental approach has generally taken. In 2024, a group of more than thirty researchers from institutions including OpenAI, Microsoft, MIT, and Harvard published a paper introducing the concept of personhood credentials — a broader category than any single company's product, describing privacy-preserving tools that could let someone prove they are a unique human without necessarily surrendering biometric data to any single company's database. The paper's authors treated the underlying need as increasingly urgent rather than speculative, while remaining notably wary of any single centralized solution, biometric or otherwise, becoming a chokepoint controlled by one company or government. Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin had raised closely related concerns a year earlier in a widely read essay examining various approaches to what he called biometric proof of personhood, weighing the tradeoffs between convenience, privacy, and the risk of building a single point of failure — or a single point of control — into the infrastructure of online identity itself.

Worldcoin's own zero-knowledge cryptographic design is worth explaining in slightly more technical detail, because it represents a genuine, non-trivial attempt to address exactly the concentration-of-data worry this book keeps returning to, even if the attempt turns out to be only partial. A zero-knowledge proof lets a person demonstrate that a specific statement is true — in this case, "I hold a valid, unique World ID" — without revealing any of the underlying information that makes the statement true, such as the specific iris pattern the ID was issued against. In principle, this means a platform checking whether a user is a verified unique human never needs to see or store the user's biometric data at all; it only needs to verify the cryptographic proof, which reveals nothing else about the person behind it. The limitation this book's later chapter presses hardest is not a flaw in the zero-knowledge proof itself, which functions largely as advertised, but in what happens one step earlier, at the moment of initial enrollment: someone still has to scan the iris in the first place, and that initial scan, along with whatever raw biometric data the company retains from it, sits outside the zero-knowledge system's protections entirely, governed only by whatever data-retention policy the company issuing the credential chooses to adopt and whatever regulators are willing and able to enforce against it.

That tension between usefulness and control is not an abstract academic worry. A credential capable of proving, cheaply and reliably, that its holder is one unique human being is also a credential capable of excluding anyone who cannot or will not obtain one, and a credential built around a centrally maintained biometric registry concentrates an enormous amount of sensitive information and infrastructural power in whatever entity maintains that registry. Those are not hypothetical downstream risks to be waved away as somebody else's problem for later. They are the direct, structural cost of the very mechanism that makes personhood credentials valuable in the first place, and they deserve their own extended treatment, which a later chapter in this book gives them in full, once the commercial landscape building around all of this verification infrastructure has been laid out.

For now, the point worth sitting with is how quickly the internet's oldest, most taken-for-granted test of humanity became obsolete. CAPTCHA worked for two decades because distinguishing a human from a machine used to be a genuinely hard computational problem, one that fell naturally out of the gap between what people could easily do and what software could not. Multimodal AI closed that gap in the space of a few years, and in doing so, forced an entire category of security infrastructure — quietly embedded in login pages, comment sections, ticket sales, and account registration forms across the internet — back to the drawing board. What has replaced it, imperfectly and controversially, and pursued simultaneously by cryptocurrency-funded startups, national governments, and a coalition of cautious academic researchers, is not a smarter puzzle but a return to something much older and much more physical: the stubborn, expensive, singular fact of having one body, one iris, one face, that no other body can perfectly replicate, at least not yet.
