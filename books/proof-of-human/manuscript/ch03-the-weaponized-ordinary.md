# The Weaponized Ordinary

In January 2024, a four-minute audio clip began circulating among parents and students connected to Pikesville High School, a public school in Baltimore County, Maryland. The voice on the recording, which listeners took to be that of the school's principal, Eric Eiswert, made disparaging and racist remarks about Black students and complained bitterly about the surrounding Jewish community. It was the kind of recording that ends careers on contact, and it did exactly that in the short term: Eiswert was placed on administrative leave amid a storm of public outrage, condemnation from local and national commentators, and threats serious enough that he required protection. The clip spread through group chats and social media faster than any official statement could catch up to it.

It was fake. The voice was not Eiswert's. It had been generated using artificial intelligence and, according to Baltimore County Police, engineered by a colleague with a specific grievance to settle: Dazhon Darien, the school's athletic director, who Eiswert had been investigating for allegedly misusing school funds and stealing money intended for the athletics program. Darien, police said, built the fake recording in retaliation, timed to inflict maximum damage on the man building a case against him.

The forgery did not hold up to expert scrutiny. Hany Farid, a computer science professor at the University of California, Berkeley, and one of the more prominent researchers working on digital media forensics, examined the recording and concluded it had been synthetically generated; the FBI reached a similar conclusion. But expert forensic analysis takes time, and reputational damage does not wait for it. By the time the audio had been debunked to the satisfaction of investigators, it had already been shared, discussed, and in some cases believed by a wide swath of the very community whose trust Eiswert needed to keep doing his job. Darien was ultimately identified through more conventional police work — he was arrested boarding a flight out of Baltimore-Washington airport on an unrelated warrant — and later took a plea deal, receiving four months in prison after entering an Alford plea on a single charge related to disrupting school operations. Eiswert, cleared of wrongdoing, moved on to a principal position at a different Baltimore County school the following school year, an outcome that reads as a relatively fortunate ending only because so many similarly targeted people never get their names fully cleared in the eyes of everyone who heard the fake the first time.

The significance of the Pikesville case has less to do with its outcome than with what it demonstrates about scale. Every fraud examined in this book's opening chapter — Arup's $25 million video call, the attempt on WPP's chief executive — required an attacker with a plausible reason to believe a large payoff was available: a multinational engineering firm, a global advertising conglomerate. Those are targets that justify investment in a sophisticated, multi-person, real-time deepfake video call. Dazhon Darien did not need anything close to that level of investment. By the accounts that emerged from the investigation, a single fabricated audio clip, built from a comparatively modest amount of publicly available speech, was enough to nearly destroy one man's career and inflame an entire community, deployed not by a criminal syndicate or a hostile intelligence service but by a coworker settling a personal score over an embezzlement investigation.

That is the shift worth naming directly. The technology that makes a $25 million corporate fraud possible is, by now, largely the same technology available to anyone with a grudge, an internet connection, and a target whose voice exists somewhere in searchable audio or video — a school board meeting recording, a local news interview, a voicemail greeting, a video posted to a community Facebook group. The corporate fraud chapter of this story gets more headlines because the dollar figures are larger and more legible to a business audience. The interpersonal fraud story is arguably more unsettling, because it removes the assumption that a victim needs to be valuable enough, in a purely financial sense, to be worth targeting. Eric Eiswert was not defrauded of money. He was nearly defrauded of his reputation, his livelihood, and his standing in a community he had spent years serving, by someone he worked alongside every day, using a tool that required no special access, no elite technical skill, and no significant budget.

This is where the liar's dividend from this book's opening chapter compounds into something crueler at the interpersonal scale. A public figure caught by a genuine recording can at least attempt to invoke the dividend — claim the recording is fake, and hope enough doubt survives to blunt the consequence. A private figure framed by a genuine fake has almost no equivalent recourse in the moment the fake is doing its damage. Eiswert did not have a communications team, a legal department, or a public relations firm capable of immediately countering a viral audio clip with expert analysis. He had, for the several days before Farid and the FBI weighed in, almost nothing but his own word against a recording that sounded, to the untrained ear, exactly like him. The forensic tools that eventually cleared him exist and work, but they operate on a timescale that is fundamentally mismatched to how quickly a piece of synthetic media can spread and inflict harm. A conclusion that takes a well-resourced digital forensics lab several days to reach can be irrelevant to the outcome by the time it arrives, because the school board meeting has already happened, the threats have already been made, and the story has already moved on to the next controversy.

A different kind of ordinary-scale weaponization, aimed not at a single person's reputation but at financial markets broadly, played out a few months before the Pikesville recording surfaced. On the morning of May 22, 2023, an image began circulating on Twitter showing a plume of black smoke rising near what appeared to be the Pentagon, accompanied by claims of a large explosion. The image was shared by an account calling itself "Bloomberg Feed," styled to resemble the real news organization and carrying one of the platform's paid blue checkmarks — the same hollowed-out verification signal examined later in this book — alongside reposts from other blue-checked accounts, including the real, official account of the Kremlin-linked broadcaster RT. Within minutes, and before anyone had confirmed the image was fake, the S&P 500 dipped roughly three-tenths of a percent and the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about eighty points, a real, measurable financial market reaction to an event that had never happened, driven by algorithmic and human traders responding to what looked like breaking news from a seemingly credible source. The market recovered within minutes once the Pentagon's own fire department and other officials confirmed no explosion had occurred, and forensic analysts, including Hany Farid, the same Berkeley researcher who would later help debunk the Pikesville recording, quickly identified visual inconsistencies in the image's architecture and fencing consistent with AI generation rather than a real photograph.

It is worth noting how quickly the correction actually arrived, because the Pentagon case is one of the few in this book where the gap between fabrication and correction closed within minutes rather than days. The Pentagon's own fire department and local authorities were positioned to issue an immediate, authoritative, first-party denial — a fire department either has responded to an explosion at its own building or it has not, and it can say so within moments — a speed of authoritative correction the Pikesville case, dependent on a slower forensic audio analysis, and the Slovak election case, constrained by a legally mandated media silence period, could not match. The market's rapid recovery once that denial reached traders is itself informative: when a credible, fast, first-party source of ground truth exists and can be reached quickly, even a financial market moving on algorithmic momentum can correct itself within minutes. The cases that do lasting damage throughout this book are disproportionately the ones where no comparably fast, authoritative correction was available — where the truth existed but took days to surface, by which point the fabrication had already done its work.

The Pentagon image is worth placing alongside Pikesville because it demonstrates the same underlying vulnerability operating on a completely different target: not a person's reputation, but the trust infrastructure of financial markets themselves, which depend on breaking news reaching traders through channels assumed to be reasonably reliable. A single fabricated image, posted by an account wearing a corrupted version of a verification badge that was supposed to mean something, was enough to move a multi-trillion-dollar index for several minutes, a demonstration that the liar's dividend and its inverse — a fake believed as real — can move markets as easily as they can move a school community's judgment of its principal.

It is worth pausing on why audio, specifically, proved to be such an effective weapon in this case, a pattern that recurs across nearly every incident this book examines involving a private individual rather than a company or a government. Video deepfakes, even good ones, tend to carry visual artifacts that a suspicious viewer might notice: an unnatural blink rate, a strange interplay of light and shadow, a mouth that doesn't quite match the words. Audio has no equivalent tell for most listeners, because human beings are simply less practiced at forensically evaluating sound than at evaluating sight. We watch faces for a living, in the sense that human social cognition has spent an evolutionary lifetime getting good at reading them; we do not have an equivalent, finely tuned instinct for detecting the subtle seams in synthesized speech. A voice clip also asks less of its target platform. It does not need to be watched in a specific format on a specific device to land with full force; it can be forwarded as a plain audio file, played over a phone speaker at a dinner table, or embedded in a text message, and it will sound, to most people in most contexts, exactly as real as a genuine recording would.

None of this means video is safe, only that audio has so far offered attackers a better ratio of damage to effort, a calculation that will keep shifting as video generation tools continue to improve and as detection tools for both media types continue their own uneven progress. What the Pikesville case demonstrates most clearly is that the verification problem this book is building toward is not exclusively, or even primarily, a problem for large institutions with fraud-detection budgets. It is a problem for an ordinary school district, an ordinary workplace conflict, an ordinary community rumor mill — the kind of setting that has no dedicated security team, no incident-response plan, and no expectation that it would ever need one. The corporate fraud and the state-level election interference examined elsewhere in this book get the bulk of the policy attention, because they are legible in dollar figures and electoral outcomes. The version of this problem playing out in ordinary workplaces and school communities may ultimately be the more common one, precisely because it requires so little to execute and offers no institutional buffer to the person it targets.

There is a second, quieter lesson buried in how the case was actually resolved, and it previews an argument this book returns to repeatedly. What ultimately cleared Eric Eiswert's name was not a new piece of consumer verification technology, a watermark, or a content-provenance standard — none of that infrastructure existed in a form that could have helped him in January 2024, and much of it remains unevenly deployed today. What cleared him was old-fashioned forensic expertise, applied by a researcher with the specific technical training to examine the file and identify the signatures of synthetic generation, combined with conventional police investigative work that established motive and traced the fabrication back to its likely source. The infrastructure chapters later in this book describe an emerging set of tools meant to make this kind of after-the-fact verification faster, more accessible, and less dependent on finding the right expert on short notice. Whether that infrastructure reaches an ordinary school district before the next Dazhon Darien reaches for the next available voice-cloning tool is, at the time of this writing, an open question rather than a settled one.
