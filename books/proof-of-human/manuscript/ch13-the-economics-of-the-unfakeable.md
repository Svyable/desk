# The Economics of the Unfakeable

Every institution examined in this book's opening chapters, in one way or another, is grappling with the same underlying economic puzzle, so it is worth pausing here, before the biology and the game theory, to state that puzzle in its plainest possible terms. Trust between strangers has always required some way to make a lie more expensive than the truth for the person telling it. When that extra cost disappears, honesty stops being the cheaper option, and an entire edifice of institutions built on the assumption that lying costs more than telling the truth has to either find a new source of cost or watch its own credibility quietly hollow out. Three fields that rarely speak to one another — evolutionary biology, labor economics, and game theory — converged on essentially the same answer to this puzzle independently, which is itself a reason to take the answer seriously rather than treating it as one field's idiosyncratic theory.

A peacock's tail is, from an engineering standpoint, a bad decision. It is heavy, metabolically expensive to grow, and makes the bird slower and more visible to predators than a plainer tail would. For a long time this was a genuine puzzle for evolutionary biology, since natural selection is supposed to favor traits that improve an organism's odds of survival, and a gaudy, cumbersome tail looks like exactly the kind of trait selection should have eliminated. In 1975, the Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi proposed an answer that was initially controversial and later became one of the more influential ideas in the study of animal signaling: the tail is not a bad decision despite its cost. It works because of its cost. A weak or unhealthy peacock cannot afford to grow and carry an enormous, extravagant tail; only a genuinely strong bird can absorb that handicap and still survive. The tail is credible precisely because it would be self-defeating for a lesser bird to fake it. Zahavi called this the handicap principle, and it explains why so much of animal signaling — the deep roar of a stag, the elaborate plumage of a bird of paradise, the antlers a bull must grow and shed every year — trends toward costly, wasteful-looking displays rather than cheap, efficient ones. A signal that costs nothing to produce is a signal anyone can produce, healthy or not, which means it carries no information at all.

Economists arrived at a strikingly similar insight from a completely different direction, working on human markets rather than animal mating displays. In 1970, George Akerlof published a paper built around the used car market, asking a simple question with an uncomfortable answer: what happens when sellers know more about the quality of what they are selling than buyers do? Akerlof's answer was that markets can fail entirely under those conditions. If buyers cannot distinguish a good used car — a "plum" — from a bad one — a "lemon" — they will rationally offer a price that reflects the average quality of what's on the market, discounted for the risk of getting a lemon. But that average price is unattractive to anyone actually holding a plum, since they know their car is worth more than the market is willing to pay for an unknown quantity. Plum owners exit the market rather than sell at a lemon-adjusted price, which lowers the average quality of cars actually available, which lowers the price buyers are willing to pay further still, in a spiral that can leave a market thin, distorted, or in the extreme case gone altogether. Akerlof pointed to real-world analogues beyond used cars: the reluctance of some credit markets to develop in places where a lender cannot easily verify a borrower's reliability, and the difficulty older adults have historically faced buying affordable health insurance, since insurers cannot easily distinguish the healthy elderly from the unhealthy and price accordingly.

Michael Spence, working on a related problem a few years later, asked what a plum owner — or, in his case, a genuinely capable job applicant — could do to escape that trap. His answer, developed in a 1973 paper on job market signaling, was that a costly, hard-to-fake credential could break the deadlock even if the credential itself taught the applicant nothing directly useful for the job. A university degree, in Spence's framing, does not need to make someone more productive to be valuable to an employer; it only needs to be disproportionately easier for genuinely capable people to obtain than for less capable people, so that its possession reliably correlates with the underlying quality an employer actually cares about. For the signal to work, Spence argued, it has to be costly enough — in time, money, or effort — that faking it isn't worthwhile for someone the signal is supposed to screen out, and it has to be something the employer can actually observe. Strip away either the cost or the observability, and the signal collapses into noise, exactly the way an inexpensive tail would collapse a peacock's mating display into meaninglessness.

Cheap talk theory helps explain a puzzle this book's earlier chapters raise without quite naming: why a fabricated video call, a cloned voice, or a synthetic recording so often works even on people who are, in the abstract, perfectly aware that such fabrications exist. The Arup employee almost certainly knew, in some general sense, that deepfakes were technologically possible; general awareness of a technology's existence does not by itself change the incentive structure of any specific interaction. What made the fraud work was not ignorance of the technology but the specific interaction's apparent alignment of interests: a colleague, on a familiar video call, asking for something urgent but plausible, in a context where the ordinary, honest version of that same request happens routinely. Cheap talk theory predicts precisely this vulnerability. A request that would trigger real suspicion coming from a stranger with obviously misaligned interests can pass through, essentially as cheap talk, when it appears to come from someone whose interests the target has learned, over years of normal interaction, to treat as aligned with their own — which is exactly the trust generative fabrication now knows how to counterfeit.

A separate line of economic research, developed by Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel in a 1982 paper on strategic information transmission, makes the same point from the opposite direction by examining what happens when a signal costs nothing at all. Crawford and Sobel modeled "cheap talk" — communication that carries no inherent cost to send, regardless of whether it is true — and showed that its informativeness depends entirely on how closely the sender's interests align with the receiver's. When interests align well, cheap talk can convey real information, because the sender has no reason to lie. When interests diverge, as they typically do between a con artist and a mark, or between a fraudster on a video call and the employee they are trying to defraud, cheap talk collapses toward uselessness, because nothing stops a low-quality sender from mimicking a high-quality one. Costly signals matter precisely in the situations where cheap talk fails: exactly the adversarial situations — a stranger's video call, an unfamiliar company's financial statement, a job applicant's résumé — in which the sender has an obvious incentive to claim more than is true, and in which words alone, however sincere-sounding, cannot be trusted to convey it.

Human courtship rituals supply an almost too-perfect illustration of costly signaling operating in a domain most people never think to analyze this way. An engagement ring is, functionally, a peacock's tail rendered in carbon: an expensive, impractical, difficult-to-fake commitment to a single choice, whose cost is precisely what makes the underlying declaration credible. That the specific size and cost of a diamond ring became culturally mandatory in America is itself a manufactured costly signal rather than a natural one — the De Beers cartel's mid-twentieth-century advertising campaigns, built around the slogan "a diamond is forever," are widely credited with inventing the now-unquestioned convention that an engagement ring should cost roughly a month's or two months' salary, turning what could have been a cheap talk gesture into an artificially expensive one specifically because an artificially expensive gesture is harder to fake and therefore more informative about the seriousness of the person making it. The example matters here because it shows that costly signals are not always found in nature or discovered by economists; sometimes they are manufactured deliberately by an interested party, which raises its own question — worth holding onto for later chapters on biometric verification and content provenance — about who benefits when a new costly signal gets built, and whether the entity building it is disinterested or has its own reasons for wanting a particular signal to become the new standard.

A closely related refinement to Spence's original signaling model, developed by economists Nick Feltovich, Rick Harbaugh, and Ted To in a 2002 RAND Journal of Economics paper, adds a wrinkle worth naming because it complicates the simple story that more signaling is always better. Their work on what economists call countersignaling shows that in some markets, the very highest-quality participants deliberately decline to send a costly signal at all, precisely because sending it would put them in the same visible category as merely above-average participants who need the signal to distinguish themselves from low-quality ones. A sufficiently established expert sometimes gains more credibility by pointedly not bothering to display a credential than by displaying it, because the act of visibly working to prove one's quality can itself imply uncertainty about whether that quality would otherwise be recognized. This has an intriguing, if speculative, echo in this book's chapters on verification infrastructure: a public figure or an institution secure enough in its own established reputation may find that visibly adopting every new authentication badge, watermark, or personhood credential signals anxiety about being doubted, while a genuinely trusted source can sometimes afford to let its accumulated reputation do the work instead — a luxury unavailable, notably, to exactly the newer, less established, or historically marginalized voices this book's later chapters worry may be excluded by costly verification requirements in the first place.

Four very different fields — evolutionary biology, labor economics, game theory, and the social history of courtship — converge on the same structural idea, which is worth stating plainly because it is the load-bearing concept for everything else in this book. A signal is only informative if it is more expensive, in some relevant currency, for a low-quality sender to produce than for a high-quality sender to produce. It does not need to be expensive in an absolute sense. It needs to be differentially expensive — cheap enough for the genuine article to bother with, costly enough that faking it isn't worth it for whoever the signal is meant to filter out.

This is precisely the mechanism that generative AI attacks, across every case this book has examined so far. A familiar face on a video call used to be differentially expensive to fake: forging one convincingly required real skill and real effort, which meant only a determined, resourced adversary would bother, and most video calls could be trusted on the reasonable assumption that the juice wasn't worth the squeeze for casual fraud. That assumption is exactly what the Arup fraud from the opening chapter exploited once it stopped holding. A recognizable voice on the phone worked the same way, until it was cheap enough for a disgruntled athletic director to fake a principal's voice, or for a single political consultant to fake a sitting president's. A financial audit worked the same way in principle, though the chapters on Wirecard, Enron, and FTX showed that the differential cost of corrupting an audit had already fallen further than most people appreciated, well before any of this involved a neural network — sometimes because incentives eroded the auditor's willingness to look closely, and sometimes, as with FTX's auditor, because the checking institution was never competent enough to make looking closely worth anything. In every case, the value of the signal depended on a gap between how expensive it was to produce honestly and how expensive it was to fake, and in every case that gap has been narrowing — sometimes because verification quietly got cheaper to corrupt from the inside, and sometimes, as with deepfakes and voice cloning, because fabrication got dramatically cheaper from the outside.

The peacock's tail survives as a reliable signal for a straightforward reason: nothing in a peahen's environment is developing a technology that lets a weak, unhealthy peacock cheaply grow a convincing fake tail. Biological signals evolve alongside biological cheats, but the arms race moves at the pace of generations, giving evolution time to find new, harder-to-fake signals when old ones get compromised. Human trust signals do not have that luxury. A signature that took a forger weeks to imitate convincingly in 1990 can, with the right tools, be approximated by a machine learning model in seconds today, and the underlying signal — a signature on a document — has not had generations to evolve a costlier, more robust replacement. It has had, in some cases, months.

This is the useful, if slightly uncomfortable, lens the rest of this book applies to the question of trust in an age of cheap fabrication. The question worth asking about any verification mechanism — a watermark, a biometric scan, a content credential, a professional credential, an audit, a paid platform subscription marketed as identity verification — is not simply whether it works today, but whether the cost of faking it is holding steady, rising, or falling relative to the cost of producing it honestly. A mechanism whose fabrication cost is falling faster than its verification cost is a mechanism on borrowed time, no matter how official it looks or how long it has been trusted. A password is a weak signal because copying it costs almost nothing once it is known. A costly biometric scan tied to a single physical body is a stronger signal, in the narrow sense that duplicating a body is much harder than copying a string of characters, though as later chapters will show, "harder" is not the same as "impossible," and the institutions built around biometric verification carry their own costs, some of them uncomfortable in different ways than a stolen password.

What the handicap principle, the market for lemons, job market signaling, and cheap talk theory all suggest, taken together, is that the search for trustworthy verification in an age of artificial intelligence is not really a search for new technology so much as a search for new sources of differential cost — some property of a genuine person, a genuine document, or a genuine transaction that remains meaningfully harder to fake than to produce honestly, even after the easy fakes have been automated. And because, as the diamond ring example shows, differential cost can be manufactured as easily as it can be discovered, the search also has to ask a second question alongside the first: who is doing the manufacturing, and whose interests does the resulting signal actually serve? The next several chapters look at the specific mechanisms currently being built to try to reestablish that gap: cryptographic content provenance, biometric personhood verification, and the commercial industry rapidly assembling itself around the simple, suddenly valuable claim that a given face, voice, document, or transaction is exactly what it appears to be — along with the limits, and in some cases the self-interested motives, built into each of those solutions.
