# Cracking the Lock

Within a week of Apple releasing the iPhone X in November 2017, the Vietnamese security firm Bkav announced it had defeated Face ID, the phone's flagship facial-recognition unlocking system, using a mask built from a combination of 3D-printed plastic, silicone, makeup, and carefully placed paper cutouts, assembled for a reported cost of around $150. Bkav's researchers were candid about their method: rather than brute-forcing their way past the system, they studied how Apple's own machine-learning model was likely to weigh different facial features and built a mask specifically engineered to satisfy those weightings, focusing particular attention on the skin-like texture around the cheeks and eye sockets where they judged the system's confidence threshold to be most exploitable. Apple disputed the practical significance of the demonstration, noting reasonably that Face ID was designed to resist casual attackers rather than a well-resourced security research team building a custom mask specifically calibrated to one phone, and pointed out that the demonstration required extensive prior access to detailed scans of the specific victim's face — a meaningful caveat, though not one that changes the underlying fact that a biometric system marketed as a uniquely secure, un-fakeable replacement for a passcode had been defeated within days of its public release.

Voice, the biometric signal this book's opening chapters have already shown to be dangerously easy to synthesize, turns out to be just as easy to weaponize against the banks that adopted it as a security feature rather than merely a fraud vector for scammers impersonating strangers. In February 2023, the journalist Joseph Cox set out to test a specific, unsettling question: could he break into his own bank account using nothing but a cloned version of his own voice? Cox recorded roughly five minutes of his own speech, ran it through ElevenLabs' voice-cloning tool, and called Lloyds Bank's automated voice-authentication line — a system the bank marketed under the tagline "my voice is my password," explicitly promoting the idea that a customer's vocal signature was a secure, convenient replacement for a traditional PIN or security question. After a handful of attempts, the cloned voice, reciting the bank's own required passphrase, was accepted as genuine, granting access to Cox's account balance and recent transactions. The experiment prompted Ohio Senator Sherrod Brown to send formal inquiries to six major American banks offering similar voice-authentication services, asking directly what safeguards, if any, they had in place against exactly the attack Cox had just demonstrated against a British bank using a five-dollar-a-month consumer subscription and five minutes of publicly available audio of his own voice.

The Lloyds case matters for this book's argument because it shows a costly signal collapsing from the inside of an institution that had explicitly, publicly marketed it as a security upgrade rather than a legacy convenience nobody had gotten around to replacing yet. A traditional PIN or password is a weak signal, as this book's economics chapter noted, precisely because it can be copied at zero cost once known. Banks adopted voice authentication specifically because a voiceprint seemed to solve that problem, tying access to a biological signal that, before 2023, had been genuinely difficult for anyone to replicate convincingly without extensive access to a target's recorded speech. ElevenLabs' consumer product removed exactly that difficulty, for the price of a subscription any customer, honest or otherwise, could afford. Several of the banks that received Senator Brown's inquiry defended their systems by pointing to additional fraud-detection layers operating behind the voice check itself, a reasonable technical point that nonetheless concedes the underlying argument: the voiceprint alone, marketed as sufficient security in its own right, was not.

Fingerprint authentication, the biometric standard Face ID was itself partly displacing, has an even longer and more thoroughly documented history of exactly this kind of defeat. Security researchers have demonstrated fingerprint spoofing using techniques ranging from lifted latent prints reconstructed in gelatin, to high-resolution photographs of a target's fingers processed through image-editing software and printed onto conductive material, to, in a demonstration presented at the DEFCON security conference in 2020, prints produced on a consumer-grade ultraviolet resin 3D printer for a reported cost of about ten dollars in materials. The specific techniques have evolved over two decades of this research, but the underlying lesson has remained remarkably stable across every iteration: a fingerprint, unlike a password, cannot be changed once compromised, and a sufficiently motivated attacker with access to a clear image of the target's print — lifted from a glass, photographed from a distance, or in some documented cases obtained from a print left on the very phone being attacked — can often reproduce it well enough to fool a consumer sensor.

These findings deserve to sit uncomfortably alongside this book's earlier, more optimistic-sounding chapter on proof of personhood, which examined biometric verification, including iris-scanning systems like Worldcoin's Orb, as a potentially durable answer to AI's erosion of every other verification signal this book has examined. That chapter's argument was not wrong, exactly, but it was importantly incomplete without the evidence in this chapter. Biometric signals are harder to fake than a password specifically because reproducing a body part convincingly requires physical materials, specialized tools, and often direct or photographic access to the target that a stolen password does not require — the differential-cost logic this book has returned to throughout. But "harder to fake" is not the same claim as "impossible to fake," and the specific attacks documented against Face ID and fingerprint sensors show that the gap between those two claims has narrowed considerably faster, for consumer-grade biometric hardware, than most users assume when a phone manufacturer markets a feature using language like "your face is your password."

The pattern across Face ID, consumer fingerprint sensors, and Lloyds Bank's voiceprint system shares a specific structural cause worth naming directly, because it recurs across nearly every biometric deployment examined in this book: the verification system's designers were optimizing against the attackers they could imagine at the time of deployment, and each subsequent attack succeeded specifically by introducing a capability the original designers had not weighted heavily enough, or had not anticipated becoming cheaply available to an ordinary attacker at all. Apple's Face ID team built defenses against flat photographs and simple two-dimensional spoofing, reasonably assuming a three-dimensional mask represented a much higher bar for an attacker to clear; Bkav's team cleared that bar within a week using consumer-grade 3D printing that had only recently become cheap enough for a small security firm to use for this exact purpose. Lloyds Bank's voice-ID system was almost certainly built and tested against the voice-cloning technology available when the feature launched, which required more data, more processing time, and more technical sophistication than the ElevenLabs consumer product that ultimately defeated it just a few years later. In every case, the gap between "secure against currently available attacks" and "secure against attacks that will be cheaply available within a few years" turned out to be far narrower than the institutions deploying these systems seem to have planned for, a pattern this book's earlier chapter on watermarking's limits already identified in a different technical context and that recurs here in yet another: security architects tend to benchmark a new verification technology against the previous generation's attack tools, not against the next generation's, and generative AI has made the gap between those two generations shrink faster than most institutional planning cycles are built to accommodate.

There is a meaningful difference in degree, if not in kind, between spoofing a consumer smartphone's fingerprint sensor with a ten-dollar 3D-printed mold and spoofing the considerably more sophisticated infrared depth-sensing and liveness-detection hardware built into a system like Worldcoin's Orb, which was engineered specifically with the knowledge that biometric spoofing techniques already existed and with countermeasures — depth sensing to detect a flat photograph or a two-dimensional mask, liveness checks to confirm a genuine pulse or eye movement — built in from the outset rather than added reactively after a security researcher's embarrassing public demonstration. Whether those countermeasures hold up over the coming years against attackers now armed with the same generative AI tools examined throughout this book — which can, at least in principle, be trained specifically to defeat a known liveness-detection algorithm the same way the watermark-removal techniques examined earlier in this book were trained specifically to defeat known watermarking schemes — is an open question rather than a settled one, and it is precisely the kind of question this book's economics chapter would insist on asking of any verification mechanism, however sophisticated it currently appears: not whether it works today, but whether the cost of defeating it is rising or falling relative to the cost of legitimately using it.

This is precisely why the North Korean IT worker scheme examined earlier in this book, which relies on live, real-time deepfake video rather than a pre-recorded fabrication, represents a meaningfully harder problem for liveness detection than a static mask or photograph does. A liveness check designed to catch a flat image or a rigid mask is specifically looking for the absence of natural movement and depth; a live deepfake, generated frame by frame in response to an actual moving, blinking, speaking human operator sitting in front of a camera somewhere, already contains exactly the kind of dynamic, three-dimensional-seeming motion a liveness system is designed to reward as evidence of genuine presence, because there is, in a narrow technical sense, a genuine live human being on the other end of the call — just not the one the deepfake is depicting. Liveness detection built around detecting a static spoof therefore does very little against an adversary who has moved past static spoofing entirely and is instead manipulating a genuinely live video feed in real time, which is exactly the frontier this book's remote-worker chapter shows criminal and state-linked actors already operating on.

Liveness detection — the specific technical countermeasure meant to distinguish a real, present human body from a photograph, a mask, or a recording — deserves a closer look precisely because it is the feature every biometric system examined in this book, from Face ID to Worldcoin's Orb, ultimately depends on to defeat exactly the attacks this chapter has documented. A liveness check typically looks for signs a static image or mask cannot easily produce: subtle involuntary movements, blood-flow patterns visible under infrared light, the natural three-dimensional parallax of a real face as it turns, or a response to an unpredictable, randomly generated challenge like blinking on cue or reading a number aloud. Each of these countermeasures raises the cost of a successful spoof, in exactly the differential-cost sense this book's economics chapter describes, by requiring an attacker to defeat not merely a static image match but a dynamic, hard-to-predict behavioral signal as well. But liveness detection is itself a machine-learning system, trained on examples of real human presence and known spoofing attempts, which means it inherits the same vulnerability every other detector examined in this book's chapter on watermarking's limits does: a sufficiently well-resourced adversary who can study how the liveness model makes its decisions can, in principle, train a countermeasure specifically calibrated to satisfy it, the same adversarial dynamic that let the University of Maryland researchers defeat image watermarks and that Bkav's team used against Face ID's own model of a human face.

The honest lesson from two decades of biometric spoofing research, applied to this book's broader argument, is not that biometric verification is worthless, any more than the Getty Images watermark's vulnerability to duplication meant watermarking was worthless, or Turnitin's false-positive rate meant AI-detection tools in education were worthless. It is that biometric signals, like every other costly signal this book has examined, sit on a spectrum of differential cost rather than occupying some separate, permanently unfakeable category simply because they involve a physical body rather than a document or a password. A body is harder to fake than a signature, considerably harder than a password, and, for now, meaningfully harder than the deepfaked video calls and cloned voices examined throughout this book's opening chapters. Harder is not the same as impossible, and a verification system built on the unexamined assumption that a biometric signal is permanently, uniquely secure is repeating exactly the mistake this book has documented across auditors, ratings agencies, peer reviewers, and watermarking schemes alike: mistaking a signal's current cost of defeat for a permanent, structural guarantee, rather than the temporary, contestable advantage it has always actually been.
