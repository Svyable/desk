# Proof of Work for Reality

For most of the twentieth century, a photograph carried a kind of default credibility that had nothing to do with anyone examining it closely. The assumption was structural: producing a convincing fake photograph took a darkroom, real skill, and real time, so most photographs in circulation were probably close to what they appeared to be, if only because faking them wasn't worth the trouble for most of the people who might have wanted to. That default collapsed gradually with the arrival of consumer photo editing software, and it has now collapsed close to completely with generative image and video models capable of producing a convincing fabrication from a text description in seconds. The response several major technology and media companies converged on was not to try to make fabrication harder — an arms race against ever-improving generative models is a race verification is unlikely to win outright — but to make the provenance of genuine content easier to prove, shifting the burden from "prove this is fake" to "show where this actually came from."

That shift is the organizing idea behind the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, known as C2PA, which formed in February 2021 out of a merger between Adobe's Content Authenticity Initiative and a project called Origin, led by Microsoft and the BBC. The founding members — Adobe, Arm, the BBC, Intel, Microsoft, and Truepic — were an unusual mix of a software company whose tools are used to edit images, a hardware company whose chips could embed provenance information at the point of capture, a broadcaster with an obvious institutional stake in trustworthy news footage, and a startup built specifically around image authentication. The coalition operates under the Joint Development Foundation, a nonprofit housed within the Linux Foundation, and its technical standard works by attaching a cryptographically signed manifest to a piece of media — a record of what created it, what software touched it, and what edits were made along the way — which the group brands publicly as Content Credentials. Adobe released the first version of the C2PA specification in January 2022, and the steering committee has since grown to include Amazon, Google, Meta, OpenAI, and Sony, a genuinely unusual coalition of rivals cooperating on the argument that an image's history should be verifiable independent of which company's tools produced it.

Google took a related but technically different approach through its DeepMind research division, launching a tool called SynthID in beta in August 2023, initially for images generated through its Imagen model on Google Cloud. Rather than attaching an external record the way C2PA's manifests do, SynthID embeds a watermark directly into the pixel data of an image, imperceptible to a human viewer but detectable by software even after common alterations such as cropping, resizing, or applying a filter. Google has since extended the technique to audio and continued work on text applications, developed in partnership with Google Research and the counter-disinformation group Jigsaw. The two approaches, provenance manifests and embedded watermarks, are complementary rather than competing: a manifest can be stripped by someone determined to hide an image's origin, while a watermark, if it survives common editing, persists even when the accompanying metadata does not, though a sufficiently motivated adversary can attack either approach given enough effort and the right tools.

Both approaches share a limitation that is worth stating plainly rather than glossing over, because much of the coverage of these systems in their early years tended to oversell what they could accomplish. Neither content credentials nor invisible watermarks can, by themselves, stop someone from generating a convincing fake in the first place; both are designed to make it possible to check a piece of content's history after the fact, provided the platform showing the content preserves and displays that history, and provided the viewer bothers to check. A watermark embedded by a cooperative company's own generation tool does nothing to flag content produced by a model that declines to add one, and neither system yet has anything close to universal adoption across the tools capable of producing convincing synthetic media. The infrastructure being built is aimed less at making fabrication impossible than at making authenticity provable for the content whose creators want it to be provable — a meaningfully different and more modest goal, but one that at least gives news organizations, courts, and platforms somewhere to point when a genuine recording's authenticity is challenged.

Governments have started treating the labeling half of this problem as a matter for regulation rather than voluntary industry standards alone. The European Union's AI Act includes a transparency provision, Article 50, requiring that deployers using AI systems to produce deepfakes disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated, defining a deepfake broadly as AI-produced image, audio, or video content that resembles a real person, object, place, or event closely enough that it would otherwise appear authentic. Guidance developed under the Act has been specific about what does not satisfy the requirement: a disclosure buried in terms and conditions is not sufficient, and neither is a purely technical label such as metadata or an invisible watermark that an ordinary viewer would never encounter. The recommended approach instead combines visible, plain-language notices with audible cues and persistent visual indicators, aimed at making the disclosure something an actual viewer is likely to see or hear rather than something that exists only for compliance purposes. Formal guidelines and a voluntary code of practice under this provision took effect in August 2026, giving companies operating in the European Union a concrete, if still developing, standard to meet rather than a purely aspirational one.

The commercial stakes behind all of this became explicit in a lawsuit that had nothing directly to do with deepfakes but everything to do with provenance. In January 2023, Getty Images sued Stability AI, alleging that the company had scraped more than twelve million copyrighted photographs, videos, and illustrations from Getty's library to train its Stable Diffusion image generator without permission or compensation. Part of Getty's complaint centered on an almost embarrassingly literal provenance failure: because Stable Diffusion had been trained on Getty's watermarked stock images, it would sometimes generate new images bearing a garbled but recognizable version of the Getty Images watermark itself, a synthetic image inheriting a mark that was supposed to certify it as licensed, authentic Getty content, stamped onto an entirely fabricated picture. Getty's chief executive later described the litigation as extraordinarily expensive to pursue, and the company eventually dropped its copyright infringement claims in the UK proceeding as closing arguments approached, while continuing to press trademark infringement allegations tied specifically to the watermark. Whatever the case's ultimate legal resolution, it captured something important about this whole period: the same visual mark that had spent decades signaling "this image is licensed and real" could be reproduced by a generative model with no relationship to the licensing or the reality it was meant to certify, turning a trust signal into exactly the kind of costless-to-fake symbol the previous chapter's economics would predict becomes worthless.

None of these efforts — content credentials, invisible watermarks, regulatory labeling requirements, litigation over inherited trademarks — amounts to a solved problem. Adoption remains partial, enforcement remains uneven across jurisdictions, and every technical safeguard invites a corresponding effort to defeat it, an arms race with no obvious finish line. What they represent instead is an attempt to rebuild, in cryptographic and regulatory form, the kind of institution described two chapters earlier: an independent, costly-to-corrupt mechanism that lets a stranger check a claim without personally verifying the underlying process. A C2PA manifest is, in this sense, a direct descendant of William Henry Merrill's UL mark — a small, standardized certification embedded in a product, backed by an institution with its own reputation staked on the certification meaning something. The difference is that Merrill's laboratory was checking wiring against a fixed physical standard that did not change year to year, while the coalitions building today's provenance infrastructure are trying to certify authenticity against an adversary that improves every few months. Whether that infrastructure can hold up as well as a century-old safety mark has, is the open question the rest of this book keeps circling back to.
