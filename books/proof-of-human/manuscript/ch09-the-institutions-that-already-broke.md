# The Institutions That Already Broke

Elizabeth Holmes told investors, doctors, and eventually the public that her company, Theranos, had built a machine called the Edison that could run more than two hundred different blood tests from a few drops of blood pricked from a fingertip, instead of the vials a conventional draw required. It was an appealing story for reasons that had nothing to do with whether it was true: cheaper, less painful testing promised to catch disease earlier and make routine bloodwork accessible in drugstores rather than hospitals. Board members, investors, and partners including a national pharmacy chain accepted the story on the strength of Holmes's presentation and a closely guarded set of demonstrations, without ever being permitted the kind of independent technical verification that might have surfaced the truth sooner: the Edison could reliably perform only a small fraction of the tests it was marketed as running, and for most of the rest, Theranos quietly used conventional machines purchased from other manufacturers while continuing to imply the results came from its own technology. Patients received blood test results that were, in a meaningful number of documented cases, simply wrong, leading to false diagnoses ranging from miscarriage indicators to cancer markers.

The fraud held together for years, through several funding rounds that valued the company near ten billion dollars at its peak, until a Wall Street Journal reporter named John Carreyrou received a tip in 2015 and spent months verifying it before publishing an investigation that unraveled the story from the outside, since the story had never been sound enough to unravel from within. Holmes was convicted in January 2022 on four counts of wire fraud and conspiracy related to defrauding investors, and sentenced later that year to more than eleven years in prison. The verdict is notable less for its severity than for what it took to reach it: an outside journalist doing independent reporting, because the institutions ordinarily positioned to catch exactly this kind of fraud — a board of directors, a slate of prominent investors, partnership due diligence at a national retail chain — had, for years, functioned as an audience rather than a check.

Wirecard offers a similar arc with a different cast. The German payments company had grown into one of the country's most valuable financial technology firms, a rare non-bank member of the prestigious DAX stock index, when it disclosed in June 2020 that €1.9 billion supposedly held in trustee accounts in the Philippines could not be located. Within days, the company admitted the money likely did not exist at all; the Central Bank of the Philippines confirmed the funds had never entered the country, contradicting Wirecard's initial attempt to blame two local banks. The missing sum amounted to roughly a quarter of the company's entire reported balance sheet. Wirecard's shares, which had traded near record highs only weeks earlier, collapsed by more than ninety percent, and the company filed for insolvency by the end of June. Its longtime chief executive was arrested on suspicion of falsifying accounts and market manipulation. What makes Wirecard particularly instructive is that this was not a case of an obscure private company slipping past scrutiny; it was a major public company, audited annually by a global accounting firm, whose fabricated revenues and nonexistent cash reserves survived years of financial statements before the fiction became too large to sustain.

Wirecard's auditor was not Arthur Andersen, but Andersen's own collapse two decades earlier is the reference point against which every subsequent audit failure gets measured, because it demonstrated how completely an institution built specifically to verify other institutions could fail at its one job. Andersen had audited Enron for years as the energy trading company built an increasingly elaborate structure of off-balance-sheet partnerships to hide debt and inflate reported profits. When Enron's fabrications became impossible to sustain in late 2001, Andersen's Houston office, rather than cooperating fully with the regulatory scrutiny that followed, instructed employees to destroy Enron-related documents, a shredding campaign that continued until the Securities and Exchange Commission formally served the firm with a subpoena in November of that year. The Justice Department indicted Arthur Andersen for obstruction of justice in March 2002; the firm was convicted, effectively ending a firm that had employed tens of thousands of people and once stood among the most respected accounting practices in the world, even though the Supreme Court later overturned the conviction on narrow legal grounds after the firm had already ceased to exist in any meaningful sense. Enron's own audited financial statements, the ones investors and pension funds had relied on for years, turned out to have been vetted by an auditor with every incentive not to look too closely, since Andersen was collecting substantial consulting fees from the same client it was supposed to be checking.

Two decades after Enron, in a market that did not exist when Andersen collapsed, the same pattern reappeared with almost eerie fidelity. FTX, a cryptocurrency exchange founded by Sam Bankman-Fried, had by 2022 become one of the most prominent firms in an industry built almost entirely on the premise that customer funds were safely segregated and independently accounted for. Bankman-Fried had cultivated a public image as an unusually scrupulous operator in a famously unscrupulous industry, testifying before Congress and courting mainstream investors and celebrity endorsers on the strength of that reputation. In November 2022, that reputation collapsed within days, when reporting revealed that Alameda Research, a trading firm Bankman-Fried also controlled, had been using billions of dollars in FTX customer deposits to cover its own trading losses — funds that were supposed to exist, untouched, in customer accounts, and did not. FTX filed for bankruptcy within the week, and Bankman-Fried was later convicted on multiple counts of fraud and conspiracy. What makes FTX belong in the same chapter as Theranos, Wirecard, and Enron rather than in a chapter of its own is what its own auditor turned out to have missed, or chosen not to see: FTX's outside accounting firm, Prager Metis, had signed off on the exchange's financial statements in 2021 and 2022 without, according to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's later findings, meaningfully examining Alameda's role in the business at all, and without a lead partner who the SEC concluded adequately understood either FTX's operations or the cryptocurrency markets it operated in. The firm settled the resulting SEC charges in 2024, paying roughly two million dollars without admitting wrongdoing — a sum that reads, next to the eight billion dollars in customer funds FTX could not account for, less like a real deterrent than like the cost of doing business in a niche corner of the audit industry still catching up to the assets it had agreed to certify.

A fifth case belongs alongside these four, and it differs from the others in an instructive way: rather than a single founder or a small executive circle deceiving outside auditors and investors, it involved thousands of ordinary employees deceiving the bank's own customers, at the direct instruction of internal incentive structures the bank's own leadership had built. Wells Fargo employees, facing aggressive internal sales quotas requiring them to cross-sell a minimum number of financial products to each customer, began opening millions of savings accounts, checking accounts, and credit cards in existing customers' names without those customers' knowledge or consent, sometimes forging signatures and moving money between a customer's real and fraudulently created accounts to make the new accounts appear active. Employees reportedly referred to the practice internally as "gaming," a euphemism that captures how thoroughly the fraud had become woven into ordinary daily operations rather than treated as a shocking aberration. When regulators and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau finally documented the scale of the practice in 2016, they found more than two million fraudulent accounts opened between 2011 and 2016 alone; by the time Wells Fargo's own subsequent accounting was complete, the figure had grown to roughly 3.5 million fraudulent accounts dating back to 2009, sustained for more than a decade by thousands of employees across the bank's retail branch network. Wells Fargo ultimately agreed to pay $3 billion to resolve the resulting civil and criminal claims brought by the Justice Department.

What makes Wells Fargo a genuinely distinct case from Theranos, Wirecard, Enron, and FTX is where the verification failure actually occurred. In each of the other four cases, an external institution — a board, an auditor, a due-diligence process — failed to catch a small number of executives deceiving outsiders. At Wells Fargo, the fraud was distributed across an enormous, ordinary workforce, each employee individually committing a comparatively small act of forgery, and the verification failure was internal: a corporate culture and incentive structure that made cheating the path of least resistance for thousands of otherwise ordinary bank employees simultaneously, for years, before internal compliance functions, external regulators, or investigative journalists caught up to the pattern. It is a useful reminder that institutional trust failures do not require a single charismatic liar at the top; sometimes an organization can manufacture the same result by simply building incentives that make a small, repeated act of dishonesty rational for thousands of people at once.

None of these five collapses — Theranos, Wirecard, Enron, FTX, Wells Fargo — involved a single line of synthetic media. Each involved a network of institutions specifically designed to prevent exactly this kind of fraud: boards, auditors, regulators, credentialing bodies, financial press. Each institution failed for a reason that turns out to recur across all five cases and across a great many others: verification had quietly become a formality performed for an audience rather than a genuine, adversarial check, and the people positioned to do the checking had reasons — financial, social, or simply the reluctance to be the person who says an admired founder, a lucrative client, or a top-performing branch network is lying — not to look too hard. It is worth noticing, too, how little the underlying failure mode changed across two decades and across industries as different as blood diagnostics, German fintech, American energy trading, cryptocurrency, and consumer banking. Andersen's Houston partners understood accounting extremely well and still failed to apply it honestly to a client generating substantial consulting fees. Prager Metis's audit partner, by the SEC's own account, did not understand the underlying business at all, and failed for a more mundane reason: competence, not just incentive, can be the missing ingredient in a verification system, and a checking institution can fail either because it does not want to see the truth or because it is not equipped to find it, with much the same result either way.

Holmes's own conviction is worth returning to briefly for a detail this chapter's summary omitted: the jury that convicted her acquitted her on several other counts, specifically those alleging she had defrauded patients rather than investors, a split verdict legal commentators have read as reflecting a meaningful distinction between deceiving sophisticated financial parties who had at least some capacity and duty to conduct their own diligence, and deceiving ordinary patients who had no comparable ability to verify a blood-testing company's underlying technology before trusting it with their own diagnoses. That distinction matters for this book's broader argument about who bears the cost of a verification failure: investors, however deceived, at least occupied a position where independent diligence was theoretically available to them; patients receiving a wrong cancer marker or a false miscarriage indicator from an Edison machine had no comparable position from which to verify anything at all, and bore consequences far more immediate and irreversible than any investor's financial loss, even though the jury's formal verdict ultimately centered on the harm to the parties best positioned to have caught the fraud themselves.

It is worth pausing on the specific mechanism that let each of this chapter's five frauds run for years rather than months, because the mechanism recurs with almost mechanical regularity. Theranos's board included prominent former statesmen and military officers with deep expertise in foreign policy and almost none in laboratory diagnostics, chosen, critics have since argued, more for their names and connections than for their capacity to ask the specific technical questions that would have exposed the Edison's limitations. Wirecard's supervisory structure allowed a single dominant executive enormous unchecked latitude over the exact subsidiary operations where the fabricated cash was supposedly held. FTX's board, to the extent it existed as an independent check at all, was reported to consist of a strikingly small number of people with close personal and financial ties to Bankman-Fried himself. Wells Fargo's own internal whistleblowers reported raising concerns about the sales-quota pressure driving fraudulent accounts years before regulators caught up, concerns that, by later accounts, were dismissed or the employees raising them were fired rather than heeded. In every case, the institution nominally responsible for independent oversight was structurally compromised in some specific, identifiable way well before the fraud itself began — a board without the right expertise, a governance structure without real independence, or a whistleblower channel that punished exactly the people it was supposed to protect. The fraud was the visible symptom. The compromised oversight structure was the underlying condition that made the symptom possible to sustain for years rather than days.

A closely related pattern, on a smaller and less financially costly but arguably more corrosive scale, has been unfolding for well over a decade across academic science, beginning with a single psychology paper that seemed, on its surface, to have nothing to do with fraud at all. In 2011, the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology published nine studies by the psychologist Daryl Bem reporting evidence that people could be measurably influenced by events that had not yet happened — a claim for precognition, dressed in the ordinary statistical language of experimental psychology, peer reviewed through the same process that vetted every other paper in the same issue. Its impossible conclusion forced an uncomfortable question that had been building for years: if a paper this conventional in form and this rigorously reviewed could produce a result this implausible in substance, how much confidence should anyone place in the rest of the discipline's published findings? The next chapter follows that question out of psychology and into a wider, more recent, and in some ways more troubling set of failures in how science checks its own work — failures that, like Prager Metis's audits, often involve institutions that were never deceived by a deepfake, a cloned voice, or a fabricated video call, and broke down anyway.

What Theranos, Wirecard, Enron, FTX, and Wells Fargo share is not fraud in the same legal sense as what the next chapter describes — Bem was never accused of fabricating data, and the scientific failures ahead involve a mix of outright fraud and more ordinary methodological weakness. What every case in this chapter shares is a verification apparatus that had been built, in some instances over a century earlier, to catch a category of failure that had since evolved past what the apparatus was designed or resourced to detect. A board evaluates a charismatic founder's demonstrations rather than commissioning independent technical audits. An accounting firm collects consulting fees from the client it is supposed to be checking without incident, until the incentive finally produces the failure it was always capable of producing. A newer accounting firm takes on a client in an unfamiliar, fast-moving industry without building the specific expertise that industry demands, and signs off on statements it was never actually equipped to evaluate. A bank's own internal sales incentives quietly turn ordinary employees into a distributed fraud engine long before any outside auditor is positioned to notice. This is the condition generative AI arrived into. Not a set of institutions functioning at full strength and suddenly under siege from a new external threat, but a set of institutions that had already been quietly drifting from adversarial verification toward ceremonial approval, in industries as different as blood diagnostics, financial technology, energy trading, cryptocurrency, and consumer banking. The tools described in earlier chapters did not create this vulnerability. They found it already there, in an economy full of seals that had stopped costing anyone anything to fake, and made the rest of the problem dramatically cheaper to exploit.
