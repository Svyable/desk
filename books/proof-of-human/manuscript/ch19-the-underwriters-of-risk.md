# The Underwriters of Risk

When Arup's Hong Kong employee authorized fifteen wire transfers totaling roughly $25.6 million, the immediate financial question for the firm was not only how the fraud had happened but who, if anyone, would absorb the loss. That question sits inside an industry that predates every technology this book has discussed by centuries — insurance — and that industry's own struggle to price this particular new risk turns out to be a case study in exactly the differential-cost logic this book has argued underlies every trust mechanism it examines.

Insurance itself is one of the oldest verification institutions this book examines, considerably older than the wax seals and guild marks this book's chapter on historical trust mechanisms traced back through the medieval period. Modern marine insurance grew directly out of Edward Lloyd's coffee house, established near London's docks around 1687, where ship captains, merchants, and financiers gathered to trade the maritime news and shipping intelligence Lloyd made a point of collecting for his patrons. The coffee house gradually became the natural meeting place for a specific kind of costly wager: financiers willing to bet on whether a given ship would complete its voyage safely, in exchange for a premium paid up front by the ship's owner or cargo merchant, a bet that only made commercial sense if the underwriters doing the wagering had genuinely reliable information about a ship's condition, captain, cargo, and route — precisely the same underlying verification problem, translated into maritime terms, that this book's Chapter Six traced through electrical safety testing and financial audits two centuries later. Lloyd's of London, the marketplace that grew out of that coffee house and still operates today, has spent more than three centuries refining the same basic exercise this chapter's cyber insurers are now attempting from scratch: building enough historical loss data and underwriting expertise to price an unfamiliar risk accurately, a process that took Lloyd's underwriters generations to develop for hazards as comparatively stable and well-understood as storms, piracy, and hull failure.

The cyber insurance market this chapter examines is itself a relatively young and still rapidly maturing corner of the broader insurance industry, which matters for understanding why it has struggled specifically with deepfake risk rather than adapting to it as smoothly as it has adapted to other emerging threats. Industry estimates place the global cyber insurance market at roughly $15 to $16 billion in premiums for 2024, with most major reinsurers, including Munich Re, projecting the market could double again within the following five years as awareness of both cyber risk generally and insurance solutions for it continues to spread. Even at that scale, cyber insurance still represents a small fraction of overall property and casualty insurance premiums globally, a reminder that the entire category — not merely its deepfake-specific corner — remains a young line of business still building the multi-decade loss history that older insurance categories like fire, auto, and life insurance have long since accumulated.

Standard commercial cyber insurance policies did not, as a rule, include a distinct line item labeled "deepfake fraud" when the Arup and WPP incidents made headlines in 2024, because no such category existed yet in most insurers' underwriting frameworks. Losses from AI-assisted impersonation fraud have instead been evaluated under existing coverage structures built for older threats: social engineering coverage, designed originally for email-based business email compromise scams, and funds transfer fraud coverage, designed for cases where an employee is tricked into wiring money to a fraudulent account through conventional means. Several carriers have moved to clarify, in policy language, that their existing social engineering provisions are intended to cover AI-assisted attacks as well, an accommodation that extends old coverage to a new mechanism rather than pricing the new mechanism as its own distinct risk. Other insurers have gone the opposite direction, moving as of the start of 2026 to explicitly exclude AI-generated deepfake fraud from standard social engineering coverage on renewed policies, narrowing protection precisely as the underlying threat has grown, a response that reads less like confidence the risk is manageable and more like an industry still unsure how to price something whose loss curve has not yet stabilized.

A small number of insurers have taken the opposite bet, treating affirmative deepfake coverage as a product differentiator rather than a risk to exclude. BOXX Insurance, a cyber-focused insurtech that is part of the Zurich Insurance Group, added explicit AI and deepfake coverage to its commercial policy offering, betting that clients will pay a premium for coverage competitors are still hedging against. Whichever side of that bet proves correct, nearly every policy in the market — whether it excludes, includes, or ambiguously covers AI-assisted fraud — imposes the same underlying condition on the insured company as the price of coverage: dual-channel or callback verification procedures for any unusual payment request, the exact institutional habit that would have stopped the Arup fraud in its tracks had it been rigorously enforced before the call ever happened. This is the insurance industry doing, in its own commercial language, precisely what this book's chapter on costly signaling would predict: an insurer cannot personally verify that a client's internal controls are sound, so it makes the existence of a specific, auditable, costly-to-fake control — a documented callback to a known number, a second employee's independent sign-off — a condition of the policy itself, transferring the underlying verification problem back onto the client in exchange for covering what slips through despite it.

The broader fraud environment these insurers are trying to price has been getting measurably worse by any available accounting, even setting aside the specific difficulty of isolating the AI-generated share of it. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center logged more than 859,000 complaints in 2024 alone, with reported losses exceeding $16.6 billion — a thirty-three percent increase over the previous year's already elevated total. Adults sixty and older accounted for close to $4.8 billion of that figure, filing over 147,000 complaints, largely tied to tech-support scams, romance scams, and cryptocurrency investment fraud, the same demographic and the same broad categories of scam this book's earlier chapter on grandparent voice-cloning fraud examined in detail, now folded into a nationwide loss figure large enough to give any insurer pricing this risk considerable pause. Cryptocurrency-related fraud alone accounted for more than $9.3 billion of the year's total losses, a reminder that the fraud environment cyber insurers are underwriting against is not a narrow, contained category but a broad and still-growing one, of which AI-assisted impersonation is only the newest and fastest-moving component.

Even where coverage exists, it is frequently capped well below the scale of loss a sophisticated attack can produce. A cyber policy carrying an overall limit in the low millions of dollars might carry a social-engineering sublimit only a fraction of that size, meaning a company that suffers an Arup-scale loss could find that even a policy nominally covering the fraud absorbs only a modest share of the total damage, with the remainder falling to the company's own balance sheet regardless of what the policy's declarations page promised. Industry data on deepfake-assisted losses reported to insurers has shown wire-transfer fraud losses reaching as high as the Arup case's own $25 million figure, alongside a steadier stream of smaller ransomware and business-email-compromise claims averaging in the hundreds of thousands of dollars, a loss profile insurers are still working to model with any real statistical confidence, since the category is too new and too fast-changing to have produced the kind of multi-decade actuarial history that prices more established risks like fire, flood, or conventional theft.

Reinsurance, the mechanism by which primary insurers themselves buy protection against unusually large or correlated losses, adds a further layer of difficulty this chapter has not yet named. A reinsurer's willingness to backstop a primary insurer's cyber book depends on the reinsurer's own confidence that losses across many different policyholders are not all going to spike simultaneously from a single shared cause, in the way a single hurricane can simultaneously damage thousands of individually insured properties across one coastline. Deepfake fraud techniques, once developed and published, spread almost immediately to every attacker capable of downloading the relevant tool, which means an improvement in voice-cloning or live-video-manipulation technology can plausibly raise the loss probability across an insurer's entire book of policyholders at once, rather than affecting one unlucky policyholder in isolation the way a single building fire would. That correlated-risk profile is closer to the kind of systemic risk reinsurers price most cautiously and most expensively, which helps explain why affirmative deepfake coverage, where it exists at all, tends to arrive with the tighter sublimits and stricter procedural conditions this chapter has already documented.

There is a further wrinkle specific to insuring against deepfake fraud that has no clean analogue in Lloyd's older maritime risks, and it concerns the peculiar difficulty of even defining the covered event precisely enough to write an enforceable policy around it. A ship either sinks or it does not, a fact a claims adjuster can verify through wreckage, survivor accounts, and maritime records with reasonable confidence. Whether a given fraud loss "involved AI" in the sense a policy's deepfake exclusion or affirmative coverage clause contemplates is a considerably murkier factual question, particularly in a case like Arup's, where the fraud combined a phishing email, a fabricated video call, and an employee's own judgment call about following an unusual instruction — several distinct causal ingredients an insurer's claims team has to somehow untangle and assign relative responsibility to before deciding whether a specific policy provision applies at all. This definitional fuzziness gives insurers considerable room to dispute claims after the fact, a dynamic already visible in reporting on the sector, where policyholders and carriers have publicly disagreed about whether a given loss falls under traditional social-engineering language or a newer, narrower AI-specific carve-out, adding a layer of litigation risk on top of the fraud loss itself.

Lloyd's own centuries-long history offers one further, cautionary data point about how long it can take a genuinely new risk category to become reliably insurable, one worth remembering before assuming cyber insurers will simply catch up to deepfake fraud within a normal business planning cycle. Marine insurance underwriters spent generations refining their understanding of storms, piracy, and navigational hazard before those risks became routinely, confidently priced; even then, entirely new categories of maritime risk — steam propulsion, containerized cargo, offshore drilling — each required their own multi-decade recalibration once the underlying technology or practice changed enough to invalidate the old loss tables. If underwriting a stable, physically bounded risk like a wooden ship's Atlantic crossing took Lloyd's the better part of a century to price with real confidence, underwriting a risk whose generative technology is still meaningfully improving every few months, in the way this book's chapters on watermarking and biometric spoofing both document, is not a problem cyber insurers should expect to solve quickly simply because they want to.

A comparison to how the insurance industry eventually handled an earlier, unrelated correlated risk is instructive here. Cyber insurance more broadly, covering data breaches and ransomware rather than deepfake fraud specifically, went through its own version of this recalibration a decade earlier, as insurers initially underpriced ransomware coverage before a wave of high-profile attacks in the late 2010s forced a painful, market-wide repricing, complete with tightened underwriting requirements, mandatory security controls as a condition of coverage, and sharply higher premiums for policyholders who had grown accustomed to cheap, loosely underwritten protection. Deepfake-enabled fraud appears to be tracing a similar arc on a compressed timeline: early coverage offered with minimal specific underwriting, a wave of high-profile losses forcing insurers to confront the gap between assumed and actual risk, and an ongoing, still-unsettled recalibration toward the dual-channel verification requirements and narrower sublimits this chapter has already described. If that parallel holds, the current uncertainty this chapter documents is less a permanent feature of insuring against AI-assisted fraud than a transitional phase the ransomware market has already modeled a version of — uncomfortable and expensive for policyholders while it lasts, but not necessarily permanent.

This chapter's underlying argument is not really about insurance specifically; it is about what happens to a risk-pricing institution when the risk it is trying to price is evolving faster than the institution's own data can keep pace with it. Actuarial science, at its core, is a costly-signal-detection exercise not unlike the ones examined throughout this book: an insurer prices a policy by estimating, from historical loss data, how often a given category of bad outcome occurs and how expensive it tends to be, then charges a premium sufficient to cover the expected loss plus a margin. That exercise depends on the underlying risk holding reasonably still long enough for historical data to remain predictive of future losses. Deepfake-enabled fraud, unlike a house fire or a stolen car, is a risk whose "unit economics" — to borrow the phrase this book has used to describe the plunging cost of fabrication itself — are actively falling as the underlying generative technology keeps improving, which means every year's loss data is, to some degree, already stale by the time an actuary tries to build a model from it. An insurer pricing this risk in 2026 is, in a real sense, trying to underwrite a moving target using a ruler that was calibrated for a target that had mostly stopped moving.

The practical upshot, at least for now, is that companies cannot simply outsource this book's central problem to an insurance policy the way they might outsource a fire risk to a property insurer backed by a century of reliable actuarial tables. The dual-channel verification procedures insurers increasingly require as a condition of coverage are, in effect, the insurance industry publicly conceding that no policy yet exists which can substitute for an organization actually rebuilding its own internal verification habits — the same conclusion this book's opening chapters reached by watching what happened to companies that had not yet rebuilt them. An insurance policy, in this reading, is not a replacement for the differential-cost signals examined throughout this book. It is, at best, a financial backstop for the gap that remains after an organization has done everything reasonably available to make fabrication expensive again on its own terms — and, at worst, a false sense of security for organizations that mistake the existence of a policy for the existence of a control.
