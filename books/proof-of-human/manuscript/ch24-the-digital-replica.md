# The Digital Replica

When the union representing American film and television performers walked off the job in July 2023, joining screenwriters already on strike, the dispute was widely reported around a single, easily quotable grievance: studios wanted the right to scan a background actor's face and body once, pay for a single day's work, and then reuse that digital likeness indefinitely, in any production, without further payment or consent. SAG-AFTRA's leadership described the practice, once generative AI made convincing digital doubles commercially viable, as an existential threat to the entire profession of acting rather than a routine labor dispute over wages — a fear that reframes almost every case examined earlier in this book, from a slightly different angle: what happens when the very thing a person's likeness, voice, and performance are worth can be extracted once and then reproduced indefinitely by someone else, without that person's ongoing involvement or consent?

The strike, which ran nearly four months before a tentative agreement in November 2023 and formal ratification the following month, produced a contract that created two specific new legal categories for how a performer's digital likeness could be used. An "employment-based digital replica" covers a version of a performer generated from footage created during an actual, paid engagement — a stunt double's face digitally replaced with the lead actor's, for instance, or a background performer's scanned likeness inserted into a crowd scene the performer never actually appeared in. An "independently created digital replica" covers something closer to what most people imagine when they hear the phrase "AI actor": a synthetic performer built from a real person's scanned likeness but then used and directed for work the actual performer never did at all. The agreement requires producers to secure a performer's "clear and conspicuous" consent before creating either kind of replica, to provide advance notice — 48 hours in most circumstances — before a specific use, and to pay for that use under terms roughly analogous to the compensation a live performance would have earned.

Critics on the union's own side of the negotiating table, and outside labor researchers examining the ratified contract afterward, have raised a specific concern that echoes this book's recurring skepticism about consent-based verification frameworks generally: a contractual requirement for "consent" is only as protective as a performer's actual leverage to withhold it. A prominent, in-demand actor can plausibly refuse a digital-replica request and suffer little professional consequence. A background performer or a lower-tier working actor, dependent on a production company for future bookings, faces a much harder version of the same choice this book's chapter on Worldcoin's biometric registrations examined in an entirely different context: formal consent exists on paper, but the practical power imbalance between the party requesting a biometric scan and the party being asked to provide it means "consent" may function, in practice, closer to "the price a person facing limited alternatives feels able to refuse." Some organizing critiques of the ratified deal argued that its consent language contained loopholes broad enough — particularly around bundled, blanket consent obtained as a condition of employment on a given production, rather than consent negotiated separately for each specific future reuse — to leave exactly the performers with the least bargaining power most exposed to exactly the practice the strike was fought to prevent.

What makes the SAG-AFTRA agreement worth its own chapter in this book, rather than a passing mention inside the chapter on the verification economy, is that it represents a fundamentally different kind of response to the underlying problem than anything else this book has examined. Every other institution traced in these chapters — content credentials, personhood verification, identity-checking businesses, cyber insurance — is built around verifying whether a piece of content or a claimed identity is authentic. The SAG-AFTRA contract does something adjacent but distinct: it does not ask whether a digital replica is "real" in any of the senses this book has spent its chapters examining. A studio-created digital double, built with full consent from a real actor's scanned likeness, is not a fabrication in the sense the Arup fraud or the Baltimore recording were fabrications; everyone involved knows exactly what it is and where it came from. The problem the contract is trying to solve is not detection but consent and compensation: not "is this authentic," but "did the person whose face, voice, and performance this technology can now reproduce indefinitely actually agree to that reproduction, and are they being paid for it."

That distinction matters because it identifies a genuinely separate category of harm from everything else in this book, one this book's earlier chapters on nonconsensual synthetic imagery previewed without fully naming. A digital replica used with full consent and fair payment is not a fraud, a fabrication, or a deception in any sense that requires new verification infrastructure to catch; the harm, if there is one, lives entirely in the terms of the underlying agreement — whether the consent was meaningful, whether the compensation was fair, whether the performer retained any ongoing say over how the replica would eventually be used. This is closer to a labor and property-rights question than an authentication question, and it suggests that the verification apparatus this book has spent most of its pages describing — watermarks, content credentials, personhood proofs — is necessary but not sufficient for the full range of problems generative AI's ability to reproduce a specific human being's likeness actually creates. A watermark can tell a viewer that a video is synthetic. It cannot tell them whether the real person whose face and voice it reproduces ever agreed to that reproduction, on what terms, or for how long.

Video game performers, represented by the same union, fought a nearly identical battle on a slightly longer timeline and reached a considerably more detailed resolution. SAG-AFTRA's interactive media members went on strike in July 2024 after negotiations over AI protections with major game studios broke down, and the strike continued for nearly a year before a tentative agreement was reached in June 2025. The video game contract went further than the original film and television agreement in several specific, quantifiable ways: it required informed consent for every distinct use of a performer's voice, likeness, or movement in an AI system, gave performers the right to suspend a company's ongoing right to use their digital replica during a labor dispute, and set a minimum compensation rate for replica use, with "real-time generation" uses — a digital replica voicing an interactive chatbot character that can respond to a player's unscripted input, rather than delivering pre-recorded lines — priced at a minimum of seven and a half times the ordinary scale rate for direct human performance, reflecting how much more extensively a real-time, conversational replica exposes the underlying performance to unpredictable, unreviewed reuse than a fixed line of recorded dialogue does. Studios had originally proposed language allowing something close to an unlimited buyout of a performer's replica rights in a single upfront payment; that language was reportedly stripped from the companies' final offer only after sustained pressure from the union, illustrating in miniature the same asymmetry of bargaining power this chapter's discussion of the original SAG-AFTRA contract raised: the protections that ended up in the contract were not offered freely, but extracted through nearly a year of the affected performers' own lost income.

The gap between the film-and-television agreement's "clear and conspicuous consent" language and the video game agreement's more granular, quantified protections is itself worth dwelling on, because it demonstrates something this book's economics chapter would predict: a costly signal — here, a genuinely informed, adequately compensated consent process rather than a boilerplate signature buried in a broader contract — only holds up as costly and meaningful when the party relying on it has enough bargaining leverage to make the other side actually pay the cost, rather than merely go through the motions of asking. The original 2023 film and television contract, negotiated at the tail end of an exhausting, four-month strike that had already cost performers substantial lost income, produced consent language critics on the union's own side judged vulnerable to exactly the kind of bundled, take-it-or-leave-it consent this book's earlier discussion flagged as weak. The video game contract, negotiated across nearly a year of continued job action and evidently benefiting from lessons learned in the earlier fight, produced considerably more specific, harder-to-circumvent protections: quantified minimum rates, an explicit real-time-generation premium, and language studios' own initial proposals had to be visibly stripped of rather than merely softened. Consent, in other words, is not a fixed property of a contract clause; it is a costly signal like any other examined in this book, and its actual strength depends on how much leverage the party giving it had at the moment of signing, not merely on what words appear on the page.

Other unions and professions facing similar pressure have moved toward comparable frameworks since the original SAG-AFTRA agreement, including voice actors pushing for parallel protections as AI voice-cloning tools of the kind examined in this book's technology chapter made an actor's recorded voice, not just their face, newly reproducible at scale without their ongoing involvement.

The distinction this chapter has drawn between an "employment-based" and an "independently created" digital replica maps onto a deeper question this book's economics chapter would recognize immediately: who bore the cost of producing the original signal being replicated, and does the replica's creator still owe anything to the person who bore it? A digital replica assembled from footage a performer was paid to generate for one specific production, then reused without further payment in a different context, is a case of the replica's creator capturing value from a cost the performer already paid for once, without compensating them for the second, third, or thousandth use extracted from that same original investment of time, skill, and likeness. This is structurally similar to the free-rider problem this book's chapter on identity verification touched on in passing: a costly signal, once produced, can often be copied and reused far more cheaply than it was originally created, and the question of who captures that reuse value — the original producer, or whoever finds a way to extract it afterward — recurs across nearly every domain this book has examined, from a stolen fingerprint to a scraped stock photograph to a digitally replicated performance. The common thread across all of these agreements is an attempt to convert what would otherwise be a purely technical capability — the ability to extract a costly, hard-to-fake biometric signal (a specific person's face, voice, and mannerisms) and reproduce it indefinitely at near-zero marginal cost — back into something requiring an ongoing, compensated, revocable relationship with the person whose signal it originally was. Whether that conversion holds, or whether the underlying economic pressure toward cheaper, more scalable digital replicas eventually erodes it the way this book's earlier chapters describe other costly signals eroding, is not a question the 2023 contract can answer by itself. It is a question that will be answered by how consistently performers with real bargaining power, and the ones without it, are actually able to enforce the consent the contract nominally guarantees them.

Several U.S. states moved to address exactly this gap for individuals outside any union's bargaining structure, extending a form of the SAG-AFTRA protections into general state law rather than leaving them confined to a specific industry's collective contract. Tennessee's ELVIS Act, signed in 2024 and named for the state's most famous former resident, updated the state's existing right-of-publicity statute to explicitly cover a person's voice alongside their name, image, and likeness, giving any Tennessee resident — not merely a unionized actor or musician — a legal claim against an unauthorized AI-generated replica of their voice used for commercial purposes. Similar legislative efforts advanced in other states over the same period, extending a version of the protection this chapter's entertainment-industry contracts negotiated collectively to individuals who never had a union bargaining on their behalf at all. This is precisely the pattern this book's earlier chapters describe recurring across nearly every institution examined: a protection first won through costly, sustained collective action by an organized, resourced group eventually gets generalized into broader law, extending its benefit to people who could never have won it through individual bargaining on their own.

The entertainment industry's experience is likely to function as an early proving ground for consent-and-compensation frameworks that other professions built around a recognizable personal likeness will eventually need as well, even where no union exists to negotiate collectively on their behalf. Motivational speakers, podcast hosts, fitness instructors, and online educators all build businesses substantially around a recognizable face and voice in exactly the way an actor does, without SAG-AFTRA's bargaining apparatus or any equivalent collective structure standing behind them individually. Whatever consent and compensation norms eventually stabilize around actors' digital replicas, by contract or by law, will likely become the reference point every other public-facing profession's own negotiations over AI likeness rights get measured against, for better or worse, in the absence of a comparable union to fight the same fight on their behalf from scratch.
