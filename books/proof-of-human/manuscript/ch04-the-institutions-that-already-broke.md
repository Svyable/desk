# The Institutions That Already Broke

Elizabeth Holmes told investors, doctors, and eventually the public that her company, Theranos, had built a machine called the Edison that could run more than two hundred different blood tests from a few drops of blood pricked from a fingertip, instead of the vials a conventional draw required. It was an appealing story for reasons that had nothing to do with whether it was true: cheaper, less painful testing promised to catch disease earlier and make routine bloodwork accessible in drugstores rather than hospitals. Board members, investors, and partners including a national pharmacy chain accepted the story on the strength of Holmes's presentation and a closely guarded set of demonstrations, without ever being permitted the kind of independent technical verification that might have surfaced the truth sooner: the Edison could reliably perform only a small fraction of the tests it was marketed as running, and for most of the rest, Theranos quietly used conventional machines purchased from other manufacturers while continuing to imply the results came from its own technology. Patients received blood test results that were, in a meaningful number of documented cases, simply wrong, leading to false diagnoses ranging from miscarriage indicators to cancer markers.

The fraud held together for years, through several funding rounds that valued the company near ten billion dollars at its peak, until a Wall Street Journal reporter named John Carreyrou received a tip in 2015 and spent months verifying it before publishing an investigation that unraveled the story from the outside, since the story had never been sound enough to unravel from within. Holmes was convicted in January 2022 on four counts of wire fraud and conspiracy related to defrauding investors, and sentenced later that year to more than eleven years in prison. The verdict is notable less for its severity than for what it took to reach it: an outside journalist doing independent reporting, because the institutions ordinarily positioned to catch exactly this kind of fraud — a board of directors, a slate of prominent investors, partnership due diligence at a national retail chain — had, for years, functioned as an audience rather than a check.

Wirecard offers a similar arc with a different cast. The German payments company had grown into one of the country's most valuable financial technology firms, a rare non-bank member of the prestigious DAX stock index, when it disclosed in June 2020 that €1.9 billion supposedly held in trustee accounts in the Philippines could not be located. Within days, the company admitted the money likely did not exist at all; the Central Bank of the Philippines confirmed the funds had never entered the country, contradicting Wirecard's initial attempt to blame two local banks. The missing sum amounted to roughly a quarter of the company's entire reported balance sheet. Wirecard's shares, which had traded near record highs only weeks earlier, collapsed by more than ninety percent, and the company filed for insolvency by the end of June. Its longtime chief executive was arrested on suspicion of falsifying accounts and market manipulation. What makes Wirecard particularly instructive is that this was not a case of an obscure private company slipping past scrutiny; it was a major public company, audited annually by a global accounting firm, whose fabricated revenues and nonexistent cash reserves survived years of financial statements before the fiction became too large to sustain.

Wirecard's auditor was not Arthur Andersen, but Andersen's own collapse two decades earlier is the reference point against which every subsequent audit failure gets measured, because it demonstrated how completely an institution built specifically to verify other institutions could fail at its one job. Andersen had audited Enron for years as the energy trading company built an increasingly elaborate structure of off-balance-sheet partnerships to hide debt and inflate reported profits. When Enron's fabrications became impossible to sustain in late 2001, Andersen's Houston office, rather than cooperating fully with the regulatory scrutiny that followed, instructed employees to destroy Enron-related documents, a shredding campaign that continued until the Securities and Exchange Commission formally served the firm with a subpoena in November of that year. The Justice Department indicted Arthur Andersen for obstruction of justice in March 2002; the firm was convicted, effectively ending a firm that had employed tens of thousands of people and once stood among the most respected accounting practices in the world, even though the Supreme Court later overturned the conviction on narrow legal grounds after the firm had already ceased to exist in any meaningful sense. Enron's own audited financial statements, the ones investors and pension funds had relied on for years, turned out to have been vetted by an auditor with every incentive not to look too closely, since Andersen was collecting substantial consulting fees from the same client it was supposed to be checking.

None of these three collapses — Theranos, Wirecard, Enron and Andersen — involved a single line of synthetic media. Each involved a network of institutions specifically designed to prevent exactly this kind of fraud: boards, auditors, regulators, credentialing bodies, financial press. Each institution failed for a reason that turns out to recur across all three cases and across a great many others: verification had quietly become a formality performed for an audience rather than a genuine, adversarial check, and the people positioned to do the checking had reasons — financial, social, or simply the reluctance to be the person who says an admired founder or a lucrative client is lying — not to look too hard.

The same pattern, on a smaller and less financially costly but arguably more corrosive scale, unfolded across an entire academic discipline. In 2011, the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, a mainstream and well-regarded outlet, published nine studies by the psychologist Daryl Bem reporting evidence that people could be measurably influenced by events that had not yet happened — a claim for precognition, dressed in the ordinary statistical language of experimental psychology. The paper had been peer reviewed. Its methods looked, on the surface, like the methods used throughout the field. And its impossible conclusion forced an uncomfortable question that had been building for years: if a paper this conventional in form could produce a result this implausible in substance, how much confidence should anyone place in the rest of the discipline's published findings?

The Open Science Collaboration set out to answer a version of that question directly, organizing a multi-year effort that attempted to replicate one hundred studies published in three prominent psychology journals. When the results were published in Science in 2015, fewer than half of the replications reproduced a statistically significant effect in the same direction as the original study, and even among the studies that did replicate, the measured effect sizes were typically only about half as large as originally reported. The paper's authors pointed to structural incentives rather than individual dishonesty as the likely explanation: researchers and journals alike were rewarded for novel, striking findings and rarely rewarded at all for confirming or disconfirming earlier work, which meant the field's own machinery for self-correction had gone largely unused for years. Peer review, the discipline's equivalent of an audit, had been checking whether a paper's methods looked sound on paper, not whether the underlying result would hold up when someone else tried to find it again.

What Theranos, Wirecard, Enron, and the replication crisis share is not fraud in the same legal sense — Bem was never accused of fabricating data, and most of the psychology studies that failed to replicate were the product of ordinary methodological weakness rather than deliberate deception. What they share is a verification apparatus that had been built, sometimes over a century earlier, to catch a category of failure that had since evolved past what the apparatus was designed to detect. A board evaluates a charismatic founder's demonstrations rather than commissioning independent technical audits. An accounting firm collects consulting fees from the client it is supposed to be checking without incident, until the incentive finally produces the failure it was always capable of producing. A journal rewards a surprising result and treats peer review as confirmation that a study looks methodologically standard, which is a different and much weaker claim than confirming the result is true.

This is the condition generative AI arrived into. Not a set of institutions functioning at full strength and suddenly under siege from a new external threat, but a set of institutions that had already been quietly drifting from adversarial verification toward ceremonial approval, in industries as different as blood diagnostics, financial technology, energy trading, and academic psychology. The tools described in the previous chapter did not create the vulnerability. They found it already there, and made it dramatically cheaper to exploit.
