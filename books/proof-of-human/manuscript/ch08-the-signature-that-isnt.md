# The Signature That Isn't

Mark Hofmann was, by the assessment of the document examiners and historians who spent years trying to catch up with him, one of the most technically accomplished forgers of the twentieth century, and he built his career on a target audience with a particular vulnerability: an institution eager to believe that a newly discovered document confirmed or complicated its own founding history. Beginning around 1980, Hofmann began selling purported historical documents connected to the early Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, correctly calculating that a church deeply invested in its own origin story would pay well, and ask relatively few hard technical questions, for documents that appeared to shed new light on that story. His most famous forgery, the so-called Salamander Letter, purported to be an 1830 account by an early Mormon associate describing founder Joseph Smith's discovery of the church's foundational golden plates in terms involving a white salamander rather than the angel of the church's official account — a document explosive enough, if genuine, to complicate a faith's founding narrative, and therefore valuable enough that the church itself purchased it and other Hofmann forgeries rather than risk a damaging document surfacing elsewhere. By the time investigators finished cataloging his output, the church's own archives contained 446 documents Hofmann had forged, an almost unbelievable volume of successful fabrication sustained over roughly five years before anyone caught him.

What makes Hofmann worth placing directly alongside William Henry Merrill's Underwriters Laboratories and the Good Housekeeping Institute's testing seal, the institutions this book's chapter on historical trust mechanisms examined as the constructive side of this same story, is that he defeated a verification apparatus that was, on paper, considerably more rigorous than an ordinary consumer trusting a safety mark. Rare document authentication in the early 1980s already involved forensic ink analysis, paper-aging techniques, and comparison against known handwriting samples — a real, technically grounded verification process, not merely an institution's word taken on faith. Hofmann defeated it by attacking the assumptions embedded in the verification process itself rather than trying to brute-force his way past it. He researched period-appropriate paper and ink recipes meticulously, sourced blank endpapers from genuinely old books to get period-correct paper stock, and used a chemical process involving iron gall ink formulated to age convincingly under the specific tests examiners of the era knew to apply — a forger, in other words, who had done his own homework on exactly what a verification system was checking for and engineered his fabrications specifically to satisfy those checks, rather than hoping the checks would simply be skipped or performed carelessly.

The specific mechanics of the murders Hofmann committed to protect his scheme deserve slightly more attention than the summary above gives them, because they show how a sufficiently threatened fabrication can escalate past financial fraud into violence in ways this book's other cases, largely confined to reputational and financial harm, do not. By 1985, Hofmann had made commitments to buyers and creditors that outstripped his ability to keep manufacturing convincing new forgeries at the pace his finances required, and rather than simply disappearing or declaring the scheme over, he built pipe bombs specifically targeting people connected to the deals that were closest to exposing him, apparently hoping investigators would conclude a collector's business rivalries, rather than the collector's own dealer, were responsible for the violence. The plan collapsed when a third bomb detonated inside Hofmann's own car, injuring him and giving investigators the physical evidence and motive that eventually led back to the forgeries themselves. Few of the fabrications examined elsewhere in this book have required their perpetrators to escalate to lethal violence to protect the underlying deception, which makes Hofmann's case a useful, if extreme, reminder that a sufficiently valuable fabrication, once exposure becomes an existential threat to the person who created it, can motivate consequences far beyond the original financial or reputational harm the forgery was designed to produce.

Van Meegeren's own eventual unmasking depended on a similarly specific institutional incentive rather than any routine authentication procedure catching him on its own. His forgeries had circulated for years without serious challenge from the art establishment he had successfully fooled; what actually exposed him was the postwar Allied investigation into looted and trafficked art, which was searching for evidence of collaboration with the Nazi regime rather than evidence of forgery specifically, and which stumbled onto the Göring painting's dubious origins only because tracing a suspiciously well-provenanced Vermeer back to its seller was a natural step in an investigation built for an entirely different purpose. Neither Hofmann nor van Meegeren was caught by the specific verification apparatus their forgeries were designed to defeat. Both were caught, indirectly and somewhat accidentally, by an entirely separate institutional process — a murder investigation in one case, a war-crimes inquiry in the other — that happened to intersect with their schemes for reasons that had nothing to do with authenticating art or documents as such.

It is worth pausing, too, on why the Mormon church proved such a receptive market for Hofmann's specific fabrications, because the vulnerability was not primarily technical. The church's own leadership had genuine institutional incentives, at various points, either to acquire documents that might complicate embarrassing narratives before outside scholars found them first, or to authenticate materials that seemed to support a preferred version of church history — incentives that made the church, at moments, a more credulous customer than a fully disinterested archive would have been. This is the same conflict-of-interest dynamic this book's chapter on Wirecard, Enron, and FTX identifies in corporate auditing: a checker with something to gain or lose from a particular finding applies a different standard than a checker with nothing at stake either way, and Hofmann, whether by instinct or careful calculation, seems to have understood exactly which of his potential buyers had the most complicated incentives to look too closely at what he was selling them.

The scheme ended not because the documents were finally exposed through some more rigorous authentication method but because Hofmann's business model began to collapse under its own weight. By 1985, he had taken on debts and made promises to buyers that outstripped his ability to keep producing convincing new material fast enough, and rather than face exposure as his finances and his supply of forgeries both ran short, he built and planted pipe bombs that killed two people connected to his business dealings — Steven Christensen, a document collector and one of his primary marks, and Kathleen Sheets, the wife of one of Christensen's former business partners — in an apparent attempt to redirect suspicion and buy himself more time. A third bomb detonated in Hofmann's own car, injuring him and ultimately leading investigators back to the forgeries at the center of the murders. He pleaded guilty to two counts of murder in 1987 and was sentenced to life in prison, bringing an unusually violent end to a case that began as a story about historical document authentication and ended as a story about what a sufficiently cornered fraudster is willing to do to protect a fabrication from being exposed.

Four decades before Hofmann began forging Mormon history, a Dutch painter named Han van Meegeren ran a scheme against a different, equally rigorous-seeming verification apparatus: the community of art historians and connoisseurs who authenticated Old Master paintings by eye, training, and accumulated expertise. Van Meegeren, a technically skilled but critically dismissed painter in his own right, spent years developing techniques to fake the seventeenth-century Dutch master Johannes Vermeer convincingly enough to fool the era's leading Vermeer scholars, mixing his own pigments to match period recipes and baking his canvases to crack the paint in a manner consistent with three centuries of aging. During the Nazi occupation of the Netherlands, van Meegeren sold one of his fabricated Vermeers, through an intermediary, to Hermann Göring, one of the most powerful figures in the Nazi regime and an obsessive art collector, reportedly receiving as much as 137 genuine paintings in exchange for the single forged canvas. When Allied investigators traced the painting back to him after the war, van Meegeren faced a charge far more serious than fraud: collaboration with the Nazi occupation, a crime that could have carried a death sentence. His defense was as audacious as his original scheme — he confessed to being a forger rather than a collaborator, and to prove it, he painted an entirely new "Vermeer" while under direct observation by Dutch authorities, demonstrating his technique live rather than merely asserting it. The demonstration worked. Van Meegeren was convicted of fraud rather than treason and sentenced to a single year in prison, a remarkably light outcome for a case that had begun with him facing the possibility of execution.

Both men also shared a trait worth naming because it recurs, in different form, throughout this book's later chapters on generative AI: each forger's success depended on correctly modeling the specific judgment of the specific experts he was trying to fool, rather than on some abstract, universal notion of a convincing fake. Van Meegeren studied the particular scholars who had built their reputations authenticating Vermeer and calibrated his forgeries to satisfy the specific stylistic and technical criteria those particular experts were known to apply, understanding correctly that a scholar invested in discovering a major new Vermeer had every professional incentive to want the discovery to be genuine, and would extend a version of good faith to evidence that confirmed rather than undermined that hope. Hofmann performed a similar study of the specific document examiners and church historians who would evaluate his forgeries, understanding that an institution eager to find support for a particular version of its own founding story would, likewise, be inclined to see what it wanted to see in a sufficiently well-executed fake. This is not merely a forger exploiting gullibility; it is a forger exploiting the same structural weakness this book's chapter on the economics of costly signaling identifies in every verification system examined throughout these pages — a checker whose incentives are not perfectly adversarial to the party being checked will, consciously or not, apply a weaker standard than a checker with nothing to gain from a favorable finding. Modern generative AI systems accomplish something functionally similar without any of Hofmann's or van Meegeren's individual psychological insight into their specific victims: trained on vast quantities of data including, increasingly, examples of what has previously fooled detection systems and human reviewers alike, they can implicitly learn and reproduce the statistical patterns most likely to satisfy whatever verification process stands in their way, achieving through brute-force pattern-matching across millions of examples what once required a single forger's years of patient, individualized study of a small number of specific experts.

Van Meegeren's case adds a dimension Hofmann's does not: it shows a forger successfully defeating not merely a documentary authentication process but the trained, subjective aesthetic judgment of an entire professional field, connoisseurs who had spent careers learning to recognize a specific painter's brushwork, composition, and technique. That verification apparatus was, if anything, considered more rigorous and less mechanically replicable than the paper-and-ink analysis Hofmann later learned to defeat, precisely because it depended on the kind of accumulated, hard-to-articulate expert judgment this book's later chapters describe as difficult for even sophisticated technology to fake. Van Meegeren defeated it anyway, by the same method Hofmann would later apply to documents: studying exactly what the experts were looking for and engineering his fabrications specifically to satisfy those criteria, down to the physical aging process a chemist might otherwise use to unmask him.

The Hofmann case offers a useful corrective to any assumption that pre-digital forgery was somehow a gentler, lower-stakes affair than the AI-assisted fabrications examined throughout this book. It also demonstrates something this book's chapter on the economics of costly signaling argues in more abstract terms: a verification system's real strength depends not merely on having rigorous-sounding procedures but on those procedures actually being expensive and difficult for a sufficiently determined, well-informed adversary to satisfy fraudulently. Hofmann did not skip past document authentication; he studied it closely enough to engineer forgeries that would pass it, precisely the kind of adversarial arms race this book's chapter on watermarking's limits describes playing out today between generative AI models and the detection tools built to catch their output. The gap between Hofmann's era and this book's present moment is not that forgery has become possible where it once was impossible — Hofmann proves conclusively that a sufficiently skilled, patient individual forger could already defeat a real, technically grounded verification apparatus using nothing more advanced than period-appropriate chemistry and painstaking historical research. The gap is that what took Hofmann years of specialized self-education, chemical experimentation, and meticulous research to accomplish against a small number of high-value targets can now be approximated, for a much wider range of media, by anyone with access to a generative model and no specialized training in forgery whatsoever. Hofmann was a genuine expert, arguably a savant, operating at the very edge of what an individual human forger could achieve. The tools examined in the rest of this book make a version of his skill available to people with no expertise at all.
