# The Homework Machine

In May 2023, Dr. Jared Mumm, an agricultural sciences instructor at Texas A&M University-Commerce, ran his students' final essays through ChatGPT with a simple prompt: had the chatbot itself written this? The idea seemed reasonable enough on its face, an instructor using the tool students might be misusing to check whether they had, in fact, misused it. ChatGPT, playing along with the framing of the question, obligingly told Mumm that yes, it had written the essays — a claim that was not true, since large language models have no memory of their own prior outputs and no reliable capacity to distinguish text they generated from text a human wrote in a similar style, but a claim delivered with exactly the same fluent, confident tone the model uses for accurate answers. Mumm, taking the chatbot's confident assertion at face value, informed an entire class that their final papers had been flagged, held their grades, and in several documented cases delayed graduating seniors from receiving diplomas they had already walked across a stage to collect, until the university intervened and the professor's methodology was publicly exposed as unsound.

The Texas A&M incident became a widely circulated cautionary tale, but the underlying problem it exposed did not go away once one professor's mistake was corrected; it simply moved to more sophisticated tools built specifically for the purpose, which turned out to carry their own, better-disguised version of the same unreliability. Turnitin, the plagiarism-detection company already embedded in the workflow of a large share of American universities and secondary schools, rolled out an AI-writing detection feature that assigned each submitted paper a percentage score purporting to reflect how much of the text was likely AI-generated. Independent researchers and educators who tested the tool against known human-written samples found a persistent, troubling pattern: the detector produced false positives at a rate high enough to represent a serious risk to any student whose paper happened to be flagged, and those false positives were not randomly distributed. Multiple studies examining AI-detection tools broadly, not only Turnitin specifically, found that these systems disproportionately misclassified writing by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, apparently because such essays often share surface-level statistical properties — simpler sentence structures, more predictable word choices — with the kind of text large language models tend to produce, even when every word was written by a human student doing their own honest work in a language they had not grown up speaking.

The scale of actual AI use in schoolwork, set against this backdrop of unreliable detection, makes the underlying problem considerably harder than a simple contest between honest students and a flawed tool. Pew Research Center's surveys of American teenagers found the share who said they had used ChatGPT for schoolwork roughly doubling between 2023 and 2024, from thirteen percent to twenty-six percent, with older high schoolers considerably more likely to report using it than younger students. A separate February 2024 survey of college students found even higher rates of admitted use, with more than a third of respondents currently using ChatGPT and the overwhelming majority of those users applying it specifically to schoolwork rather than personal projects. These are not fringe numbers describing a small population of committed cheaters; they describe what appears to be a genuine, rapid shift in how a meaningful share of an entire generation of students does at least some of its academic work, which means any detection system built on the assumption that AI-assisted writing remains a rare, aberrant event is being asked to operate in a population where the behavior it is trying to catch may already be closer to a widespread norm than an exception — a very different detection problem, statistically, than catching a small minority of violators against a background of overwhelmingly honest submissions.

The stakes attached to a single false positive can be severe and are frequently resolved through a process that offers the accused student little of the due process a legal proceeding would require. A flagged paper typically triggers an academic integrity investigation, sometimes adjudicated by a single instructor with wide discretion, sometimes by a university disciplinary board, and the burden of proof in many institutions' policies effectively falls on the student to demonstrate a negative — that they did not use AI — using whatever evidence they happen to have preserved: draft histories, browser records, a personal writing process the student may never have thought to document because, until the accusation arrived, there was no reason to expect they would need to prove their own authorship of their own words. This is the liar's dividend's near-mirror image, playing out inside a classroom rather than a courtroom or a newsroom: instead of a guilty party invoking AI to escape accountability for something true, an innocent student is accused of AI use for something they actually wrote themselves, and the accusation itself, regardless of its accuracy, can carry lasting academic consequences — a failing grade, a disciplinary record, a delayed degree — before it can be meaningfully contested.

The deeper structural problem, familiar by now from nearly every chapter in this book, is that AI-detection tools are attempting to solve exactly the kind of arms-race problem the chapter on watermarking's limits examined in a different context: a detector trained to recognize the statistical signature of one generation of language models has no durable advantage against the next generation, which is trained, whether deliberately or as a side effect of ordinary improvement, to produce text with fewer of the exact statistical tics the previous detector learned to flag. Multiple published studies and companies' own disclosures have shown AI-writing detectors' accuracy degrading meaningfully as the underlying language models they were built to catch continue improving, while the confidence with which these tools present their percentage scores to instructors has not correspondingly declined — a mismatch between demonstrated reliability and displayed certainty that echoes this book's earlier warning about the CAPTCHA-solving arms race and the deepfake-detection startups racing against an equally fast-moving adversary.

Universities have responded to this instability in strikingly different, sometimes contradictory ways, itself a sign of how unsettled the underlying verification problem remains. Vanderbilt University disabled Turnitin's AI-detection feature across its campus in August 2023, and its teaching-center staff laid out their reasoning in unusually specific terms worth repeating: Turnitin had claimed a false-positive rate of only about one percent at launch, but Vanderbilt alone had submitted roughly 75,000 student papers to the service in the previous year, meaning even a genuinely low error rate could have mislabeled on the order of 750 individual student papers as AI-generated when they were not — a concrete number that turns an abstract accuracy statistic into a very specific number of students who could plausibly have faced an unwarranted academic-integrity investigation. Vanderbilt's staff also noted that Turnitin declined to disclose the specific patterns its detector actually screened for, leaving instructors unable to evaluate the tool's reasoning even in principle, a transparency problem layered on top of the accuracy problem. Several campuses within the University of California system reached similar conclusions around the same period and disabled the feature for comparable reasons. Others have kept the tool active but layered additional human review on top of any flagged submission, treating the AI detector's score as a prompt for a conversation with the student rather than as a verdict. A smaller number of instructors have moved in the opposite direction, abandoning take-home written assignments altogether in favor of in-class, closed-book writing or oral examinations — a return to older, more labor-intensive, but more verifiably human forms of assessment, precisely the kind of "raise the cost of faking it" solution this book's economics chapter would predict emerging once a cheap, contestable technical signal proves unreliable.

Some institutions have tried a middle path between full detection reliance and full abandonment of take-home work, requiring students to submit a documented process alongside a finished essay: a version history from a word processor showing incremental composition over time, a screen-recording of the drafting session, or a brief oral defense of the submitted work's key claims and sources. Each of these approaches shifts the verification burden from the finished product, which a generative model can produce indistinguishably from human work, to the production process itself, which remains considerably harder and more time-consuming to fake convincingly — a student would need to actually script and perform a plausible drafting session to fool a version-history or screen-recording check, a far larger investment than simply pasting in a chatbot's output. This is, again, this book's differential-cost argument applied directly to a classroom: rather than trying to detect a cheap fake after the fact, these institutions raise the cost of faking the specific evidence they now require, betting that the added cost of faking a drafting process exceeds most students' motivation to fabricate it, at least for now.

This return to older assessment formats carries its own uncomfortable cost, worth naming rather than glossing over as a clean solution. In-class writing and oral examinations advantage students who process language quickly under time pressure and disadvantage students with anxiety disorders, certain learning differences, or simply a personal writing process that benefits from revision time a fixed class period does not allow — accommodations the take-home essay format had, for many students, quietly provided by default, without anyone needing to formally request them. Raising the cost of fabrication at the point of production, in other words, is not a costless fix; it substitutes one kind of unfairness, a false accusation from an unreliable detector, for a different kind, a format that structurally disadvantages students whose difficulty was never dishonesty in the first place. This is the same tradeoff this book's chapter on verification's darker side raises about biometric and government-issued credentials generally: every mechanism that raises the cost of faking a signal also raises the cost, for some genuinely honest population, of producing it legitimately, and a education system moving toward blue books and oral exams has to reckon with exactly who it is inadvertently making the assessment harder for, not merely who it is making cheating harder for.

A smaller but telling countercurrent has emerged among instructors who decided the entire premise of detecting AI use after the fact was a losing proposition, and chose instead to redesign the assessment itself around the one thing generative AI still cannot easily fake: a student's presence, in real time, producing work under direct observation. Blue-book examinations, handwritten and completed within a fixed class period, saw a documented revival at several universities specifically as an AI-resistant alternative to the take-home essay, alongside a renewed interest in oral examinations and in-class Socratic questioning that requires a student to defend and elaborate on ideas on the spot rather than simply submit a finished document. These formats are not new; they are, if anything, older and more labor-intensive for instructors than the take-home essay they are replacing, requiring more grading time and more scheduling complexity than collecting a batch of typed PDFs ever did. Their revival is a direct, practical instance of this book's recurring argument about differential cost: rather than trying to detect a cheap fake after it has already been submitted, these instructors chose to raise the cost of cheating at the point of production instead, by requiring the work to happen somewhere a generative model cannot easily be smuggled in — a return to exactly the kind of costly, hard-to-fake, real-time production this book's chapter on the economics of the unfakeable identifies as the durable alternative whenever an after-the-fact verification signal proves too cheap to trust.

The financial incentives on the vendor side of this market deserve one final mention, because they echo the same structural conflict this book's earlier chapters raise about auditors and rating agencies being paid by the parties they evaluate. Turnitin and its competitors are paid by the educational institutions deploying their detection tools, not by the students those tools evaluate, which at least avoids the most direct version of the conflict-of-interest problem this book has traced elsewhere. But a subtler version persists: a detection vendor's commercial success depends on institutions continuing to believe the underlying detection problem is one their product can meaningfully solve, which creates an institutional incentive to keep selling a capability the vendor's own more candid technical staff may privately doubt is achievable with real reliability, a tension no different in kind from the ratings agencies this book's earlier chapters describe continuing to sell confident-sounding grades on securities whose underlying risk had become considerably harder to assess than the rating implied.

What the education sector's struggle demonstrates, arguably more starkly than any other institution examined in this book, is how quickly an entire sector can be forced to renegotiate its basic verification practices once the assumption underlying them — that a student's essay is presumptively the student's own work, absent specific evidence otherwise — stops holding cleanly in either direction. Before generative AI, an instructor's default assumption that a submitted essay was written by the student who submitted it was reasonably safe, not because cheating never happened, but because producing a convincing, essay-length piece of fraudulent writing took real effort, exactly the differential-cost logic this book has traced from wax seals through peer review. That assumption is now unsafe in both directions at once: a student really might have used AI to write an entire essay in seconds, and a detector built to catch that possibility might just as easily condemn an honest student whose only offense was writing in a style a flawed statistical model associates with machines. Few institutions examined elsewhere in this book have had to rebuild their basic operating assumptions this quickly, for a population — students — this poorly equipped to defend themselves once accused.
