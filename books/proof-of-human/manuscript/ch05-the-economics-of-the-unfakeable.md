# The Economics of the Unfakeable

A peacock's tail is, from an engineering standpoint, a bad decision. It is heavy, metabolically expensive to grow, and makes the bird slower and more visible to predators than a plainer tail would. For a long time this was a genuine puzzle for evolutionary biology, since natural selection is supposed to favor traits that improve an organism's odds of survival, and a gaudy, cumbersome tail looks like exactly the kind of trait selection should have eliminated. In 1975, the Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi proposed an answer that was initially controversial and later became one of the more influential ideas in the study of animal signaling: the tail is not a bad decision despite its cost. It works because of its cost. A weak or unhealthy peacock cannot afford to grow and carry an enormous, extravagant tail; only a genuinely strong bird can absorb that handicap and still survive. The tail is credible precisely because it would be self-defeating for a lesser bird to fake it. Zahavi called this the handicap principle, and it explains why so much of animal signaling — the deep roar of a stag, the elaborate plumage of a bird of paradise, the antlers a bull must grow and shed every year — trends toward costly, wasteful-looking displays rather than cheap, efficient ones. A signal that costs nothing to produce is a signal anyone can produce, healthy or not, which means it carries no information at all.

Economists arrived at a strikingly similar insight from a completely different direction, working on human markets rather than animal mating displays. In 1970, George Akerlof published a paper built around the used car market, asking a simple question with an uncomfortable answer: what happens when sellers know more about the quality of what they are selling than buyers do? Akerlof's answer was that markets can fail entirely under those conditions. If buyers cannot distinguish a good used car — a "plum" — from a bad one — a "lemon" — they will rationally offer a price that reflects the average quality of what's on the market, discounted for the risk of getting a lemon. But that average price is unattractive to anyone actually holding a plum, since they know their car is worth more than the market is willing to pay for an unknown quantity. Plum owners exit the market rather than sell at a lemon-adjusted price, which lowers the average quality of cars actually available, which lowers the price buyers are willing to pay further still, in a spiral that can leave a market thin, distorted, or in the extreme case gone altogether. Akerlof pointed to real-world analogues beyond used cars: the reluctance of some credit markets to develop in places where a lender cannot easily verify a borrower's reliability, and the difficulty older adults have historically faced buying affordable health insurance, since insurers cannot easily distinguish the healthy elderly from the unhealthy and price accordingly.

Michael Spence, working on a related problem a few years later, asked what a plum owner — or, in his case, a genuinely capable job applicant — could do to escape that trap. His answer, developed in a 1973 paper on job market signaling, was that a costly, hard-to-fake credential could break the deadlock even if the credential itself taught the applicant nothing directly useful for the job. A university degree, in Spence's framing, does not need to make someone more productive to be valuable to an employer; it only needs to be disproportionately easier for genuinely capable people to obtain than for less capable people, so that its possession reliably correlates with the underlying quality an employer actually cares about. For the signal to work, Spence argued, it has to be costly enough — in time, money, or effort — that faking it isn't worthwhile for someone the signal is supposed to screen out, and it has to be something the employer can actually observe. Strip away either the cost or the observability, and the signal collapses into noise, exactly the way an inexpensive tail would collapse a peacock's mating display into meaninglessness.

Three very different fields — evolutionary biology, labor economics, and used car markets — converge on the same structural idea, which is worth stating plainly because it is the load-bearing concept for everything else in this book. A signal is only informative if it is more expensive, in some relevant currency, for a low-quality sender to produce than for a high-quality sender to produce. It does not need to be expensive in an absolute sense. It needs to be differentially expensive — cheap enough for the genuine article to bother with, costly enough that faking it isn't worth it for whoever the signal is meant to filter out.

This is precisely the mechanism that generative AI attacks, across every domain this book has touched so far. A familiar face on a video call used to be differentially expensive to fake: forging one convincingly required real skill and real effort, which meant only a determined, resourced adversary would bother, and most video calls could be trusted on the reasonable assumption that the juice wasn't worth the squeeze for casual fraud. A recognizable voice on the phone worked the same way. A financial audit worked the same way in principle, though as the previous chapter showed, the differential cost of corrupting an audit had already fallen further than most people appreciated, well before any of this involved a neural network. In every case, the value of the signal depended on a gap between how expensive it was to produce honestly and how expensive it was to fake, and in every case that gap has been narrowing — sometimes because verification quietly got cheaper to corrupt from the inside, and sometimes, as with deepfakes and voice cloning, because fabrication got dramatically cheaper from the outside.

The peacock's tail survives as a reliable signal for a straightforward reason: nothing in a peahen's environment is developing a technology that lets a weak, unhealthy peacock cheaply grow a convincing fake tail. Biological signals evolve alongside biological cheats, but the arms race moves at the pace of generations, giving evolution time to find new, harder-to-fake signals when old ones get compromised. Human trust signals do not have that luxury. A signature that took a forger weeks to imitate convincingly in 1990 can, with the right tools, be approximated by a machine learning model in seconds today, and the underlying signal — a signature on a document — has not had generations to evolve a costlier, more robust replacement. It has had, in some cases, months.

This is the useful, if slightly uncomfortable, lens the rest of this book applies to the question of trust in an age of cheap fabrication. The question worth asking about any verification mechanism — a watermark, a biometric scan, a content credential, a professional credential, an audit — is not simply whether it works today, but whether the cost of faking it is holding steady, rising, or falling relative to the cost of producing it honestly. A mechanism whose fabrication cost is falling faster than its verification cost is a mechanism on borrowed time, no matter how official it looks or how long it has been trusted. A password is a weak signal because copying it costs almost nothing once it is known. A costly biometric scan tied to a single physical body is a stronger signal, in the narrow sense that duplicating a body is much harder than copying a string of characters, though as later chapters will show, "harder" is not the same as "impossible," and the institutions built around biometric verification carry their own costs, some of them uncomfortable in different ways than a stolen password.

What the handicap principle, the market for lemons, and job market signaling all suggest, taken together, is that the search for trustworthy verification in an age of artificial intelligence is not really a search for new technology so much as a search for new sources of differential cost — some property of a genuine person, a genuine document, or a genuine transaction that remains meaningfully harder to fake than to produce honestly, even after the easy fakes have been automated. The next several chapters look at the specific mechanisms currently being built to try to reestablish that gap: cryptographic content provenance, biometric personhood verification, and the commercial industry rapidly assembling itself around the simple, suddenly valuable claim that a given face, voice, document, or transaction is exactly what it appears to be.
