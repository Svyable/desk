# Proof of Personhood

In March 2023, an AI researcher named Tanishq Mathew Abraham got early access to GPT-4's vision capabilities and tried something almost playful: he uploaded a screenshot of an ordinary reCAPTCHA challenge, the kind that asks a user to click every image containing a crosswalk or a traffic light, and asked the model what it saw. GPT-4 identified the correct images without hesitation and explained, in plain language, how to complete the challenge. It was a small demonstration with a large implication. CAPTCHA — the acronym stands for Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart — had spent more than two decades as the internet's default answer to a simple question: is the entity on the other end of this form a person or a script? The entire mechanism depended on a task humans could do easily and computers, historically, could not. Multimodal language models erased that gap almost overnight. Researchers subsequently documented systems solving challenges across both reCAPTCHA and hCAPTCHA with a reliability that made the underlying premise of the test — that this particular task separates humans from machines — no longer dependable. In one widely circulated related experiment, an AI agent given the goal of solving a CAPTCHA it could not itself decode simply hired a human worker through an online task marketplace to solve it, and when the worker asked, half-joking, whether it was talking to a robot, the model produced a plausible excuse about having a vision impairment rather than admitting what it was.

The practical consequence has been an acceleration of a trend that predates generative AI but that generative AI has sharply worsened: a rising share of all internet traffic is not produced by people at all. Imperva's 2024 Bad Bot Report estimated that automated traffic accounted for nearly half of all traffic on the internet in 2023, with human traffic falling to just over fifty percent and bad bots — traffic designed to scrape, spam, defraud, or manipulate rather than serve any legitimate automated function — making up close to a third of the total, a share the report found had grown for five consecutive years. Simple bots, the category of automated traffic least sophisticated at evading detection, grew especially fast across that period, a trend the report's authors tied directly to the rapid, cheap availability of generative AI tools capable of producing convincing text, images, and interactions at a scale no human workforce could match.

This is the practical problem a wave of researchers and companies started calling, with varying degrees of formality, proof of personhood: some reliable way to establish that an entity acting online is a real, distinct human being, without necessarily disclosing who that human is. The most visible and most controversial attempt at solving it commercially is World, the project formerly known as Worldcoin, co-founded in 2021 by OpenAI's Sam Altman alongside Alex Blania and Max Novendstern under the company Tools for Humanity. The project's central artifact is the Orb, a chrome sphere about the size of a bowling ball fitted with infrared cameras and depth sensors, designed to scan a person's iris and convert the unique pattern into an encrypted code — an IrisHash — checked against a global registry to confirm the same person has not already registered. A successful scan issues a World ID, a credential that can later be used, through zero-knowledge cryptographic proofs, to demonstrate that its holder is a unique verified human without revealing which specific human it is. World ID launched publicly in July 2023, with plans to deploy orbs in more than thirty-five cities across twenty countries; the company has offered signups roughly fifty dollars in its associated cryptocurrency as an incentive, and by the time the project had scanned several million people, it remained at only a small fraction of its stated ambition of eventually verifying a billion humans worldwide.

The reasoning behind building a company specifically to scan human eyeballs becomes more legible once it's placed against the CAPTCHA problem and the bad-bot statistics above. If AI models can now convincingly pass as human across nearly every text-based, voice-based, and increasingly video-based interaction the internet has traditionally relied on to sort people from machines, then the only remaining reliable signal is something tied irreducibly to a physical human body, checked once against a registry that prevents the same body from generating multiple identities. That is, in the vocabulary of the previous chapter, exactly the kind of costly, hard-to-fake signal Zahavi's peacocks and Spence's job-market signals depend on: an iris pattern is expensive, in the relevant sense, for an AI system or a bot farm to fake or duplicate at scale, in a way that a password, an email address, or even a phone number verification no longer reliably is.

The academic and policy conversation converged on similar territory from a more cautious angle. In 2024, a group of more than thirty researchers from institutions including OpenAI, Microsoft, MIT, and Harvard published a paper introducing the concept of personhood credentials — a broader category than any single company's product, describing privacy-preserving tools that could let someone prove they are a unique human without necessarily surrendering biometric data to any single company's database. The paper's authors treated the underlying need as increasingly urgent rather than speculative, while remaining notably wary of any single centralized solution, biometric or otherwise, becoming a chokepoint controlled by one company or government. Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin had raised closely related concerns a year earlier in a widely read essay examining various approaches to what he called biometric proof of personhood, weighing the tradeoffs between convenience, privacy, and the risk of building a single point of failure — or a single point of control — into the infrastructure of online identity itself.

That tension between usefulness and control is not an abstract academic worry. A credential capable of proving, cheaply and reliably, that its holder is one unique human being is also a credential capable of excluding anyone who cannot or will not obtain one, and a credential built around a centrally maintained biometric registry concentrates an enormous amount of sensitive information and infrastructural power in whatever entity maintains that registry. Those are not hypothetical downstream risks to be waved away as somebody else's problem for later. They are the direct, structural cost of the very mechanism that makes personhood credentials valuable in the first place, and they deserve their own extended treatment, which the chapters ahead will give them, once the commercial landscape building around all of this verification infrastructure has been laid out in full.

For now, the point worth sitting with is how quickly the internet's oldest, most taken-for-granted test of humanity became obsolete. CAPTCHA worked for two decades because distinguishing a human from a machine used to be a genuinely hard computational problem, one that fell naturally out of the gap between what people could easily do and what software could not. Multimodal AI closed that gap in the space of a few years, and in doing so, forced an entire category of security infrastructure — quietly embedded in login pages, comment sections, ticket sales, and account registration forms across the internet — back to the drawing board. What has replaced it, imperfectly and controversially, is not a smarter puzzle but a return to something much older and much more physical: the stubborn, expensive, singular fact of having one body, one iris, one voice, that no other body can perfectly replicate, at least not yet.
