# When Verification Gets Weaponized

Worldcoin's own compensation model, offering roughly fifty dollars in cryptocurrency for an iris scan, deserves scrutiny on its own terms before this chapter turns to the specific national bans that followed, because the model's structure previews exactly which populations end up bearing the greatest share of a new verification technology's risk. Fifty dollars represents a meaningfully different incentive in Kenya, where it can approach a significant fraction of a month's income for many residents, than it does in a wealthy country where the same sum barely covers a dinner out — meaning the offer's persuasive power to induce someone to permanently register an unchangeable biometric identifier was never evenly distributed across the populations Worldcoin approached globally. A company seeking to build a global biometric registry as quickly as possible has an obvious commercial incentive to concentrate its enrollment efforts in the places where a fixed dollar incentive goes furthest, which is precisely why so much of Worldcoin's early enrollment activity concentrated in lower-income countries across Africa, Latin America, and South Asia rather than being spread evenly across its wealthier target markets — a pattern regulators in several of those same countries would go on to cite explicitly as evidence the arrangement was exploiting exactly the population least equipped to evaluate a permanent biometric tradeoff against an immediate cash incentive.

Kenya's Ministry of the Interior suspended Worldcoin's operations in the country on August 2, 2023, after the project had already registered hundreds of thousands of people, many drawn by the roughly fifty-dollar cryptocurrency incentive offered for an iris scan in a country where that sum represents a meaningful amount of money. Regulators cited concerns about national security and about what would happen to a large, centrally held database of biometric data collected from a population with comparatively limited legal or practical recourse if that data were later misused, sold, or breached. Kenya was the first country to act, but not the last. Spain's data protection authority ordered Worldcoin to halt biometric data collection in March 2024, following complaints that the company made it difficult for users to withdraw consent and had collected data from minors; the company ultimately agreed to suspend its Spanish operations for the remainder of that year. Hong Kong's privacy regulator followed in May 2024, ruling that Worldcoin's practice of retaining sensitive iris and facial data for up to ten years, ostensibly to train its own AI models, was not justified relative to the stated purpose of issuing a one-time verification credential, and ordered the company to halt local operations entirely.

The regulatory pattern across these three very different jurisdictions converges on the same underlying worry, stated with varying degrees of bluntness: a company asking people to permanently register a piece of their own body — an iris pattern that cannot be changed the way a password or even a face can be altered by surgery or simply aging — in exchange for a modest cash incentive, is not obviously a fair transaction, particularly when the people most likely to accept the incentive are disproportionately poorer, younger, or less equipped to evaluate the long-term privacy tradeoff against an immediate, tangible payment. This is precisely the criticism Vitalik Buterin raised in his own examination of biometric proof-of-personhood systems: whatever privacy protections a system builds in through zero-knowledge cryptography at the verification stage, the initial act of scanning and registering a biometric identifier still requires trusting the company doing the scanning not to retain, misuse, sell, or lose the underlying biometric data itself, and that trust is being asked of exactly the population with the least leverage to withhold it.

The concern generalizes well beyond any single company's iris-scanning orb. Any infrastructure built to answer the question "is this a real, unique human being" inherits a structural tension that the previous chapters' celebration of costly signaling glossed over somewhat too neatly: a signal that is differentially expensive to fake is also, almost by definition, differentially expensive or difficult for some real humans to produce. A biometric scan assumes a functioning iris or face or fingerprint, which is not a universal assumption across people with certain disabilities or injuries. A government-issued identity document assumes access to a functioning government willing to issue one, which is not a safe assumption for stateless people, many refugees, and a meaningful population of undocumented residents even in wealthy countries. A verification system that requires a smartphone, a stable internet connection, and comfort navigating a digital onboarding flow assumes exactly the kind of access that a portion of any population, disproportionately the elderly and the poor, does not reliably have. Every one of these gaps existed before generative AI made verification newly urgent; what has changed is that verification is becoming a precondition for participating in ordinary digital life — opening a bank account, applying for a job, posting on a platform without being throttled as a suspected bot — rather than a rare, occasional requirement, which raises the practical stakes of exclusion considerably.

China's decades-old real-name internet registration system, examined several chapters earlier as a third model alongside Worldcoin's corporate approach and the European Union's government wallet, is the clearest existing example of exactly this harder concern already realized rather than merely feared. Real-name verification there is not optional, is not limited to a single platform or use case, and is enforced through a national legal framework — the 2016 Cybersecurity Law and its successors — that requires nearly every domestic internet service to tie a user's account to their verified government identity before allowing them to participate at all. That infrastructure has demonstrably been used for purposes well beyond fraud prevention or bot detection: identifying and prosecuting government critics, enforcing content restrictions tied to a specific, traceable individual rather than an anonymous account, and closing off the kind of pseudonymous political speech that has historically given dissidents in authoritarian states at least some protective distance from consequences. None of this required a hypothetical future government to repurpose a well-intentioned system after the fact, the scenario the Worldcoin regulators and Buterin both worried about in the abstract; the same infrastructure serves both the mundane fraud-prevention function and the surveillance function simultaneously, by design, because in a system built and controlled by a single state authority, there was never a meaningful boundary between the two purposes to begin with.

There is a harder version of this concern that moves from accidental exclusion to deliberate control. A centralized biometric identity system, once built and normalized for a benign purpose like reducing bot traffic or preventing fraud, is also, mechanically, a tool that could be repurposed by a government to track, deanonymize, or exclude specific individuals or populations from digital participation entirely. This is not a hypothetical concern invented by privacy advocates with an axe to grind; it is the explicit reason several of the regulators who acted against Worldcoin gave for their decisions, and it echoes a much older pattern in which identification infrastructure built for one stated purpose — a national identity card meant to streamline benefits administration, a biometric voter registry meant to prevent electoral fraud — has, in various countries and various periods, later been used for purposes its original architects did not intend and its original subjects did not consent to. A system's designers building in good intentions and privacy-preserving cryptography at launch is a meaningfully different guarantee than a system remaining safe from misuse across every future government or corporate owner who might eventually control it, particularly once biometric registries begin to be aggregated, sold, subpoenaed, or seized.

Even the more modest, content-focused verification infrastructure described earlier in this book carries a milder version of the same tension. A world in which unlabeled content is treated with suspicion by default, and labeled, provenance-verified content is treated as more credible, creates a real incentive for exactly the kind of authoritarian or corporate actor least deserving of extra credibility to obtain the labeling infrastructure's blessing anyway, whether through compliance, capture, or simply having the resources to adopt an expensive verification standard that a smaller, more resource-constrained but entirely honest publisher cannot afford to implement. Differential cost, the mechanism that makes a signal meaningful in the first place, cuts in an uncomfortable direction here: if verified provenance becomes expensive enough to obtain, the mechanism risks systematically favoring well-resourced institutional actors over small, independent, or foreign-language publishers who may have neither the technical capacity nor the standing invitation to join a coalition like C2PA, regardless of how honestly they report. The state-linked influence operations examined in the previous chapter add a further, more direct version of the same worry: an entity with the resources of a government, as both the Doppelganger and Spamouflage networks had, is precisely the kind of actor most capable of eventually meeting whatever costly verification bar gets built, while a lone independent journalist or a small newsroom in a country with limited institutional support may not be.

The government-issued alternative to a corporate biometric registry, examined several chapters earlier in the European Union's Digital Identity Wallet program, does not escape this tension so much as relocate it. A state-run identity wallet avoids handing a private company like Tools for Humanity a global biometric database funded by venture capital and a speculative cryptocurrency token, which is a genuine improvement by the standard Buterin and the personhood-credentials researchers have both articulated. But it does so by concentrating the same underlying capability — the power to decide who counts as a verified person, and to track when and where that verification is used — inside a different kind of institution, one with its own history of biometric registries being repurposed for surveillance, denial of services, or exclusion of specific populations once a government's priorities or leadership change. Neither the corporate nor the state-run model eliminates the concentration-of-power problem this chapter is describing; each merely locates that concentration in a different actor, with a different, but not necessarily smaller, set of historical reasons to worry about how the power might eventually be used.

There is an additional dimension of exclusion this book has not yet directly named, one that combines the accessibility problems already raised with the arms-race dynamics examined in the chapters on watermarking's limits and biometric spoofing: verification infrastructure built around expensive, sophisticated countermeasures tends to serve well-resourced institutions considerably better than it serves individuals or small organizations trying to defend themselves against the same threats using the same infrastructure. A national government or a major technology platform can afford to continuously update its deepfake-detection models, commission its own forensic experts, and absorb the cost of an occasional false accusation; an individual targeted by a fabricated recording, like Eric Eiswert in this book's chapter on the Pikesville case, or a small newsroom targeted by a spoofed domain, like the outlets Doppelganger impersonated, typically cannot. The same verification infrastructure that promises to protect everyone equally in the abstract tends, in practice, to arrive first and work best for the parties who least need protecting, precisely because they are the parties who can afford to build and maintain it, while the parties most vulnerable to the underlying harms this book has documented are frequently the last to receive any of the infrastructure meant to guard against them.

The exclusion concerns this chapter has raised are not evenly distributed across the verification mechanisms examined throughout this book, which is worth stating explicitly rather than treating every system as equally suspect. A biometric orb requiring physical presence at a specific location excludes anyone without the time, mobility, or proximity to reach one, disproportionately burdening rural populations, people with disabilities, and anyone working multiple jobs with no flexibility to travel for a voluntary registration process. A government-issued digital wallet excludes, by definition, anyone the issuing government does not recognize as a citizen or resident with a documented right to one — precisely the stateless people, refugees, and undocumented residents this chapter raised earlier, for whom a state-run identity system is not merely inconvenient but structurally unavailable regardless of how well-designed its privacy protections are. A paid platform verification badge excludes, more simply, anyone unwilling or unable to pay a recurring subscription fee, converting a fraud-prevention signal into something closer to a wealth signal, a substitution this book's chapter on the two-tier internet examined directly. Each mechanism solves the underlying differential-cost problem this book's economics chapter identifies, but each imposes its exclusion along a different axis — geography, citizenship, income — meaning a genuinely careful designer has to ask not simply "does this verification system work" but "along which specific axis does this particular system's cost fall, and who sits on the wrong side of that particular line."

None of this is an argument for abandoning verification infrastructure and returning to an internet where nobody can distinguish a real video from a fabricated one, or a human commenter from a bot farm. The problems described in the earlier chapters of this book — the Arup fraud, the Slovakia and New Hampshire election deepfakes, the collapsing reliability of CAPTCHA, the sheer volume of automated traffic documented in the Imperva reports, the industrial-scale influence operations examined in the previous chapter — are real, expensive, and getting worse rather than better on their own. It is an argument for building that infrastructure with an unusually clear-eyed accounting of who bears its costs, and an unusually skeptical eye toward any single company or government positioned to become the sole gatekeeper of what counts as a verified human being or a verified piece of content. The personhood credentials researchers referenced several chapters earlier reached a similar conclusion from inside the field itself: the paper's authors treated a decentralized, privacy-preserving, multi-provider approach as considerably safer than a single dominant verification monopoly, corporate or governmental, precisely because a monopoly on proving who is human concentrates a kind of power that has historically been abused whenever it has been concentrated, independent of the intentions of whoever concentrates it first.

The uncomfortable truth this chapter has been building toward is that trust infrastructure has never been neutral, in any era. The seals, audits, and safety marks described earlier in this book were built by particular institutions, serving particular interests, at particular moments, and every one of them has, at some point in its history, been used to exclude someone unfairly or to protect an incumbent at a newcomer's expense, alongside the legitimate fraud prevention it also provided. The verification infrastructure being built now, in response to a genuinely new and genuinely serious problem, will not be exempt from that same duality. The question worth asking of every proof-of-human system now being proposed is not only whether it works, but who it works for, who it leaves out, and who ultimately controls it once it is built.
