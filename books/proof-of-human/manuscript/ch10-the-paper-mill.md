# The Paper Mill

The Open Science Collaboration set out to answer the question Daryl Bem's precognition papers had raised, directly and empirically, rather than argue about it in the abstract. Beginning in 2012, more than two hundred researchers organized a multi-year effort to replicate one hundred studies drawn from three of psychology's most prominent journals, following each original study's methods as closely as the published material allowed. When the results were published in Science in 2015, fewer than half of the replications reproduced a statistically significant effect in the same direction as the original study, and even among the studies that did replicate, the measured effect sizes were typically only about half as large as originally reported. The paper's authors pointed to structural incentives rather than individual dishonesty as the likely explanation: researchers and journals alike were rewarded for novel, striking findings and rarely rewarded at all for confirming or disconfirming earlier work, which meant the field's own machinery for self-correction had gone largely unused for years. Peer review, the discipline's equivalent of an audit, had been checking whether a paper's methods looked sound on paper, not whether the underlying result would hold up when someone else tried to find it again.

That distinction — between a method that looks sound and a result that is sound — turns out to describe a problem considerably larger than experimental psychology, and considerably more resistant to good intentions than the 2015 reproducibility project alone might suggest. Some of the most consequential examples surfaced not through a coordinated replication effort but through the patient, often unpaid work of individual scientists willing to look closely at material everyone else had accepted at face value.

Elisabeth Bik, a Dutch microbiologist, built an unlikely second career doing exactly that. Starting around 2014, Bik began systematically examining published biomedical papers for a specific, mechanical kind of evidence: duplicated or manipulated images, particularly the Western blots — a common laboratory technique for detecting specific proteins — that fill the results sections of molecular biology papers. In a landmark 2016 analysis, Bik and her coauthors examined roughly twenty thousand biomedical papers and found that about four percent contained images that had been inappropriately duplicated, and that around half of those duplications showed clear signs of deliberate manipulation rather than innocent copy-paste error. She has since made a full-time practice of this work, examining papers freely and posting her findings publicly, and by the mid-2020s had flagged problems in several thousand papers, with more than a thousand of those eventually retracted and hundreds more corrected or flagged with a formal expression of concern — a level of output that a single unpaid volunteer with a trained eye and a search engine achieved that outpaced what the journals' own editorial and peer-review processes had caught on their own. Bik has been characteristically blunt about what her work implies about the state of the field's self-policing: papers get published, cited, and built upon for years before anyone with the right expertise looks closely enough at the underlying images to notice they don't add up, and the industrial-scale production of fraudulent papers by so-called paper mills — commercial operations that manufacture fake or plagiarized research for paying authors seeking publication credit — has continued to grow faster than publishers' detection efforts, in her own description something closer to a rat race than a solved problem.

The most consequential case Bik-style image forensics helped surface in recent years did not involve an obscure junior researcher publishing in a low-tier journal, but the president of one of the most prestigious universities in the world. Marc Tessier-Lavigne, a neuroscientist who became president of Stanford University in 2016, saw his tenure end abruptly in 2023 after a series of articles in the student-run Stanford Daily raised detailed questions about data manipulation in papers he had coauthored earlier in his career. The university convened an independent panel, led by a former federal judge and staffed with outside scientists, that spent months reviewing more than fifty thousand documents and commissioning forensic image analysis before concluding that data had indeed been manipulated in several of the papers under review — including duplicated and digitally spliced Western blot images and altered microscopy — though the panel found no evidence that Tessier-Lavigne himself had known about or personally engaged in the manipulation, which appeared to have originated with individual lab members working under him. What the panel did find him responsible for was a broader failure of institutional culture: a lab environment that produced an unusually high rate of data-integrity problems across multiple papers over multiple years, and a pattern, once concerns began to surface, of failing to correct the scientific record decisively or promptly. Tessier-Lavigne announced his resignation and committed to retracting several of the affected papers. The case is instructive less for any particular finding of misconduct than for how long it took, and how much external pressure it required, before an internal review reached the same conclusion that outside sleuths using publicly available images and a critical eye had already been circling for months.

A parallel case, unfolding on an almost identical timeline at Harvard, involved a scholar whose entire academic reputation had been built studying the very behavior her critics accused her of engaging in. Francesca Gino, a professor at Harvard Business School, had become one of the most prominent researchers in behavioral science, publishing widely cited work on dishonesty, ethics, and decision-making — including, with a certain grim irony that did not go unnoticed once the allegations surfaced, research specifically about when and why people cheat. In 2021, a group of research-integrity bloggers who write collectively as Data Colada — Uri Simonsohn, Leif Nelson, and Joseph Simmons, three academics at separate institutions who had built a reputation for forensic statistical detective work — began publishing a series of posts alleging that at least four of Gino's papers contained fabricated data, based on inconsistencies in the underlying datasets that were difficult to explain as innocent error. Harvard Business School conducted its own investigation, ultimately concluding that Gino was responsible for research misconduct and recommending her termination; the university placed her on unpaid leave and moved to revoke her tenure, a step so rare that no full professor is known to have lost tenure at Harvard for misconduct in roughly eighty years. Gino has denied the allegations and sued Harvard, its business school dean, and the Data Colada bloggers for defamation and gender discrimination, seeking $25 million in damages; a federal judge dismissed her claims against the individual bloggers and much of her case against Harvard in 2024, though aspects of the broader dispute continued in litigation afterward. Whatever its ultimate legal resolution, the Gino case illustrates the same lesson as the Tessier-Lavigne case from a different angle: the detection came not from the journals that had published the papers, not from the peer reviewers who had approved them, and not from Harvard's own oversight structures operating proactively, but from independent outside researchers who took it upon themselves to scrutinize a colleague's data closely enough to find the seams — a service the scientific publishing system has never adequately built into its own architecture, and instead depends on unpaid volunteers like Bik and Data Colada to provide, largely for free and often at real professional and legal risk to themselves.

Neither the American nor the immediate transatlantic cases examined so far are unusual in an international sense; comparable failures, uncovered through comparably belated and often accidental means, have recurred across the scientific establishments of other countries as well. Diederik Stapel, a prominent Dutch social psychologist at Tilburg University, was for years one of his field's most prolific and widely cited researchers, publishing striking findings on subjects ranging from the psychology of meat-eating to how physical disorder affects discrimination. In 2011, three of his own graduate students grew suspicious after Stapel supplied them with datasets for papers without ever letting them participate in the underlying research himself, and their suspicions triggered an investigation across three Dutch universities. Stapel ultimately admitted to fabricating and manipulating data across at least fifty-five publications dating back to 2003, along with at least ten doctoral theses built on the same fabricated foundations; by 2015, the retraction count connected to his work had climbed to fifty-eight papers, making it one of the largest documented cases of individual scientific fraud on record. What makes the Stapel case particularly relevant to this book's argument is how it was caught: not by peer review, not by any journal's editorial process, and not by any of his university's oversight structures operating proactively, but by junior researchers within his own lab who noticed that the data they were being asked to analyze had never actually been collected in front of them — an internal, almost accidental discovery rather than the product of any systemic verification process functioning as intended.

A separate, internationally prominent case unfolded in Japan the same decade with an even higher public profile and a far darker outcome. In January 2014, a young researcher named Haruko Obokata, working at Japan's prestigious RIKEN research institute, published two papers in Nature describing what she called STAP cells — a purportedly simple method of inducing ordinary cells to become pluripotent stem cells merely by exposing them to acid stress, a discovery that would have been revolutionary if it had held up. Laboratories around the world attempted to replicate the results and consistently failed. RIKEN's own investigative committee concluded within months that Obokata had falsified and doctored data and images in the papers, and both were formally retracted from Nature by mid-2014. The scandal's human cost extended well beyond the retraction: Yoshiki Sasai, a senior RIKEN scientist who had supervised aspects of Obokata's work and had staked considerable personal credibility on the STAP cell claims, died by suicide in August 2014 amid the ongoing fallout, a tragedy that added a grim, human dimension to what might otherwise have remained a comparatively abstract story about failed replication and damaged institutional reputations. Obokata herself was later stripped of her doctorate, and RIKEN's own credibility as a national scientific institution took years to recover.

Retraction Watch, the outlet that has tracked scientific retractions systematically since 2010, has documented a steadily rising retraction count across the scientific literature as a whole over the past decade, a trend its own editors attribute to some mixture of more fraud, more sophisticated fraud detection, and a publishing industry more willing than it once was to act on the evidence detection tools surface — three explanations that are not mutually exclusive and that together paint a genuinely ambiguous picture of whether science is getting more corrupt, better at catching corruption, or both simultaneously. Complicating that picture further is the specific, rapidly escalating threat of paper mills: commercial operations, some traced to networks operating primarily out of a handful of countries, that manufacture entire fabricated studies, complete with plausible-sounding data and sometimes purchased or coerced co-authorship slots, sold to researchers under career pressure to publish regardless of whether they conducted or even understand the underlying "research." Major publishers have begun deploying their own automated screening tools specifically to catch paper-mill submissions before they reach peer review at all, an arms race that mirrors, in its basic shape, nearly every other verification contest this book examines: as detection improves, the mills adapt, and the same generative AI tools discussed earlier in this book have begun showing up on the production side of that arms race too, capable of manufacturing plausible-sounding scientific prose, invented citations, and even fabricated data tables at a volume no earlier generation of paper mill could have matched by hand.

Some fields have tried to build an equivalent forcing function deliberately, through a practice called preregistration, which requires researchers to publicly commit to a study's hypotheses, sample size, and analysis plan before collecting any data, closing off the after-the-fact flexibility that lets a researcher quietly reshape a null result into a publishable positive finding once the data are already in hand. Preregistration functions as a costly signal in exactly the sense this book's economics chapter describes: it is costly precisely because it forecloses options a researcher would otherwise have to adjust the analysis after seeing inconvenient results, and a study's preregistered plan can be checked against its eventual published findings by anyone with the time to compare the two documents side by side. Adoption has grown substantially across psychology and medicine since the replication crisis this chapter opened with, though it remains far from universal, and it addresses only one specific failure mode among the many this chapter documents — it does the least to stop the kind of outright, deliberate data fabrication Stapel and Obokata each engaged in, since a fabricator willing to invent results from nothing is equally willing to invent them within a preregistered design as outside one. Preregistration raises the cost of a specific, common form of self-deception. It does little against a researcher who has already decided to lie outright.

One structural feature separates the scientific cases in this chapter from the corporate ones examined in the previous chapter, and it is worth naming before drawing the connection between them. A company's fraud is eventually checked, however belatedly, by the hard discipline of cash: a business that has been lying about its finances eventually runs out of real money to cover the gap between its reported and actual position, which is what finally forced Wirecard's and FTX's collapses into the open regardless of how long their respective auditors had been looking the other way. Science has no equivalent forcing function. A fabricated result that nobody attempts to replicate can simply sit in the literature indefinitely, cited, built upon, and taught to students, with no analogous moment at which the underlying fiction is forced to reconcile against an external, unforgiving reality the way a bank balance eventually must. This is precisely why the detection burden in every case this chapter examines fell to unpaid, uncredentialed volunteers — Elisabeth Bik, the Data Colada bloggers, three suspicious graduate students — rather than to any structural mechanism built into the system itself: without cash flow's blunt discipline forcing an eventual reckoning, science's self-correction has always depended more heavily on individual vigilance than finance's does, a dependency generative AI's ability to manufacture plausible-looking fabricated data threatens to overwhelm.

What unites Bem, Tessier-Lavigne, Gino, Stapel, Obokata, and the broader paper-mill economy is the same structural lesson the previous chapter drew from Theranos, Wirecard, Enron, FTX, and Wells Fargo, arriving by an entirely different route. Peer review, like an audit, is a costly signal only if it is actually costly — if reviewers meaningfully interrogate a submission's methods and, ideally, its underlying data, rather than checking that the paper looks like the kind of paper that normally gets published in that journal. For most of its modern history, peer review has functioned closer to the latter than the former: reviewers are unpaid, working on their own time, evaluating a manuscript's plausibility and methodological presentation rather than independently reproducing its results or forensically examining its raw images, because no journal has ever built the infrastructure, time, or budget to ask that of them at scale. That gap between what peer review is popularly believed to guarantee and what it has ever actually been resourced to check is not a new vulnerability introduced by generative AI. It is a preexisting condition, discovered gradually over more than a decade by outside volunteers doing forensic work the system was never designed to do internally — and it is a condition that the same generative tools now threaten to make dramatically worse, by making it cheaper than ever to manufacture the kind of paper that looks, on the surface, exactly like the kind of paper peer review has always been willing to wave through.
