# The Body Double

Saddam Hussein, by the estimate of several journalists and defectors who studied his security apparatus, employed as many as fifty men over the course of his rule whose primary function was to look like him. The most thoroughly documented of these doubles, a man named Mikhael Ramadan, has described in subsequent interviews and a memoir a process that verged on the surgical: cosmetic procedures to adjust his features closer to Hussein's own, and years of coaching in the dictator's specific gait, gestures, vocal cadence, and even his particular way of smoking a cigar, all in service of standing in for the real man at public events, television appearances, and processions where the risk of assassination or the simple need to be in two places at once made a convincing substitute valuable enough to justify the extraordinary investment required to produce one. Uday Hussein, Saddam's son, maintained his own double, an Iraqi military officer named Latif Yahia who has since written extensively about being coerced into the role and the psychological toll of essentially ceasing to exist as himself for years at a stretch.

What makes the Hussein doubles worth examining in a book otherwise focused on artificial intelligence is not the specific regime but the economics of the underlying deception, viewed through the lens this book's later chapters develop in more theoretical terms. Producing a single convincing human body double required finding a person who already, by pure biological accident, resembled the target closely enough to be a viable candidate — a scarce starting condition entirely outside anyone's control — and then investing years of surgery, coaching, and rehearsal to close whatever gap remained. The entire apparatus produced, at the end of that costly process, exactly one substitute, usable in a narrow set of circumstances where the double's own limitations in knowledge, voice, and improvisational ability could be concealed: a scripted public appearance, a wave from a balcony, a brief scripted statement, never an unscripted conversation that might expose the substitute's ignorance of information only the real man would know. This is costly signaling in its most literal, physical form, the same logic this book's chapter on the handicap principle and job market signaling will examine in the abstract: the double was expensive to produce, limited in what it could convincingly do, and detectable by anyone who spent enough unscripted time with it.

It is worth being explicit about the economics that made a body double program like Hussein's rare even among history's most paranoid and well-resourced rulers, because the rarity itself is evidence for this book's central argument. Producing a single viable double required first locating a candidate through pure chance — a person who, by an accident of genetics, already resembled the target closely enough to make the rest of the investment worthwhile, a starting condition no amount of money could manufacture on demand. It then required years of surgery, coaching, and rehearsal, all of it invisible to any outside observer and all of it wasted the moment the double aged, gained weight, was injured, or simply proved unable to master the target's specific mannerisms convincingly. Only a small number of the wealthiest and most powerful individuals in history had reason to make that investment at all, and even they, by every credible account, maintained only a handful of doubles at any given time, each reserved for narrow, low-scrutiny occasions. This was never a capability available to an ordinary criminal, an ordinary political operative, or an ordinary disgruntled employee; it was a bespoke, artisanal capability restricted to the apex of state power, precisely because its cost floor was so high and so resistant to being lowered by anything except more money and more luck.

Political and military history is thick with variations on the same theme, each shaped by the specific technology and stakes of its era. British planners during the Second World War reportedly considered using a body double for Field Marshal Bernard Montgomery ahead of the Normandy invasion, a decoy meant to appear in Gibraltar and North Africa to confuse German intelligence about the timing and location of the coming Allied offensive, exploiting the simple fact that photographs and brief public sightings, unlike sustained conversation, could not easily distinguish a well-selected look-alike from the genuine article at the distances and durations involved. Soviet leadership under Stalin was rumored, credibly by some historical accounts and less credibly by others, to have employed doubles for public appearances during a period when assassination attempts and wartime security concerns made the leader's exact movements a matter of state secrecy. What unites these cases, real and apocryphal alike, is that the underlying deception was always bounded by the physical limitations of a human substitute: a double could stand in a specific place, wave in a specific way, and deliver a specific memorized line, but could not sustain a real-time, unscripted conversation, could not respond convincingly to an unexpected question, and could not be deployed at scale, since producing even one usable double required rare physical resemblance and years of preparation.

There is an irony worth drawing out explicitly here, given everything the rest of this book documents. Putin and Kim's rumored doubles, if they exist at all, would represent exactly the older, expensive, physically constrained model of impersonation this chapter has spent its pages describing — a costly, rare, carefully guarded human resource, deployed for narrow public occasions. Neither leader appears to have needed anything more sophisticated than that older model to sustain decades of persistent doubt about their own whereabouts and appearances, because the rumor itself, true or false, does the political work regardless of whether a physical double actually exists. Compare that to the New Hampshire robocall or the Slovak election recording examined later in this book, where a single operator with a few dollars of consumer software produced a fabrication capable of disrupting an actual democratic process within days, and the contrast becomes sharp: state-level political theater built around body-double rumors has apparently found the mystique of a merely possible impersonation sufficient for its purposes, while ordinary individual fraudsters, freed from needing any physical resemblance at all, have already moved well past rumor and into functional, deployable fabrication.

The practice, or at least the rumor of it, has never entirely disappeared from authoritarian politics, which is itself worth noting before this chapter turns to what changed. A former chief of Britain's foreign intelligence service has said publicly that he believes Vladimir Putin uses body doubles for security reasons, citing assassination risk and the difficulty of otherwise explaining certain public appearances; speculation intensified sharply after Putin's June 2024 visit to North Korea, when social media users pored over footage arguing the man on screen did not quite match the Russian president's known mannerisms. The Kremlin has dismissed every such claim as fabrication, and no conclusive public evidence has settled the question either way. Kim Jong-un has faced parallel, similarly unresolved speculation, including footage from 2017 that international media described as showing him alongside a suspected decoy. What is notable about both cases is less whether the doubles are real than what the persistent rumor itself accomplishes: a leader who governs through opacity and fear gains a certain mystique from the mere possibility that he can appear in two places at once, an aura that costs nothing to cultivate precisely because it can never be fully disproven, only endlessly speculated about. That the speculation persists into an era when deepfake technology could make the underlying claim trivially verifiable or fabricable in a video, rather than requiring an actual physical stand-in, is itself a sign of how slowly some older forms of political theater adapt to newly available tools.

Generative AI collapses every one of those limitations simultaneously, which is precisely why this book's opening chapters spend so much time on cases that would have been physically impossible under the body-double model of impersonation. A synthetic video call, unlike Mikhael Ramadan's years of surgery and coaching, requires no person who happens to resemble the target; it requires only enough recorded footage and audio of the target to train a model, footage that a public figure or even an ordinary corporate executive generates constantly and involuntarily simply by attending video conferences, giving interviews, or posting to social media. A synthetic voice can respond to unscripted questions in real time, at least well enough to sustain a brief fraudulent phone call or video meeting, a capability no human body double, however well trained, could offer under sustained real-time pressure. And where Hussein's apparatus could produce, at enormous cost, a small number of doubles for a small number of specific occasions, the tools examined throughout this book can produce an effectively unlimited number of synthetic impersonations of an effectively unlimited number of targets, simultaneously, at a marginal cost approaching zero.

Ancient and medieval rulers relied on a related but distinct practice worth mentioning briefly for how it illuminates the specific function a double serves. Rather than physical impersonation, many courts relied on tightly controlled access and formalized ceremony to accomplish something similar: a ruler rarely seen up close, appearing mainly at a distance, through ritual, or via trusted intermediaries, made an impersonation attempt both harder to stage convincingly and less likely to be detected if it succeeded, since few people had a clear enough memory of the ruler's actual appearance and mannerisms to notice a substitution. This is a variant of the same underlying strategy examined throughout this chapter: rather than paying the cost of maintaining an actual trained double, a ruler could achieve some of the same security benefit by simply making the genuine article scarce and hard to observe closely, reducing the number of people positioned to notice a discrepancy in the first place. Scarcity of access functioned as its own kind of costly signal protection, a strategy modern public figures, who now perform for cameras constantly and are recorded in exhaustive, searchable detail across years of footage, no longer have available to them at all.

Latif Yahia's own published account of standing in for Uday Hussein illustrates exactly where the body-double model's physical limits actually bit, in a way worth dwelling on because it maps so precisely onto the gap this book's later chapters describe between a synthetic voice's fluency and a real relationship's accumulated depth. Yahia has described the role as less an acting performance than an exhausting, years-long impersonation under constant threat of exposure and violence, requiring him to absorb Uday's mannerisms, preferences, and history closely enough to survive encounters with people who had known the original for decades — people whose long, cumulative familiarity with the real man was precisely the kind of signal a double, however well trained, could not fully counterfeit. Where the double's performance held up in public, scripted settings, it was reportedly at its most fragile in private, unscripted moments with people who knew Uday well enough to notice small inconsistencies: a preference misremembered, a story told slightly wrong, a reaction that did not quite match the pattern of years of prior acquaintance. A double could rehearse a public role indefinitely. He could not rehearse every private relationship the man he was impersonating had ever built, because he had not lived the years that built them.

This is the same story this book's economics chapter tells in more abstract, general terms, but the body-double comparison makes the scale of the change viscerally concrete in a way abstract economic language sometimes does not. A twentieth-century dictator with a state's full resources behind him could produce perhaps a few dozen convincing human impersonations of himself across an entire lifetime, each one a scarce, carefully guarded asset deployed only when the stakes justified the investment and the risk of exposure. A single scammer working the Arup fraud examined in this book's opening pages needed no state resources, no rare physical resemblance, and no years of preparation to produce a passable synthetic impersonation of several different corporate executives simultaneously, deployed within days of deciding to attempt the fraud at all. The underlying human desire to convincingly appear as someone else, for reasons ranging from personal safety to fraud to political theater, is ancient and well documented across history. What generative AI changed is not that desire but its price, taking a capability that used to require a nation-state's resources and a rare stroke of physical luck and making a version of it available, cheaply and instantly, to anyone with an internet connection and a target whose face and voice already exist somewhere online.
