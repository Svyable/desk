# The Two-Tier Internet

Twitter introduced its blue checkmark in June 2009 to solve a narrow, specific problem: a small number of celebrities and public figures were being impersonated by parody and fraud accounts convincingly enough that the platform decided some visible marker of confirmed identity was worth building. For more than a decade, the checkmark worked as a version of the costly signal this book has examined throughout — not costly in money, but costly in scarcity and process. Getting verified required Twitter's own staff to confirm, through some combination of documentation and notability, that an account belonged to the specific real person or organization it claimed to represent. The checkmark's value came precisely from the fact that not everyone could get one; it was differentially available to accounts Twitter had specifically vetted, which is what made its presence meaningful to anyone encountering an account for the first time.

Elon Musk's acquisition of Twitter in late 2022 severed that link almost immediately. Beginning in November 2022, and formalized through the rollout of a paid subscription called Twitter Blue, the checkmark became available to any account willing to pay a monthly fee and provide a phone number, with the platform's identity-vetting function largely dropped from the criteria entirely. By April 2023, the company had stripped the checkmark from accounts that had earned it under the old, identity-verified standard unless they also subscribed to the new paid tier, producing the specific, almost poetic irony of long-verified news organizations and public officials losing their badges while anyone willing to pay eight dollars a month could acquire an identical-looking one. The checkmark did not disappear; its underlying signal simply changed from "this identity has been confirmed" to "this account has an active payment method on file," a transformation that, read through this book's economics chapter, replaced one costly signal — genuine identity verification, expensive in staff time and vetting rigor — with a different, much weaker one: a small recurring subscription fee, cheap enough that it filtered out almost nobody a bad actor would actually want filtered out.

Meta, watching a competitor experiment with monetizing verification, launched its own parallel product within months. Meta Verified, rolled out across Instagram and Facebook beginning in early 2023 at a monthly price in roughly the ten-to-fifteen-dollar range depending on platform and device, offered a blue badge in exchange for a subscription fee and a government ID check at signup — a hybrid model that retained a nod toward the older identity-verification standard while still making the badge available to any paying subscriber willing to submit documentation, rather than reserving it for the narrower set of accounts a platform's own staff had judged independently notable or at meaningful risk of impersonation. Both companies' pivot toward paid verification reflects an unglamorous but entirely explicable business logic: subscription revenue is a much more reliable, scalable income stream than an unpaid identity-vetting staff has ever been, and a platform under financial pressure has an obvious incentive to convert a free trust signal into a paid product, whether or not doing so preserves the signal's original informational value.

This history matters for this book's broader argument for a reason that goes beyond social media trivia. The blue checkmark's transformation is a uniquely clean, publicly documented case study of exactly the mechanism the economics chapter earlier in this book described in the abstract: a signal's value depends entirely on what it actually costs to obtain, not on what it visually appears to represent, and changing the underlying cost structure — from "pass an identity check" to "pay a monthly fee" — can hollow out a signal's meaning while leaving its outward appearance completely unchanged. A user encountering a blue-checked account in 2024 had no simple way to tell, from the badge alone, whether they were looking at a verified public figure under the platform's old standard or a stranger who had paid eight dollars that morning specifically to look more credible while running a scam, impersonating a celebrity, or amplifying a coordinated influence campaign of the kind examined in an earlier chapter of this book. Adoption of the new paid tier, at least in its early months, turned out to be far more modest than the platform's public messaging implied. Internal company documents reported by journalists in early 2023 indicated that the relaunched paid verification service had attracted fewer than 300,000 subscribers at a time when the platform counted its active user base in the hundreds of millions, a subscription rate low enough to suggest that most users, whatever they thought of the new badge's meaning, were not actually willing to pay for it. That modest uptake matters for this book's broader argument: a weak signal does not necessarily spread everywhere just because it has been made available cheaply. Plenty of users apparently judged the new checkmark not worth even its reduced price, which meant the accounts that did carry a paid badge were, if anything, a more selected group than the platform's redesigned system intended — sometimes genuinely eager fans of the platform, and sometimes, as the following paragraph documents, exactly the bad actors for whom a cheap, purchasable badge was worth the price precisely because it made a scam look more credible.

Researchers and journalists documented exactly this pattern in the months following the changeover: paid-verification badges being used to lend false credibility to impersonation accounts, cryptocurrency scam profiles, and accounts impersonating public officials and companies, precisely the abuse the original 2009 verification system had been built to prevent.

YouTube has pursued a third variant of verification worth noting briefly, distinct from both Twitter's payment-based badge and Meta's ID-checked one: a subscriber-count threshold, generally requiring well over one hundred thousand subscribers alongside strong overall channel engagement before granting a badge automatically, rather than requiring either a fee or a submitted identity document. TikTok's own approach layers a related but distinct criterion on top of that same instinct, explicitly declining to key its badge to follower count alone and instead requiring evidence of outside notability — credible press coverage, an established public profile beyond the platform itself — meaning a TikTok account with ten thousand followers and real media coverage can be verified while a purchased-follower account with a million cannot. Both models substitute a different kind of cost for the ones already examined in this chapter: not money, and not a document check, but accumulated audience size or outside recognition, signals closer in spirit to Spence's job-market signaling than to either of the other two models, since both are, in principle, harder to fake quickly than a subscription fee. Both carry a related failure mode, though: subscriber counts can themselves be inflated through bot farms and engagement rings of the kind this book's chapter on bad-bot traffic already documents, and even outside media coverage can, as this book's chapter on pink slime demonstrates, be manufactured by an operation willing to build its own network of ostensibly independent outlets to cite as evidence of the notability a platform is checking for.

The deeper pattern this episode reveals is what might fairly be called a two-tier internet, though not in the sense that phrase is sometimes used to describe unequal access to bandwidth or connectivity. This is a two-tier internet of credibility rather than access: one tier consisting of content and accounts carrying some form of verification — a paid badge, a content credential, a personhood credential, a professional certification — and a second, much larger tier consisting of everything else, increasingly treated by both human viewers and automated moderation systems as suspect by default, simply for lacking a marker that costs money or effort to obtain. That default suspicion is not entirely irrational, given everything this book's opening chapters document about how cheap fabrication has become. But it produces a genuinely uncomfortable distributional effect, one this book's chapter on verification being weaponized already previewed from a different angle: the credibility marker increasingly goes to whoever can pay for it or navigate its bureaucracy, rather than to whoever is actually telling the truth, and an anonymous or low-resource account making an entirely accurate claim can find itself treated with more suspicion than a paid, badge-holding account spreading confident nonsense, simply because one of them cleared a cost barrier that had nothing to do with either account's honesty.

A different, more encouraging model for what a paid or partnered verification badge could look like emerged the same year Twitter's checkmark was hollowed out, and it is worth holding up as a point of contrast. In April 2023, LinkedIn partnered with Clear — the same biometric identity company examined in the chapter on the verification economy for its airport security business — to offer nearly two hundred million American LinkedIn users free identity verification, extending the option to Mexican users later that year. A LinkedIn member who opts in submits a government ID and a selfie to Clear's existing biometric infrastructure and, if the two match, receives a badge indicating their identity has been independently confirmed against a real document, at no cost to the user and with no bundled subscription fee attached to the badge itself. The distinction between this model and Twitter's or Meta's is not subtle: LinkedIn's badge signals "an independent third party matched this account to a real, verified identity," restoring something closer to the original, pre-2022 meaning of a platform verification checkmark, while Twitter and Meta's paid tiers signal, at bottom, "this account is willing to pay a recurring fee." That LinkedIn could offer verification for free, subsidizing the cost rather than monetizing the badge directly, suggests the earlier platforms' shift toward paid verification was a business choice rather than a technical or economic necessity — trust infrastructure can still be built as a public good rather than a revenue line, when the platform building it decides that is worth doing.

There is a narrower, more defensible case for Meta's specific version of the ID-checked paid badge worth acknowledging before this chapter moves on, because not every paid verification product deserves the same skepticism this book has applied to Twitter's. Because Meta Verified requires an actual government ID check rather than merely a payment method, its badge retains at least a version of the original 2009 identity-vetting standard this chapter opened with, layered underneath the added engagement perks rather than replacing it entirely. A Meta Verified badge tells another user something a Twitter Blue badge, in its original form, did not: that a specific document was checked against a specific identity claim at the moment of signup, even if the badge's other bundled features are, as this chapter has argued, mostly unrelated engagement incentives. The lesson is not that every paid verification product is equally hollow, but that the specific design choice of whether to retain a genuine identity check underneath the subscription, as Meta did, or to drop it entirely in favor of a payment method alone, as Twitter initially did, produces two badges that look nearly identical on screen while carrying meaningfully different amounts of actual verified information behind them.

Instagram and Facebook's Meta Verified subscription, launched within months of Twitter's own relaunch, followed a strikingly similar bundling logic, pairing a government-ID-checked badge with proactive account protection, direct customer support, and increased visibility in search and recommendations — the same mix of genuine verification and unrelated engagement perks that made Twitter's tier such a mixed signal. Financial analysts covering the launch projected the service could attract on the order of ten million or more paying subscribers across Meta's platforms within its first year or two, a projection that, whatever its ultimate accuracy, reveals the same underlying commercial calculation driving Twitter's approach: a paid badge is easier to justify internally as a subscription-revenue product bundled with visibility perks than as a pure security feature, because subscription revenue shows up cleanly on a quarterly earnings call in a way that "we reduced impersonation fraud by some hard-to-measure amount" never quite does.

It is worth asking, too, what an ordinary user actually gains from a paid checkmark beyond the badge's visual signal, since the honest answer complicates any simple story about consumers rationally paying for security. Twitter's and Meta's paid tiers bundled the verification badge with a grab bag of largely unrelated features — algorithmic boosts to a subscriber's visibility in other users' feeds, longer post limits, reduced advertising, priority customer support — meaning a subscriber's eight or twelve dollars a month was never cleanly a payment for identity verification in the way a LinkedIn or Clear user's free enrollment was. Many subscribers, by the platforms' own marketing, signed up primarily for the algorithmic visibility boost or the reduced ad load, with the verification badge arriving as an automatic, almost incidental side effect of a purchase made for entirely different reasons. That bundling matters for this book's argument because it means the badge's remaining informational content is even weaker than a simple "willing to pay" signal suggests: a checkmark earned this way tells another user only that its holder wanted more visibility or fewer ads badly enough to pay for it, which is a considerably noisier signal about a stranger's trustworthiness than either the platform's original 2009 identity-vetting standard or LinkedIn's Clear-verified alternative examined above.

There is a genuine case to be made that some verification, however commercially motivated and imperfectly targeted, is better than none, and that a badge signaling "this account holder was willing to attach a payment method and, in Meta's case, a government ID to this identity" still raises the cost of casual impersonation above zero, even if it does nothing to stop a well-resourced bad actor willing to pay the modest fee. That is a real, if modest, improvement over no signal at all, consistent with this book's recurring observation that raising the cost of fabrication, even without eliminating it, changes who bothers to attempt it. But it is a considerably weaker signal than the one it replaced, and the platforms that built it have mostly marketed the badge using the visual and psychological register of the old, more rigorous verification system — the same blue checkmark shape, the same connotation of platform-endorsed credibility — without always being transparent with ordinary users about how substantially the underlying criteria had changed. The badge looks the same. What it actually costs to obtain, and therefore what it actually proves, is not the same at all, and the gap between those two facts is precisely where the two-tier internet's least visible harms tend to live.
