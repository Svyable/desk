# Skin in the Game

In late January 2024, sexually explicit images of Taylor Swift, generated by artificial intelligence and depicting her in scenarios that never occurred, began circulating on X, the platform formerly known as Twitter. They spread with a speed that outran the platform's ability, or willingness, to contain them: one widely cited image was viewed more than 45 million times before it was removed, accumulating hundreds of thousands of likes and reposts along the way. Swift's fans organized a countermeasure of their own, flooding the relevant hashtags with unrelated content to bury the images in search results — a grassroots response that arrived faster than any official one. X eventually blocked searches for Swift's name entirely, a blunt and temporary fix, and took down the original images while restating a policy against nonconsensual intimate imagery that had evidently done little to prevent this particular case from reaching tens of millions of views first.

Swift is, by a wide margin, one of the most famous and most legally resourced people on Earth, and the images still spread faster than any individual or platform response could contain them. That fact is the whole point of this chapter. If a global celebrity's team, a dedicated fan base numbering in the tens of millions, and a major platform's trust-and-safety apparatus could not prevent this category of harm from reaching a mass audience within hours, the same tools aimed at an ordinary person — a private citizen with no publicist, no legal team on retainer, and no fanbase capable of organizing a counter-campaign — face a problem with essentially no institutional backstop at all.

The pattern the Swift case exposed at a global scale had already been playing out in American high schools for at least a year, with almost none of the attention a global superstar's case commanded. In October 2023, male students at Westfield High School in New Jersey were found to have used a widely available "nudify" application called Clothoff to take ordinary, fully clothed social media photographs of female classmates and generate fake nude images from them, which then circulated through group chats among students at the school. Francesca Mani, one of the students targeted, became a prominent advocate for stronger legal protections after concluding that her school's disciplinary response left the boys responsible facing no meaningful consequences, and her subsequent advocacy work earned her a spot on Time's 2024 list of the most influential people in artificial intelligence — an unusual honor for a teenager whose primary qualification was having been victimized by the technology in question. Westfield was not an isolated case; a nationwide survey conducted by the Center for Democracy and Technology during the 2023–2024 school year found that forty percent of surveyed students and twenty-nine percent of surveyed teachers were aware of deepfake imagery depicting people at their own school, and journalists identified comparable incidents at schools in Pennsylvania, California, Texas, and Florida within roughly the same period. The "nudify" apps behind these incidents require no technical sophistication whatsoever to operate — a user uploads a photograph and receives a fabricated nude image within seconds — which means the barrier to committing this specific harm has fallen to functionally zero for any teenager with a smartphone and access to a classmate's public social media photos.

Non-consensual intimate imagery generated by AI, sometimes called deepfake pornography in casual usage, predates the Swift incident by years; researchers and journalists had been documenting the phenomenon, and its disproportionate use against women, since the original "deepfake" techniques emerged on Reddit in 2017. Sensity AI, a company that has tracked synthetic media online, estimated in earlier research that a substantial majority of deepfake videos in circulation were non-consensual pornographic content, and that the overwhelming majority of the people targeted were women who had never consented to appearing in pornography at all, let alone the specific fabricated scenario a stranger had generated using their face. What changed with the Swift case was not the existence of the harm but its visibility to an audience — legislators, journalists, and the general public — that had mostly been able to treat the problem as marginal or hypothetical until a sufficiently famous victim made it impossible to ignore.

Nothing that happened to Taylor Swift, however, approached the scale of what South Korean investigators uncovered a few months later, in August 2024, when journalists and the country's media regulator exposed a network of Telegram channels dedicated to generating and trading sexually explicit deepfake images of women and girls, many of them minors, using photographs stolen from their own social media accounts. One of the largest channels had accumulated more than 220,000 subscribers; police estimated that as many as 60,000 men had paid for access using gift vouchers and cryptocurrency. South Korea's government media watchdog received nearly 6,500 requests to remove sexually abusive deepfake content in just the first seven months of 2024, roughly four times the volume of the same period the year before, and reported crimes involving sexually explicit deepfakes climbed from 180 for all of 2023 to 297 in the first seven months of 2024 alone. What distinguished the South Korean case most sharply from the Swift incident was the age and identity of both victims and perpetrators: a large share of the material targeted schoolgirls and, in some documented instances, teachers, generated by classmates and, according to reporting on the case, sometimes by the victims' own family members, and of the suspects booked by police during the period studied, nearly three-quarters were between the ages of ten and nineteen — meaning much of this specific epidemic of sexual exploitation was, in a very literal sense, children victimizing children, at a scale and speed no purely adult-run criminal enterprise had achieved with nonconsensual imagery before generative AI made the tools this cheap and this easy to use anonymously in a group chat.

The DEFIANCE Act's civil remedy, examined below, is worth stress-testing against the South Korean case specifically, because the American statute's design assumes a traceable, identifiable defendant a victim can actually sue — the classmate who ran the app, the specific person who generated and shared the image. South Korea's Telegram network complicates that assumption considerably: with more than 220,000 subscribers accessing content through layers of pseudonymous accounts and paid access gates, many individual perpetrators were never identified at all, and a purely civil remedy modeled on the American statute would have given the vast majority of South Korean victims no realistic path to any individual defendant to sue in the first place. This is why South Korea's legislative response emphasized criminal prosecution and platform accountability — investigating Telegram itself, criminalizing mere possession to lower the evidentiary bar against a large anonymous audience — rather than primarily building out a civil litigation remedy of the kind American lawmakers reached for first. Different underlying network structures, in other words, called for different legal tools entirely, even though the harm both statutes were responding to was substantively the same.

Telegram's own role in the South Korean case deserves examination alongside X's role in the Swift incident, because the two platforms responded to nearly identical categories of harm in almost opposite ways. X, confronted with a single, highly visible, globally reported incident targeting one of the world's most famous women, moved within days to block search terms and remove the offending images, under enormous, concentrated public pressure focused on a single celebrity. Telegram, confronted with a far larger, more diffuse, and initially less internationally visible pattern of abuse spread across thousands of smaller private and semi-private channels rather than one viral public post, responded far more slowly, and South Korea's government ultimately opened a formal investigation into the platform itself for failing to adequately police the channels operating on its infrastructure. The contrast illustrates an uncomfortable asymmetry this book's chapters have raised in other contexts: platform response speed appears to track the visibility and concentration of public outrage at least as closely as it tracks the actual scale of harm, meaning a single famous victim can sometimes generate a faster institutional response than tens of thousands of anonymous ones, even when the anonymous victims collectively suffer far greater aggregate harm.

By early 2025, South Korean police had arrested 682 suspects connected to the Telegram network, and the National Assembly moved to stiffen the country's Sexual Violence Punishment Act specifically to criminalize mere possession of sexually explicit deepfake material, not merely its creation or distribution — a legislative response considerably broader in scope than the American DEFIANCE and TAKE IT DOWN Acts examined below, reflecting a crisis that had, by scale alone, become impossible for South Korean lawmakers to treat as anything short of a national emergency. The South Korean case belongs in the same chapter as the Swift incident and the Westfield nudify scandal not because the three cases are identical, but because together they trace a single continuous curve: a global celebrity targeted at a scale that briefly overwhelmed one of the world's largest social platforms; American teenagers targeted by classmates using a consumer smartphone app; and, at the far end of that same curve, an entire country's adolescent population targeted by a criminal network operating at an industrial scale that outstripped any single platform's, school's, or family's capacity to respond before the damage had already reached hundreds of thousands of people.

The legislative response arrived with unusual speed by the standards of American federal lawmaking, a pace that says as much about how thoroughly the Swift case broke through public attention as it does about the merits of the underlying policy problem. Within days, a bipartisan group of senators — Dick Durbin, Lindsey Graham, Amy Klobuchar, and Josh Hawley among the lead sponsors — introduced the DEFIANCE Act, an acronym standing for Disrupt Explicit Forged Images and Non-Consensual Edits. The bill created a federal civil cause of action allowing a person depicted in a sexually explicit "digital forgery" to sue whoever produced, distributed, or received it with intent to distribute, provided the forgery was made without the depicted person's consent and the producer knew or recklessly disregarded that the person had not consented. The bill passed the Senate and was eventually signed into law, giving victims of this specific harm a private legal remedy that had not clearly existed before, independent of whatever a platform's own content moderation policy happened to say on a given day.

A companion piece of legislation, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, approached the same underlying harm from a different angle, focused less on civil liability after the fact and more on the practical mechanics of getting nonconsensual intimate imagery off the internet quickly once it existed, requiring covered platforms to establish a notice-and-takedown process modeled loosely on existing copyright takedown procedures, with a defined response window rather than an indefinite one. Together, the two bills reflect a recognition that neither remedy alone was sufficient: a civil cause of action gives a victim recourse against the person who created the material, but does nothing to speed up removal of images that have already gone viral; a takedown mechanism can accelerate removal, but does nothing to hold the original creator accountable, and does nothing at all for a victim whose images were shared through channels outside a covered platform's reach.

The Swift case forces an uncomfortable question that the corporate fraud and election-interference cases examined in the preceding chapters do not raise in quite the same way: what, exactly, is the "authentic" thing being defended against a fake, when the fake never had a genuine counterpart to be confused with in the first place? The Arup fraud worked because a fabricated video call was mistaken for a genuine one; the Pikesville recording worked because a fabricated voice was mistaken for the real principal's voice; the New Hampshire robocall worked because a fabricated Biden was mistaken for the real Biden speaking on that day. In each of those cases, the harm depends on somebody believing the fake is real. Non-consensual intimate imagery does not require that belief to cause damage. A viewer who understands perfectly well that an image is AI-generated can still cause devastating harm to the person depicted simply by creating, sharing, or dwelling on a fabricated sexual image of a real, identifiable, non-consenting human being. The wrong is not fundamentally an epistemic one — a mistaken belief about what is real — the way it is in a corporate fraud or a stolen election message. It is closer to a violation of a person's control over their own image and likeness, regardless of whether anyone involved is actually deceived about the image's provenance.

That distinction matters for how this book's central argument about verification and provenance applies here, and where it runs out of usefulness. A content-provenance standard like the ones examined later in this book — a cryptographic signature attesting that a given image came from a specific camera at a specific time — can help establish that a genuine photograph is genuine. It does very little to prevent the creation of a wholly synthetic image in the first place, since the image was never claiming a false provenance; it was never claiming a real camera captured a real moment at all. A viewer sharing a Swift deepfake did not need to believe a camera had captured a real event. The harm exists independent of the belief, which places non-consensual synthetic imagery slightly outside the main line of argument this book has otherwise been building — the idea that verified authenticity is the scarce resource — and closer to a more traditional legal category: a violation of a person's control over their own likeness, akin to how the law has long treated defamation, harassment, or the unauthorized commercial use of someone's image, just executed with a tool that makes the underlying violation dramatically cheaper and faster to commit at scale.

This is also, not incidentally, a story about whose harms get addressed quickly and whose do not. Nonconsensual deepfake pornography targeting private individuals — disproportionately women, and disproportionately women without Taylor Swift's resources, fame, or platform leverage — had been documented by researchers for years before 2024 without producing comparable legislative urgency. It took a victim whose fame made the harm impossible for lawmakers, platforms, and the press to treat as a niche problem before a federal civil remedy and a federal takedown mechanism materialized within the same legislative session. That is not an argument against the DEFIANCE Act or the TAKE IT DOWN Act, both of which extend protection to every victim of this harm going forward, not merely to famous ones. It is an argument that the "verification economy" chapters later in this book — which examine, among other things, who can afford identity verification, content authentication, and legal recourse, and who cannot — need to be read with an eye toward exactly this kind of asymmetry. The infrastructure being built to address synthetic media's harms tends to arrive fastest and most completely for the people who least need it, and slowest, if at all, for the people who need it most.
