# The Liar's Dividend

The employee at Arup's Hong Kong office had every reason to be careful. The email arrived first, purporting to come from the firm's UK-based chief financial officer, asking about a confidential transaction. It read like the kind of phishing attempt the finance department had been warned about for years, and the employee was skeptical enough to hesitate.

Then came the video call.

On the screen were people the employee recognized: the CFO, and several other colleagues, their faces and voices matching what years of video conferences had trained the employee to expect. The meeting looked ordinary. It was not. Every person on that call except the target employee was a fabrication, built from company video and audio that had circulated in earlier virtual meetings and conference recordings. Over the course of the call and the days around it, the employee authorized fifteen separate wire transfers, totaling roughly $25.6 million, or about 200 million Hong Kong dollars. The fraud came to light only when the employee, following up on what had been billed as a secret transaction, called Arup's actual corporate headquarters to check in. By then the money was gone. Hong Kong police disclosed the case in February 2024; Arup did not confirm publicly that it was the victim until May, and as of early 2025 none of the funds had been recovered.

It is worth sitting with how mundane the failure point was. Nobody broke an encryption scheme. No one exploited a zero-day vulnerability in Arup's network. The attackers simply generated a convincing simulation of the people the employee already trusted, using footage that had been sitting in ordinary corporate archives, and asked for money through the same channel — a video call — that companies have relied on for two decades as a reasonably safe way to confirm that the person requesting something unusual is who they claim to be. The video call had been trustworthy for so long that almost nobody had built a backup plan for the day it stopped being trustworthy.

A few months later, in May 2024, WPP's chief executive Mark Read discovered how close the same technique had come to working on his own company. Scammers created a WhatsApp account using a public photo of Read, then set up a Microsoft Teams meeting that appeared to include him and another senior executive. During the meeting, an AI voice clone of the executive, paired with real YouTube footage, did the talking, while the fake "Read" communicated through the chat window rather than speaking — a detail that likely helped mask the seams in the impersonation. The scammers were trying to get an agency leader to set up a new business relationship that would ultimately extract money and personal information. WPP later said the attempt failed because the targeted employee stayed alert. The company that makes its living helping other companies control their public image had just watched someone attempt to steal its chief executive's image and voice for a fraud.

Both cases involve corporate finance, but the same mechanics have already reached the ballot box. Two days before Slovakia's September 2023 parliamentary election, an audio recording began circulating on social media. It featured what sounded like Michal Šimečka, the liberal candidate who led the Progressive Slovakia party, discussing with a journalist how to rig the vote using ballots from the country's Roma minority. It was synthetic. Researchers and journalists who examined it concluded the audio had been generated, not recorded, and the timing was almost surgical: it landed during Slovakia's mandated pre-election media silence period, a rule written for an earlier media era that left broadcasters unable to publicly debunk it before voters went to the polls. Tens of thousands of people heard the clip. Šimečka's opponent, Robert Fico, went on to win an election Šimečka had been favored to take, and while no one can cleanly separate the deepfake's effect from Slovakia's many other political currents, the episode is now widely cited as a candidate case for the first election meaningfully shaped by synthetic audio.

What connects an engineering firm in Hong Kong, an advertising conglomerate in London, and a parliamentary election in Bratislava is not simply that fabrication got better. It is that fabrication got cheap enough, and good enough, to compete with the ordinary evidence people have relied on for their entire lives: the sound of a familiar voice, the sight of a familiar face on a screen, a recording of someone saying something incriminating. For most of human history, those forms of evidence were expensive to fake convincingly. A forger needed real skill, real time, and usually a good reason to think the fake would survive scrutiny. That asymmetry — cheap to verify, expensive to fabricate — is what allowed societies to treat certain kinds of evidence as reasonably solid without demanding a chain of custody for every video call and every voicemail.

The asymmetry has now reversed for a meaningful slice of what we see and hear, and reversing it changes more than the odds of getting defrauded by a fake CFO. It changes what a lie can accomplish even when nobody believes it.

The legal scholars Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron named this second-order effect in a 2019 paper for the California Law Review, calling it the liar's dividend. Their argument runs roughly like this: as the public becomes more aware that convincing fake video and audio exist, it becomes correspondingly easier for a person caught by real, accurate footage to claim that the footage is fabricated. The dividend does not require that the denial be believed by everyone. It only requires enough uncertainty, among enough people, to blunt the consequence that real evidence used to reliably produce. A recording that would once have ended a political career can instead become one more contested claim in a landscape where authenticity itself is negotiable. Chesney and Citron were writing about deepfakes years before the technology reached the fluency it has today, and their warning has aged in the least comfortable way a prediction can age: it has been increasingly borne out by events, in courtrooms, in newsrooms, and in the aftermath of leaked recordings that public figures now sometimes dismiss as synthetic, whether or not that claim survives forensic examination.

This is the paradox at the center of the story this book tells. The interesting threat from synthetic media was never only that people would be fooled by fakes, though the Arup case shows that danger is real and expensive. The deeper threat is that the mere existence of convincing fakes lets people discount evidence that happens to be genuine. Both effects point toward the same underlying scarcity. When fabrication is cheap, the thing that becomes valuable is not content — content has never been more abundant — but a credible answer to the question of where a given piece of content actually came from, and whether the person on the other end of a call, an email, or a headline is who they claim to be.

That scarcity did not appear all at once, and it is not confined to video calls and campaign audio. It shows up in academic journals that published findings nobody could later reproduce, in financial statements that auditors signed off on for years before the fraud beneath them collapsed, and in a blood-testing company that convinced boards, investors, and patients that its technology worked long after the technology had stopped working, if it had ever worked in the way its founder claimed. The institutions built to catch those failures were designed for a world in which fabrication was hard and slow. Generative AI did not invent the problem of trust. It arrived at a moment when a great deal of the trust infrastructure built across the last two centuries was already under more strain than most people realized, and it removed one of the few advantages that infrastructure had left: the assumption that convincing fakes required unusual skill, unusual time, or unusual motive.

The chapters that follow trace both halves of that story — how trust used to be manufactured, and why the machinery for manufacturing it is now being asked to do a job it was never built for. The Arup fraud is a useful place to begin because it strips the problem down to something almost embarrassingly simple. A person believed what they saw and heard, because for their entire working life, seeing and hearing had been good enough. It no longer reliably is. What replaces it, and who gets to decide what counts as proof, is the argument this book is trying to have honestly, without pretending the answer is either hopeless or easy.
