# The Liar's Dividend

The employee at Arup's Hong Kong office had every reason to be careful. The email arrived first, purporting to come from the firm's UK-based chief financial officer, asking about a confidential transaction. It read like the kind of phishing attempt the finance department had been warned about for years, and the employee was skeptical enough to hesitate.

Then came the video call.

On the screen were people the employee recognized: the CFO, and several other colleagues, their faces and voices matching what years of video conferences had trained the employee to expect. The meeting looked ordinary. It was not. Every person on that call except the target employee was a fabrication, built from company video and audio that had circulated in earlier virtual meetings and conference recordings. Over the course of the call and the days around it, the employee authorized fifteen separate wire transfers, totaling roughly $25.6 million, or about 200 million Hong Kong dollars. The fraud came to light only when the employee, following up on what had been billed as a secret transaction, called Arup's actual corporate headquarters to check in. By then the money was gone. Hong Kong police disclosed the case in February 2024; Arup did not confirm publicly that it was the victim until May, and as of early 2025 none of the funds had been recovered.

It is worth sitting with how mundane the failure point was. Nobody broke an encryption scheme. No one exploited a zero-day vulnerability in Arup's network. The attackers simply generated a convincing simulation of the people the employee already trusted, using footage that had been sitting in ordinary corporate archives, and asked for money through the same channel — a video call — that companies have relied on for two decades as a reasonably safe way to confirm that the person requesting something unusual is who they claim to be. The video call had been trustworthy for so long that almost nobody had built a backup plan for the day it stopped being trustworthy.

A few months later, in May 2024, WPP's chief executive Mark Read discovered how close the same technique had come to working on his own company. Scammers created a WhatsApp account using a public photo of Read, then set up a Microsoft Teams meeting that appeared to include him and another senior executive. During the meeting, an AI voice clone of the executive, paired with real YouTube footage, did the talking, while the fake "Read" communicated through the chat window rather than speaking — a detail that likely helped mask the seams in the impersonation. The scammers were trying to get an agency leader to set up a new business relationship that would ultimately extract money and personal information. WPP later said the attempt failed because the targeted employee stayed alert. The company that makes its living helping other companies control their public image had just watched someone attempt to steal its chief executive's image and voice for a fraud.

Both cases involve corporate finance, and both share a structural feature worth naming before this book goes anywhere else: the deception did not need to survive forever, or even survive very long. It needed to survive exactly as long as it took someone to authorize a payment or share a credential, after which the fabrication's job was already done. That is a much lower bar than the one older frauds had to clear, and it is one of the reasons cheap, imperfect synthetic media can already produce expensive, real-world harm even while remaining detectable, in hindsight, to a careful forensic examiner. Fraud has never needed to be undetectable. It has only ever needed to be convincing enough for the length of a single decision.

The employee at the center of the fraud deserves a word of defense this book has not yet given directly, because the instinct to ask "how could someone fall for that" is itself part of the problem this book is trying to describe. Fifteen separate wire transfers sounds, from a distance, like a conspicuous pattern an attentive person should have caught partway through. From inside the employee's own experience, each individual transfer was authorized in the context of an ongoing, apparently legitimate secret transaction that senior, recognized colleagues were actively managing on a shared video call, with each new request arriving as a continuation of instructions already partially executed rather than as a suspicious pattern visible all at once. Hindsight compresses fifteen decisions into a single, obviously reckless-looking sequence; the employee lived through fifteen individually plausible moments, each one consistent with everything that had come before it. This is not a defense of any specific decision the employee made. It is a caution against reading this book's cases as morality tales about individual gullibility, when the more accurate and more useful reading treats them as case studies in how a well-designed fabrication can make each step of an unfolding fraud feel, at the time, like the reasonable continuation of something already established as legitimate.

Arup's own public accounting of the incident after the fact is notable for what its leadership chose to emphasize, which was not embarrassment at having been fooled but a broader warning to other firms that the underlying vulnerability was structural rather than a failure specific to one careless employee. A senior Arup executive who later spoke publicly about the fraud described it as "extraordinarily sophisticated" while also stressing that similar attempts against the firm, and against peer companies in its industry, were happening with much greater frequency than most organizations' public disclosures suggested — an admission that framed the incident less as a one-off embarrassment to be quietly managed and more as an early, visible instance of a threat every company with a finance department and a video-conferencing habit should now assume it faces routinely. That framing matters for how this book proceeds: the point of examining the Arup fraud in this much detail is not that Arup was uniquely careless, but that Arup was, if anything, more transparent than most of its peers about a vulnerability nearly every similarly structured organization shares and few have yet been forced to discuss this publicly.

It is worth being precise about what actually failed in both the Arup and WPP cases, because the same failure recurs, in different costumes, across nearly every case this book goes on to examine. Neither company lacked a security policy on paper. Arup, by its own subsequent account, had internal controls that should have flagged an unusual, urgent, secretive wire-transfer request as exactly the kind of anomaly warranting a second channel of confirmation before money moved. The controls existed; what failed was the assumption, baked into how those controls were actually exercised in the moment, that a live video call showing multiple recognized colleagues was itself already a sufficiently strong form of confirmation, redundant with whatever secondary check the policy nominally required. That assumption had been reasonable for decades, precisely because producing a fake video call convincing enough to satisfy it had, until very recently, been far more expensive than any ordinary fraud could justify. The policy was not absent. The specific piece of evidence the policy implicitly trusted to satisfy its own requirements had simply stopped being trustworthy, faster than the policy's authors had any reason to anticipate.

What connects an engineering firm in Hong Kong to an advertising conglomerate in London is not simply that fabrication got better. It is that fabrication got cheap enough, and good enough, to compete with the ordinary evidence people have relied on for their entire lives: the sound of a familiar voice, the sight of a familiar face on a screen, a recording of someone saying something specific. For most of human history, those forms of evidence were expensive to fake convincingly. A forger needed real skill, real time, and usually a good reason to think the fake would survive scrutiny. That asymmetry — cheap to verify, expensive to fabricate — is what allowed societies to treat certain kinds of evidence as reasonably solid without demanding a chain of custody for every video call and every voicemail.

The asymmetry has now reversed for a meaningful slice of what we see and hear, and reversing it changes more than the odds of getting defrauded by a fake CFO. It changes what a lie can accomplish even when nobody believes it.

The legal scholars Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron named this second-order effect in a 2019 paper for the California Law Review, calling it the liar's dividend. Their argument runs roughly like this: as the public becomes more aware that convincing fake video and audio exist, it becomes correspondingly easier for a person caught by real, accurate footage to claim that the footage is fabricated. The dividend does not require that the denial be believed by everyone. It only requires enough uncertainty, among enough people, to blunt the consequence that real evidence used to reliably produce. A recording that would once have ended a political career can instead become one more contested claim in a landscape where authenticity itself is negotiable. Chesney and Citron were writing about deepfakes years before the technology reached the fluency it has today, and their warning has aged in the least comfortable way a prediction can age: it has been increasingly borne out by events, in courtrooms, in newsrooms, and in the aftermath of leaked recordings that public figures now sometimes dismiss as synthetic, whether or not that claim survives forensic examination.

It is worth pausing to note that none of the machinery this book has described so far — generative adversarial networks, diffusion models, voice cloning — was even necessary for an early, instructive preview of the liar's dividend problem. In May 2019, a video of House Speaker Nancy Pelosi began spreading rapidly on Facebook and YouTube, appearing to show her slurring her words and struggling to speak coherently during a public appearance. The video was not a deepfake in any technical sense. It had simply been slowed to about seventy-five percent of its original speed and had its pitch corrected to mask the change, a manipulation achievable with consumer video-editing software that had existed for years, requiring no artificial intelligence and no unusual skill. Media researchers Joan Donovan and Britt Paris coined a useful term for this category of manipulation, distinct from the deepfakes this book focuses on: a "cheapfake," built from cheap, conventional editing techniques — slowing, splicing, selectively cropping — rather than machine-learning-generated fabrication. One version of the Pelosi video, posted by a partisan Facebook page, was viewed more than two million times before major platforms took meaningful action, and Facebook in particular drew sustained criticism for declining to remove it outright, opting instead to attach a fact-check label while leaving the video up.

The Pelosi video matters to this book's argument for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that the liar's dividend and the underlying fabrication problem do not actually require the sophisticated generative technology this book's later chapters describe; a considerable amount of damage can be done with tools that have existed since the earliest days of consumer video editing, provided the manipulation is subtle enough and the platform's moderation slow enough. Second, and more importantly for this chapter's argument, it previews the liar's dividend from the opposite direction: rather than a guilty party dismissing real evidence as fake, the Pelosi case involved a fabricated impression built from genuinely real, unaltered footage, repackaged just enough to support a false narrative. Both directions of the same underlying problem — real evidence dismissed as fake, and fake evidence passed off as real — depend on the same eroding public capacity to tell the difference, and neither direction requires the most advanced technology this book examines in order to do real damage.

This is the paradox at the center of the story this book tells. The interesting threat from synthetic media was never only that people would be fooled by fakes, though the Arup case shows that danger is real and expensive. The deeper threat is that the mere existence of convincing fakes lets people discount evidence that happens to be genuine. Both effects point toward the same underlying scarcity. When fabrication is cheap, the thing that becomes valuable is not content — content has never been more abundant — but a credible answer to the question of where a given piece of content actually came from, and whether the person on the other end of a call, an email, or a headline is who they claim to be.

That scarcity did not appear all at once, and it is not confined to corporate video calls. The chapters immediately ahead follow it into three very different settings before this book turns to history: a high school in suburban Maryland, where the same underlying technique was used to try to end one man's career rather than move one company's money; a presidential primary in New Hampshire, where it was aimed at the machinery of an election rather than a bank account; and a case built entirely around images that never should have needed defending as fake, because they never should have existed at all. Each of those cases shows the liar's dividend and the fabrication problem operating at a different scale — personal, institutional, national — and each shows how little the underlying mechanism changes as the stakes rise. Someone believed, or was meant to make others believe, something manufactured, because for their entire working or civic life, the kind of evidence in front of them had been good enough. It no longer reliably is. What replaces it, and who gets to decide what counts as proof, is the argument this book is trying to have honestly, without pretending the answer is either hopeless or easy.
