# The Enemy's Airplane

In 1968 the United States flew a Soviet MiG-21 over Nevada under American control, measured it, fought mock battles against it, and turned the results into tactics. The airplane was not a rumor. It was not an anonymous piece of metal in a bag. It had an acquisition history, a test program, pilots, maintainers, accidents, briefing material, bureaucratic owners, operational consequences, and, eventually, a public history.

That makes HAVE DOUGHNUT and the programs that followed useful for a reason larger than Cold War aviation. They are a control case for secrecy.

The modern literature of hidden technology is full of claims about retrieved vehicles, unusual materials, inaccessible compartments, special-access programs, contractor custody, and reverse engineering. Some of those claims are made by serious people in formal settings. None should be promoted from testimony to proof because the subject matter is dramatic or because classification makes direct checking difficult. A better approach is to ask what a known, genuinely secret foreign-materiel exploitation enterprise looked like while it was alive and what traces it left after secrecy loosened.

This book does that with Soviet aircraft. It does not argue that a MiG-21 is analogous in engineering difficulty to a hypothetical non-human vehicle. It argues something narrower and more useful: real hidden exploitation creates dependencies. Machines need transport, hangars, people, spares, fuel, instruments, test ranges, safety procedures, paperwork, doctrine, budgets, and recipients for whatever is learned. Secrecy can obscure those dependencies. It does not abolish them.

The evidentiary rule throughout is deliberately uneven. Declassified documents and official program histories get one weight. Participant recollection gets another. Claims without independent corroboration get a third. Speculation about how the historical control case bears on modern allegations is labeled as speculation rather than smuggled in as conclusion.

The subject is strange enough without helping it.
