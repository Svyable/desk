# The Classification Tax

A secret that cannot travel is a private fact.

That is useful sometimes. It is not the same as capability.

HAVE DOUGHNUT produced information that American fighter crews could use, but the source of the information was sensitive. The United States had access to a Soviet-designed fighter through an allied intelligence success. Revealing too much could compromise relationships, tell adversaries what had been measured, or cause the Soviets to modify tactics and equipment.

The normal bureaucratic solution is classification. The operational problem is also classification.

Every restriction narrows the number of people who can connect the finding to another finding. That may be worth the security benefit. It may also create what I think of as the classification tax: the friction paid when knowledge cannot easily cross organizational boundaries.

The tax appears in mundane forms. A pilot cannot be briefed on the real source. A maintainer cannot consult the obvious vendor. An engineer cannot publish a failure mode. A training unit receives the conclusion but not the data behind it. Two laboratories unknowingly duplicate work because neither can see the other's compartment. A senior official sees polished summaries while the people closest to the hardware cannot explain uncertainty directly.

None of this requires conspiracy. It is what access control does.

The Red Eagles became one way to pay less of the tax. If the full exploitation record could not be distributed widely, American pilots could still train against representative adversary behavior. The knowledge moved through experience.

That matters when evaluating the claim that extraordinary technologies could remain hidden for decades. The usual debate is childish. One side says no government can keep a secret. The other points to the Manhattan Project, stealth aircraft, codebreaking or covert operations and says governments plainly can. Both statements are too coarse.

The serious question is what kind of secret, involving how many dependencies, for how long, with what operational use, under what legal and contracting environment, and with what acceptable leakage.

HAVE DOUGHNUT was highly secret during the period when the source and tactical findings were most valuable. CONSTANT PEG remained classified for years while thousands of aircrew were exposed to the aircraft under controlled conditions. The existence of the program eventually became public and was formally acknowledged. That history shows that fairly large operational secrets can be protected for substantial periods.

It also shows that secrecy is not binary.

Thousands of people can know fragments. A much smaller group knows the acquisition story. Another group knows the full technical exploitation. Students know they are fighting a MiG but may not know where it came from. Locals know unusual aircraft fly nearby. Intelligence adversaries may suspect the program without being able to prove it. Public knowledge accumulates before formal acknowledgment.

This layered leakage is more realistic than the cinematic secret in which nobody outside one conference room knows anything for seventy years.

Modern retrieval allegations should be analyzed at the same resolution. When a witness says a program is hidden in a special-access structure, the next questions are organizational. What is compartmented: the object's existence, origin hypothesis, material location, test result, contract, or operational derivative? Are all workers read into the origin claim, or only into the task? Could a metallurgist study a sample without knowing why it matters? Could a machinist produce a fixture under an ordinary contract? Could a security office protect a facility without seeing the payload?

The answer to all of these could be yes. Compartmentation can fragment awareness effectively.

But fragmentation creates its own artifacts. Access lists. secure facilities. special communications. contract language. unusual custody. transportation. safety reviews. classification guides. recurring budgets hidden inside broader lines. Insider threat procedures. The details vary, and many would remain legitimately secret, but the program has to occupy administrative reality.

This is why claims of an indefinitely invisible exploitation enterprise need to specify their secrecy model. 'Special access' is not an invisibility cloak. It is an access-control architecture.

The historical record also teaches a second caution: security can become part of the myth-generation machine.

When witnesses are unable to explain details, listeners fill gaps. When officials deny or refuse to discuss real programs, later audiences learn that denial is compatible with truth. When a classified aircraft is seen behaving strangely, observers may construct explanations from the categories available to them. Years later declassification validates the observation but not necessarily the interpretation.

The state then inherits a credibility problem of its own making.

This is relevant to UAP history without requiring any claim that UAP reports are primarily secret aircraft. Some undoubtedly have involved classified or sensitive activities; official historical reviews have said as much about portions of earlier UFO reporting. Other cases remain unresolved for different reasons. The important point is that secrecy contaminates the evidentiary environment. The government may know a mundane explanation it cannot reveal. It may lack an explanation but refuse to disclose sensor capabilities. It may suspect an adversary and avoid confirmation. The public sees only the silence.

Silence is information, but it is underdetermined information.

That is why 'they won't tell us' cannot carry an origin hypothesis.

The classification tax is paid by the government too. Excessive secrecy can prevent scientific scrutiny, duplicate effort, slow countermeasure development and make later oversight difficult. A program may protect itself so well that it cannot recruit the best expertise. A technical claim may survive inside a compartment because independent laboratories cannot challenge it. A failure may be interpreted as a need for more secrecy rather than as evidence against the premise.

This is not hypothetical. Every large classified research ecosystem has to manage the risk that security reduces epistemic competition.

A recovered MiG had a useful defense against this problem: it flew. Performance claims could be tested against the sky.

The more speculative the technology, the more important an equivalent reality check becomes.

Secrecy can protect evidence.

It can also protect mistakes.
