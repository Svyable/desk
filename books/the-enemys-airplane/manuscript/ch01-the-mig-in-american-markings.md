# The MiG in American Markings

The useful thing about a secret airplane is that it still has to land.

On January 23, 1968, the Air Force Flight Test Center joined a clandestine Department of Defense examination of a Soviet-built MiG-21F-13. The airplane had only 135 hours on its airframe. For the next three months it was not a rumor, a silhouette in a reconnaissance photograph, or a line in a threat estimate. It was a test article. The Foreign Technology Division-led team used it to check existing intelligence and to find out whether American combat tactics actually worked against the fighter North Vietnam was flying. Source: https://www.aftc.af.mil/News/On-This-Day-in-Test-History/Article-Display-Test-History/Article/2458475/january-23-1968-flight-test-center-examines-soviet-mig-21/

That change in noun—from enemy fighter to test article—is where this story begins. Once the airplane is yours, secrecy is no longer the hardest engineering problem. Tires wear. Hydraulic fluid leaks. Somebody has to decide which fuel truck may approach it. A pilot needs a checklist. The aircraft must be hidden from people who are not cleared to see it while remaining legible to the people expected to keep it from killing the test crew.

The National Museum of the United States Air Force now says plainly what would once have been an extraordinary disclosure. In the late 1960s the United States secretly acquired and tested MiG-17 and MiG-21 aircraft under programs including HAVE DOUGHNUT and HAVE DRILL. Those tests fed a much larger enterprise. From 1977 to 1988 the 4477th Test and Evaluation Squadron, the Red Eagles, flew MiG-17s, MiG-21s and MiG-23s; the museum credits the unit with more than 15,000 sorties and with exposing almost 6,000 American aircrew to the aircraft they might face in combat. Source: https://www.nationalmuseum.af.mil/Visit/Museum-Exhibits/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/1682967/constant-peg-secret-migs-in-the-desert/

Fifteen thousand sorties are not evidence that secrecy failed. They are evidence that secrecy had to coexist with an organization large enough to fly airplanes repeatedly: operations desks, range scheduling, fuel, spare parts, weather calls, medical response, mishap procedures, security compartments, maintenance shifts, and people who knew which switch not to touch. A black program still has a parts bin.

The first MiG-21 exploited in HAVE DOUGHNUT had reached Israel after Iraqi pilot Munir Redfa flew one there in August 1966. Israel tested the aircraft and later made it available to the United States. The American evaluation took place at Groom Lake. In public memory Groom Lake has become a synonym for the unknowable, a landscape onto which almost any technological rumor can be projected. HAVE DOUGHNUT is useful because it runs the film the other way. An extraordinary secret became an ordinary technical history: an adversary's frontline fighter arrived in Nevada, American crews flew it, and the point was to replace inference with measurement.

The Air Force eventually described MiGs in this lineage as flying under American markings. The image is better than a metaphor. The airplane is Soviet in design, Iraqi in prior service, Israeli by acquisition history, American by temporary custody and test purpose. The metal does not change nationality when its roundels do. What changes is the question being asked of it.

Some questions can be answered with calipers, scales, photographs and bench tests. Others require fuel and sky. How does the engine respond when a pilot asks for power now rather than in a specification table? What can the pilot actually see over the nose? Where does the radar lose track? How does the airplane behave against an F-4 flown by someone who knows he is allowed to lose the engagement in order to learn from it? Which feared advantages disappear in the cockpit, and which overlooked ones become unpleasantly real?

That is a more useful picture of reverse engineering than magic performed on wreckage. Possession is only the beginning. A captured artifact can answer some questions directly. Others require operation, comparison, maintenance, failure, and repetition. A machine in a hangar is not the same epistemic object as the same machine flown against a friendly fighter.

The distinction matters because modern extraordinary-technology claims often compress all of those stages into one word: retrieval. The compression is seductive. If an object has been recovered, perhaps understanding follows; if a material has been identified, perhaps manufacture follows; if unusual performance has been observed, perhaps mechanism follows. HAVE DOUGHNUT is a useful corrective precisely because the technology was not miraculous. American engineers already understood turbojets, delta wings, radar, cannon, missiles and supersonic flight, and they still needed the airplane.

The reason was implementation. A MiG-21 embodied particular compromises in turning behavior, visibility, control forces, engine response, radar performance and cockpit ergonomics. Intelligence photographs could suggest some of them. Combat reports could suggest others. The aircraft itself could veto both imagination and fear.

That veto cuts in both directions. Captured hardware can reveal an advantage analysts underestimated. It can also show that a frightening performance number was purchased by accepting a weakness somewhere else. And sometimes it mostly confirms what intelligence already believed. The Air Force Test Center's own retrospective says the 1968 examination confirmed existing threat data as well as evaluating tactics. A recovered machine is therefore not guaranteed to deliver a revelation. One of its most valuable products may be the much less cinematic conclusion that the analysts were basically right.

That is also the strongest limit on using HAVE DOUGHNUT as a control case for more exotic retrieval claims. A MiG-21 belonged to an industrial world the United States already understood. Its fuel could be characterized, its fasteners machined, its aerodynamics instrumented, its engine run, its pilot controls mapped. A genuinely unfamiliar artifact—if one existed—might resist exploitation for reasons that have no analogue in Soviet aircraft work. The comparison cannot tell us how quickly an unknown technology should yield its secrets.

It can tell us something narrower and sturdier: exploitation is an activity, not a possession. Even familiar foreign hardware generated test programs, maintenance burdens, safety decisions, trained specialists, comparative flights, revised tactics and eventually a historical residue substantial enough to reconstruct.

The residue includes failure and boredom. The MiGs were difficult to support. Replacement parts were scarce. Maintainers improvised. Aircraft aged. The Red Eagles eventually retired their MiG-17s because of obsolescence and safety concerns. None of that is incidental texture added after the exciting part. It is the exciting part's invoice.

Secrecy edits invoices out. A sentence saying that the United States possessed Soviet fighters sounds clean. The program itself had weather, broken components, unfamiliar design choices, security compartments and the possibility that a rare aircraft could be lost through an ordinary mistake. The secret had to function on Tuesday morning.

That is why the MiG in American markings belongs at the beginning. The interesting question is not merely whether a government can hide an airplane. Governments plainly can. It is what has to accumulate around a hidden airplane before anyone can truthfully say they understand what they have.

The airplane still has to land.
