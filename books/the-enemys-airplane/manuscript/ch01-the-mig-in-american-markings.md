# The MiG in American Markings

The useful thing about a secret airplane is that it still has to land.

At some point the exotic object stops being an intelligence success and becomes a maintenance problem. Tires wear. Hydraulic fluid leaks. Somebody wants to know which fuel truck may approach it. A pilot needs a checklist, even if the checklist has been assembled from translations, inference, and experience. The airplane must be hidden from people who are not cleared to see it while remaining visible enough to the people expected to keep it from killing the test crew.

That is where this story begins: not with the acquisition of a MiG-21, but with the conversion of an enemy fighter into an American test article.

The National Museum of the United States Air Force now says plainly what would once have been an extraordinary disclosure. In the late 1960s the United States secretly acquired and tested MiG-17 and MiG-21 aircraft under programs including HAVE DOUGHNUT and HAVE DRILL. The museum's history of CONSTANT PEG describes those early tests as providing complete technical breakdowns of the aircraft and helping establish a later program in which American pilots trained against actual Soviet-designed fighters. From 1977 to 1988 the 4477th Test and Evaluation Squadron, the Red Eagles, flew MiG-17s, MiG-21s and MiG-23s. The same official history says the unit logged more than 15,000 sorties and exposed almost 6,000 U.S. aircrew to the real aircraft they might face in combat. Source: https://www.nationalmuseum.af.mil/Visit/Museum-Exhibits/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/1682967/constant-peg-secret-migs-in-the-desert/

Those numbers matter less as statistics than as physical evidence. A program that flies fifteen thousand sorties cannot be made of secrecy alone. It requires a fuel system, an operations desk, range scheduling, spare parts, medical response, mishap procedures, weather decisions, security, maintenance shifts, and people who know which switch not to touch. The black program has to become an organization.

The first MiG-21 exploited in HAVE DOUGHNUT had come to Israel after Iraqi pilot Munir Redfa flew one there in August 1966. Israel tested the aircraft and later made it available to the United States. The U.S. evaluation took place at Groom Lake. In public memory Groom Lake has become a synonym for the unknowable, a landscape onto which nearly any technological rumor can be projected. HAVE DOUGHNUT is interesting because it reverses the polarity. Here the impossible-sounding story became ordinary history: yes, an adversary's frontline fighter really was brought into the Nevada test world; yes, American crews flew it; yes, the purpose was to find out what it could actually do.

The Air Force eventually described the MiGs as having been flown under American markings. That image is worth lingering over. The airplane is Soviet in design, Iraqi in prior service, Israeli by acquisition history, American by temporary custody and test purpose. A machine crosses political boundaries more easily than the stories built around it. The metal does not change nationality when its roundels do. What changes is the question being asked of it.

This is the first distinction the book needs. Reverse engineering is not magic performed on wreckage. Foreign-materiel exploitation is a chain of questions. How fast? How high? How sharply can it turn? What does the pilot see? How quickly does the engine respond? What frequencies does its radar use? Where does the radar lose track? How does it appear to our sensors? What weapons envelopes are dangerous? Which assumptions in our training syllabi are wrong? What breaks most often? Which parts can we reproduce, and which are not worth reproducing because we already have a different solution?

A captured artifact can answer some of those questions directly. Others require the artifact to be operated. Still others require comparisons against friendly systems. A machine in a hangar is not the same epistemic object as the same machine flown against an F-4.

That sounds obvious until one notices how often modern extraordinary-technology claims collapse all of those stages into a single word: retrieval. The word does too much work. To possess an object is not to understand it. To understand a component is not to understand the system. To reproduce a material is not to reproduce a manufacturing process. To observe unusual behavior is not to know the mechanism that produced it. A secret program may contain all of those stages, but the evidence for one is not evidence for the others.

HAVE DOUGHNUT gives us a chance to watch the stages separate.

The MiG-21 was not a technological miracle. American engineers already understood turbojet propulsion, swept and delta wings, radar, cannon, missiles, and supersonic flight. Yet the aircraft still had to be flown because knowing a category is not the same as knowing an implementation. The Fishbed's turning behavior, visibility, control forces, engine response, radar limitations and cockpit ergonomics existed as particulars. Intelligence photographs could suggest some of them. Pilot reports from combat could suggest others. A real aircraft could be measured.

That difference between category knowledge and implementation knowledge is one of the quiet reasons foreign materiel is so valuable. The enemy's machine is a veto on imagination. It may prove less capable than feared. It may reveal an advantage that friendly analysts had discounted. It may show that a performance number has been achieved by accepting a weakness somewhere else. It may expose a doctrine embedded in hardware.

The declassified story also gives us something that more sensational retrieval narratives usually lack: failure and boredom. The MiGs were difficult to support. Some arrived in poor condition. Replacement parts were scarce. Maintainers improvised. Pilots died in accidents. The aircraft were treated as national treasures because they were hard to replace, not because they floated silently in a hangar violating physics.

This texture is not incidental. It is what real programs are made of.

Secrecy edits texture out. A one-line statement that the United States possessed Soviet fighters sounds clean. The program itself was dirty: grease, incomplete manuals, broken components, weather, dangerous ejection systems, unfamiliar design philosophy, security compartments, and the constant possibility that the only example of a useful adversary system could be lost through an ordinary mistake.

That is why the MiG in American markings belongs at the beginning. It teaches a method before it teaches a history. When confronted with a claim about hidden technological exploitation, do not begin by asking whether the government could keep a secret. Governments plainly can. Ask what had to exist around the secret for the secret to function.

The airplane still has to land.
