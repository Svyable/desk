# The Flogger Problem

The MiG-23 was not merely another enemy airplane. It was a reminder that access can be dangerous.

CONSTANT PEG added MiG-23s to the Red Eagle fleet in 1980. The Flogger was faster and more complex than the earlier MiGs, with variable-sweep wings and different handling demands. The National Museum of the U.S. Air Force describes the export MiG-23MS as capable of roughly Mach 2.4 and notes that the Red Eagles flew the type during the classified program. Source: https://www.nationalmuseum.af.mil/Visit/Museum-Exhibits/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/1162493/mikoyan-gurevich-mig-23ms-flogger-e/

The seductive version of exploitation says that possession reduces uncertainty. The MiG-23 adds the second half: possession creates risk.

An unfamiliar system may be least forgiving precisely where the intelligence value is highest. To learn the envelope, someone has to approach it. To understand failure modes, a program may encounter them. To test a radar or weapon, operators may have to run configurations with hazards that the original manufacturer understood better than the exploiter.

The problem is not unique to aircraft. Foreign missiles may contain energetic materials. Radar systems may produce high-power radiation. Batteries can burn. Propellants can poison. Nuclear components create criticality and contamination risks. Biological material can create infection risks. An unknown device would be worse because hazard models might be wrong at the category level.

This is another place where extravagant retrieval lore often feels underengineered. The stories contain security officers and physicists but rarely industrial hygiene, explosive ordnance specialists, radiation protection, emergency plans, fire suppression, contamination monitoring, structural engineers or medical surveillance.

Perhaps such details remain classified. Perhaps storytellers omit them because they are boring. But boring systems are what permit dangerous research to continue.

The MiG-23 also illustrates a problem of variant identification. Export aircraft could differ from the versions retained for Soviet service. A captured example might understate or misstate frontline capability. Technical intelligence therefore has to know not just what the aircraft is called but what specific configuration it represents.

This is painfully relevant to reverse-engineering claims built around tiny samples. If a recovered fragment genuinely came from an unknown vehicle, what part was it? Was it structural, decorative, protective, sacrificial, manufacturing waste, contamination, or repair? Without configuration context, the analyst may be holding the equivalent of an export-model radar component and inferring the entire Soviet air-defense system.

The Flogger history also demonstrates the danger of knowing enough to become confident.

Once operators have flown an adversary system repeatedly, familiarity can harden into doctrine. But the adversary is changing too. Tactics evolve. New variants appear. Maintenance quality differs among users. A training program can become a museum if it does not update.

Technical exploitation is therefore perishable.

A 1968 insight about one MiG-21 does not settle the threat in 1985. A material analysis from one recovered component does not settle every later system. A sensor signature from one flight condition may not generalize. Intelligence has a timestamp even when the hardware looks permanent.

This complicates modern historical inference. Suppose declassified documents prove that an agency investigated an exotic propulsion concept in 1974. That is evidence that the agency investigated it in 1974. It is not evidence that the concept worked, that a program continued, or that current aircraft use it. Program continuity must be demonstrated, not presumed through the convenient darkness between documents.

The same rule applies to black aircraft histories. The existence of one secret MiG squadron does not imply a continuous hidden fleet of every foreign fighter. Each program needs evidence.

This seems obvious when stated plainly. It becomes harder when a story is emotionally coherent.

The Flogger problem is useful because it forces the historian to keep multiple kinds of uncertainty alive at once. The aircraft was real. The training was real. The risk was real. The exact provenance of every airframe and the full details of all technical evaluations are not equally public. Participant memories add texture but can conflict. Official histories compress. Some records remain unavailable.

A serious book does not need to choose between total certainty and total ignorance.

That middle ground is where intelligence history actually lives.

For controversial aerospace subjects, the same discipline is more valuable than another argument about belief. Mark the established core. Mark the credible but incomplete testimony. Mark the disputed claim. Mark the speculation. Then refuse to let a fact in one column migrate into another because the narrative wants momentum.

The MiG-23 was an enemy airplane in an American secret program.

That fact is strange enough.

We do not need to make it stranger to make it useful.
