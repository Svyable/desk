# HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY

One enemy airplane could be dismissed as a curiosity. Two begin to look like a method.

After HAVE DOUGHNUT, the United States evaluated MiG-17s under programs identified in the public record as HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY. The details are less widely known than the MiG-21 story, but the pattern matters more than the glamour. The United States did not stop after discovering that access to one Soviet fighter produced useful knowledge. It built a habit of exploitation.

The MiG-17 was, by American standards, old-fashioned. It was subsonic, compact, gun-armed, and descended from a design lineage that did not look like the future. It had nevertheless proved dangerous in Vietnam. American fighters designed for high-speed interception and missile combat discovered that a smaller aircraft with different handling characteristics could create ugly problems at close range.

That mismatch between technological prestige and operational danger is one of the oldest traps in military analysis.

A system can be less advanced by one set of measures and more dangerous in a particular geometry. A cheap missile can threaten an expensive aircraft. A slow drone can force a high-end air-defense system to spend a costly interceptor. A subsonic fighter can exploit a turning fight against an aircraft optimized for another regime. Technical intelligence earns its keep by finding where the comparison changes sign.

HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY therefore extended the lesson of HAVE DOUGHNUT: do not ask whether the adversary machine is better. Ask where it is better, where it is worse, and whether your doctrine reliably keeps the fight in the part of the envelope you prefer.

That is a more mature question than the usual language of technological superiority.

The same maturity is useful when examining claims of exotic recovered material. Suppose a sample genuinely exhibited a property outside familiar aerospace practice. The first analytical temptation would be to rank it: stronger, lighter, more conductive, more heat-resistant, more advanced. But advanced compared to what function? A material that is astonishing in tension may be poor under cyclic loading. A structure with unusual electromagnetic response may be mechanically inconvenient. A manufacturing route may require conditions that make production hopelessly slow. A feature that looks like technological superiority may be a narrow optimization.

The MiG-17 was not reverse engineered into an American production aircraft because that was not the point. The point was tactical truth.

That distinction makes foreign materiel a useful antidote to cargo-cult reverse engineering. When a state acquires an adversary system, the highest-value outcome may be a one-page tactical rule, not a copied machine. Stay above this speed. Do not accept this turn. Attack from this quarter. Expect this radar limitation. Force this engine response. A small piece of knowledge can be worth more than a heroic attempt to duplicate an entire design.

This also changes how we should interpret the absence of obvious derivative hardware after an alleged retrieval. Absence of a replica does not by itself disprove exploitation. The program might have produced defensive knowledge, sensor signatures, manufacturing insights or dead ends. But that reasonable possibility is sometimes stretched into an unfalsifiable excuse: because successful exploitation might leave no public product, the absence of any product becomes compatible with every claim.

The historical programs suggest a better standard. Even when the derivative is not a copied aircraft, there are downstream consequences. Training changes. Threat manuals change. Test ranges acquire new missions. Countermeasures are developed. Procurement assumptions move. People are reassigned. Safety procedures appear. Contractors get strange requests. Knowledge has to go somewhere.

A secret can hide the source and still alter the receiver.

This is where the MiG programs begin to look less like isolated intelligence coups and more like a learning architecture. HAVE DOUGHNUT, HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY were not merely about possessing adversary aircraft. They were about converting adversary hardware into American behavior.

That conversion is difficult because classification competes with usefulness. If only a tiny group can know the real performance data, the broader force cannot benefit. If the information is disseminated too widely, the adversary may discover both the acquisition and the vulnerabilities that have been exposed. Technical intelligence has to solve the paradox of secret knowledge: valuable enough to protect, valuable only if used.

The later development of aggressor units and CONSTANT PEG can be read as an organizational solution. Instead of handing every pilot a sensitive exploitation report, expose selected aircrew to realistic adversary behavior through controlled training. Some of the knowledge can be embodied in exercises rather than documents.

That is an elegant form of declassification without disclosure. The student does not need to know the provenance of every lesson. The lesson can be operationalized.

This idea has uncomfortable implications beyond aviation. Secret research often enters the public world indirectly. A manufacturing technique appears in a contractor's capability. A sensor mode becomes standard. A doctrine changes without its original intelligence source being cited. An unexplained procurement requirement can be the shadow of a classified finding.

That does not mean every unusual commercial or military technology is descended from a hidden breakthrough. The inference runs only one way: secret knowledge can diffuse without attribution. It does not follow that unattributed innovation proves a secret source.

The MiG history lets us see the actual bridge. There was an enemy aircraft. There were tests. There were tactical findings. There were institutions built to train those findings. Eventually the institutions themselves became part of the public Air Force story.

HAVE DRILL and HAVE FERRY matter because they turn anecdote into repetition. Once an organization discovers that foreign hardware can correct its imagination, it wants more hardware.

The strange thing is not that intelligence services collect enemy machines.

The strange thing would be if they did not.
