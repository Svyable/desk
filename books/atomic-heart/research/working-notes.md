# Working Notes

## Central collision

Nuclear power did not merely fail to beat a better battery. It succeeded so strongly at source longevity that it revealed the rest of the implanted system as the real maintenance problem. The empirical collision is between unusually favorable generator-survival data and the later record of mode obsolescence, lead failures, lithium improvement and radioactive-source custody after death.

## Keep separate

- source activity vs patient dose;
- generator failure vs lead/system intervention;
- device replacement vs battery failure;
- unique patients vs implant count;
- U.S. clinical-study cohort vs worldwide totals;
- source loss vs documented exposure;
- first-human claims from secondary retellings vs terminal French records;
- AEC promotional incentive vs proof of unsafe decision-making;
- patient clinical benefit vs societal source-control burden.

## Prose guardrails

Do not use `plutonium in the chest` as a repeated shock line. Once the object is established, let the engineering and administrative contradictions carry the book. Do not turn lithium into a triumphalist inevitability. Do not call nuclear pacing suppressed technology. Do not imply a radiation-harm absence stronger than the cohorts can support. Let the funeral-home records stay bureaucratic; their ordinariness is the point.

## Book-level defeat condition

If a source-level archival reconstruction shows the technology was primarily abandoned because of a specific radiological safety defect or a formal regulatory prohibition rather than competition/system burden, rebuild the thesis. If recovery records show near-perfect lifecycle custody at trivial cost, reduce the administrative-half-life argument. If lithium adoption did not materially alter expected replacement intervals at the relevant dates, do not use `good enough` chemistry as the main competitive explanation.
