# Evidence Matrix

## Class 1 — verified / documented

- Pu-238 was used as the power-source isotope in cardiac pacemakers.
- The U.S. Atomic Energy Commission sponsored radioisotope cardiac-pacemaker development, including NUMEC work.
- Peer-reviewed experimental work on radioisotope pacing was published by 1970.
- A U.S. decade-long series reported 151 Pu-238 pacemakers implanted in 131 patients, with 92% actuarial pulse-generator survival at ten years and the last implant in January 1983.
- A separate ten-year study reported superior isotopic-generator longevity/reliability relative to its chemical-battery comparison group.
- A 1973 Numec NU-5 was reported still functioning 34 years later after an intervening lead fracture repair.
- Lithium pacemaker battery research was published by 1971 and lithium systems became the dominant chemical architecture.
- NRC license guidance imposed continuing follow-up, recovery and reporting duties for nuclear-powered pacemakers.
- NRC enforcement cases in the 1990s document missing, buried or improperly controlled Pu-238 pacemakers.

## Class 2 — credible but incomplete

- Exact total worldwide nuclear-pacemaker implantation count.
- Exact first-human implant details and model-level French regulatory record beyond later technical summaries.
- Full patient-level reasons for nuclear-device selection.
- Program-wide consent disclosures and patient understanding.
- Complete recovery fraction after death or replacement.
- Full cost comparison once federal source-production/recovery infrastructure is included.

## Class 3 — disputed / not established

- That nuclear pacemakers were abandoned because patients were being injured by radiation.
- That no nuclear-pacemaker recipient ever experienced a radiation-related adverse effect.
- That lithium immediately made isotopic pacing technically inferior on every performance dimension.
- That later lost-source cases imply widespread public contamination.
- That curie-scale source activity can be treated directly as patient dose.
- That every product described publicly as a nuclear pacemaker used identical isotope inventory, capsule design or thermoelectric architecture.

## Class 4 — speculative synthesis / open conjecture

- Nuclear pacemakers lost mainly because a moving system bottleneck devalued extreme source longevity while leaving regulatory lifecycle costs intact.
- Radioisotope pacing is a clean historical example of `administrative half-life`: institutional knowledge decays faster than a long-lived hazardous component.
- Extreme component longevity creates `progress risk`: the probability that functionality becomes obsolete before hardware fails.
- Future nuclear micro-batteries for implants should be evaluated by host-system lifetime and end-of-life custody, not energy density alone.

## Defeat conditions

The central synthesis should weaken materially if archival evidence shows that radiological harm, source-release incidence, manufacturing unreliability or another single failure dominated the program's abandonment; if lithium performance was not actually a major competitive driver; or if lifecycle licensing/recovery burden was negligible in institutional decisions. The book must change if a complete registry reveals a high unacknowledged rate of source loss, cremation, contamination or radiation injury.
