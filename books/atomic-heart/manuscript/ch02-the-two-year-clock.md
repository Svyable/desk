# The Two-Year Clock

The early pacemaker did not fail on a schedule as neat as a calendar, but patients and physicians learned to live as if a clock were embedded under the skin.

When popular accounts from the early 1970s described nuclear pacing, they often began with the same practical irritation: chemical generators required replacement after only a few years. A 1973 Time report, for example, described the attraction bluntly as escape from a recurring operation caused by battery exhaustion. The exact service life varied by device, circuit, settings and cell design, and magazine shorthand should not substitute for device-by-device survival data. Still, the underlying complaint is well documented in the technical literature. Battery failure was a dominant historical limitation of implanted pulse generators, and the first generation of long-lived sources was being judged against that burden.

The word battery hides the clinical chain it controls. A pacemaker generator sits outside the heart, usually in a subcutaneous or submuscular pocket, connected through leads to cardiac tissue. When a battery approaches depletion, the patient does not simply swap cells. The generator is surgically exposed, disconnected and replaced. If the lead is intact and compatible, it may remain. If the lead is damaged, infected or incompatible, the operation grows. The more often the generator is reopened, the more opportunities arise for infection, pocket problems and connector trouble.

This made longevity unusually valuable even before anyone believed in a literal lifetime device. Suppose a patient needed pacing at age forty and a generator lasted two years. Ten replacement intervals could arrive before sixty. If the generator lasted five years, the same period might require three or four replacements. At ten years, perhaps one. These are schematic numbers, not a historical cohort, but they reveal why engineers cared about energy density far beyond the electrical requirements of the pulse itself. Battery life multiplied into surgery count.

The clinical objective also changed the definition of reliability. In a flashlight, a dead cell is inconvenient. In a pacemaker, the device must reveal depletion early enough for planned replacement, continue operating predictably as voltage falls and avoid sudden failure. Reliability therefore includes chemistry, seals, electronic current drain, telemetry, sensing and the physician's ability to infer remaining life. A source that decays with extraordinary predictability has a conceptual advantage: the physics of Pu-238 does not suddenly discover a bad batch of electrolyte.

That predictability attracted people who were already thinking in nuclear engineering terms. Plutonium-238 has a half-life of roughly eighty-eight years. Over the useful life of a patient, its thermal output declines slowly compared with ordinary battery depletion. The heart does not need to be exposed to alpha particles for this to matter; the source can be encapsulated and its heat converted through thermocouples. The useful electrical power is small, but it is steady.

A steady source does not eliminate the pulse generator's other clocks. That became the atomic pacemaker's most important limitation.

The lead has a clock. Insulation can degrade. Conductors can fracture. The interface with tissue can change. Connectors can fail. Surgical pockets can become infected. Electronics can malfunction. Medicine can decide that a fixed-rate or single-chamber strategy is no longer the best therapy. A patient can develop atrial arrhythmias or conduction changes that make a different pacing mode desirable. The source may still be warm and productive when the therapeutic system built around it no longer deserves to remain in the chest.

This is visible in the later follow-up literature. In the 1984 decade-long U.S. report by Parsonnet and colleagues, nuclear pulse generators performed impressively by the authors' chosen reliability metric, but devices were still replaced. The most common reason was not exhaustion of Pu-238. It was inappropriate pacing mode. That is a devastatingly interesting result because it turns the original sales proposition inside out. The nuclear battery solved the component that engineers in the 1960s could see most clearly. Medicine then improved around it.

The same dynamic appears in the long-lived individual cases that survived well beyond the normal commercial horizon. A 2007 New England Journal of Medicine letter described a woman implanted in 1973 with a Numec NU-5 Pu-238 pacemaker. Eleven years later a lead fractured. The team repaired the lead connection and retained the nuclear generator. Thirty-four years after implantation, the authors reported that the source remained in service. The story is often told as a triumph of nuclear longevity, and it is. But it is also evidence that the weakest link was not necessarily the power source. The generator outlived a lead failure because clinicians could work around the failure without discarding it.

A later 2011 Heart Rhythm case report again described a Pu-238 pacemaker in the context of lead fracture. By then the device was an archaeological object inside a living system. The source had lasted long enough for clinicians trained in a different technological era to encounter it. The historical power problem had become a contemporary management problem.

The competition with lithium must therefore be understood as a moving target rather than a battery contest conducted on a bench. Wilson Greatbatch and colleagues published on a solid-state lithium pacemaker battery in 1971, at precisely the moment nuclear pacing was moving from engineering program toward clinical implantation. Lithium did not need to match an eighty-eight-year half-life. It needed to become good enough that the extra years offered by nuclear power no longer justified the nuclear source's size, cost, regulation and end-of-life burden.

By the early 1980s, the baseline had moved dramatically. A 1983 review of cardiac pacing reported cumulative survival of many lithium-battery pacemakers above 95 percent at five years, rare lead and connector problems, and a field moving toward dual-chamber, multiprogrammable devices with telemetry. Nuclear power had entered the race against mercury-zinc cells and found itself finishing against a different machine.

This matters because historical technological comparisons are often frozen unfairly. We compare the mature winner to the prototype loser. The right question is what a rational clinician, patient, manufacturer or regulator could have known at each decision date. In 1968, a radioisotope source promising a decade or more without battery depletion looked different than it did in 1983. In 1973, repeated generator surgery was not hypothetical; it was part of pacing. In 1983, lithium had accumulated years of actual service and the therapeutic value of programmability had increased.

The source also changed the economics of replacement. A nuclear generator was expensive and specialized, but if it avoided several surgeries it could plausibly compete on lifetime cost. Parsonnet's 1979 appraisal argued that total pacing cost was lower with nuclear units than with lithium or mercury-zinc models in the observed context. That conclusion is historical, not timeless. Cost depends on device price, hospital practice, complication rates, replacement intervals, reimbursement and the value assigned to source tracking. Once lithium service lives improved and manufacturing scaled, the economic crossover could move without the plutonium becoming any worse.

There is a temptation to treat this as a fable about disruption: one technology arrives, another improves, the first dies. The actual sequence is less tidy. Nuclear pacemakers remained clinically impressive even after their commercial trajectory was narrowing. The 1984 papers did not read like autopsies for an obviously failed idea. They argued that isotopic sources still had a place, especially as increasingly complex circuits demanded more energy. The authors were looking at real survival curves, not nostalgia.

What they could not know was how far low-power electronics and lithium chemistry would keep moving. The source that seemed uniquely suited to a high-reliability implant was competing with an industrial ecosystem capable of rapid iteration. A nuclear heat source has excellent endurance and terrible adaptability. It is difficult to miniaturize, repackage, license and dispose of with the casual cadence of consumer electronics. Its strength is that it does not change. Its weakness is that everything around it does.

The two-year clock was therefore the condition that made the atomic heart imaginable. Once that clock slowed, the balance changed.

This is a useful way to think about technology generally. Engineers often optimize against the dominant failure of the current system. If the dominant failure disappears for another reason, the value of the optimized component can collapse even if it performs perfectly. Early pacemaker engineers saw battery depletion because it was visible, frequent and surgically consequential. They responded with one of the most durable power sources medicine could plausibly implant. Then chemistry, circuitry and clinical practice conspired to make durability less singular.

The lesson is not that long life was overrated. Long generator life remains valuable. The lesson is that maintenance intervals belong to systems, not components. A battery can last thirty years while the therapy needs revision in ten. A source can be physically intact while the patient needs a different mode. A device can continue producing electricity after the institution responsible for tracking it has forgotten where it went.

The atomic pacemaker began as an attack on the calendar. Its fate was decided by all the other clocks it could not stop.
