# The Patient Becomes a License

A person with an ordinary pacemaker is a patient carrying a medical device. A person with a Pu-238 pacemaker was also, in an important administrative sense, the moving location of licensed radioactive material.

The patient did not personally become an NRC materials licensee, and it would be wrong to suggest otherwise. The hospital or responsible institution held the regulatory obligations. But the source followed the patient, which meant the licensee's duties followed a human body moving through ordinary life.

That changes the meaning of follow-up.

Cardiology follow-up asks whether the device is pacing correctly, whether thresholds have changed, whether the lead is intact, whether the patient has symptoms and whether the generator is approaching replacement. Nuclear-material follow-up asks an additional question: where is the source?

Model licensing guidance eventually made the difference explicit. Conditions for nuclear-powered pacemaker programs required identified physicians responsible for follow-up, explantation and return; possession limits that included sources in storage and those implanted in patients; continued patient follow-up for life; recovery and authorized disposal after death; reports after loss of contact; prompt notification of patient death; and reporting of adverse reactions or system malfunction.

The striking requirement is loss of contact. A cardiology practice may dislike losing a patient to follow-up, but the nuclear regulator sees something more specific: a radioactive source has become geographically uncertain.

The patient is not confined. They move house, change doctors, cross state lines, enter nursing homes, travel abroad and eventually die under the care of institutions that may never have participated in the implantation. The regulatory system therefore has to maintain a thread through changes that medicine normally treats as administrative noise.

That thread is a serial-number problem attached to a biography.

Long-lived implants make ordinary demographic events part of materials control. A marriage can change a surname. A move can change jurisdiction. Dementia can separate a patient from the knowledge of what is implanted. A family member can become the person who knows. Hospital mergers can move records. Manufacturers can consolidate. A device chosen partly to avoid future operations creates a future in which fewer clinicians have reasons to look closely at it.

The success of the source can reduce the frequency of attention.

This is the inverse of maintenance-driven visibility. A chemical generator nearing depletion repeatedly brings the patient into a replacement pathway where the device is identified and handled. A nuclear generator that remains electrically satisfactory for decades can stay quiet. Its very reliability creates long intervals in which institutional memory can decay.

The regulator's answer was surveillance. But surveillance here should not be confused with monitoring the patient as a security threat. It was source accountability: know who carries which device, know how to contact them, know when the device is removed, know where it goes next.

For a small program, this appears tractable. If 151 devices in a clinical study are distributed across a limited number of centers, a registry can be maintained. Scale creates another question. Public accounts suggest nuclear pacemakers were implanted in larger numbers internationally, particularly in Europe. Each national program had its own regulatory framework. A complete history needs to reconstruct how France, the United States and other countries handled lifelong tracking and source recovery.

The U.S. enforcement record proves that the task survived far beyond the development program. In 1999 the NRC proposed a civil penalty against Washington Hospital Center after a nuclear pacemaker containing 2.8 curies of Pu-238 went missing during attempted return for disposal. The pacemaker had been removed from a deceased patient months earlier. The institution learned the intended recipient had not received it but did not notify the NRC within the required period.

This was not a failure of thermoelectric conversion. It was a shipping and reporting failure.

That distinction is the whole book in miniature.

The radioactive source created obligations that no chemical battery could create. A hospital could not decide that the generator was obsolete and throw it away. The material remained regulated. The patient had carried a durable federal obligation under their skin, even if they never thought about it that way.

There is a civil-liberties edge here that should not be exaggerated. Nuclear pacemaker registries were not secret-police systems. They existed to keep track of hazardous material and protect patients. Still, the device illustrates a category of medicine in which treatment creates a justification for long-term institutional knowledge about a person. Modern implanted devices with wireless telemetry, manufacturer cloud services and remote monitoring raise far larger privacy questions. The nuclear pacemaker is an early analog version: the institution needs to remember you because of what is inside you.

The obligations also blur medical confidentiality and public safety. If a patient disappears from follow-up, how aggressively should a hospital search? What information can it share with regulators? What happens when a patient refuses follow-up? Can the source be left in place against medical advice? What happens if a patient chooses burial or cremation instructions that conflict with source recovery?

The historical record may show that many of these questions were resolved pragmatically rather than litigated. That itself is useful. Rare technologies often live in the space between formal rule and ordinary cooperation. Patients likely accepted identification cards and follow-up because the implant was lifesaving and unusual. Families often cooperated with explantation after death. The system does not need coercion to create durable obligations.

It needs continuity.

Continuity is harder than regulation looks on paper. A rule can say report death within twenty-four hours. It cannot ensure the hospital learns of the death. A rule can say recover the pacemaker. It cannot make a funeral home recognize the model. A rule can say report loss of contact within ten days. It cannot make the old address current.

This is why long-lived radioactive implants are a useful case study in what might be called administrative half-life. Physical materials can remain stable while institutional knowledge decays. Names fall out of databases. Staff turn over. Paper charts move to archives. The device becomes rarer, so training becomes less routine. The risk of forgetting can increase while the physical hazard remains constant.

A mature safety system should be designed for that divergence.

The pacemaker story suggests several ways. The implant itself can carry durable labeling. Patients can receive identification designed to survive decades. Central registries can persist across provider changes. Manufacturers or government programs can maintain source-recovery functions even after product lines close. Funeral industry guidance can identify legacy devices. Regulators can periodically reconcile inventories rather than waiting for a loss.

How well those measures actually worked is an empirical question. The book should not assume administrative decay merely because a few failures occurred. The task is to build the denominator: device-by-device status if records survive, or at least manufacturer and licensee counts reconciled against returned sources.

That work would turn an anecdotal history into a lifecycle audit.

The deeper point does not depend on the final failure rate. The atomic pacemaker makes visible something usually hidden in medical-device design. An implant can redistribute work across time. The patient experiences less surgical maintenance, while institutions accept more informational maintenance. Engineering changes who has to remember what.

For a few curies of Pu-238, somebody had to remember until the patient no longer needed a heartbeat.
