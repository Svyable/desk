# Thirty-Four Years

On April 10, 1973, a twenty-year-old woman received a plutonium-238 pacemaker.

The device was a Numec NU-5. She had complete atrioventricular block following surgical repair of a ventricular septal defect six years earlier. The details survive because more than three decades later Victor Parsonnet and colleagues wrote a short letter to the New England Journal of Medicine titled “A Lifetime Pacemaker Revisited.”

The phrase lifetime was no longer promotional by then. Thirty-four years had passed.

The device had not enjoyed a perfectly uneventful life. Eleven years after implantation, the lead fractured. The clinicians repaired the system by splicing the lead to a new connector while retaining the nuclear generator. The pacemaker continued. In 2007 they reported no subsequent untoward events and documented acceptable stimulation parameters.

It is difficult not to marvel at the generator. Most electronic products from 1973 belong in museums. This one remained inside the same person, performing the same essential task, while entire generations of pacemaker technology arrived around it.

But the case is most useful when the marvel is allowed to become inconvenient.

If the source can last thirty-four years, what else has to last with it? The lead did not. The connector required intervention. The patient's anatomy and clinical status had to remain compatible with the pacing mode. Physicians had to understand a device older than many pieces of equipment in the hospital. Regulatory responsibility had to persist. Eventually somebody would still have to recover the source.

The event therefore demonstrates both the promise and the trap of extreme longevity.

From the patient's perspective, the source may have prevented numerous generator-replacement operations. If a contemporary chemical pacemaker would have required replacement every few years during the 1970s and 1980s, avoiding those procedures was substantial. We should not minimize that benefit because modern devices are better. The correct comparator is historical.

The source also created thirty-four years of low-level radiation exposure. The report did not identify a radiation injury. A single case cannot resolve small stochastic risk. The patient was young enough that cumulative exposure and latency mattered, making her especially informative for long-term follow-up. Her continued survival with the device is reassuring evidence but not a substitute for a cohort.

There is another asymmetry. Battery depletion is almost guaranteed in a finite chemical source if the patient lives long enough. Radiation-induced cancer from a low-dose source is probabilistic. Comparing them requires translating a near-certain maintenance event against a small uncertain health risk. Humans are not naturally good at that kind of comparison, especially when one side is plutonium.

The case strips away some of the emotion because the outcome actually happened. The source kept working. The lead fracture was manageable. The patient lived for decades. Whatever theoretical objections existed, the technology delivered its central benefit to at least this individual.

That does not make it the best design in retrospect. By 2007 a new pacemaker offered capabilities unimaginable in 1973. Dual-chamber coordination, sophisticated sensing, telemetry, programmability and refined algorithms had become ordinary. The old NU-5's endurance had locked in an old therapeutic architecture.

Why keep it? Because replacement is not free. If the patient is stable and the device works, changing it merely to obtain newer features may create more risk than benefit. The clinical decision is local, not ideological. A device can be obsolete in the market and appropriate in one body.

That is another reason installed medical technology behaves differently from consumer electronics. There is no automatic upgrade cycle. The best pacemaker is not necessarily the newest pacemaker; it is the one whose risk-benefit profile fits the patient today.

Extreme device longevity therefore creates heterogeneous futures. Some patients need upgrades. Some can remain on old hardware. Some leads fail. Some patients die. The source's uniform decay curve enters a population whose medical trajectories are anything but uniform.

The 34-year report also raises a practical archival question: how many other nuclear pacemakers lasted comparably long without generating a publication? Case reports preferentially capture remarkable survivors and unusual failures. Ordinary long-lived devices may disappear into follow-up records. To know the true tail of the distribution, one needs manufacturer and licensee registries, not journals.

This is where the remaining nuclear pacemaker population becomes historically precious. Each surviving device is a natural long-duration test of materials, thermoelectrics, seals, electronics and medical management. Removing or replacing one can produce forensic data unavailable from accelerated aging tests. Source output can be measured. Capsule condition can be inspected. Corrosion can be assessed. Records can be compared with actual decades of service.

A systematic return program should therefore have been not only a disposal mechanism but a research opportunity.

Did manufacturers or national laboratories perform post-explant examinations? What did they find? Were thermocouples degrading? Did source capsules remain pristine? Did epoxy or titanium age as expected? Did electronics fail before the power supply? The answers could inform modern long-duration implant design even if no one plans to reintroduce plutonium pacemakers.

The case also exposes how technology history is distorted by market disappearance. Once a product category dies commercially, research interest shifts. A thirty-year survivor may receive a brief letter rather than a full engineering monograph because the field has moved on. The remaining devices become curiosities at precisely the moment they contain the most interesting longevity data.

This is common in infrastructure. Bridges, cables, reactors and medical implants reveal aging behavior only after decades, when the original design teams are gone and funding priorities have changed. The atomic pacemaker compresses that problem into a body.

The word lifetime deserves one more distinction. The pacemaker source can outlive the patient, but the device's clinical lifetime ends when the patient dies. The regulatory lifetime continues until the source is recovered or otherwise accounted for. The physical lifetime of the isotope continues long after disposal.

One object therefore has at least four endings: therapeutic, patient, regulatory and radioactive.

They rarely occur on the same day.

The woman implanted in 1973 made the original promise look almost literal. A battery designed for life was still working more than three decades later. The irony is that this very success helps explain why the technology is so difficult to forget. A short-lived device disappears when it fails. A long-lived radioactive device keeps demanding an ending.
