# Atomic Heart

In 1973, doctors put a few hundred milligrams of plutonium into the chest of a twenty-year-old woman because ordinary pacemaker batteries died too quickly.

Thirty-four years later, the generator was still working.

That sounds like the beginning of either a nuclear horror story or a forgotten miracle. It is neither. The plutonium was sealed, not used to irradiate the heart. Its decay heat fed a thermoelectric generator. The resulting pulse generator belonged to a serious Atomic Energy Commission program with performance targets, source-containment tests, licensing and long-term clinical follow-up. Some units were extraordinarily reliable.

They still lost.

This book is about why.

The answer runs through lithium chemistry, leads that fractured before sources failed, pacing modes that became obsolete while the isotope remained useful, federal rules that required patients to be tracked for life, funeral homes that inherited radioactive-material responsibilities, and a deceptively simple engineering question: what does it mean to build one component to last longer than everything around it?

The source record is uneven. Published clinical papers are strong on generator survival and selected patient outcomes. NRC enforcement records illuminate custody failures decades later. AEC/DOE technical reports preserve source engineering. Actual patient consent forms, complete device-by-device registries and international recovery denominators remain harder to obtain. Where the evidence stops, the prose stops with it.

The four evidence classes used in the research notes are deliberately strict. Documented program records and peer-reviewed clinical outcomes sit apart from credible but incomplete recollection, disputed claims, and the book's own speculative synthesis. A nuclear pacemaker being lost after explantation is documented. A lost pacemaker having injured someone is not established by that fact. A thirty-four-year survivor demonstrates extraordinary longevity. It does not establish universal safety.

The useful history is narrower and stranger: the engineers substantially solved the problem they set out to solve. Then the problem changed.
