# NUMEC and the AEC

The American nuclear pacemaker did not begin in a cardiology company trying to make a slightly better battery. It began at the seam between a medical need and a federal nuclear-industrial apparatus that had spent the postwar decades looking for peaceful uses for isotopes.

That apparatus mattered. The Atomic Energy Commission was not merely a safety regulator. Until its functions were split in the 1970s, it was charged with both developing nuclear technology and regulating nuclear hazards. The contradiction is familiar from reactor history, but the pacemaker makes it unusually intimate. The same state that had built the infrastructure for plutonium production could subsidize a device intended to reduce the number of times a heart patient had to undergo surgery.

The U.S. program drew on companies and laboratories already comfortable with isotope heat sources. NUMEC, the Nuclear Materials and Equipment Corporation, became one of the key contractors. Surviving technical reports in the Department of Energy's archival system preserve a development program concerned with things that disappear from later museum labels: heat-source fabrication, vibration, temperature, capsule construction, performance testing and the transition from prototype work into a clinical power system.

A quarterly report from 1971 is revealing precisely because it is dull. It describes work on Pu-238 heat sources for an implantable cardiac pacemaker under AEC contract AT(30-1)-4189. The report belongs to the same documentary world as hundreds of government-sponsored engineering programs—milestones, tests, material handling, specifications, failures. That bureaucratic texture is useful. It is evidence against the idea that the atomic pacemaker was a publicity stunt attached to the glamour of the nuclear age. Somebody had to make the capsule survive.

The program's performance target was ambitious but intelligible. Later clinical authors described an AEC goal of 90 percent pulse-generator survival at ten years. This was not a promise that 90 percent of patients would live ten years or that 90 percent of entire pacing systems would remain untouched. It was a reliability target for the generator. The distinction matters because a lead could fail while the generator remained functional, a patient could die of unrelated causes, or the device could be replaced for a pacing-mode reason even though its source continued producing power.

That kind of target also shows how the nuclear program translated between disciplines. Nuclear engineers think in containment probabilities, decay curves and source integrity. Cardiologists think in capture thresholds, heart block, arrhythmias, infection and patient survival. Reliability engineers think in component failures and confidence limits. Regulators think in possession, custody, accident conditions and public exposure. The pacemaker had to be legible to all of them.

The development program therefore built a device that was almost overqualified in one dimension. Pu-238 could provide useful heat for far longer than the required ten years. The harder work was proving that a tiny source could be made safe enough to live in a chest. That meant not simply shielding normal operation but demonstrating behavior under abnormal mechanical and thermal events. It also meant deciding how much radioactivity could be justified by the surgery avoided.

The first fifteen units in the later U.S. decade-long clinical series were described as having been designed by NUMEC under AEC contract. That number is modest. The program was not a mass deployment on the scale of ordinary pacemakers. It was a controlled attempt to establish whether nuclear longevity could become a medical product rather than a laboratory curiosity.

The number also complicates one of the common ways this history is told. Atomic-age medicine is often narrated as if public enthusiasm for nuclear technology swept aside caution. There are examples where enthusiasm plainly did distort judgment. But the pacemaker record includes a real safety engineering program, extreme-source testing, clinical follow-up and formal licensing. That does not prove the risk was optimally managed. It means the history is harder than a morality play about reckless nuclear modernism.

A skeptical account should ask different questions. Were the accident assumptions adequate? Was chronic radiation dose characterized honestly? Were patients told enough about the unusual lifetime obligations attached to the source? Were hospitals capable of maintaining records for decades? Did the AEC's promotional interest in peaceful nuclear applications create pressure to prove the program successful? Did a contractor dependent on federal funding have incentives to frame engineering results favorably? These are testable institutional questions. They do not require pretending the engineers ignored containment.

NUMEC itself carries historical baggage because of separate controversies involving highly enriched uranium accounting at its Apollo, Pennsylvania facility. Those controversies should not be imported carelessly into the pacemaker story. A company can participate in multiple nuclear activities, some controversial, without evidence that one compromises every other technical result. The pacemaker program should be evaluated through its own source fabrication records, tests, clinical outcomes and regulatory trail.

This discipline is especially important in nuclear history because names accumulate suspicion. Plutonium, NUMEC and the AEC invite readers to arrive with a story already written. The archival work has to slow that reflex. The relevant question is what the cardiac program did, what it measured, what it missed and how those facts compare with the alternatives then available.

The AEC's institutional design deserves the same restraint. Its combined promotional and regulatory mandate created structural conflicts across nuclear policy. That is documented. It does not follow that every AEC-backed medical device was unsafe or that every safety decision was compromised. The interesting possibility is subtler: a program can be technically careful while its institutional boundary conditions remain optimistic. Engineers may test the source against fire and impact while nobody has yet built a durable national system for finding the source forty years later.

That gap between component safety and lifecycle governance is one of the atomic pacemaker's deepest contributions. The source was engineered as if time were the problem. The administration around it was still human.

The Energy Reorganization Act of 1974 eventually dismantled the AEC's unified structure, placing regulatory functions in the Nuclear Regulatory Commission while development functions moved elsewhere. The pacemaker program therefore crossed an institutional transition. Devices implanted under one federal architecture remained inside people after the responsible agencies had changed names and responsibilities. A patient's body became a kind of continuity mechanism between bureaucracies.

The FDA was changing too. Comprehensive medical-device regulation arrived in 1976, after nuclear pacemaker development was already underway. This meant the technology matured during a period when federal oversight of implantable devices and radioactive material was being reorganized simultaneously. A nuclear pacemaker was not merely a medical device with an odd battery. It was a hybrid object whose legal identity depended on multiple systems learning to coordinate.

Hybrid objects expose weak interfaces. A cardiologist may know the pacing function but not isotope disposal. A radiation-safety officer may know the source license but not the patient's electrophysiology. A funeral director may know that ordinary pacemakers should be removed before cremation without knowing that a particular old device contains plutonium. A manufacturer may disappear or merge. A patient may move between states. Medical records may not follow cleanly.

The federal program could specify source strength and containment more easily than it could guarantee continuity across those interfaces.

This is why the story should not be framed as the AEC putting plutonium into patients. That happened, but the phrase is too easy. The more revealing story is that the government tried to build a medical component whose physical lifetime exceeded the expected lifetime of most of the organizations touching it. It succeeded well enough to make the governance problem real.
