# Paris, 1970

The first human nuclear pacemaker was implanted in France in 1970, and that fact immediately complicates the instinct to tell the story as an American Atomic Energy Commission adventure.

European engineers were pursuing the same problem because the clinical arithmetic did not respect national borders. Pacemaker batteries were short-lived. Reoperation was undesirable. Pu-238 offered an exceptionally durable thermal source. The Laurens-Alcatel system, coupled to Medtronic pacing technology, moved first into a patient.

Later technical literature described the Laurens-Alcatel Model 9000 as a plutonium-238-fueled cardiac pacemaker developed in 1970 and subjected to extensive reliability, shock and corrosion testing. A 1975 Nuclear Technology paper by Michel Alais and colleagues discussed the thermoelectric generator's longevity, energy density and increasingly severe containment tests. The important point is not who won a technological race. It is that separate teams converged on the same architecture because the engineering trade looked plausible.

Convergence is evidence of a problem's shape, not proof of a solution's correctness. When multiple groups choose a nuclear source, it can mean the source is uniquely attractive; it can also mean they share the same assumptions. In 1970 those assumptions included skepticism about the near-term ceiling of chemical batteries. Lithium technology was emerging, but its eventual dominance was not yet guaranteed. Engineers had to decide against a future they could not see.

The French implantation also shifted the politics of the U.S. program. Once a patient was walking around with an isotopic generator, the atomic pacemaker no longer belonged entirely to speculative R&D. Clinical experience could begin accumulating. Safety claims had consequences. Longevity could be measured in years rather than extrapolated from test benches.

Human implantation creates a peculiar kind of evidence. A device can pass vibration tests, thermal cycling and mechanical shock yet fail because living tissue behaves differently than fixtures. Conversely, a source can look frightening on paper and produce no clinically evident radiation injury in follow-up. Medicine forces hardware into an environment of salt water, motion, inflammation, scar tissue, changing anatomy and imperfect adherence to monitoring schedules. It also exposes the device to human life: travel, trauma, other operations, changing doctors and eventually death.

A nuclear pacemaker makes that last fact part of the safety case from the beginning. The source remains radioactive after death. The French and American programs therefore had to imagine the whole biography of the implant, not just the interval between incision and generator depletion.

This is where contemporary enthusiasm sometimes outran institutional imagination. Engineers could specify source capsules capable of surviving extreme physical conditions. Regulatory systems could require return or controlled disposal. But every recovery program depends on information. Someone must know the implant is present. Someone must know who is responsible. Someone must recognize the device. Someone must act before burial or cremation.

In the first years, this may have seemed manageable because the patient population was small and the programs were conspicuous. The physicians knew they were implanting an unusual device. Manufacturers knew the serial numbers. Nuclear regulators knew the licensees. The device's novelty created attention.

Time destroys novelty.

By the 1990s a surviving nuclear pacemaker could be older than some of the clinicians encountering it. The original manufacturer might have changed. The hospital's records system might have migrated more than once. The patient's original cardiologist might be retired or dead. What had once been memorable became rare, and rarity is dangerous in operational systems because people stop rehearsing the procedure.

The French case also raises a less obvious question about evidence transfer. Early reports from Europe helped validate the basic feasibility of radioisotope pacing, but device models differed. Source construction differed. Clinical practice differed. Regulatory requirements differed. It is tempting to treat all nuclear pacemakers as one technology because they share Pu-238. For a serious safety history, they must be separated by manufacturer, model, source activity, capsule design, thermoelectric architecture, pulse-generator electronics, lead system and jurisdiction.

This is one reason the aggregate number of implants is harder to use than it appears. Public accounts sometimes cite more than a thousand nuclear-powered pacemakers worldwide in the 1970s. That figure is useful for scale but cannot substitute for a model-level registry. Some devices used different isotopes or conversion approaches. Some were part of formal clinical studies. Some were commercial European products under national regulatory arrangements. The book's evidence should narrow before it widens.

The first implantation nevertheless changed the meaning of lifetime. A conventional generator with a two- or three-year expected battery life could be discussed in familiar replacement cycles. A Pu-238 unit invited a different promise: perhaps the patient would never need another operation for energy depletion. That promise was emotionally powerful because it translated an obscure nuclear property into something concrete. Fewer surgeries.

The phrase lifetime pacemaker followed naturally, and it contained an ambiguity the technology would spend decades exposing. Whose lifetime? The patient's? The source's? The pacing circuit's? The lead's? The therapeutic mode's? The manufacturer's support obligation? The regulator's tracking record?

A patient could die with decades of source life remaining. A lead could fail while the generator worked. A new pacing mode could make the old electronics clinically inferior. A hospital could lose the device after explantation. The source could outlive the very definition of the product it powered.

Paris in 1970 is therefore not simply a beginning. It is the moment a long-duration engineering claim became a long-duration social experiment.

The safest way to reconstruct that experiment is not through celebratory firsts but through follow-up. Which patients received which units? How often were devices replaced and why? What radiation doses were measured or calculated? What happened when patients underwent diagnostic procedures, surgery or cremation? How many devices were actually recovered at death? How many remained buried? Were there national registries? Did manufacturers maintain contact? How did policy change as the implants aged?

The published medical literature answers some of these questions and leaves others almost embarrassingly open. Clinical papers are strongest on generator survival, pacing performance and observed complications. Nuclear regulators become strongest at the edges—licenses, source possession, lost material, reporting. Funeral and burial records are rarely part of either literature. The complete lifecycle is split across institutions.

That split is not unique to pacemakers. It is a general property of technologies that cross professional boundaries. But the plutonium source makes the consequences unusually visible because the object cannot simply become forgotten scrap.

The French first also offers a warning against American exceptionalism in the history of nuclear medicine. European programs were not copies trailing an American invention. They were active participants in the same technical search. The strongest historical account should recover French regulatory records, Alcatel engineering documents, Medtronic collaboration records, patient follow-up and later retrieval procedures rather than using the 1970 implantation merely as a date on a timeline.

For now, the date is enough to mark the threshold. Before 1970, nuclear pacing could still be discussed as a proposed answer to battery exhaustion. After 1970, a human being was carrying the answer.
