# Last Atomic Hearts

A discontinued implant does not disappear when the catalog is withdrawn.

It becomes a census problem.

By the early 2000s health-physics museum material still estimated that dozens of people in the United States carried nuclear-powered pacemakers. Other later accounts suggested the worldwide number had fallen into the single digits by the end of that decade. These estimates are useful signals but not a substitute for current regulatory data. The exact number alive in 2026 is a question that should be answered from NRC license inventories, manufacturer records and international regulators rather than repeated from old secondary sources.

The uncertainty is itself instructive.

A technology whose entire purpose was long life created an installed base that survived public attention. Most clinicians will never see one. Most patients with modern pacemakers will never hear that plutonium pacing existed. Yet somewhere in the healthcare system, legacy procedures must remain capable of handling the possibility.

This is what technological afterlife looks like in medicine.

Old aircraft can be parked. Old software can be shut down. An implant leaves only when biology or surgery permits. If the device continues working and replacement offers no clinical advantage worth the procedural risk, leaving it alone may be the safest course.

The last nuclear-pacemaker patients therefore live inside a paradox. Their devices are obsolete and successful at the same time.

Obsolete describes the market. Successful describes the local function.

A physician facing one of these patients has to resist both nostalgia and reflexive replacement. The source's age does not by itself prove danger. Pu-238 output declines predictably. The generator may have decades of service history. Removing an old system can be technically challenging, especially if leads are chronically implanted and extraction is contemplated. Conversely, assuming the old device should remain forever ignores changing cardiac needs, component aging and the availability of modern therapy.

The decision belongs to electrophysiology, radiological safety and the patient's preferences together.

That multidisciplinary requirement can be harder to meet as expertise fades. The original nuclear-pacemaker centers accumulated tacit knowledge about models, source strengths and handling. Decades later the expertise may live in retired clinicians, old manuals and regulator files. Rare-event medicine depends on finding knowledge that is no longer routine.

This makes documentation part of patient safety.

A robust legacy packet would include manufacturer and model, serial number, isotope and activity at manufacture, implant date, responsible licensee, expected dose, source capsule design, compatibility information, explant procedure, shipping destination and regulator contact. Ideally that information exists in the medical record and a durable external registry.

Whether it consistently does is a research question.

The old devices also pose a diagnostic problem. Modern imaging and procedures are designed around contemporary pacemakers with known MRI conditions, electromagnetic susceptibility and programming support. A nuclear generator from the 1970s may not fit modern device databases. The isotope is only one concern; old electronics and leads may drive restrictions.

Again the power source is not the whole device.

The remaining population could also provide the strongest empirical answer to the radiation question. Decades of follow-up in patients implanted young could reveal whether chronic low-dose exposure produced any detectable pattern. The sample is small and heavily selected, so statistical power is limited, but careful case-series reconstruction is still valuable. Cause-specific mortality, malignancy history, source dose estimates and device duration could be assembled without claiming more than the denominator supports.

Long-term device inspection after explantation is equally valuable. A fifty-year-old returned generator could answer questions about source containment, thermoelectric degradation, corrosion and electronic aging. Every recovered unit is an archaeological sample from a real physiological environment.

There is a practical urgency because the cohort is disappearing.

When the last patient dies, the opportunity for prospective clinical follow-up ends. The devices can still be studied, but the lived history becomes archival. Clinicians who implanted them are already mostly gone. Early patients' families may be the only source for how the program was explained, what identification they were given and how follow-up felt over decades.

This book is therefore partly a salvage project.

The public story of nuclear pacemakers has condensed into trivia: in the 1970s people had plutonium batteries in their chests; lithium made them obsolete. The technical literature reveals a richer experiment. The devices were not merely possible; they were highly reliable. The clinical program generated survival data. The regulatory system maintained source-control duties long after implantation. Some units functioned for decades. Failures occurred in leads, modes, logistics and institutional memory as much as in generators.

A proper final census would reconcile at least four lists: original implants, known explants, returned/disposed sources and living patients. It would include devices buried with patients and unresolved losses. It would distinguish unique people from repeated implants. It would separate U.S. devices from European models.

This is tedious work and therefore exactly the work required.

The phrase last atomic hearts invites sentimentality, but the real value of the last cases is quantitative. They close the denominator.

How many source-years accumulated? How many generator failures occurred? How many radiation-related adverse events were plausibly observed? How many recoveries failed? How many surgeries were avoided? How many devices were replaced for nonbattery reasons? How many patients carried the same unit until death?

Without those numbers the history remains vulnerable to whichever anecdote a writer prefers.

There is a policy reason to recover them now. Radioisotope micro-batteries are again attracting engineering attention. New materials may reduce source inventory and improve direct conversion. Implantable electronics consume orders of magnitude less power than early pacemakers. A future device could reopen the same trade in a new form.

The last users of the old technology are therefore part of the evidence base for the next one.

They can tell us whether the rare long-tail problems were manageable or merely invisible. They can reveal whether tracking systems survived half a century. They can show which component actually forced replacement. They can test the premise that a nearly maintenance-free power source creates a nearly maintenance-free implant.

The answer is already leaning one way.

The source endured. Maintenance moved.
