# What Counts as Lifetime

The nuclear pacemaker was built around a simple complaint: the battery dies too soon.

Everything that followed was an argument over what too soon meant.

For the patient, too soon could mean another incision in two years. For the engineer, it could mean failure before a ten-year survival target. For the regulator, it could mean a source escaping control before authorized disposal. For the physician in 1983, it could mean a generator that remained electrically healthy long after its pacing mode had become inadequate. For a funeral home, it could mean discovering a radioactive device after the person who carried it had died.

Plutonium-238 solved the original complaint almost brutally. It supplied a timescale so long that battery depletion ceased to be the central problem.

That success exposed the rest of the system.

This is why the atomic pacemaker deserves more than a museum label. It is a controlled historical experiment in extreme component longevity. The source lasted long enough for engineers, physicians and regulators to discover which obligations had been hidden behind ordinary battery replacement.

A short-lived battery forces a periodic reset. The generator is opened, replaced, inspected, re-documented. A long-lived source removes that reset. Leads age continuously. Therapeutic standards evolve. Patient records migrate. Staff turn over. Corporate identities change. The source keeps producing heat.

Longevity creates continuity requirements.

The early developers were not naive about physical safety. They tested source capsules against severe impact, fire, corrosion and cremation conditions. Clinical programs followed patients. Regulators imposed recovery and reporting duties. The devices were not casual experiments in radioactive optimism.

The more interesting weakness was distributed across institutions. No capsule design can ensure a funeral home returns the source. No half-life can preserve a medical chart. No thermoelectric converter can keep a pacing mode clinically current.

The market eventually chose a different optimization. Lithium batteries improved enough that generator replacement intervals became acceptable. Electronics became more capable and more efficient. Pacing modes evolved quickly enough that replacing a generator could deliver therapeutic value rather than merely restore depleted energy. The nuclear source's extraordinary endurance became less decisive while its regulatory burden remained.

This does not mean lithium proved nuclear pacing wrong.

It means the objective function changed.

That distinction matters in every debate over exotic technology. We often ask whether something works as if work were a permanent property. The atomic pacemaker worked. Clinical reports document high generator survival. Individual devices remained functional for decades. The better question is whether its distinctive advantage continued to matter more than its distinctive costs.

By that measure the verdict can change without contradiction.

In 1973, a patient facing frequent chemical-generator replacement might rationally accept a Pu-238 implant. In 1983, a physician considering modern lithium devices with multiprogrammability and improving five-year survival might choose differently. In 2007, a patient with a stable thirty-four-year-old nuclear generator might rationally keep it rather than undergo elective replacement. One technology can be right in three different ways at three different times.

The history also warns against treating regulation as dead weight separate from engineering. The recovery rules were part of the system because Pu-238 remained radioactive after medical use ended. When hospitals lost devices, regulators were not objecting to paperwork for its own sake. They were responding to an uncontrolled source.

Yet regulation has opportunity costs. Tracking patients for life, recovering devices after death, maintaining special licenses and routing returned sources through authorized channels all consume effort. A safer or simpler competing technology can win partly because it eliminates those obligations.

The burden must be counted without moralizing it.

This is one reason the best remaining research project is a complete lifecycle ledger. Build the registry of implants. Track each generator through replacement, death, recovery and disposition. Match generator failures to causes. Reconstruct dose by model. Count surgeries avoided. Count source-control failures. Compare costs at historical decision dates. Recover actual consent forms. Interview the last clinicians and patients while that is still possible.

Only then can the story answer its most ambitious question: was the nuclear pacemaker a good technology that lost to a better system, or a technically impressive detour whose broader costs were visible too late?

The evidence currently leans toward the first.

The generators often performed very well. The source was capable of exceptional duration. There is no documented epidemic of radiation injury in the public clinical literature identified for this draft. The technology faded as lithium pacing improved and as pacemakers themselves became more sophisticated. Later NRC cases reveal lifecycle friction but do not, without a denominator, establish pervasive failure.

That posture is deliberately provisional. It can be defeated.

If archival records show serious unreported source releases, systematically underestimated dose, high rates of unrecovered pacemakers or clinical harms obscured by selective publication, the book's judgment should change. If a full cost accounting shows nuclear pacing was never competitive once regulatory infrastructure is included, the economic chapter should change. If early consent forms failed to disclose material information about radiation and postmortem recovery, the ethics chapter should become harder.

A thesis worth keeping should make its own defeat legible.

There is also a modern conjecture the history cannot settle. Ultra-low-power implants may make nuclear micro-batteries attractive again. New direct-conversion materials can reduce the need for thermoelectric heat engines. Devices intended to sense rather than stimulate may require so little power that isotope inventories become tiny. A future engineer may look at the atomic pacemaker and conclude that the old idea simply arrived before the right electronics.

Perhaps.

But the old devices already ask the question that the future proposal will be tempted to postpone: does the host system need to last as long as the source?

If a sensor is obsolete in eight years, a fifty-year battery is not automatically progress. If a neural interface requires revision in five, decades of power merely create an object that must be managed after usefulness ends. If a tiny radioactive source survives the manufacturer, somebody still owns the end of its story.

The nuclear pacemaker's deepest lesson is therefore not about nuclear energy. It is about the seduction of solving the hardest visible component.

Engineers saw batteries failing and built a battery that almost refused to die. Medicine then revealed that the battery had never been the whole problem.

A lifetime device is not a device whose power source lasts for life.

It is a device whose consequences have been designed all the way to the end.
