# A Few Curies in the Chest

A nuclear pacemaker forces the word dose to do more work than the word plutonium.

The source activity sounds alarming because curies are large units and plutonium carries the emotional freight of weapons, contamination and long-term radiotoxicity. Some historical pacemakers contained several curies of Pu-238. NRC enforcement records from the 1990s identify devices containing 2.8 and approximately 4.8 curies. Those numbers are real. They are not, by themselves, a patient dose.

Activity counts nuclear transformations. Dose describes energy deposited in tissue, adjusted in various ways for biological effect. Between the two stands the source design: fuel form, encapsulation, geometry, shielding, isotopic impurities, distance, tissue and the kinds of radiation emitted. Pu-238's dominant alpha radiation is easily stopped by the source capsule and nearby material; alpha radiation becomes a much more serious biological hazard if plutonium escapes and is inhaled or incorporated internally. The intact-source problem and the breached-source problem are therefore fundamentally different.

Historical health-physics summaries place whole-body patient exposure from some nuclear pacemakers around a tenth of a rem per year, with much smaller exposures to a spouse. Device-surface dose rates could be higher because proximity matters. Those figures should not be applied indiscriminately to every model, but they establish the scale of the safety argument engineers and regulators were making. The intended chronic exposure was low enough to be weighed against the clinical burden of repeat operations, not high enough to make radiation injury an expected therapeutic effect.

This distinction is easy to lose in both directions. Promotional accounts can reduce the source to a harmless battery because the alpha particles are contained. Alarmist accounts can treat the activity number as though curies in a sealed titanium device imply curies delivered to tissue. Neither helps.

The source had to solve three different radiation problems. The first was ordinary external exposure during years of implantation. The second was release during an accident, fire, corrosion or medical intervention. The third was release after death, when the pacemaker could be subjected to processes never intended as clinical environments.

The third problem is the strangest because it turns funeral practice into nuclear safety engineering.

Cremation furnaces reach temperatures capable of destroying ordinary medical devices. A source capsule therefore had to be either reliably removed before cremation or engineered to retain plutonium under severe thermal conditions. Designers pursued both layers: administrative recovery plus physical containment. This is defense in depth, but it contains an uncomfortable admission. If the paperwork fails, the metal must be good enough to rescue the system.

A 1975 report on a U.S. isotopic pacer emphasized testing against cremation, impact, accidental crush, industrial fire and corrosion. European systems underwent similarly severe containment tests. The tests were not proof against every imaginable event. They were attempts to define credible accidents and show that the source would remain intact.

The phrase credible accident deserves attention. Safety engineering always draws a boundary around what must be survived. That boundary reflects physics, experience, cost and institutional imagination. A source can be designed to survive a cremation profile but still be lost in the mail. It can survive mechanical shock but be buried because no one recognizes the implant. It can remain hermetic while the licensee loses contact with the patient. Extreme physical strength does not compensate for weak identification.

The patient-dose calculation has a similar institutional boundary. A chronic low radiation dose might be acceptable for a patient whose alternative includes repeated surgery. But what about children, young adults or patients expected to live many decades? What about workers routinely near the patient? What about pregnancy? What about diagnostic radiation and cumulative exposure from other medicine? A model that looks negligible for an older patient with severe conduction disease may deserve different scrutiny for a twenty-year-old expected to carry the source for fifty years.

The published literature includes precisely such long-duration cases. The woman whose 1973 Numec NU-5 remained in service for at least thirty-four years was twenty at implantation. That is the kind of patient for whom the lifetime source promise mattered most and for whom cumulative exposure had the longest time to accumulate. The report did not describe radiation injury. It also does not by itself settle population-level risk. One patient is evidence of feasibility and extraordinary device longevity, not an epidemiological study.

The safety case must also distinguish radiation from plutonium toxicity. Plutonium's primary danger changes dramatically if the material remains sealed versus if it becomes respirable contamination. The reason regulators care so much about retrieval is not that an intact pacemaker is a roaming radiation catastrophe. It is that unaccounted special nuclear material can eventually enter a process that defeats containment.

That is why later NRC enforcement letters use the language of control and surveillance. When a nuclear pacemaker is explanted, it is licensed material outside the body. The legal problem is no longer cardiac. A hospital that loses it has lost control of a radioactive source.

The 1998 Washington Hospital Center event is instructive. A pacemaker containing 2.8 curies of Pu-238 had been removed from a deceased patient and returned to the medical facility. It was intended for shipment to a manufacturer for disposal. The NRC later concluded that the hospital failed to maintain control and could not account for the device for months. The violation concerned source accountability and delayed reporting, not an identified public radiation injury.

That difference should remain explicit. Missing source does not equal exposure. It equals loss of assurance.

Nuclear regulation is often about preserving assurance before harm occurs. A license requires inventory, tracking, reporting and authorized disposal because waiting for measurable injury would be an absurd safety strategy. The atomic pacemaker brought that preventive logic into ordinary medical institutions not otherwise organized like nuclear facilities.

A hospital is good at many kinds of custody. It tracks controlled drugs, blood products, pathology specimens and implants. But a radioactive pacemaker demands custody over a timescale that may exceed ordinary retention schedules. The patient can leave the health system. The source can cross institutional boundaries at death. Funeral homes and families become part of the control chain even though they were not part of the implant team.

The chronic dose, then, is only one part of the radiological story. The more unusual risk lies in persistence.

Pu-238's half-life is the feature that makes the power source attractive. It is also why the material remains worth caring about decades later. The device does not become administratively harmless when the medical benefit ends. Its physical persistence keeps reopening the obligation.

Modern discussions of nuclear batteries sometimes emphasize that alpha-emitting sources can be made safe through encapsulation. That is technically relevant but incomplete. The old pacemakers demonstrate that containment is a lifecycle practice, not a material property. It includes source manufacture, implantation, patient education, records, clinical follow-up, transfer, explantation, shipping, recovery and final disposition. The capsule is one layer inside a much larger container made of institutions.

This may be the most durable lesson of putting a few curies in a chest. If the source is designed to outlive the product, safety cannot end at the product boundary.
