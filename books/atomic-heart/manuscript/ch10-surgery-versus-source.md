# Surgery Versus Source

The atomic pacemaker cannot be judged by asking whether radiation is dangerous. Surgery is dangerous too.

That sounds like the setup to a false equivalence, so the categories need to remain separate. A low chronic radiation dose, a small probability of source release and the morbidity of generator-replacement operations are not interchangeable harms. They occur through different mechanisms, on different timescales, to different populations. The engineering decision was nevertheless forced to compare them because a patient received one architecture or another.

The relevant alternative to a nuclear pacemaker in 1973 was not a perfect modern lithium device with decades of accumulated reliability data. It was the contemporary chemical generator. If the expected battery life meant repeated operations, the nuclear source could eliminate some of those operations. Avoided procedures are real benefits even if no one can point to a specific infection that never happened.

This kind of counterfactual benefit is easy to discount because it leaves no scar. A patient who keeps the same generator for ten years produces less visible evidence than a patient who undergoes three uneventful replacements. Yet the reason medicine seeks long device life is precisely to remove events from the future.

The historical economic analyses understood this. By 1979 Parsonnet argued that total pacing cost with nuclear units could be lower than with lithium or mercury-zinc units in the observed setting. The conclusion depended on device costs and replacement assumptions of its time, and it should not be carried forward as a universal fact. But it shows the correct unit of analysis: not battery price, but lifetime pacing.

Lifetime pacing includes hospital stays, operating-room use, physician time, anesthesia, generator cost, complications, lost work and the patient's willingness to return for another incision. A nuclear source imposed its own costs—special manufacturing, radioactive-material licensing, follow-up, return, disposal and long-term institutional custody. The comparison was a contest between maintenance systems.

Radiation protection added a problem with no direct chemical-battery analogue. A patient could avoid surgery yet accumulate low external dose year after year. The medical literature reported no evident radiation toxicity in the observed nuclear-pacemaker cohorts, but cohort size and follow-up constrain what absence means. A rare long-latency effect might not be detectable in a small population. Many pacemaker recipients were older or had serious cardiac disease, which can shorten the observational window for cancer outcomes.

The strongest safety argument was therefore not that radiation posed zero risk. It was that estimated doses were low, source containment was robust, and the clinical alternative carried its own measurable burdens.

Age changes that trade. A young patient may accumulate more years of exposure and has more time for latent effects to appear. The same young patient also has more years in which repeated generator surgery can accumulate. The nuclear source becomes simultaneously more attractive and more consequential.

The 1973 case later followed for thirty-four years sits exactly in that tension. A twenty-year-old woman with complete atrioventricular block after ventricular septal defect repair received a Numec NU-5. If an ordinary generator required replacement every few years, her lifetime surgery burden could have been substantial. The Pu-238 generator persisted. A lead fractured eleven years later and was repaired without discarding the source. The device's longevity was not theoretical.

One case cannot prove population safety, but it can reveal the decision structure. For that patient, battery endurance mattered because the rest of the system could be repaired around it.

The source risk also extended to people other than the patient. Spouses and clinicians could receive small external exposures. Funeral workers and waste handlers could encounter a device after death. A lost source creates a public-safety obligation even if the patient benefited. This means the architecture can shift risk outward while reducing risk inward.

That is another reason simple patient-level cost-benefit analysis is incomplete.

Nuclear technology often has this property: the user receives concentrated utility while institutions manage diffuse tail risks. A radioisotope generator on a spacecraft enables a mission while launch-safety organizations model accidents. A medical source enables therapy while hospitals and regulators manage possession. The pacemaker made the distribution personal because the source literally traveled with one person.

The patient consent question follows. What did patients understand about these tradeoffs? Were they told the source contained plutonium-238, the expected dose, the possibility of decades-long tracking, the requirement for explantation at death and the alternatives available? Clinical papers emphasize engineering and outcomes more than the texture of consent. Recovering actual consent forms from early U.S. and European programs is therefore essential.

It would be easy to impose modern informed-consent expectations backward without context. The nuclear pacemaker was introduced during an era when medical-device regulation and research oversight were changing rapidly. That does not excuse omissions. It makes the historical standard itself part of the evidence. A patient considering a novel radioactive implant needed information different from a patient receiving a standard chemical generator.

The strongest possible consent form would have made the comparison explicit: repeated future surgery is likely with current batteries; this device uses a sealed radioactive heat source intended to last much longer; radiation exposure is expected to be low but not zero; the source must remain tracked and should be removed and returned after death; other components may still fail or become obsolete; long-term experience is limited.

Whether real forms said all of that remains an archival question.

Clinical enthusiasm can distort tradeoffs without anyone lying. A physician who has watched patients return repeatedly for exhausted generators may reasonably value a source that promises to end that cycle. Engineers proud of containment tests may focus on physical failure more than institutional recovery. Nuclear agencies seeking peaceful applications may emphasize the public good of isotope technology. Patients may hear one phrase—no more battery operations—and accept uncertainties that are difficult to quantify.

The job of historical analysis is not to assume bad faith. It is to reconstruct where the burdens went.

Lithium eventually changed the distribution. As chemical generator life improved, fewer operations were avoided by choosing nuclear power. The radioactive-source burdens did not shrink at the same rate. At some point the balance could cross even if the isotopic device itself had not changed.

This is the kind of technological defeat that produces no scandal. The competitor simply gets good enough.

If a lithium pacemaker lasts long enough that most patients need few generator replacements, the marginal benefit of another twenty years of potential source life is small. If new pacing modes and electronics make planned generator upgrades clinically useful, extreme battery longevity can become less valuable still. Meanwhile the nuclear device retains all of its special licensing, manufacturing and disposal obligations.

The source loses not because plutonium becomes more dangerous, but because surgery becomes less frequent.

That should make the atomic pacemaker interesting far beyond medicine. Technologies are often compared on headline performance: energy density, range, speed, accuracy, model score. The rational choice depends on the burden of the alternative. When the alternative improves, an exotic technology can lose value without losing performance.

The atomic heart was built to avoid a knife. Its commercial fate depended on how often the knife still had to come out.
