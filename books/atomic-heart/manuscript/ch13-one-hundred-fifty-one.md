# One Hundred Fifty-One

There is a moment in the history of an experimental medical technology when anecdotes stop being enough.

The first implantation proves possibility. The first five-year survivor proves persistence. A spectacular thirty-year case proves that the ceiling is high. None of those tells you what happens to a population.

For the American plutonium pacemaker, the 1984 decade-long report by Victor Parsonnet and colleagues is one of the clearest population anchors. It described 151 Pu-238-powered pacemakers implanted in 131 patients. Four manufacturers' units were represented. The first fifteen had been designed by NUMEC under an Atomic Energy Commission contract. The clinical study began in April 1973; the last nuclear pacemaker in the series was implanted in January 1983.

At publication, ninety pulse generators remained in service. Twenty-five patients had died. Thirty-six generators had been replaced by non-nuclear units. The reported actuarial survival at ten years was 92 percent, meeting the Commission's original 90 percent target.

Every one of those numbers needs a denominator attached.

Ninety still in service does not mean the other sixty-one failed. Twenty-five belonged to patients who died. Thirty-six were replaced for various reasons, the most common being an inappropriate pacing mode. Actuarial survival is not crude survival. Censoring matters. Replacement reason matters. The unit of analysis matters.

That may sound like statistical housekeeping, but it changes the history. If the nuclear battery is being judged as a power source, replacing a still-functioning generator because a different pacing mode is desired is not a battery failure. If the technology is being judged as a promise of avoiding future generator operations, the replacement still matters to the patient.

The two interpretations are not enemies. They answer different questions.

This is the point at which the book's evidence posture needs to become almost pedantic. The nuclear pacemaker attracts extreme storytelling because the underlying object is extreme. A few curies of plutonium in the chest sounds like something that must have either been a triumph suppressed by timid regulators or an atomic-age mistake quietly abandoned. The cohort data support neither caricature.

The generators performed well.

That sentence should be allowed to stand without qualification for a moment. The nuclear source did what its advocates wanted often enough that investigators could report ten-year survival above the AEC target. In a separate 1984 study of seventy-nine isotopic pacemakers in seventy-five patients, N. P. Smyth and M. L. Millette compared them with chemical-battery controls and concluded that isotopic units had superior longevity and reliability. The authors still saw a possible future role for radioisotope power in increasingly complex pacemakers.

If the technology disappeared despite these results, the explanation must be larger than technical incompetence.

The cohort also invites a patient-level reconstruction that the summary article cannot provide. Who were the 131 patients? What were their ages, diagnoses and expected survival at implantation? Which manufacturers' devices went to which patients? What were the source activities and generator models? How many patients were pacemaker dependent? Which replacements were for lead failure, infection, pacing-mode inadequacy, elective upgrade, radiation concern or other reasons? How many devices were explanted after death and successfully returned?

A true lifecycle history would rebuild the 151-unit dataset row by row.

That work matters because averages can conceal the selection logic. Nuclear pacemakers may have been preferentially implanted in younger patients expected to outlive several chemical generators, or in patients at particular centers enthusiastic about the technology. If so, their outcomes cannot be casually compared with all contemporary pacemaker recipients. Conversely, if they were used in unusually high-risk or complicated patients, simple comparisons could undervalue performance.

The 1979 five-year appraisal provides an earlier window. Parsonnet reported personal observation of 131 radioisotope-powered pulse generators in 120 patients, with 96 percent actuarial survival at five years, two component failures and no battery failures. The paper also argued that total pacing cost was lower than with lithium or mercury-zinc units. That conclusion is historically important because it shows the nuclear device clearing not just a laboratory bar but an economic one under contemporary assumptions.

But the two reports also illustrate the problem of overlapping cohorts. One cannot add 120 patients from 1979 to 131 patients from 1984 and call the result 251 people. The later study almost certainly includes many of the earlier patients. Historical totals must be reconstructed from original registries rather than summed from publications.

The same caution applies internationally. European reports, manufacturer series and national programs may overlap. Some patients received replacement nuclear units, meaning implant count exceeds unique-patient count. A headline figure for total worldwide nuclear pacemakers is only as good as the underlying deduplication.

This book therefore treats 151 units in 131 patients as a bounded U.S. clinical-study fact from the 1984 paper, not as the total history of nuclear pacing.

The cohort is also a reminder that nuclear safety evidence can be dominated by competing mortality. Pacemaker recipients in the 1970s were often older adults with serious conduction disease and other cardiac conditions. If many patients die within a decade from unrelated causes, long-latency radiation risks become difficult to measure. The absence of observed toxicity is reassuring but statistically different from proving no incremental risk.

For a young recipient, the follow-up challenge becomes the opposite: one person can carry the device for decades, but the population of such recipients is small. Rare effects remain hard to distinguish from background disease.

This is why clinical evidence and dosimetry have to work together. Dose estimates can predict that radiation risk should be small; cohort follow-up can check whether unexpected signals appear. Neither alone is enough.

The power-source reliability evidence is stronger because the event is easier to observe. A generator either continues providing adequate electrical output or it does not. Even there, electronics and thermoelectric converter failures have to be separated from isotope depletion, which is effectively negligible over the interval.

The result is a technology with an unusually clean source and an unusually messy system.

The number 151 is therefore less important as a scale claim than as a challenge to explanation. If more than a hundred patients carried these devices, if the generators met decade-long performance targets and if the literature remained favorable, why did implantation stop?

The answer that emerges across the records is not one event. It is a convergence: lithium improved, pacing functionality evolved, source recovery imposed durable regulation, device size and flexibility mattered, and the marginal value of extreme battery life declined.

A technology can be defeated by a portfolio of small disadvantages.

That is harder to narrate than a recall or disaster. There is no single day when nuclear pacing failed. The last implant in this U.S. series occurred in January 1983. After that, the installed base became a shrinking experiment in longevity. New nuclear patients stopped arriving while old ones kept generating data.

The cohort changed from a market into a watch list.

This transition is visible in the later regulatory record. By the 1990s, the newsworthy nuclear pacemaker event was not a new implant but a source that had been lost, buried or improperly handled after death. Clinical innovation had moved on. Custody remained.

A source can exit medicine more slowly than medicine exits the source.
