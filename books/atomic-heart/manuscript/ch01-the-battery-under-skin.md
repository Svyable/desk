# The Battery Under Skin

The problem that made a nuclear pacemaker seem reasonable was not a shortage of electricity. It was a shortage of years.

A cardiac pacemaker asks very little from a power source in the ordinary electrical sense. It does not propel a vehicle or light a room. It waits, senses, and when necessary delivers a small pulse to tissue. The energy budget is tiny. The consequence of exhausting that budget is not. Once the generator is implanted under skin and connected to a lead threaded toward the heart, the battery becomes part of a surgical system. Replacing it means reopening the patient. In the first decades of implantable pacing, that happened often enough to turn battery chemistry into a clinical problem.

This is easy to forget because modern implantable devices inherit decades of quiet improvement. The early pacemaker was born before that inheritance existed. Pulse-generator circuits consumed more power. Mercury-zinc cells did not offer the long, predictable service life later associated with lithium systems. Hermetic sealing, connectors, leads, sensing, programmability, telemetry and manufacturing quality were all still moving targets. A patient could survive the condition that had once threatened to kill them and then find that survival organized around the expected depletion of a chemical cell.

That recurring operation was not merely an inconvenience. Any operation creates an incision, an infection opportunity, anesthesia exposure, bleeding risk, lead damage risk, hospital time and cost. A generator replacement is usually much less dramatic than the original problem that made pacing necessary, but medicine does not get to average away complications because the procedure is routine. Repetition converts a small per-procedure risk into a lifetime architecture.

By the early 1970s the architecture looked strangely open to nuclear technology. The Atomic Energy Commission and its contractors had spent years learning how to turn isotope decay heat into electricity for devices that could not conveniently be serviced. Spacecraft are the obvious example now, but the engineering principle is broader. A radioisotope heat source does not need sunlight, a charging cable or chemical reactants replenished from outside. Plutonium-238 decays continuously. Put that heat across a thermoelectric converter and a small electrical output can persist for years with no moving parts.

A heart is not a spacecraft. That distinction matters throughout this book. Space hardware can tolerate engineering compromises that would be absurd inside a person, and a failed space power source may threaten a mission rather than a living patient. But the shared power problem was real: how do you make a small source that keeps giving useful electricity after every ordinary battery would have been replaced several times?

The answer produced one of the oddest objects in twentieth-century medicine: a titanium-cased pulse generator holding a sealed plutonium-238 heat source. Depending on model and source, the device could contain curie-scale activity. The radioactive material was not there to irradiate the heart or treat a tumor. It was fuel. Alpha decay produced heat; a thermoelectric assembly turned part of that heat into electricity; the electronics turned electricity into timing and pulse output. The nuclear material occupied the same conceptual slot as the battery chemistry it was trying to displace.

That sentence is technically true and socially misleading. A lithium cell and several curies of plutonium-238 are not equivalent merely because both can be drawn as a box labeled power supply. A chemical pacemaker becomes ordinary medical waste only after the ordinary precautions appropriate to its materials. A radioisotope pacemaker remains a regulated source after the patient dies. It matters at the funeral home. It matters at cremation. It matters if the device is buried. It matters if a hospital ships it to the wrong place or loses track of it. It matters decades later when the clinical team encountering the patient may never have seen one before.

This is the central tension of the atomic pacemaker, and it is more interesting than the fact that someone once put plutonium in a chest. The device was designed to solve a maintenance problem by replacing a consumable component with a source whose persistence was almost embarrassingly greater than the rest of the system. The source could keep going while leads fractured, pacing modes became obsolete, patients died, manufacturers changed product lines and regulatory agencies reorganized. The power supply was made so durable that medicine inherited a custody problem measured in human lifetimes.

The case is especially useful because the nuclear pacemaker was not a spectacular scientific failure. Retrospective clinical reports were often favorable. In one 1984 account, 151 Pu-238-powered units had been implanted in 131 patients as part of a decade-long study. The reported actuarial survival of the pulse generators at ten years was 92 percent, meeting the Atomic Energy Commission's stated 90 percent performance goal. Another ten-year study compared isotopic and chemical devices and found the isotopic units more reliable and longer-lived. Those results do not mean every device or implantation was successful, and they do not settle radiation or regulatory questions, but they make one lazy explanation impossible: nuclear pacemakers did not disappear simply because they were an obviously bad battery.

That is why their disappearance deserves reconstruction. Technologies often vanish because they fail. More interestingly, some vanish because the problem around them changes. Lithium batteries improved. Electronics grew more complex. Pacemakers became smaller, more programmable, easier to monitor and more clinically sophisticated. Long generator life remained valuable, but the rest of the device stopped standing still. A power source capable of outliving several generations of circuit design can become less compelling if clinicians want to replace the generator for reasons unrelated to energy.

A long-lived component is only as useful as the architecture around it.

The nuclear pacemaker also belongs to a particular regulatory moment. The modern U.S. system of medical-device premarket review did not yet exist when the concept was being developed. The Food and Drug Administration's comprehensive device authority arrived with the Medical Device Amendments of 1976. The Atomic Energy Commission, meanwhile, combined promotional and regulatory roles in nuclear technology until the Energy Reorganization Act split functions between the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and the Energy Research and Development Administration. An implanted radioisotope pacemaker therefore sat at an institutional seam: medicine, device engineering and nuclear-material control did not share one natural regulator or one definition of success.

That seam persisted. Decades after implantation, NRC enforcement records show hospitals being cited over missing or improperly disposed nuclear pacemakers. In a 1998–99 Washington Hospital Center case, the missing device contained 2.8 curies of plutonium-238. In a separate 1996–97 Pennsylvania case, the NRC investigated loss of control over a device containing approximately 4.8 curies; supplier records also indicated additional devices had been buried with deceased patients or not returned. These are not evidence that patients were radiologically harmed by the implants. They show something else: the source's regulatory life could continue after its clinical life had ended.

The most revealing question is not whether plutonium sounds frightening. It is whether the whole system was better.

To answer that, the accounting has to be unusually literal. How often did early chemical generators actually require replacement? What was the morbidity of those operations? How much radiation dose did a patient receive from an isotopic unit? How robust was the source capsule to fire, impact, corrosion and cremation? How often did nonbattery components force reoperation anyway? What did nuclear units cost compared with chemical ones over a lifetime? How burdensome was patient tracking? How many units were lost, buried, cremated or returned? How quickly did lithium chemistry move the baseline while the isotopic program was still proving itself?

The answer cannot be extracted from one dramatic anecdote. The atomic pacemaker is a system in which the obvious hazard and the actual failure mode are often different. The plutonium may remain sealed while a lead breaks. The nuclear source may perform exactly as designed while the pacing mode becomes obsolete. A patient may benefit from decades without generator depletion while a hospital inherits a radioactive-material obligation it has trouble maintaining after the patient dies. A technology can be locally excellent and globally cumbersome.

There is a second reason to follow this device closely. Radioisotope batteries are again discussed for sensors, remote infrastructure, space missions and, occasionally, medical implants. The material science has changed. Converter designs have changed. Implantable electronics consume less power. But the old case already ran the experiment modern enthusiasm tends to skip. It put a decades-long energy source inside people and then waited long enough to discover that energy longevity is not the same as system longevity.

The patients are therefore more than historical curiosities. They are long-duration tests that no laboratory could compress. A thirty-year implant contains information about hardware, medicine, regulation and institutional memory. It tells us what happens when a device lasts longer than the team that implanted it, longer than the product cycle that justified it and sometimes longer than the administrative routines built to track it.

The strange thing about the nuclear pacemaker is not that twentieth-century engineers were willing to put plutonium in people. They were trying to stop cutting those people open every few years. The stranger thing is that, for a while, the argument worked.
