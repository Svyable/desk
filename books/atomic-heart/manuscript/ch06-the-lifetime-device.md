# The Lifetime Device

Engineers use lifetime differently from patients.

A patient hears the word and imagines never returning to an operating room because the battery has run down. A reliability engineer hears a survival curve, a confidence interval and a set of defined failure modes. A nuclear engineer hears source containment through normal operation and credible accidents. A regulator hears custody until authorized disposal. These meanings overlap just enough to create trouble.

The atomic pacemaker's most persuasive claim was that its energy source could exceed the useful life of the patient. Pu-238 made this physically plausible. Its half-life is measured in decades longer than most human implant intervals. But a pacemaker is not a lump of isotope. It is a system. The lifetime of the system belongs to the component that forces intervention first.

The U.S. clinical program made that measurable. In the decade-long study reported by Victor Parsonnet and colleagues in 1984, 151 Pu-238-powered units were implanted in 131 patients. Four manufacturers' pacemakers were represented. The first fifteen had been designed by NUMEC under Atomic Energy Commission contract. The last nuclear pacemaker in the series was implanted in January 1983.

At ten years, the reported actuarial survival of the pulse generators was 92 percent. The authors noted that this met the original Commission performance goal of 90 percent. Ninety generators were still in service at the time of publication; twenty-five patients had died, and thirty-six generators had been replaced with non-nuclear units.

Those numbers are more interesting than a simple success rate. A patient death is not a generator failure. A replacement is not necessarily a power-source failure. The authors reported that the most common indication for replacement was an inappropriate pacing mode. In other words, the energy source could still be doing exactly what it was built to do when medicine decided the device around it was no longer the right therapy.

That is technological obsolescence in its purest form: a component remains functional while the definition of useful function changes.

The nuclear pacemaker had been optimized against the visible pain of the 1960s—battery depletion. By the 1980s pacing was becoming programmable, dual-chambered and increasingly responsive to a patient's physiology. A generator that could fire for twenty years but could not provide the desired mode might be less useful than a chemical device expected to be replaced sooner.

This seems obvious in retrospect because modern electronics train us to expect software and functionality to improve faster than hardware wears out. It was not obvious when an implanted pulse generator was essentially a fixed-function life-support device and every battery change required surgery. Long life was not a marketing flourish. It attacked a clinical burden.

The 1984 authors did not conclude that nuclear pacing had been a mistake. Quite the opposite. They argued that isotopic sources offered superior reliability and might remain valuable as complex circuitry increased energy demand. Another ten-year study of seventy-nine isotopic pacemakers in seventy-five patients likewise reported superior longevity and reliability compared with chemical-battery controls. The field had not yet accepted the eventual verdict as inevitable.

This is important because histories of obsolete technology tend to backfill destiny. Lithium won, so lithium must always have been the obvious path. Radioisotope pacing disappeared, so it must have been foolish. The contemporary data do not support either simplification.

By 1979 Parsonnet's appraisal of 131 radioisotope pulse generators in 120 patients reported 96 percent actuarial generator survival at five years, only two component failures and no battery failures in that observation. The authors also argued that total pacing cost was lower with nuclear units than with lithium or mercury-zinc alternatives under the assumptions then available. Those claims deserve reconstruction with original cost inputs and survivor definitions, but they show that the technology was competing on measurable performance rather than symbolism.

The concept of lifetime also imposed a severe engineering standard. If you intend a device to remain implanted for decades, ordinary product testing becomes inadequate. Seals must survive. Materials must resist corrosion. Thermoelectric elements must remain stable. Radiation damage to nearby electronics must be controlled. Output must retain sufficient margin as the isotope decays. The source must remain intact if the patient is involved in an accident or the body is cremated.

A nuclear source creates a paradox: the safer and longer-lived the device is in normal operation, the more seriously designers must treat rare events outside normal operation because there are more years for those events to occur.

The same logic applies to institutional controls. A five-year implant can plausibly be tracked by the team that inserted it. A thirty-year implant may outlast careers. A device designed to be returned at death depends on a record remaining meaningful across hospitals, states, family decisions and changing manufacturers. The physical system is tested once; the administrative system is tested every day.

NRC guidance eventually made these obligations explicit. Model license conditions required continued patient follow-up, procedures for explantation and return for authorized disposal, reports after losing contact with a patient, prompt reporting of death and reporting of adverse reactions or malfunctions. The source was legally present whether it sat on a shelf, remained implanted or was otherwise in use.

That language is a kind of negative image of the lifetime promise. The longer the pacemaker lasts, the longer somebody must maintain the list.

A lifetime device therefore transfers maintenance rather than eliminating it. The patient may avoid battery-replacement surgery, but the system acquires monitoring, licensing, recovery and disposal work. The maintenance moves from the chest to the institution.

This can still be a good trade. Surgery is expensive and risky. Record keeping is usually cheap and safe. But only if the record keeping works. The later NRC enforcement cases show that it did not always work. Devices were buried, lost or mishandled after explantation. These failures did not erase decades of clinical benefit. They exposed the accounting that had been left outside the original comparison.

The best technology is rarely the one with the longest-lived component. It is the one with the lowest total burden for the function required.

For a pacemaker, that burden includes implantation, follow-up, battery depletion, lead performance, infection, reoperation, radiation exposure, device size, programming, replacement flexibility, regulatory compliance, end-of-life recovery and disposal. Once lithium improved enough, the nuclear source had to earn its place against all of them.

It also had to compete with uncertainty. A thirty-year promise is hard to validate before thirty years have passed. Accelerated testing can model materials and components. Nuclear decay is predictable. But clinical systems evolve. A design chosen in 1973 cannot know the pacing features physicians will consider standard in 1988. The longer the intended service life, the larger the technology-forecasting problem.

This is why the 34-year case later reported in the New England Journal of Medicine is so revealing. The device's persistence was extraordinary, but it did not prove that every patient would have benefited from keeping the same generator for thirty-four years. That particular patient and lead history allowed the source's longevity to matter. Other patients needed replacement for reasons the isotope could not solve.

The atomic pacemaker achieved something close to its narrow dream. It made battery exhaustion cease to be the limiting event for many devices.

Then the rest of medicine claimed the right to be limiting instead.
