# 1964

Some years become hinges because two evidence streams arrive close enough together to destroy an old excuse.

For naval asbestos, 1964 is one of them.

In New York, Irving Selikoff, Jacob Churg, and E. Cuyler Hammond published data on cancer and asbestos exposure among insulation workers. On the other side of the country, William Marr published an industrial-hygiene study of asbestos exposure during naval-vessel overhaul at Long Beach Naval Shipyard.

One paper looked at what happened to workers over time.

The other looked at what was still happening to workers on the job.

Together they make the distance from 1946 difficult to ignore.

Selikoff and his colleagues studied 632 members of an insulation workers' union whose exposure histories reached back decades. The mortality pattern was not subtle. In the published analysis, forty-five deaths from lung and pleural cancer were observed where roughly 6.6 would have been expected. Among 255 deaths in the cohort, four were pleural mesotheliomas. Other cancers and asbestosis also contributed to the picture.

The precise expected counts and disease categories belong to the source table, not to rhetorical memory. The broader result is secure: a mature cohort of insulation workers showed a striking burden of malignancy that the young wartime shipyard survey had not been capable of seeing.

The key word is mature.

These workers had accumulated the very thing the 1946 denominator lacked: time.

The difference between the studies was not simply that one team was better than another. They asked different questions at different moments in the life of an exposed population. Fleischer and colleagues looked cross-sectionally at many men during or just after wartime expansion and counted current asbestosis. Selikoff and colleagues examined mortality among workers whose occupational histories had aged into the period when cancer could appear.

The later study did not make the earlier observations false.

It made the earlier conclusion obsolete.

This is an important scientific distinction. Three asbestosis cases among 1,074 examined workers can remain historically true even after a later cohort demonstrates serious long-term cancer risk. The error was not the count. It was the inference attached to the count.

1964 exposed that error in a way that a theoretical argument about latency never could.

The evidence had bodies now.

Selikoff was also unusually effective at carrying the findings beyond a narrow journal audience. The 1964 conference on the biological effects of asbestos and subsequent publications helped move asbestos cancer risk into public and regulatory discussion. Insulation workers were not an obscure experimental group. They were a recognizable trade operating in buildings, ships, power plants, refineries, and industrial systems across the country.

The findings therefore traveled directly toward institutions using insulation.

This is where the Navy's state-of-knowledge problem becomes substantially different from its wartime problem.

In 1941, the Navy could be charged with knowing that asbestos dust caused serious pulmonary disease and that some workers were inadequately protected.

In 1946, it could be charged with overinterpreting a short-latency survey.

By 1964, a sophisticated occupational-health system had to confront mature epidemiology linking the insulation trade to cancer while its own shipyard study documented continuing exposure during vessel overhaul.

Marr's Long Beach paper is the institutional mirror.

He did not need to visit a foreign mine or a private asbestos factory. He was studying naval work.

The problem had changed from new construction to maintenance and overhaul. Ships built during the wartime and early postwar eras were now returning to yards where insulation had to be removed to reach piping and machinery. The material that had been installed as a fire and thermal-control solution had become a demolition exposure.

This is the second life of asbestos.

Installation creates future removal.

Marr described exposure during fabrication and installation of new insulation, but removal was central. To overhaul a valve, pipe, boiler component, or other insulated system, workers often had to disturb old lagging first. Material could be dry, aged, damaged, patched, or coated. Breaking it opened fibers into confined spaces. Nearby trades could share the air.

The original engineering decision therefore generated an occupational-health obligation decades later.

Marr's paper reported that several insulation workers at Long Beach had gone onto disability compensation for asbestosis and that one had died from the disease. This was not a speculative future risk. The yard had cases severe enough to produce disability.

He also criticized reliance on periodic chest X-rays as a control measure because the disease could take years to appear.

That statement is devastating when placed beside 1946.

The old survey had used radiography as major evidence of low disease prevalence. Eighteen years later, a Navy shipyard study was explicitly saying that radiographic surveillance could not substitute for control because the method observed disease too late.

The institution had, in effect, learned the lesson latency was teaching.

The harder question is what operational changes followed.

Marr evaluated work practices and exposure, reinforcing the need for engineering controls, respiratory protection, housekeeping, and attention to removal. The Navy's industrial-hygiene apparatus was not absent. Measurements were being made. Cases were being recognized. The scientific literature was available.

Yet asbestos would continue to be encountered in naval work for years because the installed base was enormous and material substitution was not instantaneous.

This is the point where historical judgment needs more precision than outrage.

A fleet cannot be stripped of asbestos in one year without opening every insulated system and potentially creating an even larger removal exposure. Removal itself can be hazardous. Leaving intact material in place can sometimes produce less exposure than disturbing it. Modern asbestos management often relies on that principle: identify, contain, maintain, and remove under controlled conditions when necessary.

The existence of legacy material therefore does not prove policy failure.

The quality of its management does.

By 1964, competent management should have reflected the cancer evidence and the limitations of old thresholds. Removal should have been treated as high-risk work. Bystanders should have been considered. Respiratory protection and local containment should have been more than paper rules. Workers should have received hazard communication appropriate to the state of knowledge. Substitutes should have been pursued for new installations where technically feasible.

Whether each Navy yard met that standard is an empirical question.

Long Beach provides a place to test it.

The shipyard later became one of the most important sites in the Navy's asbestos medical record. A 1979 study involving 6,640 production workers reported asbestos-related radiographic abnormalities in 1,061, or about sixteen percent. The population, exposure definitions, radiographic criteria, and selection process deserve a dedicated look later. For now the number is useful as a long shadow. The yard where Marr was measuring exposure in 1964 would produce a much larger disease-surveillance picture after more time had passed.

Again, the disease clock.

The 1964 Selikoff study also altered the politics of expertise.

Earlier asbestos knowledge had circulated through industrial hygiene, factory medicine, insurers, and specialized occupational literature. Selikoff's work helped bring the problem into organized labor and public debate. The insulation workers' union was not merely a passive population being studied. Union involvement helped identify the cohort and amplify the findings.

Workers were becoming producers of evidence about their own exposure history.

This changed the balance of information.

An employer can control workplace measurements. A government can classify reports. A manufacturer can hold product information. A union can connect people across employers and follow a trade rather than one factory. That organizational form is especially powerful for long-latency disease because workers move between jobs while remaining in the same craft.

The cohort follows the worker where the employer's records do not.

This is one reason trade-union epidemiology became important in asbestos research.

It also created new incentives and new criticisms. Industry defendants could argue that union-linked investigators were advocacy driven. Plaintiffs could argue that manufacturers and employers had spent years minimizing danger. Scientists could be attacked for appearing in litigation. Data became part of an adversarial field.

None of that makes the mortality counts disappear.

Evidence should be evaluated for methods, not purified by imagined neutrality.

Selikoff himself became a polarizing figure in later asbestos litigation. Critics questioned aspects of his exposure characterization, public advocacy, and the way his work was used in court. Admirers credited him with forcing an occupational catastrophe into view. A serious history should permit both propositions: his role became politically and legally consequential, and the core epidemiological evidence of substantial asbestos-related disease among insulation workers was not an invention created by advocacy.

The 1964 convergence also destroys another simplistic defense: that Navy exposures were fundamentally unlike the insulation-worker evidence.

Some differences were real. A full-time commercial insulator could experience heavier cumulative exposure than a sailor whose contact occurred during occasional maintenance. Different products and fiber mixtures mattered. Work intensity mattered. Job duration mattered.

But Navy shipyard pipe coverers were insulation workers.

They cut, installed, and removed asbestos-containing thermal insulation. Marr's paper confirms that the occupational process remained active inside naval overhaul. The scientific distance between Selikoff's cohort and at least some naval trades was therefore not large enough to dismiss the warning categorically.

For bystander sailors and other trades, extrapolation required more care.

That is where exposure reconstruction becomes important. A worker with intermittent contact should not automatically be assigned the same cumulative dose as a career insulator. Yet a lower dose does not equal zero dose, and the mesothelioma evidence had already made nontraditional exposure pathways medically relevant.

1964 therefore shifted the burden.

An institution continuing asbestos work could no longer rely on the absence of current asbestosis as its primary safety argument.

It needed exposure data.

It needed cancer-aware controls.

It needed long-term thinking.

It needed to explain why an old study designed around a young wartime workforce still deserved policy weight.

The year also illustrates why hidden history is often hiding in plain sight.

Neither paper was secret.

Selikoff's findings were published and discussed publicly. Marr's paper appeared in the industrial-hygiene literature. The evidence did not require a declassification request or a whistleblower. The remarkable fact is not that the government possessed an invisible file. It is that public and internal evidence streams could coexist with a material system slow to change.

Secrecy is only one way knowledge fails to become action.

Inertia is another.

Compartmentalization is another.

Uncertainty is another.

Engineering dependence is another.

Liability is another.

The asbestos story contains all of them, which is why no single accusation explains it.

By the end of 1964, the historical record had moved far beyond the state of knowledge available to the authors of the 1946 survey.

The paper itself could remain in libraries unchanged.

The sentence "not a dangerous occupation" could still be quoted.

But science had changed what those words were allowed to mean.

The next question was whether standards would change with it.
