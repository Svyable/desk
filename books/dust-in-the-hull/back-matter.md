# Back Matter

This draft is built around a documentary asymmetry that should remain visible through revision: the archive is much better at preserving what institutions wrote than what individual workers understood, and much better at preserving the existence of safety requirements than the quality of their implementation.

The book therefore does not use “the Navy knew” as a self-explanatory proposition. Knowledge is broken into dated claims: recognition of asbestosis; recognition of inadequate protection; measurement of hazardous dust; medical evidence in exposed workers; lung-cancer evidence; mesothelioma evidence; knowledge of removal/bystander exposure; and knowledge that earlier exposure limits were not protective against the full disease burden. Where a claim rests on a secondary reconstruction of an archival document, the research notes say so.

The most important unresolved document problem is the denominator in the 1945–46 pipe-covering survey. The published result—three cases of asbestosis among 1,074 workers—has been quoted for eighty years. The thesis of this book depends not on denying that count but on reconstructing what the 1,071 apparently unaffected workers could legitimately say about long-term risk. A publication-grade revision should extract the entire tenure distribution and, if the underlying report survives, rebuild the analysis by duration of exposure. If that reconstruction weakens the latency argument, the book should change.

The research folder contains the working chronology, evidence matrix, source log, contradiction/incentive audit, document appendix, research program, and explicit defeat conditions. They are part of the manuscript's epistemic apparatus, not decorative extras.

## Notes on quotation and attribution

Short phrases from wartime records appear in the manuscript because the wording itself matters: “not protecting the men as we should,” “very real asbestos hazard,” and “not a dangerous occupation.” Before publication, each should be checked against the best available image or transcript of the original document, with page/box/folder metadata added. Court-reproduced quotations are usable evidence but not the final archival endpoint.

No unpublished or classified access is claimed. Every source used in this run is lawfully public or described through lawfully public court, government, archival, or scholarly sources. References to classified wartime documents describe their historical status; they do not imply possession of non-public classified material.

## Evidence posture

This is an archival-heavy and peer-reviewed-heavy book with a meaningful litigation-source layer. Its strongest foundation is the collision between contemporaneous Navy/Maritime hazard records and the published 1946 reassurance. Its weakest current area is worker-level warning/implementation evidence and the exact duration distribution inside the 1,074-worker cohort. Claims about concealment, universal worker ignorance, and direct policy causation remain deliberately narrower than common asbestos retellings.

## Acknowledgment of uncertainty

The history of asbestos has been litigated for decades. Many historical papers and expert reports were produced by people working for plaintiffs, defendants, manufacturers, insurers, government entities, unions, or consulting firms. Conflict of interest is relevant metadata, not an automatic truth machine. The book's rule is to follow each important assertion back toward the primary document or original epidemiology and to state when that route is incomplete.

A more dramatic book would be easier to write.

The documents are better than the drama.
