# Chronology

## Before the naval wartime surge

**Late nineteenth / early twentieth century** — Asbestos use expands with steam power, electrical insulation, construction, and fireproofing. Medical observations begin associating heavy occupational exposure with pulmonary fibrosis.

**1930** — E. R. A. Merewether and C. W. Price publish the British government report on effects of asbestos dust on lungs and dust suppression. The central documented point for this book: disabling fibrosis from heavy asbestos exposure and engineering dust control were established subjects before World War II.

**1930s** — U.S. industrial hygiene develops as a more organized field. Navy yards operate occupational-health programs amid many hazards including silica, metals, solvents, welding fumes, heat, and asbestos.

## Wartime knowledge and control

**1939** — Navy Surgeon General material later reproduced in government/litigation compilations discusses medical survey experience with asbestos workers and cautions that negative current X-rays do not preclude future asbestosis under continued exposure. Original source context should be recovered before final publication.

**March 11, 1941** — Commander C. S. Stephenson, head of the Navy's Division of Preventive Medicine, writes to Surgeon General Ross T. McIntire that considerable asbestos work is being done and that, from his observations, men are not being protected as they should; he says the issue is reflected in official reports from several Navy yards.

**March 4, 1941** — A separate Stephenson memorandum, described in *GAF Corp. v. United States*, refers to possible labor unrest if inspectors observe Navy-yard conditions. Motive/context remain unresolved until full document recovery.

**1941–1942** — Navy sponsors industrial-hygiene training for government personnel at Harvard and Columbia, according to later congressional record summaries.

**1942** — Philip Drinker, Chief Health Consultant to the U.S. Maritime Commission and Harvard industrial-hygiene expert, participates in an industrial-health survey at Bath Iron Works. The report describes asbestos pipe-covering fabrication and calls conditions a very real asbestos hazard; segregation and ventilation are recommended.

**1943** — Navy and U.S. Maritime Commission issue *Minimum Requirements for Safety and Industrial Health in Contract Shipyards* after shipyard survey work. Later official summaries describe asbestos provisions requiring safety/ventilation supervision, engineering control, respiratory protection, and medical attention. Full text still needs extraction for exact wording.

**1944** — A classified Bath Iron Works report, later described in *GAF*, records significant radiographic changes among some of 38 examined workers and diagnoses several with asbestosis. Exact diagnostic categories and full report context require primary recovery.

**1945** — Drinker writes to Captain Thomas J. Carter of the Bureau of Medicine and Surgery describing Bath evidence as indicating a fairly serious dust risk and making similar findings probable at plants/yards using the same pipe-covering materials, according to the later peer-reviewed document review.

**1945** — Broad multi-yard pipe-covering survey is completed. Drinker transmits the report to Admiral Edward W. Mills of the Bureau of Ships, describes naval pipe covering as most unlikely to cause ill health under the study's interpretation, requests publication permission, and recommends that conclusions reach insurance divisions concerned with possible worker damage suits. Original correspondence remains a priority archival target.

## Reassurance enters print

**January 1946** — Fleischer, Viles, Gade, and Drinker publish “A health survey of pipe covering operations in constructing naval vessels.” The study reports three asbestosis cases among 1,074 examined pipe coverers; the diagnosed workers have long work histories. The paper recommends control of selected dusty operations yet concludes naval pipe covering is not a dangerous occupation.

**1946 onward** — Asbestos remains embedded in naval ships, procurement, maintenance, and overhaul. The exact citation/policy chain from the 1946 paper into postwar Navy decisions remains to be reconstructed rather than presumed.

## Cancer changes the disease model

**1955** — Richard Doll publishes “Mortality from Lung Cancer in Asbestos Workers,” providing major mortality evidence linking asbestos work to excess lung cancer.

**1960** — J. C. Wagner, C. A. Sleggs, and Paul Marchand publish 33 diffuse pleural mesothelioma cases from South Africa; all but one have probable crocidolite exposure. Occupational, household, and environmental pathways broaden the exposure picture.

**1964** — Irving Selikoff, Jacob Churg, and E. Cuyler Hammond publish “Asbestos Exposure and Neoplasia,” reporting striking cancer mortality in a mature cohort of 632 U.S. insulation workers.

**1964** — William T. Marr publishes “Asbestos Exposure During Naval Vessel Overhaul,” documenting continuing Navy-yard exposure during fabrication, installation, and removal and arguing that chest X-rays are inadequate as an exposure-control substitute because disease takes years to develop.

## Federal regulation and mature shipyard disease

**May 1971** — OSHA's initial standards include an asbestos permissible exposure limit of 12 fibers per cubic centimeter.

**December 7, 1971** — OSHA issues an emergency temporary asbestos standard establishing a 5 f/cc eight-hour time-weighted average and 10 f/cc peak level.

**1972** — OSHA promulgates a final asbestos standard at 5 f/cc TWA with a 10 f/cc ceiling framework. OSHA's later regulatory history states these limits were aimed primarily at asbestosis, with hoped-for incidental protection against asbestos-induced cancer.

**1972** — NIOSH publishes its criteria document for occupational exposure to asbestos, addressing biological effects, exposure monitoring, engineering/work-practice controls, warning, protective equipment, and medical surveillance.

**1976** — OSHA reduces the eight-hour asbestos TWA to 2 f/cc.

**1979** — J. S. Felton publishes a Long Beach Naval Shipyard chest-X-ray survey of 6,640 workers; 1,061 (16.0%) are classified with asbestos-related radiographic abnormalities. The population and endpoint are not directly comparable with the 1946 survey but demonstrate what a mature workforce could reveal.

## Courtroom and veteran reconstruction

**1991** — Federal Circuit decides *GAF Corporation v. United States*. The opinion reproduces key wartime Navy asbestos documents in the course of rejecting GAF's contractual-duty theory against the federal government. The decision becomes an important public route to Stephenson/Bath material but should not be mistaken for a historical verdict on Navy conduct.

**2011** — Kara Franke and Dennis Paustenbach publish a peer-reviewed state-of-the-science review of government/Navy asbestos knowledge from 1900–1970. The article reconstructs many difficult wartime documents and argues the Navy was highly knowledgeable while attempting protective measures. Its document trail is valuable; its litigation-relevant interpretive framing requires independent audit.

**2019** — U.S. Supreme Court decides *Air & Liquid Systems Corp. v. DeVries*, addressing maritime duty to warn where Navy equipment required dangerous asbestos-containing parts added or replaced in an integrated system. Useful for later legal architecture, not as a retroactive wartime duty rule.

**2022 onward** — National Archives publishes/maintains a detailed guide for researching asbestos aboard U.S. Navy vessels through 1961, emphasizing the complexity of Record Group 19, Bureau of Ships records, specifications, general-information books, and ship/class files.

**2026** — A new historical/methodological critique, “When errors become etched in stone,” argues that the 1946 Navy survey was deeply flawed and that its reassurance had long downstream consequences. This book accepts the need for methodological re-audit while treating the critique's strongest causal claims as hypotheses requiring a policy/citation chain.

**2026 current federal posture** — VA public-health materials recognize Navy/shipyard asbestos exposure contexts and long disease latency. OSHA/NIOSH history treats asbestos as a major occupational carcinogen requiring stringent exposure control. These current materials describe present knowledge, not historical wartime knowledge.
