# Research appendix

## Investigation program for the next edition

This appendix converts the book's unresolved questions into a document-recovery and quantitative-analysis plan. It is intentionally more specific than a generic “more research needed” list.

### A. Reconstruct the Fleischer cohort

Build a row-level or grouped table from the 1945 report / 1946 paper with:

- yard and job category;
- age;
- total years in pipe covering/asbestos work;
- years in current yard;
- prior dusty trades;
- smoking information if recorded;
- symptoms;
- physical findings;
- chest-film classification;
- final case classification;
- employment status and any exclusion reason.

Minimum output: tenure histogram for all 1,074 workers and disease prevalence stratified by exposure duration. The central latency claim should be revised to match this table rather than rely on general wartime-workforce inference.

### B. Rebuild the dust-measurement table

For every sample reported in the 1942 Bath survey, 1944 Bath investigation, 1945 survey, and 1946 paper:

- operation/task;
- material/product/fiber if stated;
- location/yard;
- personal versus area sample;
- instrument/method;
- unit;
- sampling duration;
- control status (local exhaust, general ventilation, respirator, wet/dry);
- observed concentration;
- contemporary maximum allowable concentration;
- investigator interpretation.

Do not convert mppcf to f/cc unless a source provides a process-specific validated relationship or the conversion is presented as a range with explicit assumptions.

### C. Trace the paper's policy life

Search Navy/Bureau of Ships/Bureau of Medicine and Surgery records for 1946–1975 citations to Fleischer et al., including:

- safety manuals;
- insulation specifications;
- occupational-health circulars;
- medical surveillance protocols;
- shipyard industrial-hygiene reports;
- procurement/substitution studies;
- compensation/claims memoranda;
- contractor correspondence;
- training material.

Classify every citation as: reassurance, methods reference, hazard/control reference, criticism, or neutral bibliography. This is the evidence needed to test whether the paper actually became a durable institutional prior.

### D. Worker warning audit

Select at least four yards with different organizational status and geography, ideally including Bath and Long Beach. For each, collect:

- induction/training manuals;
- shop safety rules;
- warning signs/labels;
- respirator instructions;
- toolbox or safety-meeting records;
- union newsletters and bargaining files;
- compensation notices;
- occupational-health exam forms;
- worker testimony/oral histories.

Code warning content by level:

0. no asbestos-specific warning located;
1. nuisance/dust warning;
2. lung-disease/asbestosis warning;
3. cancer warning;
4. latency and bystander/removal warning.

Do not infer worker understanding solely from rule existence.

### E. New construction versus overhaul exposure

Build two process maps:

**New construction:** receiving → fabrication → sawing/cutting → mixing → fitting → installation → cleanup.

**Overhaul:** identification → access → lagging removal → component repair → surface cleanup → replacement insulation → waste handling.

For each node, identify likely source workers, bystander trades, controls, peak-exposure potential, and available measurements.

### F. State-of-knowledge timeline by proposition

Use separate timelines for:

- asbestosis causation;
- exposure-duration relationship;
- lung-cancer association;
- mesothelioma association;
- fiber-type differences;
- smoking interaction;
- bystander/household exposure;
- removal/maintenance exposure;
- adequacy of chest radiography as surveillance;
- exposure-limit evolution.

This avoids the false single date when “asbestos became known dangerous.”

### G. Navy policy-update audit, 1955–1975

For Doll 1955, Wagner et al. 1960, Selikoff et al. 1964, Marr 1964, and NIOSH/OSHA 1971–72, find evidence of:

- Navy receipt/citation;
- internal assessment;
- change in exposure limits;
- changes to insulation specs/substitution;
- cancer language in worker guidance;
- changes to removal procedures;
- medical-surveillance updates;
- long-term recordkeeping.

Measure lag from external evidence to internal action instead of describing “slow response” impressionistically.

### H. Long Beach longitudinal case study

Long Beach is unusually valuable because the record spans Marr 1964 to Felton 1979. Recover:

- personal/area sampling reports;
- ventilation/control interventions;
- disability/asbestosis cases known in 1964;
- worker demographics and tenure;
- radiographic protocol/readers for the 1979 survey;
- job-specific abnormality rates;
- follow-up/compensation response.

Do not compare 16.0% radiographic abnormalities directly with 3/1,074 clinical asbestosis without harmonizing endpoint and population.

### I. Classification/dissemination audit

For every restricted/classified asbestos-related report:

- classification authority/reason if available;
- original distribution list;
- declassification date;
- whether health findings were separately released in unclassified form;
- whether worker/medical guidance changed after the report.

Goal: distinguish secrecy effects from concealment intent.

### J. Litigation provenance audit

For every wartime document currently known primarily through litigation:

1. identify docket/exhibit number;
2. find original archival file if possible;
3. compare quoted excerpt with surrounding pages;
4. identify which party introduced it and for what legal proposition;
5. track later secondary reuse.

This is especially important for Stephenson, Bath, and Drinker correspondence.

### K. Product/fiber map

Build a shipboard applications table by era:

- molded pipe covering;
- block insulation;
- asbestos cement/mud;
- cloth/tape;
- gaskets;
- packing;
- boiler/turbine insulation;
- brakes/friction materials;
- boards/panels and other fire-resistant components.

Record likely fiber types only when supported by specifications or product records. Avoid the phrase “the ship contained X tons” unless tied to a specific class/ship source.

### L. Veteran exposure reconstruction protocol

For a specific veteran claim/history, use the following sequence:

1. verify dates of service and vessel/yard;
2. identify rating/trade and known tasks;
3. establish ship class, construction/overhaul history;
4. locate relevant Bureau of Ships plans/specifications/general-information books;
5. identify likely asbestos-bearing systems;
6. establish whether maintenance/removal occurred during service window;
7. search industrial-hygiene records from that yard/ship class;
8. distinguish direct exposure evidence from occupational inference;
9. separately reconstruct post-service asbestos exposure;
10. match disease-specific latency and medical evidence without converting exposure likelihood into product-specific causation.

### M. Counterevidence search

Actively search for evidence that would make the Navy look better than the current thesis:

- early well-enforced dust-control programs;
- worker cancer/asbestosis warnings earlier than commonly reported;
- Navy skepticism about the Fleischer conclusion;
- successful early substitution programs;
- comprehensive exposure registries;
- internal long-term follow-up of the 1945 cohort.

A documentary history that searches only for failures will find them.

### N. Primary-source interview program

If living sources are available, prioritize interviews with:

- retired Navy industrial hygienists/occupational physicians;
- former shipyard insulators and adjacent trades;
- union safety representatives;
- National Archives specialists in RG 19;
- epidemiologists familiar with historical Navy cohorts;
- experts who have testified for both plaintiff and defense sides.

Interview rules: distinguish memory from contemporaneous document; ask for records; do not convert testimony into proof; preserve disagreements in notes.
