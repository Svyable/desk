# Chapter 7 audit — the denominator was young

Checked September 12, 2026.

The central quantitative weakness in the 1946 Fleischer–Viles–Gade–Drinker survey can now be stated more precisely than the manuscript's current generic warning about wartime entrants.

## What the study actually established

The published survey examined 1,074 pipe coverers in Navy and contract shipyards and found three cases of asbestosis. All three diagnosed men had worked at asbestos pipe covering for more than twenty years. The paper nevertheless concluded that shipboard pipe covering was not a dangerous occupation, while separately recommending exhaust ventilation for high-dust operations.

Primary publication record: W. E. Fleischer, F. J. Viles Jr., R. L. Gade, and P. Drinker, “A Health Survey of Pipe Covering Operations in Constructing Naval Vessels,” *Journal of Industrial Hygiene and Toxicology* 28 (1946), 9–16. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21016030/

A scan/transcription of the historical report and its associated Navy/Maritime Commission correspondence is available through Toxic Docs: https://www.toxicdocs.org/d/xzaq0eDBojDxZYLxDGMQwJjzQ

## The missing denominator

The most useful later judicial reconstruction is *Borel v. Fibreboard Paper Products Corp.*, 493 F.2d 1076 (5th Cir. 1973). The court's discussion of the study states that **95 percent of the workers examined had worked in the trade for less than ten years**. It then contrasts that duration with the roughly ten-to-twenty-year interval then commonly associated with diagnosable asbestosis.

Accessible opinion mirror: https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914c6daadd7b049347de266

That fact changes how `3 / 1,074` should be read. The raw prevalence was about 0.28%, but the overwhelming majority of the denominator had not yet accumulated the duration of employment at which the study's own positive cases appeared. A thousand observations do not buy a thousand mature disease opportunities.

The number should therefore never appear in the manuscript without its time structure:

- 1,074 workers examined;
- 3 diagnosed asbestosis cases;
- every diagnosed case had >20 years in pipe covering;
- about 95% of the examined workers had <10 years in the trade, according to the later judicial reconstruction.

The correct historical criticism is not that a cross-sectional survey was inherently illegitimate in 1945. It is that the paper's categorical trade-level reassurance exceeded what a mostly short-tenure cross-section could establish about a disease already understood to follow prolonged dust exposure.

## The paper itself contained the warning

Later court records preserve another important boundary from the original article: the authors acknowledged that their measurements did not provide a composite picture of the asbestos dust a worker might breathe over years, and they said that if pipe coverers worked steadily in operations with consistently high asbestos-dust concentrations, the incidence of asbestosis would have been considerably greater.

See *Borel* above and *Moran v. Johns-Manville Sales Corp.*, 691 F.2d 811 (6th Cir. 1982), which likewise notes the small long-term-exposure fraction and the paper's own high-dust caveat: https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/691/691.F2d.811.81-3373.html

This is stronger than a retrospective accusation that the investigators simply did not know latency existed. Their own positive cases and their own caveat already put duration and cumulative exposure inside the study.

## What not to claim

The 95% figure is preserved in later judicial records and expert testimony about the 1946 study. The final manuscript should still recover the original paper's worker-duration table directly before treating the appellate summary as the terminal source.

Do not use the 95% figure to claim that the study was fraudulent, that workers were deliberately selected for short tenure, or that the paper alone caused the Navy's later asbestos burden. The result can be methodologically overbroad without fabrication or a single-cause policy story.

Likewise, do not project mesothelioma knowledge backward into 1945. The clean contemporaneous criticism is narrower: a trade-level declaration of safety from a cross-section in which roughly nineteen of every twenty workers had less than ten years in the trade was not a lifetime-risk estimate for asbestosis, and the paper itself already knew prolonged exposure mattered.

## Later natural experiment

The downstream evidence eventually supplied the follow-up the 1946 design lacked. By the 1960s, insulation-worker studies with mature exposure histories reported much higher disease burdens; Selikoff, Churg, and Hammond's work is the major American benchmark. Navy-overhaul work also shifted attention toward removal/rip-out exposures that new-construction surveys had not represented well.

Selikoff, Churg, and Hammond, “Asbestos Exposure and Neoplasia” (1964): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14107207/

William T. Marr, “Asbestos Exposure During Naval Vessel Overhaul” (1964): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14168424/

The strongest eventual chapter structure is therefore not `old study wrong / later study right`. It is a time-series of what each design could see. The 1946 survey measured present radiographic disease in a predominantly young exposure cohort. The later cohorts finally contained the latency the earlier denominator did not.
