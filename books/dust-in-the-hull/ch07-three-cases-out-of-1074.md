# Three Cases Out of 1,074

The number is almost too neat.

Three cases of asbestosis among 1,074 pipe coverers.

If all you know about a study is that result, the conclusion seems to write itself. Whatever asbestos had done in textile mills or mining towns, naval pipe covering apparently produced very little disease. A thousand men had been looked at. Almost all appeared free of asbestosis. The three who were sick had spent more than twenty years in the trade. The occupation, therefore, did not look especially dangerous.

That is roughly how the 1946 paper entered history.

The article, published in the *Journal of Industrial Hygiene and Toxicology*, was titled "A Health Survey of Pipe Covering Operations in Constructing Naval Vessels." Its authors were William E. Fleischer, Frederick J. Viles, Paul E. Gade, and Philip Drinker. It was not written as a scandal. It was written as industrial medicine: examine workers, measure dust, compare observed disease with known experience, identify operations requiring control, and issue a judgment useful to shipbuilding.

The famous conclusion was blunt. Pipe covering in naval construction was not considered a dangerous occupation.

The paper did not say asbestos dust was harmless. It did not say controls were unnecessary. It did not say no worker would ever become ill. It recommended local exhaust ventilation for operations generating excessive dust. Its reassuring sentence therefore existed beside evidence that some work practices exceeded the accepted concentration and required intervention.

This is one reason the paper is worth reading as a whole rather than as an incriminating quote.

The authors' model was not "asbestos safe." It was closer to "this form of asbestos work, with attention to dust control, does not appear to produce the high asbestosis burden seen elsewhere."

That claim is more reasonable.

It is also still vulnerable.

The central problem is not that only three cases were found. The central problem is what the study treated the other 1,071 men as evidence of.

A normal chest film is a present observation. It is not a negative twenty-year follow-up compressed into one afternoon.

Later judicial reconstructions of the Fleischer study make the denominator problem unusually concrete: about **95 percent of the 1,074 workers had been in the trade for less than ten years**. All three diagnosed cases, by contrast, had more than twenty years in pipe covering.

Nineteen out of every twenty men in the headline denominator therefore sat on the short-tenure side of a disease already understood to favor prolonged exposure.

That does not make the 1,071 negative examinations fake. It changes what they can mean.

When a disease emerges slowly, the meaning of a negative examination depends on time since first exposure and cumulative dose. If an employee has worked around asbestos for two years, the absence of radiographic fibrosis may be exactly what one would expect even under a harmful exposure regime. Counting that worker as evidence against future asbestosis inflates the reassuring denominator.

The paper's own positive cases should have warned against this compression because all three occurred among men with long trade histories.

The modern response is obvious: stratify by duration. Better yet, follow the cohort prospectively and compare mortality and disease incidence over time. But it would be unfair to judge a 1946 paper simply for not looking like a modern occupational cohort study. The more relevant question is whether the limitation was knowable within the science of the period.

It was.

Asbestosis was already understood as a disease associated with substantial exposure and often prolonged employment. British and American literature had described workers becoming symptomatic or radiographically abnormal after years in dusty industries. The investigators themselves had occupational histories. The fact that the three identified cases had more than twenty years in pipe covering was presented in the paper. Time was not hidden from them.

Nor was cumulative exposure entirely absent from their own discussion. Later courts quoting the paper preserved an important caveat: the dust samples did not provide a composite picture of what a pipe coverer might breathe over years, and if men worked steadily in operations with consistently high asbestos-dust concentrations, the incidence of asbestosis would have been considerably greater.

That caveat matters because it prevents an easy retrospective caricature.

The investigators did not literally believe that a few spot measurements plus three cases could describe every worker's lifetime dose. The paper contained the seeds of the narrower conclusion it should have made.

What it did not do was let those seeds govern the headline.

There is a subtle psychological reason this happens in large surveys. Large numbers feel like strong evidence. A thousand is impressive. It can make a reader forget that a cohort can be large in people and small in person-years at meaningful latency. Statistical information has dimensions. Sample size is only one.

The wartime workforce made the distortion worse. Naval construction had expanded rapidly. Many pipe coverers had entered the trade during the buildup or war. A study performed in 1945 was necessarily sampling a great deal of recent exposure. The war had created a population ideal for asking what immediate respiratory effects were visible. It was much less useful for asking what the work would do over a career.

A recent 2026 historical critique has argued that this flaw was severe enough to make the study's broad reassurance scientifically untenable. That paper arrives with its own retrospective perspective and should not be treated as the final word simply because it is recent. But the basic criticism does not depend on modern moral standards. It depends on the logic of latency and on a denominator we can now state numerically.

The other weakness is diagnostic sensitivity.

The survey relied heavily on chest radiography and clinical evaluation for asbestosis. Chest films were appropriate tools for the time, but they are not exposure meters. Early disease can be missed. Reader interpretation varies. A worker may carry a substantial fiber burden without a film meeting diagnostic criteria. A normal X-ray therefore cannot be treated as evidence that exposure is biologically irrelevant.

Two decades later, William Marr would make this limitation explicit in his Navy shipyard work. X-rays, he argued, were not adequate as a control device because asbestos disease often took years to develop. The fact that this statement had to be made again in 1964 suggests how persistent the surveillance trap had become.

Management likes medical screening because it produces a visible result.

A worker is examined. A film is read. A box is checked. If nothing abnormal appears, everyone can continue. Engineering control is messier. It requires redesign, maintenance, measurements, disruption, and sometimes confrontation with production management. Screening can quietly become a substitute for exposure reduction even when the disease being screened for appears too late for that logic to make sense.

The 1946 paper did recommend engineering control. That is to its credit.

The stronger criticism is that its conclusion offered reassurance beyond what those controls and observations justified.

There is another question: what did the study mean by dangerous?

This is not semantics for its own sake. Industrial medicine in 1946 often dealt with dangerous trades in relation to recognized occupational disease, compensation, and preventable exposure above accepted limits. If naval pipe covering produced far fewer cases of disabling asbestosis than raw asbestos processing, the authors may have felt justified in saying the trade was not dangerous provided excessive dust was controlled.

A modern reader hears "not dangerous" as "does not create serious long-term health risk."

Those are not necessarily identical meanings.

The difference does not rescue the paper, but it should discipline criticism. The authors were not certifying zero lifetime risk against every disease later associated with asbestos. They were making a contemporary industrial-hygiene judgment centered on asbestosis. The proper historical question is whether even that narrower judgment was too strong.

The answer appears to be yes, because the study lacked the time depth required to know.

The paper's dust findings sharpen the problem. If all sampled operations had been comfortably below the accepted concentration, one could argue that the authors saw a controlled environment and a low prevalence of disease and reasonably linked the two. Instead, some operations produced substantial dust and required local exhaust. The study therefore documented conditions that its own preventive framework considered excessive.

This does not mean every worker experienced those concentrations continuously. Task-based peaks are not the same as an eight-hour average. A pipe coverer might spend part of a shift cutting insulation and the rest fitting it. Ventilation differed. Ships differed. Shops differed. The correct exposure reconstruction would require duration, frequency, proximity, particle characteristics, and control effectiveness.

The paper did not possess that level of personal dosimetry.

That is normal for the era.

What is less defensible is using a coarse exposure picture to make a fine statement about safety.

The sentence endured partly because institutions prefer conclusions that can be detached from methods. "Three cases among 1,074" is portable. "Ninety-five percent had less than ten years in the trade, all three cases had more than twenty years, exposure measurement was task-based, radiography had limited sensitivity, and some operations exceeded the recommended dust concentration" is not.

Once the short version enters a policy file, it can become more authoritative with each repetition.

A study is cited by a memorandum. The memorandum is summarized in a manual. The manual informs a procurement decision. Years later, somebody asks why a material remained in use and finds the reassuring sentence without reconstructing the original sample.

This is how a result can become stronger as it moves farther from its data.

The phrase "etched in stone" used by the recent historical critique is apt in one limited sense. Institutional knowledge is path dependent. Early studies can set priors that later evidence has to dislodge. If a respected Navy-associated survey says the trade is not dangerous, subsequent illness may initially be treated as exceptional, improperly controlled, or unrelated. The burden shifts toward those arguing that the earlier reassurance was wrong.

But the evidence that the 1946 paper directly governed Navy policy for decades still needs to be reconstructed rather than assumed.

It is easy to move from influence to causation too quickly. The Navy continued using asbestos for engineering reasons that existed independently of the study. Manufacturers promoted and supplied it. Existing ships already contained it. Replacement materials had to meet demanding specifications. Occupational-health standards evolved. Even if the Fleischer paper had never existed, asbestos might still have remained entrenched.

The study's role was more likely one of reinforcement.

It made continued use easier to defend within a system already committed to the material. It reduced urgency. It supplied evidence to officials and insurers who wanted to know whether shipyard pipe covering was producing a compensable occupational-disease problem. It made targeted dust control appear sufficient.

This is a different claim from saying the study caused the Navy's asbestos exposure history.

The distinction is important because monocausal scandal stories are usually wrong about infrastructure. Materials persist because many systems hold them in place: performance, cost, specifications, suppliers, training, installed base, repair practice, institutional memory, and evidence that seems to say the risk can be managed. Remove one support and the structure often remains standing.

The 1946 paper was one support.

Its association with insurance and potential damage suits makes it especially tempting to assign motive. Philip Drinker's September 1945 correspondence reportedly recommended sending the findings to insurance divisions concerned about possible suits by shipyard workers. The wording deserves to remain visible because it establishes that liability consequences were part of the study's administrative environment.

What it does not establish is fabrication.

There is no basis here to say the authors falsified X-rays, invented dust counts, or deliberately selected workers to conceal disease. The stronger critique requires none of those allegations. A study can be sincerely conducted and still produce a conclusion aligned with institutional incentives because the investigators accept a design, threshold, or inference that minimizes uncertainty in a convenient direction.

This is much more common than fraud and much harder to detect.

Consider the sequence of choices:

Study active workers rather than construct a long-term cohort. Use current radiographic asbestosis as the primary health endpoint. Compare prevalence across the entire sample. Interpret only three cases as evidence of low danger. Treat excessive dust as a correctable operational issue. Publish a categorical trade-level conclusion.

No single choice is absurd.

Together they transform a young cohort into reassurance.

A fair evaluation also has to ask what alternative was practically available in 1945. Long-term follow-up would have required tracking workers after demobilization across civilian life. Cancer registries were not what they would later become. Mesothelioma was not yet a recognized signature disease. Occupational epidemiology had fewer tools and less institutional infrastructure. A perfect study was impossible.

The absence of a perfect study, however, does not require a strong conclusion.

Uncertainty can be an output.

The paper could have said the current survey does not show a high prevalence of asbestosis but cannot determine long-term risk because most workers have relatively short exposure histories. It could have recommended continued surveillance of long-tenure employees and follow-up of the wartime cohort. It could have treated the three twenty-year cases not as evidence that only unusual veterans were at risk but as a warning about where the younger men might be headed.

This would not have stopped shipbuilding in January 1946.

It would have changed the institutional memory.

The difference between "not dangerous" and "not yet shown dangerous in this young population" is a few words. Over thirty years, it can become thousands of people.

We should be careful even with that sentence. History cannot tell us how many exposures would have been prevented by a more cautious conclusion. Controls may still have been weak. Engineering requirements already existed. Asbestos use might have continued regardless. A warning in a journal does not automatically become a hood over a saw.

What can be said is narrower: the 1946 paper had enough information to preserve uncertainty and instead published reassurance.

The later disease record would make that choice look worse with every decade.

In 1955 Richard Doll would report excess lung-cancer mortality among asbestos workers. In 1960 Wagner, Sleggs, and Marchand would connect diffuse pleural mesothelioma with asbestos exposure in South Africa. In 1964 Selikoff, Churg, and Hammond would report striking cancer mortality among American insulation workers. Navy shipyard physicians would confront disability from asbestosis during overhaul. By 1979 a study of thousands of Long Beach Naval Shipyard workers would report asbestos-related radiographic abnormalities in a substantial fraction of the examined workforce.

None of those later findings proves the 1946 authors acted dishonestly.

They prove something more useful.

A negative result can expire.

Its expiration date is set by the biology the study has not waited long enough to observe.
