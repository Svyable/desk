# Courtroom Archive

A large part of the Navy asbestos archive became visible because somebody was trying to win a case.

That does not make the documents false.

It makes their survival and selection part of the evidence.

By the late twentieth century, asbestos litigation had become enormous. Workers with mesothelioma, lung cancer, asbestosis, and other diseases sued manufacturers, distributors, contractors, premises owners, and other parties. Defendants sought contribution from the federal government. Lawyers searched old files for warnings, specifications, contracts, medical reports, safety standards, and evidence about what each actor knew.

The result is paradoxical.

An adversarial system became one of the great archival engines of occupational history.

Documents that might otherwise have remained obscure were copied, indexed, quoted, and preserved because they could move liability.

The 1941 Stephenson memorandum is one example.

The Bath reports are another.

Philip Drinker's correspondence about insurance divisions and possible damage suits is another.

A historian should be grateful to litigation and suspicious of it at the same time.

The suspicion begins with selection.

A plaintiff's lawyer looking for early knowledge will find the strongest early warning. A defendant looking for government sophistication will find the strongest Navy safety standard. A manufacturer seeking a government-contractor defense will emphasize specifications and federal control. The United States defending a contribution claim will emphasize the limits of its legal duty. Nobody is paid to assemble a neutral documentary sequence for a future book.

The court receives slices.

The historian has to rebuild the loaf.

*GAF Corporation v. United States*, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit in 1991, is unusually useful because the opinion recites portions of the wartime record while explaining a legal dispute over who owed what duty to whom.

GAF and related asbestos manufacturers argued, in substance, that the Navy and other federal entities had possessed knowledge about asbestos hazards and failed to disclose it, contributing to manufacturers' liability for claims by exposed workers. The litigation involved contractual theories and government responsibility. The Federal Circuit affirmed dismissal of GAF's claim, concluding that the government did not have the contractual duty to warn the asbestos manufacturer that GAF asserted.

That legal holding is not a historical verdict that the Navy behaved safely.

This distinction is essential.

Courts answer causes of action.

They decide whether a duty exists under a legal doctrine, whether a claim is timely, whether evidence is admissible, whether a party has standing, whether a contractual relationship supports recovery. A court can quote devastating evidence of hazard knowledge and still rule for the government because the plaintiff cannot establish the legal duty necessary for its claim.

Later readers often flatten the outcome into "the court found the Navy knew" or "the court exonerated the Navy."

Neither sentence captures the job the court was doing.

The *GAF* opinion is valuable here not because it supplies a moral verdict but because it points to documents. It recounts Stephenson's March 1941 concern that men doing asbestos work were not protected as they should be. It references official reports from yards. It discusses the 1944 Bath investigation and later survey. It also records the disputed context around inspections and labor unrest.

Each of these should be traced back to the underlying document where possible.

A judicial quotation is a source.

The original memorandum is a better source for what the memorandum says.

The distinction matters especially with ellipses and summaries. Courts quote only what is relevant to the legal issue. A sentence immediately before or after the famous language can alter tone or scope. A memorandum about foundries, industrial surveys, and administrative jurisdiction can become known solely for one asbestos paragraph.

The problem is not malicious quotation.

It is contextual compression again.

Litigation also shaped the historical debate over whether the Navy had superior knowledge to manufacturers.

That proposition sounds empirical but becomes legal very quickly. Superior in what respect? The Navy had access to shipyard medical reports, military specifications, dust surveys, and its own preventive-medicine network. Manufacturers knew their products, raw materials, industrial processes, customer complaints, and sometimes medical literature. Insurers had claims data. Unions had worker experience. Academic industrial hygienists crossed among government, universities, and industry.

Knowledge was distributed rather than owned by one actor.

This makes blanket statements like "the manufacturers knew before the Navy" or "the Navy knew more than industry" difficult to sustain across all years and propositions.

One actor may have known more about asbestosis.

Another may have had better dust measurements from shipyards.

Another may have received unpublished cancer information.

Another may have controlled the work environment.

Legal doctrines can require a hierarchy. History can tolerate a network.

The network was adversarial even before the lawsuits.

Insurance divisions cared about claims. Contractors cared about compliance and cost. The Navy cared about production, fleet performance, and worker health. Manufacturers cared about sales and product viability. Medical officers cared about disease. These incentives overlap and conflict. The presence of an incentive does not prove a false statement. It tells us where to look for asymmetric skepticism and selective communication.

This book's contradiction audit treats the 1945 Drinker correspondence exactly that way.

If a consultant says a study's reassuring conclusions should go to insurance organizations because of potential worker damage suits, that is evidence of liability awareness.

It is not evidence, by itself, that the study was designed to defeat those suits.

To make the stronger claim, one would need protocol changes, correspondence about desired results, suppressed contrary findings, manipulated selection, or other evidence of intent.

Without that, motive remains open.

This standard matters because asbestos history is full of genuine corporate misconduct in some settings. Where documents establish suppression, delayed publication, manipulated messaging, or strategic doubt, those acts should be named precisely. Inflating every ambiguous document into concealment weakens the cases where concealment is actually documented.

The courtroom archive also contains another distortion: hindsight wording.

A lawyer in 1988 can ask a witness whether the Navy "knew asbestos caused cancer" in 1943. The question compresses disease type, confidence level, population, fiber, dose, and publication history into one verb. The witness has to answer in courtroom grammar.

The archive then preserves the answer as though the original historical proposition had been equally simple.

This is why contemporaneous documents deserve priority on state of knowledge.

Later testimony can explain practice, identify files, and reveal memory. It should not automatically override a dated record about what an institution believed at the time.

The courtroom also became the place where product identification and integrated systems collided.

A sailor can know he worked on a pump aboard a Navy vessel and later develop mesothelioma. The pump manufacturer may respond that it did not manufacture the asbestos insulation or replacement gasket that released fibers. The Navy may have specified asbestos-containing components. Another company supplied them. The sailor experienced one maintenance task; the law sees several products.

This problem reached the Supreme Court in 2019 in *Air & Liquid Systems Corp. v. DeVries*.

The plaintiffs were Navy veterans who alleged asbestos exposure while working with equipment on ships. Some equipment required asbestos-containing parts, even if those parts were made or added by others. The Court adopted a maritime-law duty-to-warn rule for certain integrated products: a manufacturer must warn when its product requires incorporation of a part, the manufacturer knows or has reason to know the integrated product is likely dangerous for its intended uses, and users would not reasonably be expected to recognize the danger.

The case was about legal duty, not a finding that every equipment manufacturer in the mid-century Navy had identical knowledge.

Its importance here is architectural.

It recognizes that product boundaries can fail to describe exposure reality.

The worker encounters a system designed to function with parts that release a hazard during maintenance.

The legal system spent decades deciding which corporate boundary should carry the warning.

The lungs never cared.

Court records also teach caution about the phrase "government contractor defense."

In some product-liability contexts, contractors can be protected when they manufacture according to reasonably precise government specifications, the equipment conforms to those specifications, and the contractor warns the government about dangers known to the contractor but not the government. The doctrine, associated with *Boyle v. United Technologies Corp.*, responds to federal interests in military procurement.

Asbestos defendants have invoked government specifications and Navy knowledge in various ways.

The existence of a defense does not prove the government ordered silence.

Nor does a Navy specification automatically erase a manufacturer's independent knowledge or warning obligations. The doctrine depends on facts and jurisdiction. The same historical document—a specification requiring asbestos—can support several legal arguments without deciding the historical ethics of exposure.

This is why a book should not borrow its causal model from a pleading.

Pleadings are designed to allocate responsibility under law.

History is trying to understand how the system produced exposure.

Those are related projects with different endpoints.

The courtroom archive has one more virtue: cross-examination creates pressure for specificity.

Who received the memo?

What date?

Was the report classified?

Which yard?

What concentration?

Which product?

Did the witness work there?

What did the contract require?

These questions are exactly what hidden-history writing needs.

They prevent atmospheric accusations.

A claim such as "the Navy covered up asbestos" should trigger the same cross-examination. Which office? Which document? What information? Covered up from whom? By what act? For what period? Was the information classified for military reasons, withheld from workers, omitted from a publication, or simply not widely distributed? What evidence establishes intent?

If those questions cannot be answered, the phrase is too large.

The same discipline should apply to exculpatory claims.

"Everyone knew asbestos was dangerous" can be used to diffuse responsibility until nobody had a special duty. Everyone did not know the same thing. A worker knowing dust was bad is not equivalent to a government epidemiologist possessing a shipyard survey. A manufacturer reading medical literature is not equivalent to a sailor receiving no cancer warning. A Navy engineer knowing asbestos is in a specification is not equivalent to a physician knowing mesothelioma literature.

Knowledge has content, not just possession.

The courtroom archive is at its best when it forces that content into view.

It is at its worst when adversarial necessity turns the content back into slogans.

For this book, legal records belong in evidence class one when they reproduce or authenticate contemporaneous documents, and in evidence class two when courts or parties draw historical inferences from incomplete records. Allegations in pleadings remain allegations. Expert testimony is attributed to the expert. Judicial findings are described as findings within the issue decided.

This sounds pedantic until one sees what happens without it.

A motion becomes a fact.

A quotation becomes an institutional policy.

A dismissal becomes exoneration.

A classified stamp becomes proof of a cover-up.

A lawsuit becomes the history it was arguing about.

The better use of litigation is archaeological.

Follow the exhibit back to the file.

Then ask why the file existed before anybody knew it would be evidence.
