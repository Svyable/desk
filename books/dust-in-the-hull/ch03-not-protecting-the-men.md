# Not Protecting the Men

On March 11, 1941, Commander C. S. Stephenson wrote to Rear Admiral Ross T. McIntire, the Surgeon General of the Navy.

The memorandum survived long enough to become evidence in a fight Stephenson could not have imagined. Half a century later, asbestos manufacturers and the federal government were arguing about liability. Lawyers needed to know what the Navy had known during the war, what contractors had known, whether the government had superior information, and whether anyone had a duty to warn anyone else. Old preventive-medicine correspondence became ammunition.

This is one reason the asbestos archive has to be handled with gloves even when the paper is genuine. A document can be authentic and its later use adversarial. The quote most favorable to a defendant will be placed in one brief. The quote most favorable to a worker in another. The historian inherits both the records and the selection pressure that brought them forward.

Stephenson's memo is unusually resistant to that pressure because its central point is plain.

He was worried about industrial health in Navy yards.

The memorandum discussed foundries and dust conditions broadly. In the version quoted by the Federal Circuit decades later, Stephenson wrote that Navy foundries would not pass the inspections necessary to obtain workers' compensation insurance from ordinary insurance organizations. He doubted they would be tolerated if state industrial-health authorities surveyed them. Yard medical officers, he said, had repeatedly recommended dust-concentration studies and corrective action.

Then came the section on asbestosis.

The Navy was doing considerable work with asbestos. From Stephenson's observations, he was certain the men were not being protected as they should be. The matter, he added, was the subject of official reports from several Navy yards.

It is one of those historical sentences that invites overclaim because the verified core is already strong. So keep it where it is.

A senior Navy preventive-medicine officer in March 1941 recognized asbestosis as a problem, believed existing worker protection inadequate, and understood the problem to extend beyond one isolated shop. That is documented.

It does not establish that every admiral understood later cancer risks. It does not prove a secret policy of exposing workers. It does not reveal what a pipe coverer in Boston or Brooklyn had been told. It does not even, by itself, establish what McIntire did in response. A memo is evidence that a message was sent, not that an institution changed.

But the recipient matters.

McIntire was not a remote clerk. As Surgeon General he sat at the top of the Navy's medical bureau. Stephenson's position in preventive medicine also matters. This was not a complaint scribbled by an anonymous worker and lost under a foreman's timecard. The risk had reached an official channel designed to notice health risk.

The memo therefore allows a more precise question than "Did the Navy know?"

What happened after knowledge entered the bureaucracy?

Institutions receive warnings constantly. Most warnings are partial. Some are wrong. Some describe hazards that cannot be removed immediately. Some compete with larger emergencies. A responsible system has to triage them. That creates a legitimate defense against retrospective moral theater: the mere existence of a warning does not prove negligence. The harder task is to ask whether the response was proportionate to what the warning actually established.

In March 1941 the United States had not yet entered the Second World War. It was nevertheless preparing for one. Naval shipbuilding was accelerating. Britain was fighting Germany. The Lend-Lease Act became law that month. American industry was being pulled toward a conflict whose production demands would soon become enormous.

Stephenson's concern therefore arrived at a moment when the Navy's appetite for insulation was increasing, not declining.

This timing matters. If the warning had arrived during demobilization, officials might have had leisure to redesign work. Instead it arrived while the institution was expanding the exact industrial activities that created exposure. More ships meant more hot piping. More hot piping meant more insulation. More yards and contractors meant more workers, many of them new. Expansion made the hazard larger and the control problem harder at the same time.

The Navy's response cannot be reduced to silence. Historical accounts describe occupational-health courses for government industrial hygienists at Harvard and Columbia in the early war years. Navy and Maritime Commission officials investigated shipyards. In 1943 the agencies issued minimum requirements for safety and industrial health in contract shipyards. Those requirements addressed asbestos dust through engineering controls, respiratory protection, and medical examination.

This is important because it prevents the Stephenson memo from becoming a conspiracy talisman.

The institution did react to industrial health.

The problem shifts from recognition to implementation.

A rule can exist at four levels: written, distributed, understood, and obeyed. Histories often stop at the first. Litigation sometimes stops at whichever level helps the party speaking. A defendant can point to a 1943 safety standard and say the government was sophisticated. A plaintiff can point to dusty work and disease and say the standard failed. Both observations can be simultaneously true.

To know whether the men were protected, we need evidence closer to the work.

What did the pipe-covering shop look like? How were asbestos sections cut? Was there local exhaust at the saw? Did the hood capture dust or merely move air? Were respirators available? Were they required? Was anyone fit-tested in a modern sense? Did workers wear them in heat? Were medical examinations performed on all workers or only selected groups? What happened aboard ships under construction where fixed ventilation was incomplete? What happened when production deadlines collided with dust control?

Those questions lead to Bath Iron Works.

But before Bath, there is another Stephenson memorandum that deserves attention because of one phrase repeatedly used in later litigation: "possible labor unrest."

The Federal Circuit's 1991 decision in *GAF Corporation v. United States* described a March 4, 1941 Stephenson memorandum referring to possible labor unrest if inspectors were allowed to observe conditions in Navy yards. The phrase is striking. It can be read as evidence that officials feared workers learning how bad conditions were. It can also be read more mundanely as an administrator worrying that outside inspection during a labor-sensitive production surge might trigger disruption. Without the full memorandum and its context, the phrase should not carry more weight than the surviving record permits.

This is exactly the kind of sentence hidden-history writing mishandles.

"Possible labor unrest" sounds sinister enough to become a headline. But the responsible move is to separate three things. First: a court opinion states that the phrase appears in a March 4 memorandum. Second: the phrase indicates that worker reaction was part of Stephenson's concern about inspection. Third: the motive for that concern—concealing hazards, protecting production, managing jurisdiction, avoiding conflict, or some mixture—requires the full document and surrounding correspondence.

The difference between evidence and story is often one missing page.

Still, the memo reveals something structural. Occupational-health information was not politically neutral. If inspectors documented conditions, workers might react. If workers reacted, production might suffer. A health survey could therefore affect labor relations, compensation, public reputation, and output. The people deciding whether to inspect were not choosing only whether to collect data. They were deciding whether to create information that other actors might use.

That is an institutional incentive worth tracking even when no concealment order exists.

Information creates obligations.

A dust count can require ventilation. An X-ray can create a compensation claim. A diagnosis can alarm a crew. A published paper can change industry standards. A warning label can admit hazard. A classified report can remain within official channels while the worker remains outside them. Bureaucracies do not need to falsify data to experience the production cost of knowing more.

The war magnified that tension.

If a pipe-covering operation was declared unsafe, what followed? Work might need to stop while ventilation was installed. Respirators might need to be procured and maintained. Medical examinations might identify workers requiring reassignment. Contractors might demand compensation for new requirements. Unions might bargain. Insurance costs might change. Production engineers might dispute the medical interpretation. A hazard classification could ripple through schedules designed around a national emergency.

None of this proves that officials chose ships over lungs. It explains why implementation could lag after recognition.

The asbestosis problem also suffered from a particular visibility bias. Severe disease was easiest to find among men with long exposure. Those were exactly the men least representative of a rapidly expanded wartime workforce. If a yard employed hundreds of new pipe coverers, most could look healthy because not enough time had passed. A medical officer walking through the shop would see dust immediately but disease selectively.

The mismatch between what the eye could see and what the lung would reveal later encouraged a dangerous inference: if only a few current workers were sick, perhaps the dust controls were adequate.

That inference was not stupid in the sense of being incomprehensible. It was statistically vulnerable in a way that the medical literature was already capable of suspecting. Asbestosis had long been associated with duration and intensity of exposure. A worker with two years in the trade could not be treated as equivalent evidence to a worker with twenty years. Yet the pressure to produce a simple institutional answer—dangerous trade or not—encouraged exactly that compression.

Stephenson's memo is therefore not only evidence of prior knowledge. It is a warning about what happens next in the knowledge pipeline.

The Navy had recognized a problem. It needed to know how serious the problem was in the specific conditions of shipbuilding. British textile factories and asbestos manufacturing plants were not identical to Navy pipe-covering shops. Materials differed. Processes differed. Dust levels differed. Work histories differed. A responsible institution should not simply assume that disease rates from one industry applied unchanged to another.

So it investigated.

This point deserves emphasis because the study at the center of this book was not wrong to ask whether naval shipyard exposure differed from other asbestos industries. That was a legitimate scientific question. Pipe coverers might experience intermittent rather than continuous exposure. The form of amosite insulation used aboard ships might generate different particle distributions. Ventilation could reduce dose. Workers might rotate through tasks. The industrial setting mattered.

The failure would come in how the answer was framed.

A good investigation could have said: current radiographic asbestosis appears uncommon among the workers examined, but most lack sufficient latency to exclude future disease; certain operations produce high dust and require control; long-term follow-up is necessary; absence of present disease in recently exposed workers cannot establish safety.

Instead the later published paper would make a much stronger occupational conclusion.

Between Stephenson's 1941 warning and that 1946 conclusion sat a series of surveys that make the story difficult to caricature. Bath Iron Works became one of the most important. In 1942 Philip Drinker, a Harvard industrial-hygiene authority serving as chief health consultant to the U.S. Maritime Commission, participated in an industrial-health survey there. The report described the pipe-covering shop in concrete terms: making asbestos covers for shipboard pipes, cutting and pounding matting, cutting hard pieces on a band saw.

The prose did not sound reassured.

The processes spread asbestos fiber through the shop. The conditions, the report said, presented a very real asbestos hazard. Immediate segregation and ventilation were recommended.

That phrase—very real asbestos hazard—arrived one year after Stephenson said the men were not being protected as they should be.

At this point the documentary record no longer consists of one worried officer. It contains observations at an actual contract yard and specific recommended controls.

The Navy and Maritime Commission were not blind.

What makes the later reassurance so interesting is that many of the people involved were not outsiders arriving to contradict a complacent bureaucracy. Philip Drinker himself appears on both sides of the apparent contradiction. He could recognize a serious dust risk at Bath and later coauthor the 1946 survey whose conclusion would be read for decades as evidence that naval pipe covering was not dangerous.

That is a much better historical problem than villainy.

How does an expert move from "very real asbestos hazard" to "not a dangerous trade" without believing he has contradicted himself?

Part of the answer is that hazard and observed disease are not identical. A dusty process can be hazardous while controls reduce actual dose. A trade can contain tasks needing ventilation without producing high disease rates under prevailing conditions. An expert can recommend prevention while still believing the residual risk acceptable. The apparent contradiction may therefore reflect different questions.

Another part is that wartime investigators trusted an exposure threshold and a disease model that later science would erode. If they believed a maximum allowable dust concentration protected against asbestosis, then high measurements could be treated as control problems rather than evidence that the trade itself was inherently dangerous. Install exhaust at the saw, reduce counts, monitor workers, and the process becomes acceptable.

The most consequential part, however, was time.

If disease requires long exposure, then a survey containing many recently hired wartime workers will undercount the eventual burden. The three cases among 1,074 men did not necessarily mean the trade was safe. It might mean the sample had not been exposed long enough.

The authors knew the three sick men had long histories.

That fact should have made latency louder.

Instead it became part of the reassurance.

All three cases had worked at pipe covering for more than twenty years. Therefore, the paper reasoned, the low incidence supported the conclusion that the occupation was not dangerous.

Another reader could look at the same fact and ask the opposite question: how many of the 1,071 workers classified as unaffected had twenty years of comparable exposure?

The denominator is where history hides.

Stephenson's 1941 sentence about not protecting the men has survived because it is emotionally legible. The deeper failure is less quotable. It lives in the construction of a denominator, the distribution of work tenure, the sensitivity of chest radiography, the assumed dust threshold, and a policy appetite for a yes-or-no answer before the disease clock had matured.

That is how an institution can know a hazard exists and still convince itself the trade is safe.

Not by forgetting the warning.

By measuring the wrong future.
