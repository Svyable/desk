## Thesis

The Navy's asbestos history is more revealing than the familiar accusation that officials simply knew the full modern danger and concealed it. By the early 1940s Navy and Maritime Commission records show recognition of serious asbestos-dust hazards, calls for ventilation, respirators, and medical surveillance, and explicit concern that workers were not being adequately protected. Yet a wartime health survey of shipyard pipe coverers, published in 1946, found only three cases of asbestosis among 1,074 workers and concluded that naval pipe covering was not a dangerous trade. The study treated a long-latency disease as if a cross-sectional snapshot could settle it. Its reassuring conclusion coexisted with high dust measurements, internal warnings, production pressure, fragmented responsibility, and later evidence linking asbestos to lung cancer and mesothelioma. This book reconstructs who knew what, when; what workers were actually told; how a weak inference became durable policy; what later science changed; and where litigation-era retellings overstate or simplify the surviving record.
