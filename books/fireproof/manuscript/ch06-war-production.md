# War Production

War changes the denominator.

A material risk that might be reconsidered slowly in peacetime enters a different moral economy when ships are being built, repaired and returned to service under pressure. The immediate counterfactual is no longer an abstract healthier workplace. It may be a delayed hull, an unavailable escort, a damaged boiler left unrepaired, a transport that does not sail.

That does not erase occupational responsibility. It changes the archival question.

The late 1930s through the wartime shipbuilding expansion matter because later congressional records describe extensive Navy asbestos use beginning in that period and continuing for decades. The scale of wartime construction means decisions made under emergency conditions could become standardized material culture. Temporary necessity has a way of acquiring a stock number.

A useful history has to distinguish three things that are often collapsed: a decision made because no practical substitute existed; a decision made because substitution would have been costly or inconvenient; and a decision nobody revisited because the existing material had become normal. Those categories can look identical from inside a finished ship.

Specifications again become crucial. If asbestos-containing insulation or gaskets were qualified for particular uses, what performance problem were they solving? What substitutes existed at the time? When did nonasbestos substitutes become technically credible? Did substitution lag because of performance, supply, qualification testing, cost, institutional inertia, or some combination?

Without that engineering record, the moral story becomes too easy. It is possible to condemn a material choice retrospectively without understanding why competent engineers kept making it. But understanding is not absolution. If the health evidence strengthened while the performance case for alternatives improved, the persistence of old specifications becomes more consequential, not less.

War also complicates worker choice. A civilian shipyard worker in a mobilized economy and a sailor assigned to a machinery space do not encounter occupational risk as consumers comparing products. The institution chooses the material environment. That asymmetry creates a higher burden for warning and control because exit is constrained by employment, duty, patriotism, hierarchy or all four.

This chapter will eventually need numbers: shipyard employment, production tempo, quantities of insulation, occupational-disease observations, procurement volumes. Those numbers are not yet in this draft because they need primary sourcing rather than decorative precision.

For now, the conceptual hinge is enough. The Navy’s asbestos problem was not merely that it used a dangerous material. It used a dangerous material at enormous scale inside an institution optimized to make engineering decisions propagate.

The machinery that built ships quickly could also build a hazard quickly.

And once the war ended, the ships remained.
