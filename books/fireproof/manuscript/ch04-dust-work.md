# Dust Work

Exposure is a verb before it becomes a number.

Somebody cuts. Somebody scrapes. Somebody pulls old lagging away from a pipe. Somebody sweeps. Somebody opens a valve assembly whose gasket has spent years being heated, compressed and baked into place. Somebody works below another trade disturbing insulation overhead. The material enters the body through work.

This is obvious enough to be easy to skip, and skipping it is how institutional histories become bloodless. A procurement document tells us what could be present. An industrial-hygiene sample tells us what was measured during a particular task. Neither substitutes for reconstructing the task itself.

Shipyard exposure was also socially distributed. Civilian yard workers and sailors did not necessarily occupy the same regulatory, medical or compensation systems. Trades differed. A pipefitter’s contact with insulation was not an electrician’s, yet secondary disturbance could blur the boundary. A worker who never held asbestos insulation could still work in a compartment where another crew removed it. Clothing could carry dust away from the immediate job. Each of those pathways needs its own evidence; none should be assumed simply because it is plausible.

The Navy’s current industrial-hygiene machinery makes the contrast visible. Modern practice speaks in breathing-zone samples, exposure assessments, respiratory protection, asbestos sampling and medical surveillance. The existence of that vocabulary is not evidence that every earlier yard lacked controls. It is evidence that the problem eventually became legible in a highly formalized way.

Legibility matters. Institutions manage what they can turn into categories, forms, thresholds and responsibilities. Before that conversion, workers may experience a hazard as irritation, dirt, an unpleasant job or something “everybody knows” is bad without possessing the administrative language that forces a decision.

One research task for this manuscript is therefore to find the moment dust became data. Not the first time anyone complained about it. The moment Navy industrial hygiene began sampling specific asbestos jobs in ways that can be reconstructed: task, duration, concentration, controls, location, trade, recommendation, follow-up.

That ledger could answer a more useful question than whether a shipyard was “safe.” Safety is not a binary historical property. Which jobs produced the highest measured exposures? Which controls reduced them? Which recommendations repeated across surveys, suggesting implementation failure? Did certain yards improve earlier than others? Did emergency or wartime work suspend precautions that existed on paper?

A worker inhaling dust does not inhale policy. He inhales whatever the job put into the air that day.

The history has to get that close.
