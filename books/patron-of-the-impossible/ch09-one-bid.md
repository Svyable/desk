# One Bid

The mythology of secret programs usually begins after the procurement office has gone home.

The procurement office is where this chapter begins.

In 2008 the Defense Intelligence Agency sought a contractor for work titled Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program. The public solicitation is a dry document. That is precisely why it matters. It tells us what the government said it was buying before years of argument, journalism, memoir, whistleblower testimony, congressional interest, and internet reconstruction layered themselves onto the program.

The statement of objectives was broad and futuristic.

DIA wanted assessments of foreign advanced aerospace technologies that could emerge over a long horizon and potentially threaten the United States. The technical territory included lift, propulsion, control, power generation, spatial and temporal translation, materials, configuration, signature reduction, human interface, human effects, and other areas associated with advanced platforms. The solicitation anticipated technical reports, analysis, conferences, and personnel with experience relevant to high-performance aircraft and satellites. It contemplated access to classified information if required.

Read without the later UFO story, it looks like a speculative defense-technology contract.

That is important.

BAASS—Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies—was the sole bidder and won.

The phrase sole bidder can be made to sound suspicious or trivial depending on the narrator. Neither reaction is automatically justified. Specialized government solicitations often attract few bidders. A contractor with an unusual combination of facilities, aerospace experience, technical personnel, and willingness to pursue speculative topics may simply have faced little competition. At the same time, a sole-source competitive environment deserves scrutiny because there is less market pressure to expose weaknesses in a proposal.

The correct historical question is not why nobody stopped the weird UFO contract.

The solicitation was not written as a UFO contract.

The question is how a broadly framed advanced-aerospace procurement became associated with UAP and paranormal research.

That transformation sits at the heart of the Bigelow story.

Harry Reid, the Nevada senator and Senate majority leader, was central to creating the political space for the program. Reid had known Bigelow and was sympathetic to taking unexplained aerospace reports seriously. Other senators, including Ted Stevens and Daniel Inouye, have been described in participant accounts as supportive. These relationships matter because government programs are not spontaneous organisms. Someone decides a question deserves money.

Political sponsorship is not scientific evidence.

It is authorization.

Bigelow’s prior history made BAASS an unusual contractor. The company existed within an aerospace enterprise that had already launched Genesis I and II. It could plausibly present itself as technically capable. At the same time, Bigelow had spent more than a decade funding NIDS and owned Skinwalker Ranch. People in his network had deep interests in anomalous phenomena, remote viewing, exotic physics, and UFO reports.

The government was therefore buying advanced aerospace research from a contractor whose intellectual ecosystem extended far beyond conventional aerospace.

That fact can be read two ways.

Supporters can say this was exactly the point. A breakthrough-threat program should hire people willing to consider ideas ordinary contractors dismiss.

Critics can say the contract created a pathway for a preexisting paranormal research network to receive public money under the banner of advanced aerospace.

The public record supports elements of both interpretations.

The first-year status report is unusually revealing. DIA described BAASS performance as excellent and said the contractor was in full compliance with the statement of work. By June 30, 2009, BAASS had produced twenty-six detailed research reports, twice the minimum required at that stage. Reviews were described as overwhelmingly positive. The status document recommended exercising the next contract option.

This is not the record of an agency immediately horrified by contractor incompetence.

The report titles show what the government considered relevant to the future-technology mission. They ranged across inertial electrostatic confinement fusion, advanced nuclear propulsion, metamaterials, invisibility, programmable matter, spintronics, quantum entanglement communications, biological effects of fields, brain-machine interfaces, warp drives, wormholes, vacuum energy, antigravity concepts, and other frontier subjects.

A modern reader can laugh at the list and miss the genre.

Defense forecasting routinely studies technologies before engineering maturity. A report about wormholes is not a purchase order for a Stargate. It is an attempt to ask what established physics permits, what assumptions exotic proposals require, and whether an adversary breakthrough would matter.

Some topics were highly speculative. Others were active mainstream research areas. Grouping them in one list creates exactly the same heterogeneity problem NIDS faced with anomalies.

Metamaterials were real and advancing.

Warp-drive metrics were mathematical constructs with severe physical obstacles.

Brain-machine interfaces were real and immature.

Vacuum-energy extraction claims occupied a far more speculative category.

A program can responsibly review all of them if it keeps maturity levels clear.

This is where terminology becomes policy.

A technical review should distinguish “physically established,” “laboratory demonstrated,” “engineering plausible,” “theoretically discussed,” and “speculative.” If those categories blur, a stack of literature reviews can make a science-fiction inventory look like an emerging arsenal.

The BAASS technical reports later became known publicly as Defense Intelligence Reference Documents, or DIRDs. Many eventually entered the public record through Freedom of Information Act releases and congressional attention. Their existence became one of the most concrete legacies of the program.

They are also frequently misunderstood.

The fact that DIA commissioned or received a report on a subject does not mean DIA accepted the subject as operationally real. Intelligence organizations commission studies to reduce uncertainty. A study may conclude that a concept is infeasible, remote, or dependent on unknown physics. The title alone tells us almost nothing about the conclusion.

This is the procurement version of the unidentified-object problem.

Interest is not identification.

Bigelow’s contractor appears to have satisfied the formal technical-report requirements well enough to earn strong first-year reviews. That is a verified fact from the surviving status paper. The more controversial work sits outside the cleanest procurement language.

AARO’s 2024 historical report states that the contractor, with support from the government program manager, also conducted UAP and paranormal research. According to AARO, the work involved case investigations and activity at the Utah property owned by the head of the contracting organization. AARO says these subjects were not specifically described in the original statement of objectives and that DIA did not specifically seek or authorize them as a programmatic mission, even though a DIA employee managed the contract.

That is an official retrospective assessment.

It has been disputed by participants and advocates who describe the program differently.

The disagreement is not a footnote. It is the core governance question.

How much interpretive freedom does a contractor have under a broad research objective?

If the contract asks about advanced aerospace threats and human effects, can unusual encounter reports be treated as data relevant to those questions?

If a property is believed to produce anomalous effects on humans and equipment, can field investigation be justified as part of human-effects research?

At what point does broad exploratory scope become mission drift?

Government contracting answers these questions through statements of work, deliverables, invoices, program-manager approvals, technical reviews, and audits. The public has only part of that record.

This is where a document appendix becomes more important than a theory.

We need the solicitation.

We need the proposal.

We need the complete contract and modifications.

We need work breakdown structures.

We need invoices and labor categories where releasable.

We need technical deliverables.

We need program-manager correspondence.

We need classification decisions.

We need records showing what government officials accepted, rejected, or redirected.

Without those, later participants can sincerely remember the program through different frames.

One may remember AAWSAP as an advanced-aerospace threat study.

Another may remember it as the government’s serious UFO program.

Another may remember it as an investigation of a broad anomaly complex including human effects.

Another may describe the UAP work as unauthorized expansion around a defense contract.

Memory follows function.

The person writing technical propulsion reports did not experience the same program as the person investigating cases.

The name itself adds confusion. AAWSAP and AATIP are often used interchangeably in public accounts. AARO’s historical report draws a sharper distinction, describing AAWSAP as the official DIA program and later AATIP activity associated with Luis Elizondo as an informal effort rather than an official successor program after AAWSAP ended. Participants and journalists have contested parts of that reconstruction.

The naming dispute matters because program names carry authority.

If an informal activity is remembered as an official Pentagon program, later claims inherit institutional weight they may not deserve.

If an official program is minimized because its most controversial work sat outside a narrow reading of the contract, genuine government interest may be understated.

Again, the cure is paperwork.

Robert Bigelow sits at the center without being the only actor. He did not write every government requirement. He did not personally author every technical paper. He did not alone decide what DIA would fund. Yet BAASS was his company, and the contract placed public money inside an ecosystem he had built privately.

That was the institutional achievement.

For years Bigelow had paid to investigate questions mainstream science mostly ignored. In 2008, a defense agency paid one of his organizations to study advanced aerospace possibilities. The overlap between those missions was enough to change the history of UAP politics even if no extraordinary technology were ever demonstrated.

Government money creates a documentary afterimage.

Private NIDS activity could fade into interviews and books.

AAWSAP generated solicitations, status reports, letters, deliverables, budget decisions, and later official reviews.

That is why this period deserves more attention than the most cinematic ranch stories. It is where Bigelow’s private epistemology became auditable in fragments.

The fragments show competence and ambiguity at once.

BAASS delivered enough formal work to receive excellent early reviews.

The technical subjects were often genuinely frontier-oriented.

The contractor also appears to have pursued anomaly work connected to its prior interests.

Government officials later disagreed over how that work should be characterized.

The program ended without public proof of extraterrestrial technology.

All of those statements can be true simultaneously.

The desire for a cleaner story is political, not historical.

Believers want a secret UFO program that finally proves insiders took the phenomenon seriously.

Debunkers want a pork-barrel curiosity shop that slipped paranormal hobbies into government funding.

The documents resist both cartoons.

DIA did fund a speculative advanced-aerospace program.

Bigelow’s contractor really did produce technical work the agency initially praised.

UAP and paranormal interests really did enter the program ecosystem.

The relationship between those elements remains one of the areas where the surviving public record needs finer reconstruction.

The astonishing fact is not hidden behind an anonymous leak.

It is in the procurement file.

A self-made hotel developer who had spent years funding UFO and consciousness research also built an aerospace company capable enough to become the sole bidder on a Defense Intelligence Agency advanced-aerospace contract.

No conspiracy is required.

The institutional path itself is stranger than most theories about it.
