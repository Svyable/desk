# Triangles over the Interstate

Unidentified flying objects are easiest to misunderstand when the word unidentified is treated as an object class.

It is not.

Unidentified is a temporary state of a report.

A light seen over a highway can begin unidentified and end as a meteor. A radar return can begin unidentified and end as clutter. A pilot report can remain unidentified because the geometry is too poor to reconstruct. Another case can remain unexplained after serious analysis without becoming evidence for any particular extraordinary cause.

NIDS’s work on black-triangle reports is useful because it forces this distinction into the open.

Beginning in the 1980s and especially the 1990s, witnesses in the United States and elsewhere reported large dark triangular or boomerang-shaped objects, often described as moving slowly, hovering, displaying lights at vertices, or accelerating in ways that seemed inconsistent with familiar aircraft. The reports attracted both UFO researchers and people interested in secret aviation programs. A large triangle is almost too convenient a mystery for the late Cold War. The United States really was developing unusual aircraft behind fences. Stealth technology had already shown that vehicles dismissed as impossible could emerge later as classified programs. At the same time, human perception of size, distance, and speed at night is notoriously poor when reference cues disappear.

The category therefore arrived preloaded with two seductive explanations: secret aircraft or something non-human.

NIDS collected reports and tried to characterize the pattern.

One of the more valuable habits in serious case analysis is geographic humility. A witness sees an event from one point. Investigators can widen the frame. Was there a launch? A military exercise? A known flight route? A celestial event? Did witnesses in separated locations report compatible trajectories? Did the object cross jurisdictions? Were there radar records? Did the timing match any public event?

This work often destroys the most exciting explanation.

That is a feature.

If a bright object over several states can be matched to a re-entering rocket body, the mystery is smaller and the method is stronger. If a formation of aircraft creates an apparent triangle from a particular viewpoint, the case becomes a lesson in perception rather than propulsion. Every solved case calibrates the unsolved ones.

A good anomaly institute should therefore be proud of shrinking its own archive.

The black-triangle problem also illustrates something Bigelow’s network understood earlier than much of public discourse: eyewitness testimony contains real information without being a camera.

Witnesses are sensors with cognition in the loop.

That sounds insulting until one remembers that every expert observer is exactly the same kind of instrument. Pilots, police officers, astronomers, hunters, radar operators, soldiers, and air-traffic controllers develop specialized perceptual skill. Their reports can be highly informative. Expertise changes the error distribution. It does not eliminate error.

A trained pilot may identify conventional aircraft better than a casual observer and still misjudge the size of a distant light against a featureless sky.

An officer may accurately report that an object seemed enormous and still lack the range information needed to convert angular size into physical size.

A witness may be sincere, observant, and wrong about interpretation.

This is why testimony should be decomposed before it is believed or dismissed.

What was directly perceived?

What was inferred?

Which details appeared in the first statement?

Which appeared after media exposure?

How long was the observation?

What were the lighting conditions?

Could multiple lights have been perceived as a single dark body because the mind connected them?

Did independent witnesses describe the same feature before speaking to one another?

These questions do not accuse the witness. They protect the witness’s useful information from later narrative inflation.

Bigelow’s anomaly institutions repeatedly operated in domains where the original evidence was human perception. That made witness handling one of their most important technical tasks, even though it rarely sounds as exciting as exotic propulsion.

The triangle reports also created a bridge into aerospace.

If witnesses were seeing secret aircraft, the problem was intelligence and technology forecasting. What could an advanced platform look like? How might unconventional lift, propulsion, materials, or signature management alter the familiar profile of an aircraft? If witnesses were not seeing secret aircraft, could the reports indicate some foreign capability? If neither, did the residual cases justify considering physical models outside conventional aerospace?

This chain of questions can be reasonable at the beginning and speculative by the end.

The transition must be marked.

One of the recurring weaknesses in later UAP discourse is that conceptual possibility gets mistaken for evidence. General relativity permits unusual spacetime geometries under certain mathematical conditions. Metamaterials can manipulate electromagnetic propagation in useful ways. Quantum systems display correlations that feel strange by everyday intuition. None of those facts means a particular light over Nevada used a warp metric, metamaterial cloak, or quantum communication system.

Physics can describe possibility spaces far larger than engineering can occupy.

Bigelow’s later DIA contract funded reviews of precisely this outer territory: warp drives, wormholes, metamaterials, advanced propulsion, vacuum energy, and other topics. Many of those topics were legitimate theoretical research subjects even if practical applications remained remote. The danger was not studying them. The danger was allowing an unidentified observation to act as if it selected one of them.

A triangle-shaped report does not come with a propulsion system attached.

This sounds obvious when stated plainly. It becomes less obvious once institutions begin producing technical reports around the broader question of advanced aerospace threats. A reader sees a document titled about warp drives in a government archive, sees a report about a triangular object, and joins them into a hidden program.

The archive becomes montage.

Bigelow’s career teaches us to resist montage by reconstructing chronology.

NIDS preceded BAASS.

Bigelow Aerospace preceded the DIA contract.

Genesis I and II flew before the procurement.

The DIA solicitation asked for advanced aerospace technical studies, not a confirmation of extraterrestrial craft.

The contractor’s prior interests shaped what it was prepared to study.

Later official assessments concluded that UAP and paranormal work occurred even though those topics were not specifically stated in the contract’s objectives.

This sequence is already remarkable. It does not need an invisible step.

The triangle cases matter in another way because they sit at the boundary between secrecy and misidentification. Real classified aerospace programs generate UFO reports. This has been acknowledged repeatedly across decades. U-2 and OXCART flights occurred at altitudes unfamiliar to civilian observers and contributed to unusual sightings. Stealth aircraft and test ranges create similar effects. The existence of secret aircraft therefore increases the probability that some unresolved reports have classified conventional causes.

Paradoxically, it also gives extraordinary claims cultural fuel.

Every time a once-secret aircraft is revealed, believers can say that skeptics mocked witnesses before. Sometimes they are right. The lesson should be modest: do not assume impossibility from current public knowledge.

The stronger lesson—therefore any dramatic unexplained craft is probably a hidden breakthrough—is not justified.

Secrecy creates an asymmetric game. The public cannot rule out programs it does not know exist. Investigators can gather evidence but may hit a wall precisely where national security becomes relevant. This means some cases can remain permanently underdetermined.

Underdetermination is emotionally unsatisfying.

It is still an answer.

Bigelow seems unusually comfortable investing in underdetermined spaces. Most businesses dislike questions that cannot be closed. His anomaly work pursued them. That may have been one reason intelligence-community figures found the ecosystem interesting: intelligence itself is a profession of incomplete information.

But intelligence and science ask different questions.

An intelligence analyst may need to decide whether a 5 percent chance of a foreign technology warrants collection. A scientist may ask whether the evidence is sufficient to accept a physical claim. The thresholds are not the same.

Confusing them can make national-security interest look like scientific confirmation.

The triangle problem makes this visible. Imagine ten reports.

Six are explained as conventional aircraft or astronomical events.

Two lack enough data.

One correlates with a classified program investigators cannot access.

One remains genuinely puzzling.

An intelligence office may care intensely about the last four.

A public advocate may describe the same set as four unexplained craft.

A scientist may say only one case has enough quality to challenge current explanation, and even that case has no known cause.

Nobody has to be lying for these narratives to diverge.

The divergence becomes political when secrecy enters.

Bigelow’s organizations existed in the middle of that divergence. They could receive reports from civilians and former officials, employ people with government backgrounds, think about advanced aerospace, and later perform work for DIA. This made them natural magnets for claims that could not be publicly resolved.

It also made methodological transparency more important than ever.

One can see the shape of a better system in the NIDS experience.

Every case should begin with a time-stamped intake preserving the witness’s first description.

Sensor data should be acquired independently where possible.

Analysts should record alternative hypotheses before seeing all contextual information.

Known military and aerospace explanations should be checked through authorized channels without letting classification become a universal excuse.

Cases should be scored for data quality separately from strangeness.

A highly strange low-quality case should not outrank a moderately strange multisensor case.

Unidentified should remain a status, not a species.

Modern UAP programs have moved toward some of these principles because the problem became official again. That makes Bigelow’s earlier work historically interesting even when it did not produce decisive public evidence. The network was grappling with categories that later government offices would have to formalize: witness reliability, sensor fusion, military reporting stigma, classification, foreign technology, and the difference between unresolved and extraordinary.

The strongest contribution may therefore be procedural rather than ontological.

That is not the contribution enthusiasts wanted.

They wanted the object.

Bigelow eventually said he believed he knew what at least some objects represented. In a 2017 CBS interview, he told Lara Logan that he was absolutely convinced extraterrestrial beings had visited Earth. He described the presence as existing here and now. That is among the clearest public statements of his personal conclusion.

It is also testimony.

Bigelow did not, in that interview, place an extraterrestrial artifact on the table.

His conviction matters because it influenced his research agenda. It does not substitute for the evidence that produced the conviction.

This is where biographical writing often becomes unfair. A person’s belief is either mocked as pathology or treated as privileged access. There is a third option: treat belief as a historical fact about the person and then ask what evidence is publicly available to everyone else.

Bigelow’s belief can be sincere, informed by experiences and information outsiders lack, and still remain unverified from the outside.

The correct epistemic response is not to call him foolish.

It is to ask him to show his work.

Some of that work is inaccessible or lost. Some remains anecdotal. Some became government-funded. Some transformed into theoretical reports. Some crossed into aerospace engineering that can be evaluated directly.

The black triangle therefore serves as a symbol for Bigelow’s entire project. At a distance, the outline looks coherent. Move closer and it separates into points of light.

The mind wants to connect them.

The investigator’s job is to earn the lines.
