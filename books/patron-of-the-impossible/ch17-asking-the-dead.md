# Asking the Dead

A research program changes character when it stops asking whether a tool works and starts using the tool.

Bigelow Institute for Consciousness Studies crossed that line deliberately.

After the 2021 essay competition, BICS continued into projects involving mediums. Its own published descriptions are unusually candid about the premise. Rather than treating mediumship only as a phenomenon whose existence remained to be established, the institute described work proceeding from assumptions that the afterlife is real and that qualified mediums can obtain valid information from it. One later challenge involved dozens of mediums, multiple teams, several countries, months of work, and substantial prize money.

This is methodologically provocative.

It is also more interesting than a simple claim that Bigelow “funded psychics.”

The question is what happens when a patron decides a disputed capability has passed his personal threshold and tries to build applications on top of it.

Technology history contains many examples of this move.

Once electricity became reliable enough, research shifted from proving electrical effects to building motors, telegraphs, lighting, and instrumentation. Once germ theory matured, medicine stopped re-litigating microorganisms before every sterilization protocol. Once radio communication was established, engineers optimized antennas and coding instead of proving electromagnetic waves each morning.

Application is efficient because mature knowledge does not need to be re-proven constantly.

Premature application is dangerous because it can harden an unresolved claim into infrastructure.

Mediumship sits directly in that tension.

There is a long research literature around claimed anomalous information transfer from purported deceased communicators. Some experiments use proxy sitters, blinded readings, scoring protocols, and attempts to block ordinary information leakage. Some report statistically significant results. Critics raise concerns about sensory leakage, flexible scoring, multiple comparisons, publication bias, subjective matching, experimenter effects, and replication. The literature is neither equivalent to stage cold reading nor equivalent to established communication technology.

That middle state is hard to inhabit.

BICS chose not to inhabit it for every project.

The institute’s stated unique approach was to ask what mediums might learn if treated as information channels rather than experimental defendants.

This can be defended as exploratory research.

Suppose a capability is real but the standard proof paradigm constrains it badly. Repeatedly forcing a medium into sterile forced-choice tests might measure performance under conditions unlike the alleged phenomenon. An exploratory program could instead observe what experienced practitioners produce when allowed to work naturally, then search for patterns that generate future tests.

Ethology did something analogous when scientists stopped forcing every animal into laboratory tasks and began studying behavior in ecological context.

But naturalistic exploration does not remove the need for controls.

It postpones them.

The stronger the application claim, the more controls return.

If a medium supplies novel factual information, who had access to that information?

Was the target selected in advance?

What counts as a hit?

How are vague statements scored?

How many statements were wrong?

Were raters blinded?

Could information have been obtained through public records or private investigation?

Were unsuccessful sessions included?

Did multiple mediums independently produce the same specific information before comparison?

Was the output time-stamped?

What prediction follows that can be checked later?

These are not skeptical traps.

They are the difference between a moving experience and transferable knowledge.

Mediumship is especially sensitive to scoring because human language is elastic. A statement such as “I see trouble in the chest” can fit many deaths. “He is showing me a red car” may feel specific until dozens of statements and possible associations are available. Sitters naturally search memory for connections. This is not stupidity. It is how meaning works.

Experimental design must therefore prevent meaning from doing all the causal work.

One strong design asks several independent judges to rank which reading belongs to which target under blinded conditions. Another uses predefined specific information unavailable publicly. Another separates mediums from sitters entirely through proxies. Another registers analysis before collecting data.

No single design solves everything.

A serious field accumulates convergence across them.

BICS’s application framing creates an additional problem: wisdom is harder to verify than facts.

If the question posed to mediums is not “what is the deceased person’s middle name?” but “what should humanity know about death?”, the resulting material becomes philosophical or spiritual content. Its value may be real to readers without being empirically attributable to deceased communicators.

A statement can be wise and originate in the medium.

A statement can be banal and, in principle, originate elsewhere.

Content quality does not establish source.

This is the same source-identification problem that haunts UAP.

Observed effect first.

Cause second.

Do not let the desirability of the interpretation collapse the steps.

Bigelow’s later consciousness work is therefore not a departure from his earlier career. It repeats the structure in a different domain.

At Skinwalker Ranch, the site allegedly produced heterogeneous events whose cause was unknown.

At AAWSAP, unusual observations and future aerospace concepts sat near one another without being identical.

At BICS, anomalous experiences and information claims are organized around survival of consciousness.

In each case the patron is attracted not merely to an anomaly but to an ontological possibility.

What if the world contains more agency than conventional models recognize?

Nonhuman intelligence.

Consciousness not reducible to brain activity.

Information channels not bounded by ordinary sensory pathways.

Bigelow’s questions repeatedly challenge borders around mind and matter.

This philosophical continuity is stronger than the superficial continuity of UFOs and ghosts.

He seems to be asking whether human beings inhabit a reality with more actors, channels, and layers than ordinary materialism allows.

That is a legitimate philosophical question.

Empirical claims inside it still require local proof.

A worldview can be right while a particular séance is wrong.

A materialist worldview can be broadly productive while a specific anomalous result remains unexplained.

The temptation is to let one surprising case decide the whole metaphysics.

Bigelow’s patronage amplifies that temptation because the projects are emotionally and conceptually linked.

If UAP suggest nonhuman intelligence, if ranch phenomena seem interactive, if remote viewing suggests mind is not spatially ordinary, if near-death experiences suggest consciousness persists, the whole portfolio can begin to look like one hidden reality seen from different angles.

This synthesis is powerful.

It is also speculative.

The connections themselves are not established merely because the same people or patron studied them.

A research portfolio is not a unified theory.

BICS’s work should therefore be judged on consciousness evidence, not on whatever one thinks happened at Skinwalker Ranch.

This domain separation is difficult for both critics and advocates.

Critics may dismiss mediumship because it sits in the same cultural category as UFOs.

Advocates may strengthen mediumship with UAP evidence because both challenge conventional ontology.

Neither move is scientific.

Each claim has its own error model.

Near-death research faces physiological timing, memory, drug effects, cerebral function, and prospective-verification problems.

Mediumship faces leakage, fraud, cueing, scoring, and replication.

Reincarnation cases face information pathways, family expectations, record timing, cultural selection, and matching bias.

UAP faces sensor calibration, geometry, misidentification, classified systems, atmospheric effects, and source attribution.

Skinwalker-style field research faces baseline rates, environmental controls, witness expectation, heterogeneous event classes, and data custody.

The fact that all are weird is not a mechanism.

Bigelow’s willingness to fund across all of them may nevertheless have one scientific virtue: it encourages researchers to look for cross-domain predictions.

A good synthesis should risk something.

If consciousness can access information independent of ordinary sensory pathways, what measurable constraints govern that access?

Does distance matter?

Time?

Emotional relationship?

Target entropy?

Observer state?

Electromagnetic environment?

Can the effect be blocked?

Can it be amplified?

Does the same individual perform across remote viewing and mediumship tasks?

Can physiological markers predict success?

Does the hypothesis generate a quantitative model better than conventional information leakage?

These questions turn metaphysics into experimental territory.

Bigelow had enough resources to commission such work at scale.

The opportunity remains only partly exploited publicly.

The BICS prize ecosystem also creates an incentive worth examining. A large prize attracts people who believe they can win under the stated premise. This increases talent and effort on the affirmative side. It does not automatically recruit equally talented disconfirmers.

One solution would be paired prizes.

Half the money for the strongest evidence of survival.

Half for the strongest conventional explanation of that evidence.

Then a third fund for experiments both sides agree could discriminate.

This would turn patronage into adversarial collaboration.

Bigelow’s career suggests he valued commitment more than detached neutrality. That may be why paired adversarial structures appear less often than affirmative ones.

Yet his aerospace work shows he tolerated adversarial constraint when the environment imposed it.

The question is whether he could have imported that culture intentionally into consciousness research.

A mediumship challenge can be built like a flight test.

Define payload: specific target information.

Define interface: blinded communication channel.

Define environmental controls: no access to sitter or public records beyond protocol.

Define telemetry: complete time-stamped transcript.

Define failure: preregistered scoring below threshold.

Define independent customer: analysts who do not share the sponsor’s belief.

Define replication: a second team using new targets.

Define anomaly review: wrong statements retained, not edited out.

Define mission success before launch.

The analogy is imperfect.

It is useful because it asks the disputed capability to survive interfaces it does not control.

The irony of Robert Bigelow’s intellectual life is that he built one of the clearest examples of this discipline in orbit and then spent decades approaching harder-to-control phenomena where discipline had to be designed rather than inherited.

BICS is more transparent than NIDS, which makes this comparison possible. Its website tells us the institute’s premise. It publishes essays. It names judges. It describes challenges. The material can be criticized without relying entirely on leaks or recollection.

That is progress.

The next step would be to make the institute’s strongest claims capable of defeating the institute’s own assumptions.

Can a project built on survival conclude non-survival?

Can an experiment using mediums conclude the information channel failed?

Can null results be as visible as successful ones?

Can outside teams reproduce the strongest results without Bigelow’s network?

If yes, the field begins to escape its patron.

If no, the work remains historically interesting but epistemically enclosed.

Asking the dead is not inherently an unserious question.

Human beings have asked it for as long as we have buried one another.

The unusual thing about Bigelow is not that he asked.

It is that he had enough money to turn the question into an institution.

The institution’s responsibility is therefore greater, not smaller.

A private conviction can live on experience.

A public claim needs an instrument.
