# I Know Why

Ask somebody why they chose one shirt instead of another and you will usually get an answer.

It may be immediate.

The color.

The fit.

It goes with the shoes.

The other one looked too formal.

The remarkable thing is not that people have reasons. Of course we do. We deliberate, compare, anticipate, remember, imagine consequences, and sometimes spend absurd amounts of time explaining a decision to ourselves before making it.

The remarkable thing is how easily the existence of an answer becomes evidence that the answer was the cause.

A reason can be true without being complete.

It can be sincere without being historically accurate.

It can be discovered after the action and still become part of what the action means.

This is one of the places where ordinary language encourages a misleading model of the mind. We speak as though actions are produced in a tidy sequence.

First I decide what I want.

Then I know why I want it.

Then I act.

Then, if asked, I retrieve the reason from the shelf where it has been sitting since before the choice.

Sometimes life works roughly like that.

Often it does not.

In 1977, psychologists Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson published a paper with a title that became more famous than many of its experiments: “Telling More Than We Can Know.” Their argument challenged the assumption that people have direct introspective access to the mental processes producing many judgments and choices. People often know the content of their thoughts and can report what they believe, feel, intend, or prefer. But when asked what caused a particular response, verbal explanations can draw on plausible theories about what *would* cause such a response rather than on direct inspection of the process itself.

The distinction matters.

I can know that I prefer the blue shirt.

That does not mean I can inspect, from the inside, every causal step that produced the preference.

The mind is not an incident log.

Decades later, a wonderfully uncomfortable experiment made the problem harder to dismiss as an abstraction. Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson asked participants to choose which of two faces they found more attractive. Through a sleight-of-hand procedure, the researchers sometimes handed participants the picture they had *not* selected and asked them to explain their choice.

Many mismatches went unnoticed.

Participants then gave reasons for choosing the face they had not, in fact, chosen.

The researchers called the phenomenon choice blindness.

It is easy to turn this into a cheap joke about human irrationality. Look at us, inventing reasons for decisions we did not even make.

That is not the most interesting reading.

The interesting reading is that the machinery for explanation is constructive.

Presented with an apparent fact about ourselves—*this is the person you chose*—we are capable of building a coherent reason around it. The reason can feel introspective because it uses real preferences, real categories, real observations, and a real sense of self. The fabrication is not necessarily conscious. The person is not lying in the ordinary sense. They are making sense.

Making sense is something human beings do with extraordinary speed.

The speed is useful.

It is also dangerous.

A manager promotes one candidate and explains that the candidate showed leadership. A voter supports a policy and explains that the policy fits a long-standing principle. A consumer buys an expensive object and explains that quality matters. A person stays in a relationship and explains that loyalty matters. Another person leaves and explains that authenticity matters.

The explanations may all be correct.

They may also be descriptions selected partly because they make the action legible after the fact.

We should resist the temptation to answer every human explanation with a smug “rationalization.” That word is useful when it names a specific distortion. Used indiscriminately, it becomes another theory that explains too much. If every reason is dismissed as post hoc, then deliberation disappears by definition and the theory becomes impossible to falsify.

People do think before acting.

We sometimes write pros and cons.

We ask friends.

We lose sleep.

We postpone decisions because the reasons genuinely conflict.

We rehearse arguments until one survives.

We change our minds because new information arrives.

We make commitments explicitly because a principle matters more than the immediate incentive.

No serious account of human agency can erase these events.

The more modest claim is enough: access to our own causal process is partial.

That means “I know why” should sometimes be spoken with less confidence than it is.

Artificial intelligence makes this psychologically interesting because one of the most unnerving features of language models is their capacity to produce explanations that are fluent, specific, and wrong.

A model can give a reason for an answer that does not faithfully represent the mechanism that generated the answer. Researchers have long debated how to interpret model explanations, chain-of-thought traces, post hoc rationales, saliency methods, and other attempts to make machine decisions legible. The technical details vary across systems, and we should not pretend the human analogy solves them.

The analogy is useful in the opposite direction.

Machine confabulation has made people notice how persuasive an explanation can be even when explanation and mechanism come apart.

We knew this about humans first.

The word “confabulation” did not originate with chatbots. In clinical contexts it has been used for false or distorted memories produced without an ordinary intention to deceive. The term has since traveled into broader discussions of explanation and machine behavior. That migration can mislead if it encourages us to treat minds and models as the same kind of system.

But it carries one useful warning across the border.

Fluency is not access.

A beautiful explanation is still an explanation.

It may need evidence.

This creates an awkward problem because social life depends on asking people why.

Why were you late?

Why did you approve the loan?

Why did you reject the candidate?

Why did you stop calling?

Why did you prescribe that medication?

Why did you believe the rumor?

Why did you choose this design?

Why did you hit him?

Why did you leave?

We cannot abandon reasons simply because introspection is imperfect. Reasons are how institutions distinguish accidents from intentions, negligence from reasonable care, discrimination from legitimate criteria, strategy from luck, and promises from noise. Relationships need explanations too. “I don’t know why” can be honest, but a person who never answers for motives becomes impossible to trust.

The solution is not to stop asking why.

It is to stop treating the first answer as a privileged transcript from inside the machine.

A reason can be tested against behavior.

If I say family is my highest priority and repeatedly make decisions that place work first, the contradiction tells us something. It does not prove the stated value is fake. It may reveal conflict, constraint, fear, habit, incentives, or a gap between aspiration and action.

A reason can be tested against timing.

Did I hold the principle before the decision, or did it become prominent after the outcome made the principle convenient?

A reason can be tested against counterfactuals.

If the same facts pointed toward the opposite result, would I still invoke the rule?

A reason can be tested against other people’s observations.

Friends, colleagues, partners, children, and adversaries often see patterns we do not. Their interpretations are not automatically superior. They have biases too. But self-knowledge does not improve by declaring the self the only admissible witness.

A reason can be tested against records.

Messages, calendars, drafts, financial statements, logs, and earlier statements can reveal what mattered at the time before memory rearranged the order.

This is why keeping a journal can be so disturbing.

The page preserves a version of you that current memory has already edited.

You read an old entry and discover that the reason you now regard as obvious was barely mentioned. You were worried about something else. You misunderstood the person. You were more confident than you remember. You were less confident. A later story has moved an element from background to foreground because hindsight knows which details became important.

The old page is not necessarily more truthful than the current memory. It captures one moment with its own blind spots. But it breaks the monopoly of the present narrator.

External memory creates cross-examination.

AI may dramatically increase that possibility.

Imagine an assistant that has access, with permission, to years of your notes, messages, calendar events, purchases, work products, and prior conversations. You ask why you changed careers. The system can retrieve the salary frustration you have forgotten, the message from a friend that introduced you to a new field, the months in which your calendar filled with one kind of work while your stated goals pointed elsewhere, the draft resignation letter you never sent, the meeting after which your searches changed.

Would it know why?

Not in any simple sense.

It would have evidence.

That evidence could improve your story or bully you with false precision.

A machine might surface patterns you overlooked. It might also overinterpret correlation, miss private experiences never recorded, treat absence of data as absence of motive, or privilege whatever left the most digital trace. The more comprehensive the archive feels, the easier it becomes to confuse documentation with causation.

The assistant would be another witness.

A powerful one.

Not the final one.

This distinction is going to become important as people increasingly use models not just to answer questions about the world but to answer questions about themselves.

What career fits me?

Why am I unhappy?

What do I actually care about?

What pattern do you see in my relationships?

What am I avoiding?

These can be useful prompts because language helps people think. A model can notice recurring words, contradictions, timelines, and patterns across more text than a person can hold in working memory. It can suggest hypotheses that make a conversation with oneself more productive.

The risk appears when suggestion becomes revelation.

A machine produces a crisp story.

The story explains everything.

The person recognizes pieces of themselves in it.

Recognition feels like truth.

This is not a new vulnerability. Horoscopes, personality tests, management typologies, pop psychology, political identities, and diagnostic labels have all offered people compressed stories in which they can recognize themselves. Some are grounded more seriously than others. All can become overcomplete if the label starts deciding what evidence means before the person examines it.

The self likes a theory.

A theory makes yesterday explain today.

Theories reduce noise. They make contradictions manageable. They let us answer “Why did you do that?” without reconstructing every influence from scratch.

The trouble is that a good self-theory is supposed to help us see.

A bad one helps us stop looking.

“I am just the kind of person who…” is one of the most efficient ways to convert a pattern into a destiny.

I am just bad with money.

I am not a morning person.

I always ruin relationships.

I am the responsible one.

I do not do conflict.

I am not creative.

I am an entrepreneur.

I am a scientist.

I am a conservative.

I am a progressive.

I am the person who stays.

A label can organize behavior so effectively that behavior begins supplying the evidence the label needs.

Again, this does not make identity unreal. Some stable traits and commitments matter deeply. The error is treating the explanation as if it were upstream from every action simply because it can be applied afterward.

There is another function of reasons that introspection research can make us underrate.

Reasons are not only reports about causes.

They are also proposals for how an action should be understood.

When a person says, “I left because the work had become unethical,” the sentence may be partly historical and partly normative. It identifies a cause, but it also says which feature of the event the person is willing to stand behind. The explanation enters the future. It creates a standard against which later actions can be judged.

This is why changing your reason can change more than your autobiography.

Suppose I first explain a resignation as ambition: I wanted a larger role. Later, after more reflection, I decide the deeper issue was that I no longer wanted to participate in a harmful practice. The new explanation does not merely redescribe the past. It may commit me to behaving differently when the same conflict appears again.

A reason can become a rule.

That is socially important because people often cannot inspect another person’s motives directly. We listen to explanations partly to predict what the person will do next.

Why did you break the promise?

If the answer is, “I forgot,” we expect one kind of repair.

If the answer is, “I decided the promise no longer mattered,” we expect another.

If the answer is, “I was afraid to tell you the truth,” the future obligation may include honesty under pressure.

The explanation earns meaning by what it changes downstream.

This gives us a way to distinguish two kinds of bad explanation that otherwise look similar.

One is inaccurate self-knowledge.

The person sincerely believes status did not matter when it did.

The other is strategic excuse-making.

The person chooses whichever explanation reduces immediate cost without accepting the implication of the explanation later.

Both can produce fluent reasons.

Only the second may involve ordinary deception.

The difference matters because a culture that treats every mistaken self-explanation as a lie will make people defend the first story longer than they should. Admitting, “I think I was wrong about why I did that,” becomes dangerous if revision itself is treated as proof of bad faith.

We need room for explanations to improve.

Institutions need that room too.

A company may discover that the rationale it gave for a policy does not survive evidence. A regulator may learn that a rule created an unintended burden. A model developer may discover that the explanation offered for a system’s performance was too simple. A team may realize that what it called a technical decision was partly a commercial one.

The mature response is not to pretend the original rationale was never given.

It is to preserve the history of revision.

We believed X.

Evidence Y changed the picture.

We now believe Z.

Here is what changes because of that.

This is reason-giving as accountable revision rather than image management.

Generative systems can either help or damage this practice.

They can help by forcing a person to compare multiple explanations, surface counterevidence, reconstruct timelines, and ask what facts would distinguish one story from another.

They can damage it by making a plausible rationale available instantly for whatever decision has already been made.

The danger is not merely hallucination.

It is justification on demand.

An organization chooses the cheaper option and asks a model to produce a principled explanation.

A manager dislikes a candidate and asks for a professional rationale.

A person wants to send an angry message and asks the system to make the anger sound reasonable.

The resulting language can be coherent, tactful, and detached from the actual process.

The machine becomes a laundering service for motive.

Again, people could do this before.

The machine reduces the cost and increases the polish.

That changes the discipline we need.

Do not ask only, “Is this explanation plausible?”

Ask, “What evidence would make this explanation less plausible?”

Ask, “Was this reason present before the decision?”

Ask, “Would we accept the same rule if it pointed toward the opposite outcome?”

Ask, “What part of this account is observation, what part is inference, and what part is a commitment about the future?”

These questions do not produce perfect access to causes.

They make explanations more accountable to something outside their own fluency.

One of the quietest forms of freedom is letting a reason become provisional.

I think I did it because…

That sentence feels weaker than “I know why.”

It is often stronger.

It creates room for evidence.

The qualification is not an escape from responsibility. In some cases, confidence about motive is morally convenient precisely because it hides a less flattering cause. The manager who says a candidate “lacked polish” may need to ask why the same behavior read as confident in someone else. The investor who says a decision was based on fundamentals may need to examine the social pressure in the room. The parent who says a rule is “for safety” may need to ask how much of it is fear, control, or reputation.

Uncertainty can increase accountability because it keeps inquiry open.

This is the opposite of how uncertainty is often used rhetorically. People sometimes claim not to know enough in order to avoid judgment. That is real too. There is no magic virtue in hedging.

The goal is calibration.

Know what kind of access you have.

I know that I felt afraid.

I know that I considered the money.

I know that the argument with my colleague mattered.

I suspect status mattered more than I admitted.

I had not noticed how strongly the default shaped the decision until later.

I cannot reconstruct the order now.

The explanation becomes less elegant.

It becomes more useful.

Compare that with how organizations explain decisions.

A company launches a product and succeeds. The retrospective identifies the insight, the strategy, the disciplined execution. Another company makes a similar decision and fails. The retrospective identifies overconfidence, weak validation, poor timing. Outcome knowledge edits causation. What looks like strategy after success can look like recklessness after failure.

This is not merely corporate vanity. Humans are narrative animals living in time. Once the ending is known, earlier events become easier to organize around it.

The reason “why” is so difficult is that causation rarely respects the borders of the self-story.

A job change can be caused by money, boredom, a manager, a partner’s relocation, a chance conversation, an economic cycle, a childhood image of success, a bad week, and a recruiter’s email. Which one is *the* reason?

The demand for singularity may be the problem.

We like causes shaped like protagonists.

Reality often gives us committees.

The person still has to sign the decision.

That is where responsibility returns.

A partial explanation does not make the act belong to nobody. The fact that I cannot inspect every causal influence does not mean the choice floats free of me. It means ownership and explanation are different jobs.

“I chose this” attaches the act to the person.

“I know why” attempts to describe the process.

The first can remain meaningful when the second is uncertain.

That distinction becomes essential in law, ethics, relationships, and AI governance because we will increasingly face systems that can act reliably without producing explanations that map neatly onto their internal operation. We should not respond by pretending human explanations are transparent and machine explanations uniquely opaque.

Both require evidence appropriate to the system.

Humans have one advantage that matters socially even when introspection fails.

We can revise the explanation.

Not just update a parameter. Revise the story in relation to responsibility.

I said I left because the opportunity was better. Years later I can admit that I was frightened.

I said I stayed because I was loyal. Later I can see how much inertia mattered.

I said I bought it because the quality was superior. Later I can laugh at the status signal I was paying for.

I said I did not care. Later I can admit I cared desperately.

The revision does not recover a perfect hidden truth.

It changes the relationship between the person and the event.

Sometimes that is what growing up looks like: not discovering the one true reason that was always there, but becoming capable of a less flattering and more adequate account.

The story “I know why” protects coherence.

The better story is occasionally, “I know some of why.”

There is another story even harder to loosen because it carries evidence that feels vivid, private, and first-hand.

I remember.
