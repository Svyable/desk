# I’m Not a Robot

There is a sentence the internet has made billions of people say without speaking.

I’m not a robot.

Usually there is a box beside it. You click. Sometimes a green check appears and you are admitted. Sometimes the machine remains unconvinced and sends you to a grid of photographs: traffic lights, bicycles, buses, crosswalks, motorcycles, stairs. You make a case for your humanity by identifying pieces of the physical world. The machine grades you.

The exchange is so ordinary that the absurdity has worn off.

A computer has asked you to prove you are a person. You answer by doing cognitive work that, at least historically, computers found difficult. Earlier versions asked people to read mangled letters because humans could still read things optical character recognition could not. reCAPTCHA turned some of that effort toward digitizing printed material. Later image puzzles drew on a different gap between human and machine perception. Then the checkbox arrived, and for many users even the puzzle disappeared. The visible test became almost ceremonial. Behind it sat risk analysis, behavior, context, and everything else the system could use to decide whether the person claiming not to be a robot behaved sufficiently unlike one.

This is not a book about CAPTCHA.

It is a book about the sentence.

“I’m not a robot” sounds like an obvious fact. It is also a terrible definition of a human being.

For a long time we could get away with definitions like that because the machines were easy to distinguish from us. They were fast where we were slow, literal where we were flexible, obedient where we were improvisational, computational where we were emotional. A calculator did not make anyone wonder whether arithmetic was the core of personhood. A spreadsheet did not cause a crisis of human identity. Even a chess engine, once it became better than almost every person who had ever lived, could be put safely in a box called chess. We had other rooms to retreat into.

Language made the retreat more interesting.

So did images. Music. Code. Voice. Translation. Search. Planning. Explanation. The machine kept entering rooms we had treated as ours, and the cultural response often followed the same pattern: move the border. We are not the thing the machine just learned to do. We are the next thing.

That game can continue for a long time. It may even be useful. But as a theory of humanity it has a defect: it lets the machine write the definition. Whatever the machine cannot do today becomes sacred, and whatever it learns tomorrow quietly loses its status.

A person deserves a sturdier account of personhood than a moving list of benchmark failures.

The trouble is that the obvious alternatives are not as clean as we might like.

People run scripts.

We wake to alarms, make coffee in familiar sequences, take the same route, use phrases we did not invent, follow norms we did not write, trust defaults we did not choose, and repeat actions until the repetition becomes easier than deliberation. We outsource memory to calendars and phones. We borrow judgment from institutions. We mistake familiarity for taste. We tell ourselves reasons after choices. We reconstruct memories each time we revisit them. We absorb the vocabulary of a profession until its sentences arrive before we have consciously planned them. We become, in a thousand small ways, predictable.

None of this makes a person a machine.

It does make “humans choose, machines execute” too simple to survive contact with an ordinary Tuesday.

The title of this book is therefore both a claim and a joke I am not sure I entirely trust. *I’m Not a Robot: And Other Stories I Tell Myself.* The other stories are familiar. I chose this. I know why. I remember what happened. This is my taste. I am consistent. I am paying attention. I mean what I say. This is my work. I did it myself. I am in control.

Each contains something true. Each can also become a flattering summary pasted over a messier process.

The point is not to expose human beings as frauds. A self is not a deposition. It does not become illegitimate because some of its explanations arrive late. Stories are part of how people maintain continuity across years that otherwise contain incompatible motives, forgotten causes, revised memories, accidental encounters, and decisions made under conditions no longer visible. A life without compression would be unlivable. We need a way to say, “I did this,” even though the “I” who did it was influenced by sleep, stress, habit, architecture, recommendation systems, friends, parents, institutions, luck, language, and a thousand other things we cannot reconstruct afterward.

The compression becomes dangerous when we confuse it with completeness.

Artificial intelligence makes that confusion harder to sustain because it behaves like a rude comparison device. It does not merely automate tasks. It throws human habits into relief. A language model produces fluent sentences without having a childhood like ours, and suddenly we have to ask how much of ordinary fluency depended on conscious sentence-by-sentence invention in the first place. A recommender predicts what we will click, and we have to ask where preference ends and exposure begins. An image generator remixes a vast training history into a new picture, and we have to ask how often human originality has also involved recombination, apprenticeship, imitation, genre, and memory. A model confabulates, and the word is interesting partly because people were using it about people long before machines gave us a fresh reason to notice it.

We should be careful with every analogy in that paragraph. A statistical model is not a human mind in miniature. A habit is not software. A neuron is not a transistor with poetry in it. Metaphors that collapse unlike systems are seductive precisely because they are easy.

This book is interested in a narrower comparison.

What happens to our stories about ourselves when a machine becomes good enough to make the stories visible?

The answer I have arrived at is not that humans are secretly robots. It is almost the opposite. The most human thing may not be the absence of automaticity but our unstable relationship with it.

We automate because we have to. A person who consciously reconsidered every step of brushing their teeth would never leave the bathroom. Habit is not a moral failure. Language depends on conventions nobody reinvents. Civilization depends on procedures that outlive the mood of the person executing them. Expertise itself is partly the conversion of laborious thought into patterns that can run quickly and reliably.

But people can also notice the pattern.

Sometimes too late. Sometimes because another person points it out. Sometimes because the pattern causes pain. Sometimes because a machine predicts us so accurately that prediction feels like an insult. Sometimes because we hear ourselves say the same sentence for the hundredth time and, for once, do not believe it.

Then something unusual becomes possible. We can interrupt.

Not perfectly. Not from some imaginary place outside causation. We interrupt using other habits, other stories, other people, other tools. We replace one script with another. We make promises because future versions of ourselves are unreliable. We change environments because willpower is inconsistent. We write things down because memory is reconstructive. We ask friends because introspection is partial. We build institutions because individual judgment fails. We use machines because humans are limited, and we refuse machines because limits can be protective too.

The capacity I am interested in is not pure freedom. It is revision under responsibility.

A machine can be updated. A person can revise. The words sound similar and name different kinds of event. Revision includes the strange human burden of having to answer for an earlier version of yourself you no longer fully recognize. It includes apology, regret, reinterpretation, commitment, forgiveness, denial, rationalization, and the possibility of doing something different while remaining the same legal and social person who did the old thing. Human continuity is not merely persistence of state. It is negotiated meaning across time.

That is why the stories matter.

“I’m not a robot” is one of them. It is reassuring because it seems to put the difficult question behind us. The checkbox is binary. Human or robot. Pass or fail. Green check or another round of traffic lights.

A life is not binary in that way.

The chapters ahead begin with the machines that asked us to prove ourselves and then leave them behind for long stretches. They move through choice, explanation, memory, taste, consistency, attention, language, work, control, reputation, and the emerging market for things that feel more valuable because a human being verifiably did them. The final chapters return to the pressure that began the book: what happens when institutions, employers, schools, platforms, customers, and governments all want stronger proof that a human is really there?

We may discover that proving humanness too aggressively can make human life worse.

A biometric scan can verify a body while excluding people whose bodies or documents do not fit the system. A detector can accuse a student of using a machine when the student did not. A workplace can demand constant behavioral evidence that a remote employee is active, and turn a human being into a stream of measurable events. An artist can be pressured to perform visible imperfection because polished work is now suspicious. A person can spend so much time demonstrating that they are not automated that their day becomes more scripted than before.

This is where the joke in the title stops being cute.

If we define humanity as whatever machines cannot do, then every technical advance shrinks us. If we define humanity as whatever can pass an institution’s test for humanness, then the test begins to govern the person.

There is another option.

We can stop treating “not a robot” as the answer.

We can treat it as the question that opens the door.
