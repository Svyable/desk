# I Am Consistent

Consistency is one of the virtues that becomes suspicious only after you get too much of it.

We want the people around us to be consistent enough to trust.

The friend who says they will come should usually come. The employer who promises a salary should pay it. The bridge should meet the same engineering standard on Tuesday that it met on Monday. The parent should not reinvent every rule according to mood. The judge should not make two opposite decisions merely because lunch intervened.

A stable life depends on repeated expectations.

The self depends on them too.

If I believe something today and its opposite tomorrow, want one life in the morning and another by evening, make promises that vanish when circumstances change, and treat every previous action as belonging to somebody else, then “I” begins to lose practical meaning.

Continuity is one of the things a person owes other people.

Then continuity hardens.

I have always been like this.

I am the kind of person who does not quit.

I am not political.

I hate exercise.

I never ask for help.

I am terrible with money.

I do not forgive betrayal.

I am a founder.

I am an academic.

I am a New Yorker.

I am a person who keeps my word.

Identity makes behavior legible by linking individual acts to a pattern. The pattern can guide us before a decision and explain us afterward. It can reduce conflict. If I know I do not drink, a thousand small choices disappear. If I know I am a physician, professional obligations narrow what I will do in a clinical setting. If I know I am a parent, the identity carries duties that do not require fresh justification each time.

The same compression that gives life coherence can turn old behavior into instructions.

This is where consistency begins to behave like a script.

Daryl Bem’s self-perception theory proposed, in broad form, that people sometimes infer their own attitudes from observations of their behavior and the circumstances in which it occurs, much as an external observer might. This does not mean people have no inner experience. It means behavior can become evidence to the self.

I keep doing this.

Therefore I must be the kind of person who does this.

The inference is often useful.

It can also be circular.

I never speak in meetings because I am shy.

How do I know I am shy?

I never speak in meetings.

The behavior proves the identity. The identity predicts the behavior. The loop closes.

A recommendation system can make the loop tighter.

You watched three documentaries about architecture.

The system shows five more.

You watch two.

The system becomes more confident that architecture belongs to your profile.

Soon the home screen contains architecture often enough that your viewing history confirms what the home screen already believed.

Nothing sinister has happened. You may truly love architecture.

The system has simply turned repeated behavior into an increasingly stable model of you.

People do the same thing to one another.

The funny friend gets more opportunities to make jokes.

The organized person gets asked to organize.

The calm sibling gets assigned emotional stability during family crises.

The unreliable colleague stops receiving tasks that would allow reliability to be demonstrated.

The child who is “good at math” gets more encouragement in math, practices more, succeeds more, and supplies evidence for the original label.

Identity is social recommendation.

We show one another more of the roles we have already performed well.

Sometimes this is how talent develops.

Sometimes it is how possibility narrows.

The person who wants to change has to fight not only habit but prediction.

This is why a genuinely new behavior can feel socially expensive. The person has to violate other people’s models.

The employee known for saying yes starts saying no.

The quiet friend becomes confrontational.

The hard-driving executive wants a slower life.

The child who built a family identity around achievement admits exhaustion.

The political partisan changes a position.

The person who “never quits” leaves.

Observers experience the change not only as new information but as inconsistency.

What happened to you?

The question can be affectionate, suspicious, or accusatory.

It reveals an expectation that identity should make the future predictable from the past.

Trust requires some of that.

Growth requires an exception.

The difficult work is deciding which inconsistency is betrayal and which is learning.

A person who promises fidelity and secretly breaks the promise cannot defend themselves by announcing that humans change. A company cannot void yesterday’s warranty because identity is fluid. A public official cannot reverse a position for convenience and insist that consistency is overrated.

Revision is not a universal excuse.

The ability to change becomes morally meaningful partly because continuity remains.

You are still the person who made the promise.

That is the strange arrangement.

We allow people to become different without allowing them to become somebody else for purposes of responsibility.

A person can say, “I no longer believe what I believed when I wrote that,” and both halves of the sentence matter.

No longer believe.

I wrote that.

Human identity holds revision and ownership together.

This is one reason cognitive dissonance became such a durable idea in psychology and popular culture. Leon Festinger’s theory, developed in the 1950s, described the discomfort that can arise from inconsistency among cognitions, attitudes, and behavior, and the ways people may reduce that discomfort. The details and boundaries of dissonance research have been debated and refined for decades, but the ordinary phenomenon is recognizable.

Contradiction itches.

I think of myself as honest, but I lied.

I think of myself as environmentally responsible, but I fly constantly.

I think this product is overpriced, but I bought it.

I think the organization is unethical, but I still work there.

I think I am independent, but I care deeply about approval.

Something has to move.

Behavior can change.

Belief can change.

The importance of the inconsistency can be minimized.

A new justification can be found.

Context can absorb the conflict.

Sometimes this is hypocrisy.

Sometimes it is psychological repair.

Sometimes it is the beginning of genuine learning.

The mistake is assuming consistency always points toward truth.

A perfectly consistent person could simply be excellent at protecting an old error.

Science institutionalizes inconsistency for this reason. A result is supposed to be vulnerable to evidence. A researcher who changes a conclusion after better data is not failing the identity of scientist. The revision may be the identity functioning correctly.

Good engineering works similarly. The design changes after testing. A safety procedure changes after an incident. A forecast changes after new information.

We admire revision in systems whose purpose is accuracy.

We often punish it in people whose purpose is social identity.

Politics makes this vivid. A public figure changes a position and opponents produce old footage. Sometimes the footage reveals opportunism. Sometimes it reveals learning. The existence of contradiction alone cannot tell us which.

The old statement matters.

So does the path between old and new.

A credible revision has a history.

What changed?

What evidence arrived?

What cost did the person accept by changing?

What remains continuous underneath the revision?

Those questions are more demanding than simply checking whether two statements match.

They are also more human.

Consistency checking is easy to automate.

A system with access to years of writing can find contradictions at a scale no reader could. It can compare a politician’s speeches, a company’s promises, a scholar’s claims, a user’s preferences, or an employee’s stated goals across time.

This will be useful.

It will also create a new temptation to confuse contradiction with guilt.

Imagine a model that can instantly retrieve everything you have said publicly since adolescence.

At twenty-seven, you explain a changed position.

The system responds with a post from age sixteen.

At forty, you describe your career goals.

It surfaces the manifesto you wrote at twenty-one.

At sixty, you try to tell a family story and the archive corrects the date, the location, and a message you sent afterward.

The record becomes a permanent cross-examiner.

That can restrain convenient rewriting.

It can also make development look like inconsistency because the archive lacks a theory of permission to change.

A humane society needs both memory and expiration.

Not expiration of accountability for serious harm merely because time passed. Expiration of the expectation that every old preference, phrase, pose, mistake, and aspiration remains equally representative of the current person.

The internet has poor instincts about this because storage is cheap and retrieval is becoming intelligent.

Before searchable archives, inconsistency often disappeared naturally. Friends remembered the broad change. The embarrassing speech was lost. The bad haircut survived only in one photograph nobody could find. The self had room to become new partly because the old self was expensive to retrieve.

Now retrieval approaches zero cost.

Artificial intelligence makes semantic retrieval easier than keyword search. You may not need to remember the phrase. Ask for every time this person expressed skepticism about a topic, and a system can assemble the evidence.

This changes reputation.

It may change selfhood.

When the past is perfectly searchable, revision requires stronger cultural norms than forgetting once supplied automatically.

We will need to learn the difference between receipts and shackles.

The same issue appears in personalization.

A profile is a frozen compromise between continuity and update.

Too little memory and the system is irritating. It forgets preferences you have stated repeatedly, asks questions already answered, and treats every interaction as the first.

Too much memory and the system becomes suffocating. It keeps offering the restaurant you stopped liking, remembers the job you left as though it remains your identity, optimizes toward goals you abandoned, and interprets every new preference as noise against a long history.

A good personal system needs a theory of change.

It needs to know that old evidence can decay.

It needs explicit overrides.

It needs ways for a user to say: that was me, and it is no longer the instruction.

The phrase sounds simple.

That was me.

It is no longer the instruction.

I think it may be one of the most important sentences for human-machine systems.

A machine can store continuity more faithfully than a person. It therefore needs deliberate mechanisms for revision that human memory sometimes supplies by accident.

Forget this preference.

Do not infer from that period.

Treat this as an old goal.

Ask before using this history.

Let me reset the recommendation profile.

Mark this as a mistake rather than a trait.

These are not mere settings.

They are controls over whether the predictive past gets to govern the present.

People need similar controls in relationships, though the interface is worse.

A partner can keep responding to the person you were five years ago.

A parent can keep speaking to the adolescent version of an adult child.

An organization can promote someone based on a reputation that no longer matches current skill.

A friend group can preserve roles long after they become painful.

Changing a life often requires changing other people’s caches.

That is slow work.

You have to behave differently long enough that the model updates.

This is one reason change can feel fake at first.

The new behavior has less evidence behind it.

If I have avoided exercise for fifteen years and go to the gym twice, the identity “I am a person who exercises” sounds aspirational to the point of comedy. The old model has more data.

Yet every stable pattern begins with an outlier.

A person trying to change has to grant unusual moral weight to weak new evidence.

This is almost the opposite of statistical prediction.

The predictor says: given the history, tomorrow will probably resemble yesterday.

The person says: perhaps, but I am trying to make tomorrow the exception from which a different history begins.

The predictor is often right.

The person has to act anyway.

This is where the comparison with machines becomes more interesting than the usual benchmark contest. A predictive system is rewarded for exploiting continuity. A person sometimes has to value the possibility of discontinuity even when probability is against it.

Not because people can magically escape causation.

Because people can adopt goals that make past behavior an obstacle rather than a target.

I do not want the system to predict what I usually buy.

I want it to help me spend less.

I do not want it to maximize the videos I am likely to watch.

I want it to help me stop at ten o’clock.

I do not want it to give me more of the news that feels gratifying.

I want it to tell me when my information diet has narrowed.

I do not want it to write exactly like I usually write.

I want it to challenge the habit in my prose that has become stale.

The objective changes the meaning of personalization.

A mirror preserves consistency.

A coach sometimes interrupts it.

A good tool may need to know which role it is playing.

The same is true of self-story.

“I am consistent” is useful when it means my commitments survive mood.

It becomes dangerous when it means my past gets veto power over revision.

A person should be able to say, without contradiction:

I am still responsible for the person I was.

I am not required to remain that person.

That is not perfect consistency.

It is continuity with edit permission.

The edit permission does not erase history. It makes history something other than a script.

Which brings us to a resource every script competes for.

Attention.

We like to imagine that what we are doing is what we are attending to. Work dashboards, classroom rules, meeting norms, and our own self-assessments often rely on that assumption.

The mind is less cooperative.

I am paying attention.
