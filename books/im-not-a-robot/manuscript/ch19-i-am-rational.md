# I Am Rational

There is a way of describing a supermarket that makes every shopper sound like an economist.

The person enters with preferences, constraints, information, and a budget. They compare available goods, estimate value, select a basket, and leave having maximized something.

This description is not useless.

It is also not what Tuesday feels like.

Tuesday feels like being hungry, remembering there is no coffee, seeing something on sale, buying the cereal a child will actually eat, avoiding the aisle where you always spend too much, choosing the familiar brand because there are forty kinds of toothpaste and you have a life to get back to, and discovering at home that the one thing you entered the store for never made it into the cart.

Human beings can reason.

We do not reason from scratch about everything.

I am rational is another story we tell because it compresses a complicated relationship with reasons into a flattering identity.

The machine age makes this story interesting in two opposite ways.

Machines can appear more rational than we are because they are consistent, statistical, fast, and comparatively immune to some human distractions.

Machines can also be profoundly irrational relative to human purposes because optimization is only as good as the objective, data, assumptions, and constraints that define what counts as success.

A system can be perfectly efficient in the wrong direction.

Humans know this because we do it too.

One of the most durable contributions of twentieth-century decision theory was to make peace with the fact that actual people do not solve every choice as an ideal optimizer. Herbert Simon used the language of bounded rationality to describe decision-making under limits of information, time, and computational capacity. The concept is easy to trivialize now because the limits sound obvious. They were important precisely because many elegant models become strange when imported too literally into lives lived under those limits.

A person does not evaluate every possible restaurant before dinner.

They stop when they find one that seems good enough.

They do not research every career before choosing one.

They encounter a small subset of careers through family, geography, education, class, luck, and timing, then make a consequential decision from the local menu.

They do not calculate every risk. They use rules.

They do not inspect every belief. They trust sources.

They do not derive morality from axioms each morning. They inherit norms, revise some, violate some, and call a friend when the situation no longer fits the rule.

This is not necessarily irrationality.

It is rationality performed by an organism that must also sleep.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman’s work on heuristics and biases later showed, among many other things, how mental shortcuts that are often useful can produce systematic errors under certain conditions. Representativeness, availability, and anchoring became part of the vocabulary through which generations of researchers, managers, investors, physicians, policymakers, and airport-bookstore readers learned to distrust first impressions.

The popular lesson was often: humans are irrational.

The research is more interesting than the slogan.

A heuristic is not a defect simply because it is not exhaustive computation.

Imagine a person who refused to use any shortcut until they had analyzed every possibility. They would become a monument to rationality before choosing breakfast.

A useful mind needs compression.

This book has been arguing the same point from another direction. Habits compress action. Stories compress identity. Preferences compress prior exposure. Reputation compresses history. A reason compresses a causal web. Rules compress repeated judgment.

Rationality also depends on compression.

The question is whether the compression survives the situation in which it is used.

A rule such as “trust the familiar brand” may be perfectly sensible when the cost of analysis exceeds the difference among products. The same rule becomes dangerous when familiarity is being deliberately manipulated. “Follow the expert” can be rational under uncertainty and disastrous when incentives are misaligned. “Use the model” can improve prediction in one domain and launder a bad objective in another.

No shortcut is rational outside context.

There is a higher-order form of rationality hiding here.

Not deciding correctly.

Deciding how much deciding this decision deserves.

Most choices are not worth maximal analysis. Some are.

Spending thirty minutes comparing two brands of paper towels can be less rational than accepting a mediocre default. Spending thirty seconds on a contract that can reshape the next five years can be irrational even if the final choice happens to work out.

The problem is that importance is not always visible before the choice.

A trivial-looking email changes a relationship.

A routine medical symptom turns out not to be routine.

A small permission gives an application access it keeps for years.

A temporary workaround becomes infrastructure.

Rationality therefore includes triage: noticing which questions deserve to escape automatic mode.

This is exactly where machines can help and hurt.

A good system can detect stakes a tired person missed.

A bad system can make a consequential decision feel routine because it presents the same polished interface for everything.

The visual grammar of optimization flattens importance.

One recommendation card can contain a restaurant, a job applicant, a medical alert, a credit decision, or a military target. The interface can make them all feel like rows in a queue.

The human task is partly to refuse that flattening.

This is why the comparison between human and machine rationality is so often poorly framed.

We ask: who is better at deciding?

Deciding what?

Under which objective?

With which information?

At what cost?

Who absorbs the errors?

Can the decision be appealed?

What counts as a good outcome ten minutes later, and what counts ten years later?

The machine may dominate a human on the narrow prediction and still fail the larger decision.

Suppose a model predicts which customer is most likely to click a notification.

It can be extremely accurate.

If the organization’s real goal is long-term trust, increasing clicks may be an imperfect proxy. The model is not irrational for optimizing the proxy. It is doing exactly what the system rewarded.

The irrationality, if there is any, may belong to the organization that forgot the proxy was a stand-in.

This is one of the odd ways humans can hide from responsibility behind machine rationality.

The score made us do it.

The model says this candidate is lower probability.

The optimizer found the cheapest schedule.

The system recommends this treatment.

The dashboard says engagement is up.

Numbers create distance between a decision and the values inside it.

But values enter before the number.

Which outcome was measured?

Whose errors were expensive?

Which tradeoff was encoded?

What data counted as relevant?

Who was excluded from the training set?

How much false-positive risk was tolerated?

At what threshold did a recommendation become an action?

These are human choices even when no single person made them consciously.

Some emerge from procurement, regulation, software defaults, historical practice, budget constraints, or the habits of a profession. The system can therefore become a machine for hardening old decisions into new objectivity.

That is not a reason to reject systems.

It is a reason to put values back into the description.

A mortgage model is not simply accurate. It is accurate about an outcome under a definition of loss, a time horizon, a dataset, and legal constraints.

A medical model is not simply good. It has sensitivity, specificity, calibration, population assumptions, and consequences associated with different errors.

A recommender is not simply intelligent. It is recommending under a target.

There is another value choice hidden inside error itself.

Two systems can have the same overall accuracy and create very different worlds.

One may tolerate more false positives.

Another may tolerate more false negatives.

The difference is not merely statistical.

It decides who carries uncertainty.

A fraud system that blocks more legitimate customers may reduce one class of loss by making ordinary people absorb more suspicion. A medical screening system can be tuned to catch more cases at the cost of more unnecessary follow-up. A moderation system can remove more harmful content while also removing more lawful or benign speech. A hiring screen can reject more marginal candidates in order to reduce the number of weak hires.

There is no accuracy number that tells us which burden is morally correct.

The threshold is policy wearing mathematics.

This connects rationality to Chapter 17’s problem of proof.

When uncertainty is unavoidable, rational design is not only about minimizing expected error.

It is about deciding who gets the benefit of doubt.

Who can recover from a mistake.

Who is powerful enough to appeal.

Who experiences the false positive as an inconvenience and who experiences it as a closed door.

A perfectly calibrated system does not relieve an institution of these choices.

Calibration can tell you what a score means.

It cannot tell you what you owe the person at that score.

Humans do something similar when we call ourselves rational.

We rarely mention the target.

A person can be exquisitely rational about becoming rich and destroy a family in the process.

Can be rational about winning an argument and make future cooperation impossible.

Can optimize a career until the career becomes a prison.

Can save time so aggressively that nothing worth doing remains in the saved time.

Can make every decision locally sensible and wake up inside a life they did not mean to construct.

This is the difference between instrumental rationality and the harder question of ends.

Machines make the difference visible because optimization needs a target.

Humans can pretend we do not.

We often infer the target after observing our own behavior.

I value health, we say, while repeatedly choosing convenience.

I value time, while surrendering it to low-stakes attention loops.

I value family, while scheduling everything else first.

I value originality, while rewarding ourselves for being safely recognizable.

The gap does not prove hypocrisy in every case. Values compete. Circumstances constrain. People fail. A life can contain several real goals that cannot all be maximized at once.

This is exactly why a single objective function is such a poor metaphor for human life.

We are multi-objective systems with unstable weights and no central committee that publishes the updates.

The phrase sounds computational. The reality is domestic.

Should I take the promotion?

More money. Less time. Better work. Worse commute. More prestige. Less flexibility. A partner’s career. A child’s school. An aging parent. A mortgage. A fear of stagnation. A fear of ambition. An identity built around competence. An identity built around being available.

There is no spreadsheet cell labeled correct.

A person can make a good decision and regret it.

Can make a bad decision for reasons that were reasonable at the time.

Can choose one value and thereby discover another mattered more.

This is not noise around rationality.

It is what rationality has to operate inside.

Some values do not even behave well as quantities in a tradeoff.

A promise can function as a constraint rather than a preference.

A right can function as a boundary rather than a weight.

A professional duty can rule out an otherwise efficient option.

A person may say: I do not care how attractive the expected value is; this action is not available to me.

Optimization culture finds these statements frustrating because they remove options from comparison.

Human life depends on them.

We already met this in the distinction between preference and commitment. A preference says more of this is better, all else equal. A commitment can say this is not for sale.

That does not make commitments automatically wise. People bind themselves to destructive rules too. Institutions can protect unjust constraints by calling them principles.

But a theory of rationality that recognizes only tradable preferences will misunderstand why people sometimes accept obvious losses rather than violate a line they regard as constitutive.

The rational question is not always “what maximizes?”

Sometimes it is “what remains permitted?”

This matters for machine agents because an optimizer trained to negotiate everything may eventually negotiate the things the person did not mean to put on the table.

The machine becomes useful here not because it eliminates values but because it can separate parts of the problem.

Calculate the financial scenarios.

Model the commute.

Summarize the contract.

List what I said mattered last time.

Show me comparable outcomes.

Challenge my assumptions.

The system can expand the evidence available to a bounded person.

But at some point it will ask, implicitly or explicitly: which outcome should I optimize?

That question is not solved by intelligence alone.

People may increasingly delegate it anyway.

This is the quiet risk of personalized agents. A good agent will learn what usually satisfies us. It will observe patterns, predict preferences, reduce decision fatigue, and make thousands of small choices disappear.

That will feel wonderful.

Decision fatigue is real at the level of experience even where specific psychological claims about it are contested. Nobody needs a theory to know that choosing every trivial thing is exhausting. A capable agent can buy the usual groceries, schedule routine appointments, reject obvious spam, choose familiar travel options, and handle administrative details that currently consume hours.

The person becomes more efficient by becoming less involved.

This is exactly what delegation is for.

The problem begins when the agent moves from implementing preferences to manufacturing them.

The distinction will be hard to see because preference learning is the point.

Suppose the agent learns that you usually choose the cheaper flight even when it leaves earlier. Next time it chooses the cheaper flight automatically. Then it chooses a cheaper hotel. Then a cheaper insurance plan. It is behaving rationally relative to observed history.

But perhaps the history was produced during a period when money was tight.

The agent has inferred a value from a constraint.

Humans make the same mistake about one another constantly.

She always takes the bus, so she must prefer public transit.

He never travels, so he must be a homebody.

They always choose the cheap option, so they care most about price.

Behavior is evidence. It is not a complete declaration of values.

A rational agent therefore needs a concept of uncertainty about the person.

Not merely uncertainty about what they will do.

Uncertainty about what they want to keep wanting.

That sounds awkward because prediction systems are typically evaluated on predicting behavior, not on protecting a person’s capacity to revise it.

A system that perfectly predicts my next click may be doing the opposite of what I want if my goal is to stop clicking.

This is why rational self-government sometimes requires acting against prediction.

People install website blockers.

Set spending limits.

Ask friends to intervene.

Choose automatic savings.

Remove alcohol from the house.

Move cities.

Change social circles.

Commit publicly.

Sign contracts.

Join groups with rules.

These actions look like restrictions on choice.

They are strategies for making a different choice more likely later.

A rational person is not simply a person with maximum freedom at every moment.

They may rationally reduce future freedom to protect a value the future self cannot be trusted to honor under pressure.

Machines can help with this if they understand the hierarchy.

The agent should not simply optimize revealed preference.

It should know when a person has explicitly asked to be protected from revealed preference.

Do not let me order this after midnight.

Do not schedule over that day even if the meeting is valuable.

Do not show me engagement-optimized recommendations during work hours.

Do not accept a contract above this risk threshold without asking.

These are rules written by one version of the person for another.

They are rationality made institutional inside the self.

The machine age adds another temptation: reasons themselves become cheap.

A language model can produce a persuasive explanation for almost any plausible choice.

Take the job: growth, income, opportunity.

Do not take the job: family, autonomy, risk.

Move: reinvention, lower costs, new community.

Stay: continuity, support network, hidden switching costs.

The ability to articulate reasons used to be at least weak evidence that somebody had thought through a decision.

It was never strong evidence. Chapter 6 already dismantled that comfort.

Generated explanation weakens it further.

Now a person can make the decision first and obtain an elegant rationale afterward at almost zero cost.

This does not make the rationale false.

It makes fluency even less diagnostic of deliberation.

An institution can do the same thing.

A system produces a recommendation. Another system produces the explanation. The explanation is coherent, individualized, and legally polished. Everyone downstream encounters reasons without encountering the actual process that selected the outcome.

Rationality becomes theater when explanation is optimized separately from decision.

The sentence “we had reasons” is no longer enough.

Were the reasons causally connected to the choice?

Would changing the reason change the outcome?

Could contrary evidence matter?

Was the explanation available before the decision or generated to defend it afterward?

Can somebody name the tradeoff that was accepted?

This is not a demand for perfect transparency into every model or mind. Humans cannot provide that either.

It is a demand that reasons remain capable of doing work rather than merely decorating authority.

The funny thing is that the more we study human irrationality, the less plausible the isolated rational chooser becomes and the more impressive civilization looks.

We built external correction systems because we are not individually reliable.

Double-entry bookkeeping.

Peer review.

Checklists.

Audits.

Appeals.

Independent courts.

Conflict-of-interest rules.

Cooling-off periods.

Clinical trials.

Two-person authorization.

Randomization.

Blind review.

These are not monuments to human rationality.

They are monuments to our awareness that rationality needs scaffolding.

The machine age should extend this tradition rather than replacing it with a fantasy of perfect computation.

A model can be another scaffold.

Ask it for the counterargument.

Ask it to find base rates.

Ask it to expose inconsistencies.

Ask it to simulate consequences.

Ask it to remind you what you promised before the pressure arrived.

But do not let the scaffold quietly become the architect of the values.

That is the boundary to watch.

The machine may be more internally consistent than you.

Consistency is not enough.

A fanatic can be consistent.

A bureaucracy can be consistent.

A badly specified optimizer can be consistent all the way into disaster.

The human advantage, when there is one, is not inconsistency itself. Chapter 9 already rejected the romantic idea that contradiction is proof of humanity.

The valuable capacity is the ability to reconsider the rule under which consistency was being judged.

This may sound like moving the goalposts.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes the goalposts were in the wrong field.

A company optimizes call time and discovers customers are furious.

A school optimizes test performance and discovers students stopped learning what was not tested.

A platform optimizes engagement and discovers that engagement can be purchased with outrage.

A person optimizes productivity and discovers they have become productive at work they no longer believe in.

Rationality includes the possibility of changing the objective when the objective’s consequences reveal what it left out.

Machines can participate in that process. They can detect the unintended consequence. They can propose new objectives. They can even optimize across competing values if those values can be represented.

But somebody has to care that the objective changed.

This is where reason meets responsibility.

A person cannot escape moral judgment by saying the optimizer was rational. The optimizer was rational relative to something.

Who chose the something?

Who kept choosing it after the harm became visible?

Who could have stopped?

The questions are similar to the control questions because rationality without control becomes explanation after the fact.

I followed the recommendation.

I followed the policy.

I followed the metric.

I followed the market.

I followed what people like me usually do.

Every one of these can be a rational reason.

Every one can also become a way to avoid authorship of a decision.

There is no clean escape.

If we ignore systems, we waste accumulated knowledge and make preventable errors.

If we obey systems, we can outsource judgment to objectives we no longer examine.

The adult position is calibration.

Use the machine where its reasoning is better defined than yours.

Use the rule where repeated human improvisation causes error.

Use the expert where the expert has earned authority.

Use the institution where individual incentives need correction.

Then preserve the right to ask what the whole arrangement is for.

That last question will become more important as machines become better at intermediate reasoning.

A system may plan better than you, forecast better than you, remember more than you, search faster than you, negotiate more patiently than you, and compare options with a consistency you cannot maintain.

The temptation will be to infer that it should choose the destination too.

Sometimes it should. If I ask for the fastest route, I want the route planner to choose.

Sometimes the destination is the only part I cannot delegate without changing the meaning of the task.

What kind of parent do I want to be?

Which promise matters more?

Is this work worth doing?

How much risk should another person bear for my gain?

What do I owe somebody who cannot repay me?

When should efficiency yield to dignity?

These questions contain facts and consequences that machines can illuminate.

They also contain commitments that become mine by being lived, not merely selected from a menu.

I am rational is therefore another story we should keep after removing the halo.

You are not rational because you optimize everything.

You are not rational because you can produce a reason.

You are not rational because a model agrees with you.

You are not rational because every value can be converted into one score.

You are rational, when you are, because you can use reasons under constraint, notice when a shortcut no longer fits, distinguish a proxy from a purpose, decide which uncertainties deserve deeper thought, and revise an objective without pretending the old objective was never yours.

That is not perfect rationality.

Perfect rationality was never available.

What we have is something stranger and more useful: minds that build tools to correct themselves, then sometimes need correction from the tools they built.
