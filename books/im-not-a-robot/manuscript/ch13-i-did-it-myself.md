# I Did It Myself

There is a particular kind of pride hidden inside the word *myself*.

A child ties a shoe without help and says it. A person assembles a table from a box and says it. Someone fixes a sink after watching three videos, writes a program after reading documentation, cooks dinner without a recipe, finishes a tax return without calling an accountant, and gets to enjoy the same small sentence.

I did it myself.

The sentence is rarely an inventory.

The child did not manufacture the laces. The table came with milled boards, hardware, instructions, standardized fasteners, and a tool whose dimensions were chosen by someone else. The repair depended on plumbing conventions and probably a person on the internet who had already made the same mistake. The program imported libraries written by strangers. The dinner relied on a stove, refrigeration, agriculture, a knife, a supply chain, and years of other meals. The tax return depended on a legal system so elaborate that nobody involved could truthfully claim to have produced the underlying task by themselves.

None of this makes the sentence ridiculous.

We know what it means.

It means the speaker crossed some boundary of agency that mattered to them. Nobody else tied the shoe. Nobody else stood over the table and made the pieces meet. Nobody else took over the repair at the moment the fitting would not turn. The sentence marks independence relative to the kind of assistance that was in question, not independence from civilization.

This distinction has always been obvious enough that we rarely need to say it. Then artificial intelligence arrives and makes *myself* feel like a forensic term.

Did you write this yourself?

Did you make this yourself?

Did you solve this yourself?

Did you come up with this yourself?

The questions look familiar, but the boundary they are trying to locate has moved. A spellchecker does not normally threaten authorship. Neither does a dictionary, a calculator, a camera’s autofocus, a compiler, a search engine, a recorded lecture, an editor’s margin note, a friend who asks the one useful question, or the memory of a book read ten years ago. Generative systems are harder because they can return something that already resembles the thing we normally count as the work: the paragraph, the image, the code, the melody, the plan.

The tool is no longer merely shaping the hammer. It can hand you something that looks like the cabinet.

So we reach for the old sentence with new urgency.

I did it myself.

The trouble is that the sentence was never precise enough to carry this much weight.

Human beings have always done their best work through systems of distributed competence. We store facts outside our heads, recruit other people’s expertise, inherit standards, copy forms, use instruments, consult examples, and turn difficult operations into reliable procedures. Cognitive scientists use the phrase *cognitive offloading* for one part of this ordinary behavior: changing the environment so that the mind has less to hold or calculate internally. Writing down a phone number is cognitive offloading. Setting a reminder is cognitive offloading. Arranging objects so that the next step is visible can be cognitive offloading. The practice is so basic that describing it as a special technique makes ordinary life briefly look exotic.

A person who refuses all cognitive offloading is not more fully human. They are simply harder to schedule.

Andy Clark and David Chalmers pushed the idea further in their famous argument about the “extended mind.” Their point was philosophical rather than a claim that notebooks literally grow neurons. They asked what happens when an external resource is so reliably integrated into a person’s thinking that treating it as merely outside the cognitive system starts to look arbitrary. Their imagined case involved a notebook serving a function that biological memory serves for somebody else. Whether one accepts the strong version of the argument or not, the example survives because it notices something obvious: thinking has never respected the clean boundary we like to draw around the skull.

The calendar remembers for us. The diagram lets the page hold relationships our working memory would drop. The spreadsheet performs arithmetic while preserving a chain of assumptions. The map changes what it means to know a route. The search box changes what it means to know where information is. A contact list changes what it means to remember a number. A library changes what it means for a society to remember anything at all.

We do not usually call these failures of self-reliance because self-reliance is itself a social technology. It means knowing how to recruit the world without requiring another person to perform the task for you at every step.

The competent person is often the person who knows what not to keep in their head.

This is especially clear in professions that advertise expertise. A surgeon uses imaging, checklists, instruments, anesthesiology, pathology, nursing, scheduling, sterile supply chains, and the accumulated literature of the field. An airline captain uses automated systems, procedures, dispatch, air-traffic control, weather services, and machines whose operation no single pilot could reconstruct from first principles. A lawyer works through statutes, precedents, clerks, databases, templates, opposing counsel, and a court. A scientist works through instruments, software, prior papers, lab protocols, collaborators, reviewers, and statistical methods developed by people they may never meet.

We do not conclude that nobody did anything.

We conclude that agency is organized.

That is the first thing generative AI makes easy to forget. A tool can contribute more without turning all contribution into authorship. A person can remain responsible for a result that they did not personally produce atom by atom. Modern life would collapse if responsibility required solitary manufacture.

The second thing AI makes easy to forget is the opposite: using a tool does not automatically make the result yours in the sense you might want the word *yours* to carry.

There is a difference between directing and receiving.

If I tell a skilled carpenter, “Build me a walnut table, six feet long, with soft edges,” I have commissioned a table. I may have specified it carefully. I may have paid for it. It may fit my house perfectly. I would still sound strange saying I built it myself.

If I design the joinery, choose the proportions, cut the stock, ruin two boards, change the design after the legs rack, ask a carpenter how to fix the problem, use a power planer, and finish the surface with a method learned from a video, the phrase becomes much more natural even though the work remains crowded with other people’s knowledge and industrial tools.

Where did the boundary move?

Not to purity. Not to the number of technologies involved. Not even to the number of hands.

It moved toward judgment that shaped the work and responsibility that remained attached to the outcome.

This is why the debate over AI-assisted creation becomes confused when we treat “human” and “machine” as mutually exclusive ingredients, as though a work contains a measurable percentage of each and the right answer can be found by laboratory assay. Creative work has never been a recipe in which authorship corresponds cleanly to the mass of material contributed by a single source.

A film director may not operate the camera, sew the costumes, compose the score, or speak a line. An architect does not lay every brick. A book’s author may be transformed by an editor who deletes three chapters and asks for another one. A musician can perform a song written by someone else and make a performance unmistakably their own. A chef can cook from inherited techniques with ingredients grown by people they will never know. Open-source software can contain code from thousands of contributors, while a maintainer’s judgment about what enters, what stays out, and what must be rewritten shapes the project’s identity.

Contribution is real. So is selection.

The temptation, once machines can make large contributions, is to pretend selection is trivial. Sometimes it is. Clicking “generate” and keeping the first thing returned may involve almost no meaningful authorship beyond deciding to ask. But selection can also be the core of a discipline. Curators do not paint the paintings. Editors do not necessarily write the sentences. Judges do not create the evidence. Producers do not play every instrument. Their work is partly constituted by choosing among possibilities under constraints and then standing behind the choice.

The difficulty is that generative systems make the supply of possibilities very cheap.

When options are scarce, selection can inherit prestige from scarcity. When a model can produce fifty versions before breakfast, “I picked this one” can sound less like authorship and more like shopping. That may be fair in some cases. It may also cause us to underestimate what serious selection requires when the selector has taste, a purpose, and enough knowledge to reject attractive wrong answers.

Abundance makes judgment easier to hide and harder to fake.

A person without a clear idea can generate forever. Every new output postpones the need to decide what the thing is. The tool becomes a slot machine for avoiding commitment. A person with a clear idea can also generate many options, but the relationship is different: the options are tested against something. Most are discarded. Some reveal a possibility the person had not considered. The final work may contain machine-produced material, but its shape can still be governed by a human standard that existed beyond any single output.

That distinction is not visible in the finished artifact.

Neither, for that matter, is most human process.

We see the photograph, not the hundred frames deleted. We read the chapter, not the notes that failed. We use the interface, not the prototypes. We hear the performance, not the rehearsals. We eat the dish, not the burnt version. Finished work has always concealed the path that produced it. Artificial intelligence increases the number of possible paths and therefore makes provenance more important, but it did not invent hidden process.

The law has had to approach one narrow version of this problem with more precision than ordinary conversation. In its 2025 report on copyrightability and generative AI, the U.S. Copyright Office reaffirmed a human-authorship requirement while also making an important distinction: using AI as an assistive tool does not by itself prevent copyright protection for human-authored expression, and human selection, arrangement, or modification can matter. At the same time, merely giving prompts to a system is not automatically enough to make the resulting expressive details human-authored.

Copyright law is not a complete philosophy of creativity, and the rules differ by jurisdiction. Still, the distinction is useful beyond law because it refuses two lazy answers at once. The presence of a machine does not erase the human. The presence of a human prompt does not magically convert everything a machine returns into human creation.

The interesting territory is control over expressive decisions.

That phrase sounds cleaner than real work is.

Control is often partial. A photographer controls framing and timing but does not control the weather. A novelist controls sentences but not every association that caused a sentence to occur. A jazz musician controls a performance while responding to other musicians in real time. A documentary filmmaker can control editing without controlling what happened in front of the camera. A programmer chooses architecture while relying on libraries whose internal implementation they may not fully understand. A user of a generative system may specify composition, revise outputs, combine fragments, redraw areas, rewrite passages, constrain style, reject errors, and impose a structure the system never supplied on its own.

At some point the phrase “did it myself” stops helping.

We need better verbs.

Directed. Wrote. Edited. Designed. Selected. Commissioned. Programmed. Performed. Curated. Built. Assisted. Generated. Revised.

The verbs are less romantic. They are also more informative.

The cultural prestige of *myself* comes partly from a suspicion that dependence diminishes us. That suspicion has a long history because dependence can be dangerous. A person who cannot perform a basic task without a particular institution, platform, employer, device, or person can become vulnerable to whoever controls the dependency. Self-reliance can be freedom.

But self-reliance achieved by denying dependence is counterfeit freedom.

You do not become independent of the electrical grid by forgetting that the lights require it.

The same is true of cognitive dependence. If a system remembers for you, you should know what happens when it forgets. If a navigation app routes for you, you should retain enough situational awareness to notice when the route becomes absurd. If a model drafts for you, you should know enough about the subject and the purpose to notice when fluent language is wrong. If a recommender shapes your information diet, you should know that your apparent preferences are being fed back into the system that predicts them.

The question is not whether to use external intelligence. Humans have been externalizing intelligence since marks on clay.

The question is whether the external intelligence is legible enough, contestable enough, and replaceable enough for the person using it to remain an agent rather than merely a endpoint.

This returns us to a distinction that runs underneath the entire book: automation is not the opposite of humanity. Unexamined automation can happen inside a person as easily as outside one.

A habit can run you.

A bureaucracy can run you.

A recommendation loop can run you.

A familiar phrase can run you.

A model can run you.

The relevant human capacity is not mystical independence from influence. It is the possibility of noticing what is doing the running.

That possibility is fragile. The better a tool works, the easier it is to stop noticing the tool. A calculator disappears into arithmetic. Autocorrect disappears into spelling. Search disappears into knowledge retrieval. Navigation disappears into movement. When a system becomes infrastructure, competence shifts from performing the old task to understanding when the infrastructure can be trusted and what to do when it cannot.

This shift can feel like loss because sometimes it is loss.

Skills atrophy. Procedures become opaque. People inherit outputs without understanding the path that produced them. Entire occupations can become dependent on systems maintained elsewhere. A pilot who monitors automation is performing a different cognitive job from a pilot who manually controls an aircraft. A physician who reviews a machine recommendation is performing a different task from a physician who generated the recommendation unaided. A writer who edits generated prose is doing different work from a writer who drafts every sentence from a blank page.

Different does not mean equal.

It does not mean inferior either.

We should resist the urge to settle every comparison in advance. Sometimes the old skill should be preserved because failure without it is catastrophic. Sometimes automation removes drudgery and frees attention for the part that actually matters. Sometimes the new supervisory task is harder because it requires maintaining judgment while receiving a steady stream of plausible answers. Sometimes it is easier because the machine is simply doing the difficult part and the person is taking credit.

The only honest answer is local.

What did the person contribute?

What did the tool contribute?

What decisions determined the result?

Who could recognize a bad outcome?

Who had the power to reject it?

Who is answerable now that the work exists?

These questions do something the phrase “I did it myself” cannot. They separate pride from provenance.

Pride is allowed to be emotional. Provenance should be factual.

A person can be proud of assembling a table from a kit. Another can be proud of milling the lumber. Another can be proud of designing the jig that lets thousands of people make the joint safely. The existence of upstream assistance does not cancel downstream accomplishment. But if the first person sells the table as entirely hand-built from raw timber, the problem is no longer philosophical. It is a description problem.

Artificial intelligence will force many fields to become better at description.

Schools will need assignment rules that specify which forms of assistance are part of the learning objective and which bypass it. Employers will need to decide whether they care about process, outcome, confidentiality, attribution, or some combination. Publishers will need disclosure norms that distinguish ordinary assistive uses from substantial generated material without turning every spellcheck into a confession. Artists will need language for hybrid practices that does not collapse into either “human-made” purity theater or “the tool did everything” fatalism.

And individuals will need to decide what kinds of competence they want to keep inside themselves even when outsourcing would be cheaper.

That last choice may become one of the most important luxuries of the next decade.

There are things we learn not because the unaided performance is economically optimal but because doing them changes us. We cook when delivery is easier. We walk when transportation is faster. We memorize poems despite searchable text. We draw despite cameras. We play instruments despite recordings. We write first drafts even when a model can generate one because wrestling with the sentence can be part of discovering the thought.

This is not efficiency failure.

It is cultivation.

A machine can change the cost of an output without changing the value of acquiring the capacity that once produced it.

The confusion comes from treating all tasks as if the only point of the task were the artifact at the end.

If the point of algebra homework is the correct answer, a solver destroys the assignment’s scarcity and improves the student’s productivity. If the point is to build algebraic reasoning, the same solver can remove the exercise that was supposed to produce the capacity. If the point of writing is to transmit a routine update, generated prose may be a gift. If the point is to discover what you think, outsourcing the first encounter with the blank page may change the thought itself.

The same tool can be assistance in one setting and substitution in another.

That is why “AI was used” tells us almost nothing.

So does “AI was not used.”

A person can outsource a paper to another person, copy a template they do not understand, stitch together search results, or repeat a professor’s preferred language without a generative model anywhere in sight. Human provenance does not guarantee human engagement. Conversely, a person can use a model aggressively while remaining deeply engaged in the intellectual work—testing claims, rejecting structures, supplying evidence, rewriting arguments, and assuming responsibility for every sentence that survives.

The medium does not settle the moral question.

The structure of agency does.

This book began with a machine asking a person to certify a binary identity. Human or robot. The chapters since have made that binary less useful each time we touch it. Here the failure is especially clear. The fantasy of solitary authorship asks us to prove humanity by removing tools and collaborators from the story. But humans are the species that made tools and collaborators central to the story.

Our work has always been crowded.

The question is whether we know who is in the room.

“I did it myself” can remain a lovely sentence. It belongs to the child with the shoe and the adult under the sink and anyone else crossing a boundary that mattered yesterday.

It just should not be mistaken for a theory of creation.

Very little worth doing is done alone.

What matters is whether the person who says *I did this* can give a truthful account of what the *I* actually did—and whether they are willing to answer for the result anyway.
