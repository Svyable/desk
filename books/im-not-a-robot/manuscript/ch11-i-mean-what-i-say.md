# I Mean What I Say

There are sentences we say because we mean them.

There are also sentences we mean because we have said them.

The order is less stable than polite conversation suggests.

“How are you?”

“Fine.”

The exchange can happen between people who are not asking and not answering in any literal sense. Nobody has necessarily lied. The words are doing social work. They acknowledge presence, signal normalcy, open a channel, or let both parties keep moving.

“Have a good one.”

“Sounds good.”

“Let’s circle back.”

“I’m sorry for your loss.”

“Congratulations.”

“Best.”

“Love you.”

Some of these phrases can carry enormous feeling. The same words can also be routine, strategic, automatic, insufficient, sincere, exhausted, ceremonial, or all of those at once.

Language is not a meter that reads intention directly.

This should have made the cultural arrival of fluent language models less philosophically surprising than it did.

We had already spent our lives using words whose social function exceeded their literal novelty.

Then a machine began producing plausible sentences and people understandably asked a new version of an old question.

Does it mean them?

The answer depends heavily on what kind of system we are discussing and what we mean by “mean.” Current language models do not acquire words through the human biography that gives our utterances their stakes. They do not use “I’m sorry for your loss” because a death has entered a shared social world in the way it has for two people at a funeral. The same string can be generated without the lived relationship that makes the human sentence matter.

That difference is important.

It does not imply that every human utterance carries rich interior intention while every machine utterance is empty.

Humans automate language too.

A receptionist says the greeting hundreds of times. A lawyer uses clauses inherited from earlier contracts. A doctor dictates familiar language. A customer-service worker follows a script. A politician repeats a line tested with audiences. A lover borrows a lyric. A child learns “please” before possessing a theory of politeness. A priest, judge, officiant, teacher, flight attendant, dispatcher, salesperson, coach, and parent all rely on phrases that existed before the current moment and will outlive it.

Formula does not eliminate meaning.

Sometimes formula is what makes meaning possible.

At a funeral, originality can be cruel. The bereaved person may not need a brilliant sentence. “I’m sorry” can carry exactly what language is capable of carrying because both people know the phrase is inadequate.

At a wedding, ritual language can matter because others have said it before.

In law, standardized wording can be valuable precisely because idiosyncratic expression would introduce uncertainty.

In aviation, a shared phraseology can reduce ambiguity.

The human capacity to use inherited forms is not evidence that language is fake.

It is one of the ways language becomes social.

The mistake is treating novelty of wording as proof of depth.

Generative AI exposes this because it is very good at producing novel surfaces from learned patterns. A sentence can be new in the narrow sense that the exact sequence was not copied from a source and still feel generic. A human can write an entirely original cliché.

Originality and meaning are not the same property.

Neither are effort and meaning.

A person can spend an hour finding words they do not mean.

A person can type “yes” in one second and alter a life.

The word acquires force from context, commitment, relationship, and consequence.

This is why the earliest chatbot history remains so instructive.

Joseph Weizenbaum’s ELIZA, described in *Communications of the ACM* in 1966, could conduct certain kinds of text conversation using pattern-matching and transformation rules. Its most famous script, DOCTOR, adopted a style associated with nondirective psychotherapy. User statements could be turned back into questions or prompts. The machinery was limited by modern standards, but the conversation could feel surprisingly responsive because the script exploited a setting in which reflecting the user’s own language was socially plausible.

Weizenbaum later became troubled by how readily some users attributed understanding to the system.

The lesson is often summarized as “people anthropomorphize machines.” True, but incomplete.

ELIZA also revealed something about conversation itself.

A response does not need to contain much new information to feel meaningful if the listener supplies enough context.

Human communication is cooperative. We infer. We repair. We fill gaps. We decide what a remark probably meant based on who said it, when, with what history, under what circumstances. A sentence receives part of its meaning from the listener.

This is why sarcasm survives despite text being literal.

It is why “nice job” can be praise or contempt.

It is why a one-word message from one person can matter more than a page from another.

It is why the same sentence generated by a model, copied by a stranger, and written by a spouse can be three different social acts.

The string is not the act.

This gives us a better way to think about AI-assisted language.

Suppose a person wants to apologize but cannot find the words. They ask a model to help draft the message. The model produces:

I was defensive, and I hurt you. I am sorry.

Does the person mean it?

You cannot answer by examining the sentence’s origin alone.

If they read it, recognize it as accurate, send it, change behavior, and accept the consequences of the apology, the assistance may have helped intention become language.

If they send it because a model predicted that these words will calm the recipient while privately rejecting the substance, the same sentence becomes strategic performance.

Human authorship was never enough to distinguish those cases.

People have always written apologies they did not mean.

The machine lowers the cost.

Lower cost changes frequency.

That matters.

When expressive labor becomes cheaper, signals that depended on the labor can weaken.

A three-page letter once implied, at minimum, that somebody spent time producing three pages. A long email no longer carries that implication reliably. A thoughtful response may have cost the sender ninety seconds of prompting and review. A personalized sales message can be sent to ten thousand recipients while preserving the grammatical appearance of individual attention.

The words can remain good.

The signal changes.

This is a recurring economic theme of the AI transition: abundance separates properties that used to travel together.

Length separated from effort.

Polish separated from training.

Personalization separated from personal attention.

Fluency separated from expertise.

The separation is not inherently bad. Accessibility often improves when effort falls. A dyslexic writer can express themselves with less friction. A person writing in a second language can communicate at the level of their ideas rather than the speed of recall. Someone with motor limitations can produce text without typing. A harried doctor can spend more of an appointment looking at a patient if documentation becomes easier.

Cheap expression can return attention to the part that matters.

It can also flood the world with language nobody particularly meant.

That is already familiar from bureaucracy.

Organizations have been generating semantically inflated text long before generative models. Mission statements. Compliance notices. press releases. performance reviews. committee language. policy updates. automated emails. Terms and conditions. Apologies approved by six departments.

Humans discovered large-scale unowned prose first.

Nobody exactly wrote the sentence.

Everyone approved it.

The organization meant it in the institutional sense and perhaps nobody meant it personally.

This matters because people sometimes contrast AI language with a romantic image of human speech as direct self-expression. Much human language is role-expression.

A judge speaks as a judge.

A spokesperson speaks for a company.

A teacher grades in the language of an institution.

An employee writes in a brand voice.

A diplomat says something whose wording has been negotiated by people not in the room.

Meaning can be distributed across roles.

The important question is whether responsibility is distributed with it.

If an AI assistant drafts a corporate statement, who means the statement?

Not the model in the ordinary institutional sense.

The company does, if authorized people adopt it as the company’s position.

That adoption is an act.

It should not disappear simply because generation was automated.

This is where the phrase “the AI said” can become a responsibility trick.

A bank deploys a system that tells a customer no.

A company deploys a bot that makes a false promise.

An employer deploys a screening tool that rejects an applicant.

The visible language comes from software, but the institution selected, configured, and placed the system in a role.

“The AI said” does not answer who stands behind the speech act.

Human institutions should not acquire plausible deniability by putting language generation one layer away from the people who authorized it.

The user has a similar responsibility at smaller scale.

If I send it under my name, I should know what it says.

That sounds embarrassingly obvious.

Abundance makes obvious disciplines harder.

When a system can draft ten emails faster than I can carefully read two, throughput creates pressure to approve without inhabiting. The message carries my name while my attention never fully enters it.

This is how language becomes disconnected from meaning operationally, not philosophically.

The sender cannot answer for the words because the sender barely encountered them.

A useful norm therefore has nothing to do with whether AI touched the sentence.

Can the named sender stand behind it?

A person can stand behind machine-assisted prose.

A person can fail to stand behind unaided prose.

The distinction is responsibility again.

This is especially important in high-stakes contexts. Legal arguments, medical advice, financial representations, academic claims, public policy, safety instructions. If language has consequences, the accountable party cannot outsource understanding along with drafting.

The ease of generation increases the value of reading your own words.

That sentence feels absurd until you watch a world in which people send summaries of documents they never read, respond to messages through suggested replies, and approve text produced faster than human comprehension.

We may enter an era in which authorship is less scarce than attention to authorship.

The person whose name appears may become valuable not because they typed every token but because they actually inspected the claim.

This returns us to meaning as a relationship between words and commitments.

“I mean what I say” can be rewritten more carefully.

I accept what this sentence commits me to.

That is stronger than claiming perfect introspective origin.

A wedding vow uses conventional words and can still be profound because the commitment is real.

A contract uses boilerplate and can still bind because parties adopt it.

A child can use a phrase learned yesterday and mean it completely.

A writer can accept an editor’s sentence as the truest version of what the writer was trying to say.

A person can reject a sentence generated from their own prompt because seeing it on the page reveals that it is not what they mean after all.

Tools can externalize intention enough to judge it.

This is one of the underrated benefits of language generation.

Sometimes you do not know whether a sentence is yours until something writes it down.

Writers have always experienced a version of this through drafting. The sentence arrives. You look at it. No. Not that. The draft becomes an object against which thought can push.

A model can accelerate the process by producing candidate language.

That can reduce authorship if the person accepts everything lazily.

It can increase reflection if the alternatives make distinctions visible.

The tool does not decide which process occurs.

Practice does.

Institutions will need to become much more specific about this. “No AI” and “AI allowed” are crude labels when assistance can range from spellcheck to ideation, transcription, translation, outlining, drafting, fact retrieval, rewriting, critique, simulation, and full autonomous production.

The educational question is what the student is supposed to learn.

The workplace question is what the employee is responsible for knowing.

The relationship question is what the gesture is supposed to represent.

The legal question is who adopted the statement.

The artistic question is what contribution the creator is claiming.

Those are meaning questions.

They cannot all be answered by detecting machine contact.

This is why I do not think the future divides neatly into human language and AI language.

Language will be braided.

A person speaks into a device. Software transcribes. A model edits for clarity. The person restores a strange phrase the model removed. Another model checks the claim. A collaborator changes the ending. The person signs the result.

Who wrote it?

The answer can be accurate only if “wrote” is allowed to describe roles rather than magic origin.

Who dictated?

Who drafted?

Who edited?

Who sourced?

Who approved?

Who stands behind the claim?

Precision about process will become more valuable than purity theater.

This is where the sentence “This is my work” enters.

Work has always included tools, collaborators, inherited methods, and cognitive offloading. The machine age did not suddenly contaminate solitary authorship because solitary authorship was never as solitary as the mythology suggested.

But new tools do change the division of labor enough that old labels sometimes become misleading.

If a model writes the whole report and I sign it unread, calling it “my work” asks the phrase to do too much.

If a model catches grammar in a report I researched, argued, drafted, and revised, saying the work is no longer mine asks the machine to do too much.

The interesting territory is between.

Before we enter it, one last word about meaning.

Human beings are not valuable because every sentence we produce is original, deliberate, and deeply felt.

That standard would disqualify most conversation before breakfast.

We are valuable in language partly because words can bind us to one another across time.

I promise.

I admit.

I consent.

I refuse.

I forgive.

I love you.

The words matter because a person can be held to what happens after them.

Meaning is not only inside the sentence.

It is downstream.

And downstream is where work leaves a trace.

This is my work.
