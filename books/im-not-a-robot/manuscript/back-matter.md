# Back Matter

*I’m Not a Robot: And Other Stories I Tell Myself* began with an ordinary security ritual and kept finding that the ritual’s categories were too small for the thing they were being asked to explain.

The book is not an argument that human beings and artificial systems are secretly the same. It is not an argument that present-day models are conscious, that human agency is an illusion, or that every historical distinction between human and machine will disappear. Those claims would require evidence this manuscript does not pretend to possess.

The narrower claim is that a human identity built from whatever machines cannot yet do is unstable by design. It lets technical progress rewrite the definition. The book therefore looks instead at the automatic, inherited, social, reconstructive, and tool-dependent parts of ordinary human life—and at the capacity to notice, contest, revise, and remain responsible within them.

Several chapters draw on research traditions whose strongest findings are narrower than their popular summaries. Choice blindness does not show that people never know why they choose. Reconstructive memory does not mean memory is useless. Heuristics do not make people generally irrational. Automation bias does not mean humans should always override machines. Algorithm aversion and algorithm appreciation can both appear under different conditions. Research on anthropomorphism does not establish machine consciousness. Studies of handmade products do not establish a universal consumer preference for human labor. AI-writing detector failures do not prove that every detector performs equally badly in every context.

Those limits are part of the argument. A book about stories people tell themselves should not replace them with cleaner stories about psychology.

The source trail lives in `research/README.md`. It prioritizes original papers, official regulatory and legal materials, and primary technical documents for the concrete claims the manuscript makes. Where the book moves from evidence into interpretation, the prose tries to leave enough room for the distinction to remain visible.

## About the author

Sven Hardy Benson writes about intelligence, institutions, technology, economic structure, and the strange ways new capabilities expose assumptions that used to be cheap enough to ignore.
