# I Have Taste

Taste is one of the nicest ways to believe in a private self.

Nobody had to tell me to like this song.

I just do.

The meal works for me. The chair is ugly. The building is beautiful. The book is overrated. The jacket is perfect. The color is wrong. The comedian is not funny. The painting is trying too hard.

Taste arrives with the emotional certainty of perception. We do not usually experience preference as an argument. A thing appears and some part of us leans toward or away from it before the reasons have finished putting on their shoes.

This immediacy makes taste feel like evidence of an inner core untouched by calculation.

Then somebody plays you the same song twenty times.

Robert Zajonc’s 1968 paper on what became known as the mere-exposure effect gathered evidence for a simple, durable proposition: repeated exposure to a stimulus can increase liking for it under certain conditions. The effect is not universal, and repetition can eventually irritate. Preexisting dislike matters. Context matters. Human taste is not a meter that rises mechanically with every encounter.

The useful fact is smaller.

Familiarity can change preference.

The object did not change.

The relationship did.

This is obvious in ordinary life once you notice it. A song you initially ignore becomes tolerable, then catchy, then attached to a period of your life. A style that looked strange becomes normal after enough exposure. A food once rejected becomes comforting. A neighborhood once invisible becomes desirable because friends moved there and you started visiting. A phrase you disliked becomes part of your vocabulary.

We call the eventual result “my taste.”

That description is true.

It simply leaves out the path.

Taste has a history.

The history matters because modern recommendation systems are machines for arranging exposure.

A recommender does not merely guess what you already like. It participates in deciding what you have the chance to like next.

That sentence can be made too sinister if we are careless. A music platform does not implant a preference by force. A streaming service cannot make everybody love the same series. People skip, dislike, search, share, abandon, and surprise the models predicting them. Recommendation systems fail every day.

But a system that controls a meaningful portion of exposure does not need perfect influence to matter.

It needs to decide which doors appear in the hallway.

This is especially clear in culture because the available catalog is effectively infinite relative to a person’s time. No one can listen to every song, read every book, watch every film, visit every restaurant, or look at every image. Discovery is not optional. Something has to choose what becomes visible.

For most of history, that something was a messy mixture of geography, family, class, language, institutions, gatekeepers, friends, retailers, critics, broadcasters, teachers, and chance.

Algorithms joined the mixture.

They did not corrupt a previously pure system of discovery.

They made the selection machinery more explicit, more personalized, and often more measurable.

That is why people experience a strange mix of delight and insult when recommendation becomes accurate.

Delight: this system found something I love.

Insult: apparently I am the kind of person a system can model.

The second feeling reveals how much identity we store in taste.

A recommendation can feel invasive even when the data behind it is banal because it turns a private aesthetic response into a predictable pattern. The song felt like *mine*. The model says people with this history often like it too.

Both can be true.

Shared patterns do not make preference counterfeit.

The discovery that many other people with similar histories enjoy the same thing does not reduce your experience of enjoying it. A favorite song does not become less moving because a recommender ranked it well.

What changes is the story of origin.

I found this.

Did you?

A friend sent it. The platform placed it in a playlist. You heard it in a store. An editor reviewed it. A parent played it in the car. A film used it in a scene. A label paid for promotion. A creator you follow mentioned it. The song was already traveling through a network before it arrived at the private moment where you decided it mattered.

Taste often feels individual at the point of consumption and social at the point of formation.

This is not new. Pierre Bourdieu made an entire sociology out of the relationship among taste, class, distinction, cultural capital, and social position. We use aesthetic preferences not only to enjoy things but to locate ourselves and others. What you like can signal education, aspiration, rebellion, belonging, age, region, subculture, wealth, or a refusal of wealth.

People can turn almost anything into a status signal, including not caring about status.

The machine makes this legible because recommendation systems operate on patterns of similarity. If taste were entirely private and unstructured, collaborative filtering would have very little to work with. The fact that recommendation works at all tells us that preferences contain social regularity.

Again, regularity is not destiny.

The most interesting recommendation is often the adjacent surprise.

Not the obvious thing you already know.

Not the random thing with no connection.

The item far enough from your history to enlarge it but close enough to be intelligible.

This is one of the places where a good recommender and a good friend can resemble each other functionally without being the same kind of relationship.

A friend knows you like one writer and hands you another book with the sentence, “This is different, but I think you’ll understand why I gave it to you.”

The recommendation contains a model of you.

It also contains a wager on change.

That second part is what automated systems can miss if optimization becomes too tightly tied to immediate response.

A recommendation system trained to maximize clicks has no inherent reason to care whether your taste expands.

It may discover that familiar outrage works better.

A music system optimized for low skip rates may learn to protect you from demanding songs that become rewarding only after patience.

A shopping system optimized for conversion may show more versions of what you already buy.

A news system optimized for engagement may serve the emotional texture most likely to keep you inside the system.

The problem is not personalization.

It is objective design.

What is the system trying to help taste become?

That question sounds odd because we are used to treating taste as a thing to be measured rather than developed.

But people cultivate taste deliberately all the time.

A person learns wine and begins noticing differences once invisible.

A reader moves from plot toward style, or from style back toward plot after becoming tired of books that admire their own sentences.

A musician learns to hear structure.

A cook trains a palate.

A designer studies old buildings and starts seeing proportions that used to disappear into “nice” or “ugly.”

A child’s preferences change through exposure and instruction without becoming less sincerely the child’s.

Taste can be educated.

That phrase has an aristocratic smell for good historical reasons. Who gets to decide what counts as cultivated taste has often been entangled with class and exclusion. Institutions have turned particular cultural preferences into evidence of refinement and used them to rank people who never had equal access to the same education.

So “develop your taste” can become code for “learn the preferences of the group with power.”

That is not what I mean.

I mean that perception changes with experience.

You can learn to notice more.

The ability to notice more can increase or decrease liking. It can make a formerly beloved object look shallow. It can make a difficult object newly available. It can produce stronger disagreement, not consensus.

This is why human taste should not be romanticized as raw preference.

Taste includes attention.

And attention can be trained.

Generative AI complicates the story because it can produce culture tailored to preference rather than merely recommend existing culture.

Recommendation chooses from the shelf.

Generation can change the shelf.

Imagine music that continuously adapts toward what keeps you listening. Fiction tuned to your preferred pacing, themes, sentence length, and emotional arc. Visual environments generated to match your aesthetic history. News summaries framed in the register most persuasive to you. Advertising that no longer belongs to a demographic but to a moment-by-moment model of one person.

The economic appeal is obvious.

The aesthetic consequence is less obvious.

If culture can become perfectly responsive to taste, taste may lose one of its most productive sources of growth: resistance.

A difficult book does not care that you prefer short chapters.

A painting does not update because you are confused.

A live band does not regenerate the song for every audience member.

Another person’s taste can be irritating precisely because it is not yours.

The world has edges.

Some of those edges are what change us.

A fully personalized cultural environment risks becoming a mirror that flatters the past.

This does not mean synthetic culture must be bland. A generative system can be instructed to challenge, surprise, contradict, diversify, or sample far outside a user profile. It can become a machine for exposure rather than comfort.

The choice moves upstream again.

What do we want recommendation to do?

Satisfy?

Broaden?

Teach?

Challenge?

Sell?

Retain?

The answer changes the future self whose taste the system will later claim to know.

This recursive relationship is easy to overlook.

A model predicts preference from behavior.

Its prediction changes exposure.

Exposure changes preference.

Changed preference produces new behavior.

The system learns from the behavior partly shaped by the system.

The model then becomes better at predicting a person who has been living inside the model’s recommendations.

This is not proof of manipulation.

It is a feedback loop.

Feedback loops can be helpful. A language-learning app observes errors, selects exercises, and changes the learner it is measuring. A fitness coach does the same. Education is full of systems whose purpose is to alter the person from whom the next measurement will be taken.

The ethical problem depends on whether the direction of change aligns with the person’s interest and whether the person can see or contest what is happening.

Taste systems rarely state the direction so plainly.

They simply recommend.

That invisibility can make preference feel more self-originating than it is.

The human response should not be paranoia.

It should be curiosity about provenance.

Where did this preference come from?

Who exposed me to it?

What changed after repetition?

Which things do I claim to dislike because my group dislikes them?

Which things do I claim to like because they make the right statement about me?

Which preferences survive outside the audience whose approval helped create them?

Which ones would I like to develop even though they are weak today?

Those questions can become insufferable if applied to every lunch order. A person does not need a genealogy of soup.

The point is that taste becomes a better expression of self when we stop demanding that it emerge from nowhere.

Influence is not contamination.

Your vocabulary is influenced.

Your cooking is influenced.

Your sense of humor is influenced.

Your politics are influenced.

Your aesthetic standards are influenced.

An untouched self would be an empty one.

Human originality is not purity from influence. It is what a particular history does with influence.

That history includes chance.

One record played in a friend’s room.

One teacher assigned the strange book.

One trip made a material feel beautiful.

One breakup ruined a song for ten years.

One recommendation arrived on the right day.

One creator opened a door.

The same exposure in a different life would not produce the same taste.

This is why prediction can be accurate without exhausting meaning.

A recommender may know that I will probably like a song.

It does not thereby know what the song will become attached to.

It cannot precompute the argument during which the song plays, the person who sends it, the city where I hear it, the year I return to it after avoiding it, or the sentence I eventually use to explain why it matters.

Taste is not merely the ranking.

It is the history of the ranking.

This is also why machine-generated culture does not automatically become valueless because a machine helped produce it. Value can arise in reception, relationship, use, performance, curation, context, and human judgment around an artifact. At the same time, the provenance of a work can be part of why it matters. Knowing that a person spent years learning an instrument can change the meaning of hearing the performance. Knowing that a friend painted the picture changes the picture’s place in your life.

The object is not the entire experience.

We will return to this in “The Human Premium,” where markets begin charging for the fact that a person was there.

For now, taste gives us another reason to distrust the clean contrast between human spontaneity and machine prediction.

We are predictable because we have histories.

We have histories because we have been exposed.

We can also alter exposure deliberately.

That last move is where agency reappears without needing to pretend the prior influence never happened.

If you are tired of your feed, follow someone who annoys your categories.

If your music has become too comfortable, ask a person rather than the model.

If your reading confirms you too efficiently, choose a shelf the system would not have selected.

If you discover that you like something because of repetition, you are still allowed to like it.

Awareness does not invalidate the preference.

It gives you another lever.

This is the recurring structure of the book.

The script becomes visible.

The person gains the possibility—not the guarantee—of revision.

Taste is useful because it shows how little revision requires an untouched inner essence. You do not need to locate the preference that was truly yours before society got involved. There is no pre-exposure museum of the authentic self.

You can decide which influences deserve another encounter.

That is enough.

The next story is harder because it takes yesterday’s pattern and turns it into a promise about tomorrow.

We want other people to be consistent. We want ourselves to be consistent. Trust would be impossible if every person woke as a stranger to their prior commitments.

But consistency has a shadow.

Once I have said who I am, changing becomes evidence against the story.

I am consistent.
