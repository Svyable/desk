# This Is My Work

A carpenter does not become less of a carpenter when the saw is sharp.

Nobody asks whether the table is really hers because electricity turned the blade.

The question becomes more complicated when the tool starts making suggestions.

Then more complicated again when the tool can produce a plausible table without waiting for the carpenter’s hand.

This is where a lot of arguments about artificial intelligence get trapped. We have an old category called “tool” and an old category called “worker,” and a new class of systems keeps crossing the intuitive line between them.

A spellchecker feels like a tool.

Autocomplete usually feels like a tool.

A calculator feels like a tool.

A model that proposes three paragraphs begins to feel like a collaborator.

A model that writes the entire report from a sentence of instruction may feel like a substitute.

The boundaries are real enough to matter and fuzzy enough to resist slogans.

So we reach for a sentence.

This is my work.

What does “my” mean?

The easiest answer is physical production. I made the marks. I moved the material. I typed the words.

Civilization abandoned that definition almost immediately.

Architects do not lay every brick. Composers do not play every instrument in an orchestra. Film directors do not operate every camera. Scientists publish papers containing work performed by teams, instruments, software, technicians, collaborators, and prior literatures. Executives sign strategies built from analyses they did not personally calculate. Judges issue opinions shaped by clerks and precedent. Authors publish books changed by editors. Engineers design systems fabricated by other people and measured by tools that perform calculations no unaided person could complete at useful speed.

We have always separated authorship from total physical execution.

The separation does not mean anything goes.

A person cannot commission a novel from somebody else and honestly claim they wrote every word merely because they paid. A student cannot hire another student to take an exam and call the answers their own demonstration of knowledge. A researcher cannot add a name to work in ways that violate authorship standards and solve the problem by saying all creation is collaborative.

Credit depends on norms about contribution.

Those norms are contextual because work is contextual.

A photographer can claim a photograph without manufacturing the camera.

A mathematician cannot claim a proof they copied from a book because they happened to use a pen they owned.

The tool is not the problem.

The relationship between tool, task, representation, and contribution is.

Andy Clark and David Chalmers made a provocative version of this point in philosophy long before generative AI. Their 1998 paper “The Extended Mind” asks whether cognitive processes must be located entirely inside the skull. The famous thought experiment compares a person relying on biological memory with a person who, because of memory impairment, reliably relies on a notebook. If the notebook functions in the right integrated way, Clark and Chalmers ask why cognition should be defined so that the external resource is excluded simply because it sits outside the skin.

You do not have to accept every part of the extended-mind thesis to see why the example persists.

Humans think through things.

Paper.

Maps.

Abacuses.

Notebooks.

Diagrams.

Whiteboards.

Spreadsheets.

Databases.

Search engines.

Code.

Calendars.

Other people.

Evan Risko and Sam Gilbert’s work on cognitive offloading gives the practice a more empirical frame. People use physical actions and external resources to reduce the cognitive demands of a task: rotate an object instead of mentally rotating it, write a reminder instead of rehearsing it, set an alarm instead of relying on prospective memory.

The boundary of unaided cognition has never been the boundary of human accomplishment.

That is why “AI did some of the thinking” cannot by itself settle authorship.

Tools have been doing cognitive work for us for a long time.

A calculator computes.

A search engine retrieves.

A grammar checker evaluates certain patterns.

A statistical package estimates models.

A compiler translates instructions.

A GPS system plans routes.

A simulation explores outcomes.

We accepted these forms of offloading because the human contribution remained legible enough. The analyst chooses the model, the engineer interprets the simulation, the driver decides the destination, the writer accepts or rejects the correction.

Generative systems unsettle us because they move into activities we associated more directly with expression and judgment.

The machine does not merely calculate a number inside the argument.

It can produce the argument-shaped thing.

That changes the burden of ownership.

If I ask a model to draft a chapter, receive four thousand words, read the first paragraph, and publish the whole file under my name, the sentence “this is my work” becomes thin. I may own the file legally under whatever rules apply. I may have initiated the task. I may even have specified the subject. But the claim that the chapter represents my considered work would be hard to defend if I do not know what it says.

Now change the process.

I spend weeks researching a question. I develop an argument. I dictate rough passages. A model reorganizes notes. I reject most of its structure. It identifies a missing counterargument. I verify the source. I write the chapter, then use the model to flag repetition and unclear transitions. I make final decisions and stand behind every claim.

The machine performed real cognitive labor.

The work can still meaningfully be mine.

The difference is not a metaphysical percentage of tokens.

It is authorship as exercised judgment.

That phrase needs care because “judgment” can become a flattering catch-all for whatever humans do after machines become capable. I do not mean that humans possess a permanent monopoly on evaluation. Models can compare, rank, criticize, test, and select according to criteria.

I mean something more institutional.

Whose purposes organize the work?

Who decides what standards apply?

Who accepts or rejects consequential changes?

Who understands enough of the result to answer for it?

Who is making the representation “this came from me” and what does the audience reasonably think that representation implies?

These questions make authorship less pure and more accountable.

They also explain why the same AI use can be acceptable in one context and unacceptable in another.

A student in a writing course may be assigned a personal essay because the teacher wants to evaluate the student’s ability to structure prose. Having a model generate the essay defeats the task even if the student agrees with every sentence.

The same student in a course on editing might be asked to improve machine-generated prose. There, using the model is the task.

A software engineer may use code completion as part of ordinary work while remaining responsible for tests, security, architecture, and behavior.

A candidate taking a programming assessment designed to measure unaided reasoning may be prohibited from using the same tool.

A journalist may use transcription software without altering the reporting claim.

The journalist who generates a quote and attributes it to a real person has committed a different act entirely.

The technology did not determine the ethics.

The representation did.

This is why disclosure debates become confused when they begin with “AI was used.”

Used how?

For what?

In a context where the process itself carries meaning, disclosure can matter greatly. In other contexts, exhaustive disclosure would become absurd.

This paragraph was typed on a computer.

The computer corrected spelling.

The operating system rendered the characters.

Version control stored changes.

A markdown parser may later convert syntax into HTML.

Nobody expects a book to disclose those facts on every page because the tools do not conflict with the ordinary meaning of authorship.

Generative assistance may eventually become equally ordinary for some functions.

For others it may remain material.

Norms will settle unevenly because audiences care about different things.

The most interesting response is to become more specific about claims.

“I wrote this” may come to mean something different from “I directed this,” “I edited this,” “I approved this,” “I performed this,” or “I generated this with assistance.”

That is not bureaucratic pedantry.

Language evolves when old verbs carry too many processes.

Photography created distinctions among taking, developing, printing, retouching, directing, and compositing. Film created credits because “made by” could not contain everyone. Software created maintainers, committers, reviewers, contributors, and authors. Science developed contribution taxonomies partly because long author lists made a single undifferentiated authorship label insufficient.

AI will create new verbs whether we like it or not.

The healthier future is not one in which every work product carries a confession.

It is one in which people can accurately describe their contribution without shame or inflation.

That requires abandoning the myth that tool use diminishes human work by definition.

It also requires abandoning the opposite myth that prompting a system automatically makes its output ours.

Prompting is a contribution.

Sometimes a substantial one.

Sometimes barely one at all.

“Write me a bestselling novel about grief” is an instruction.

It is not yet a novelistic achievement merely because the output arrives.

A more elaborate prompt can encode deep domain knowledge, constraints, examples, aesthetic decisions, and evaluation criteria. In some creative workflows, prompt design becomes meaningful craft. But the existence of craft in prompting does not convert every prompt into authorship of everything downstream any more than shouting “make it blue” across a studio makes you the painter.

Contribution has to be described at the level where it happened.

This is emotionally hard because status attaches to credit.

People want the strong verb.

I built it.

I wrote it.

I discovered it.

I founded it.

I designed it.

The pronoun compresses a team.

This is not an AI problem either.

The mythology of the lone inventor has always hidden technicians, assistants, suppliers, predecessors, public research, infrastructure, and collaborators. Corporate histories attach complex achievements to charismatic individuals because stories are easier to remember when they have protagonists.

The machine can intensify this because the hidden labor becomes less visible, not more. A founder types a prompt and receives analysis built on models trained through enormous technical, organizational, and human effort. The interface makes the result feel immediate and personal.

The shorter the interaction, the easier it is to overclaim self-sufficiency.

This is why gratitude is not merely a moral decoration.

It is causal literacy.

Knowing what helped produce your work makes you less likely to confuse initiation with total authorship.

The toolmaker mattered.

The dataset mattered.

The editor mattered.

The colleague who said the idea was wrong mattered.

The person who built the library mattered.

The worker who labeled data mattered.

The public institution that funded research mattered.

The customer who exposed the failure mattered.

The language you inherited mattered.

You still did something.

Acknowledging the system does not erase the person.

It locates them.

This is what the argument about AI authorship needs more of: location.

Where, exactly, did human judgment enter?

Where did the machine act?

Where did another person contribute?

Which part of the result is being claimed?

What would have happened without the claimant?

That last question can be revealing.

If I disappear and the work remains identical, my claim may be weaker than I think.

If the work changes substantially because my choices shaped direction, standards, selection, revision, or interpretation, the contribution is easier to see.

This resembles counterfactual reasoning about teamwork. A film director may not touch every frame, yet removing the director could transform the film. An editor may add few words but change the structure. A research advisor may never collect the data but alter the question that made the experiment possible.

Contribution is not proportional to keystrokes.

That is why counting AI-generated tokens is a crude authorship metric.

The machine may contribute ninety percent of the visible text to a document whose decisive human work lies in selecting, verifying, and making one consequential judgment.

Or the machine may contribute ten percent of visible text and that ten percent contains the core idea the human then claims as original.

Surface share does not settle intellectual share.

We are going to need better norms than percentages.

One useful principle is that responsibility should not be smaller than credit.

If I want my name on the work, I should accept a proportionate obligation to understand and answer for it.

This becomes especially important in professional contexts. A physician cannot defend a harmful note by saying the model drafted it if the workflow presented the note under the physician’s authority. An attorney cannot cite nonexistent cases and point to software as though the software held the professional license. An executive cannot automate a decision and then treat automation as moral distance from the result.

Credit without responsibility is vanity.

Responsibility without credit is exploitation.

Good systems try to align them.

This will matter for the people whose labor sits behind AI as well. If a product markets itself as autonomous while relying on poorly paid human reviewers to handle the difficult residue, the distribution of credit and responsibility is distorted in the other direction. The machine gets the headline. The people get the exceptions.

The Mechanical Turk returns.

The cabinet keeps changing shape.

There is another reason “this is my work” matters beyond credit.

Work changes the worker.

A student who struggles through a proof is not merely producing the proof. The struggle changes what the student can do next. A musician practicing scales is not manufacturing scales. The repetition builds a musician. A writer revising a bad paragraph develops perception. A junior employee preparing an analysis learns the domain through the preparation.

If a tool removes the difficult part of the process, it may preserve the output while removing the transformation.

This is the strongest reason not to automate certain work too early.

Not because struggle is holy.

Because some struggle is the mechanism of learning.

A calculator is wonderful after a student understands what multiplication means. It can be disastrous if used to avoid ever building number sense. A model can help an experienced programmer move faster because the programmer can evaluate the code. The same model can help a novice ship code they cannot debug, creating the appearance of competence without the internal structure that normally supports it.

Output and capability separate.

The workplace will have to decide when that separation is acceptable.

If the goal is simply to accomplish the task safely and well, automation can be rational.

If the task is also how future expertise is produced, full automation can consume the apprenticeship pipeline that the organization will later need.

This is not a reason to preserve drudgery for tradition’s sake.

It is a reason to identify which parts of drudgery were secretly training.

The industrial revolution repeatedly forced societies to distinguish craft from unnecessary exertion. AI will do the same for cognitive labor.

Some effort will turn out to have been waste.

Some will turn out to have been practice.

We should not confuse the two merely because both were difficult.

A human being is not more human because a task was inefficient.

But a person can become capable through processes that efficient systems would happily skip.

This creates a design challenge for education and work: how do we use powerful tools without outsourcing the formation of judgment needed to supervise them?

One answer is staged delegation.

Do the task unaided until the underlying concept is learned.

Use assistance for routine components.

Require verification.

Rotate between production and critique.

Ask the person to explain the result.

Let tools expand capability while preserving the parts of the process that build discernment.

That is not a universal formula. Different domains will need different balances.

The important point is to remember that work has two products.

The artifact.

The worker.

Automation can improve the first while weakening the second if deployed without thought.

It can also improve both by removing noise and giving people faster feedback.

Again, the architecture matters more than the slogan.

This is my work.

A mature version of that sentence does not mean I touched every part.

It means I can locate myself honestly inside the causal chain.

I know what I contributed.

I know what I delegated.

I know what I used.

I know what I learned.

I know what I am claiming.

I know what I remain responsible for.

The next chapter takes away even the comfort of the tool sitting clearly outside the self.

Because much of what we call individual achievement has always depended on other people, infrastructures, institutions, and inherited knowledge we barely notice.

The machine did not create dependence.

It made dependence easier to see.

And that exposes another story.

I did it myself.
