# I Am in Control

The elevator button is one of modern life’s smallest arguments about control.

You press it. The light turns on. Something happens somewhere you cannot see. A machine begins, continues, or has already begun moving toward you. If the elevator takes too long, you may press the button again even though you know the second press adds no useful information. Your finger wants a relationship with the outcome.

The button gives you one.

Sometimes that relationship is real. Sometimes it is ceremonial. Buildings have used buttons, thermostats, pedestrian signals, and other controls whose apparent authority exceeds their actual authority, whether because the system has been reconfigured, centralized, placed on a timer, or designed to accept human input only within a narrow range. The person still receives the small psychological reward of action. I pressed. The system responded. I am participating in what happens next.

Control is one of the stories people tell themselves because the alternative is difficult to inhabit.

I am in control.

The sentence can mean several different things. I can cause the outcome. I can stop the process. I understand what is happening. I chose the goal. I can change my mind. Somebody will listen if I object. The system cannot act without me. I remain responsible. These are not the same claim, but interfaces have a way of compressing them into a button.

Approve.

Confirm.

Accept.

Continue.

Send.

A human finger at the end of a machine process creates a reassuring picture: automation proposed; a person decided.

Sometimes that picture is accurate.

Sometimes the person is the elevator button.

Psychologists have studied exaggerated beliefs about personal control for decades. Ellen Langer’s 1975 work on the “illusion of control” examined situations in which cues associated with skill—choice, familiarity, involvement, competition—could increase confidence even when outcomes were governed substantially or entirely by chance. The underlying human tendency is easy to recognize without turning the research into a universal diagnosis. We like actions that make outcomes feel connected to us. A chosen lottery number feels different from an assigned one. A knob feels different from a forecast. A steering wheel feels different from a passenger seat even when the road ahead is the same.

The mistake is not wanting control.

Control matters.

The mistake is confusing the presence of an action with the presence of meaningful authority.

Modern automated systems create extraordinary opportunities for this confusion because they can leave the human gesture intact while moving the important decisions upstream.

A hiring manager may still click the final button while a ranking system determines which applicants are visible. A physician may still sign an order while a clinical system chooses which warning appears, when it appears, and how urgently it is framed. A moderator may still remove the post while software decides what enters the review queue. A loan officer may still technically approve an application after a scoring system has defined the range of plausible decisions. A driver may still hold the wheel while automation handles most ordinary corrections and silently changes what the driver is practiced at noticing.

The human remains present.

Presence is not control.

This distinction becomes more important as “human in the loop” becomes a phrase institutions use to reassure themselves. The phrase sounds like a design principle and sometimes names a good one. A consequential system should often preserve routes for human judgment, override, escalation, and appeal. But a loop can contain a person without giving that person enough time, information, expertise, independence, or authority to matter.

Imagine a reviewer asked to approve one hundred machine recommendations an hour. The screen presents a recommended action in bold, a confidence score, and three supporting signals. The reviewer can disagree. Technically, this is human oversight.

Now add the actual conditions.

Disagreement requires six extra clicks and a written explanation. Agreement requires one. Supervisors track throughput. The machine is usually right. The reviewer sees only the evidence the system selected. False alarms have trained the reviewer to trust the default. There is no easy way to inspect how the score was produced. The reviewer knows that overturning the recommendation will be audited more aggressively than accepting it.

The person has an override button.

Do they have control?

The question cannot be answered from the interface diagram.

Human-factors research has spent decades showing why. Raja Parasuraman and Victor Riley’s classic 1997 paper on automation distinguished use, misuse, disuse, and abuse. Their categories remain useful because they refuse the simplistic idea that more automation or less automation is automatically safer. People can over-rely on automation. They can underuse a good system after false alarms destroy trust. Designers can automate functions that should remain elsewhere. Operators can stop monitoring a process that rarely demands intervention until the rare intervention becomes the only thing that matters.

Later work by Parasuraman and Dietrich Manzey reviewed automation complacency and automation bias, including the conditions under which people fail to monitor automated systems or give machine recommendations too much weight. One important finding is uncomfortable for anyone who thinks expertise alone solves the problem: complacency is not confined to naive users. It can appear precisely because a reliable system has earned trust.

Reliability creates the conditions for inattention.

That sounds like an accusation until you imagine the opposite. If an automated system requires the person to independently verify every operation, it has failed to automate much. The value of reliable automation is that attention can go somewhere else.

This creates a design paradox.

The more successful the machine becomes at ordinary cases, the less practice the person gets at the cases in which the machine needs help.

A driver-assistance system handles miles of routine highway travel. The person’s hands and attention become less engaged. Then a strange construction pattern, weather event, sensor failure, or object appears and the system asks the person to take over. The transfer sounds reasonable: let the machine handle the easy part and the human handle the hard part.

Except the hard part arrives without rehearsal.

The human is asked to become excellent at the thing they are no longer regularly doing.

This is sometimes called the out-of-the-loop problem, and it is not new to artificial intelligence. Aviation, process control, military systems, medicine, and industrial automation have all wrestled with versions of it. The lesson is not that automation is bad. Commercial aviation would be an absurd place to make that argument. The lesson is that control has a temporal dimension. It matters what the person has been doing before the moment they are suddenly declared responsible.

Responsibility can be handed back faster than competence.

That sentence should make every organization using generative or agentic systems slightly uncomfortable.

Consider a worker whose job changes from producing ten documents a day to reviewing one hundred generated documents a day. The organization may describe the change as augmentation. The human remains in control because nothing is sent without approval.

But approval changes character at scale.

Reading is slower than generating. Verification is often slower than fluent production. A factual error can hide inside an otherwise polished page. A subtle legal, medical, technical, or reputational problem may require reconstructing sources and assumptions the generated text does not expose. The person’s role can become less like author and more like customs inspector at a border where the trucks arrive continuously.

The machine has speed. The human has liability.

Calling that “human in the loop” does not make it healthy.

The more useful question is whether the loop is designed around human strengths or merely around institutional reassurance.

A person can be positioned after a machine because somebody wants a name on the decision. That is not the same as designing a process in which human judgment meaningfully changes outcomes.

This is where the story “I am in control” intersects with the previous chapter’s story “I did it myself.” Both are often really claims about responsibility.

If I am responsible, I want to believe I controlled enough of the process to deserve the credit when it works and the blame when it fails.

Organizations want the same thing in reverse. They may automate aggressively and then preserve a final human approval partly because the approval produces a legible place to locate accountability. The system recommended. The employee clicked.

The click becomes a moral checksum.

This arrangement is unstable.

If the employee is genuinely expected to independently evaluate the machine, the organization must give them the time, information, training, and authority required to do so. If it does not, the approval is theater. If the machine is trusted enough that independent evaluation is unnecessary, then pretending the human made the substantive decision is theater of another kind.

The design should admit which one it is.

There is another way control becomes ceremonial: the person can refuse one action but cannot realistically refuse the system.

A worker may be allowed to override a recommendation while having no authority to stop using the tool that generates recommendations. A customer may decline one personalized suggestion while having no practical way to prevent the platform from building the profile behind it. A manager may reject a score while the score remains mandatory evidence in every review. A doctor may disagree with a workflow while the hospital has already organized staffing, documentation, reimbursement, and liability around the workflow.

The local veto is real.

The larger dependency is real too.

This matters because control includes exit.

Not absolute exit from every institution. Nobody has meaningful control over civilization by threatening to leave it. But at the scale where a person or organization claims to be governing a tool, there should be some answer to a basic question: what happens if we decide this arrangement no longer serves us?

Can we pause it?

Can we fall back to another process?

Can we export the records required to leave?

Can we compare the new system with the old one?

Can we survive a week without it?

Can we change vendors without rebuilding the organization from scratch?

A system can begin as assistance and become infrastructure before anybody formally decides that dependence is acceptable.

Convenience accelerates the transition because every successful use creates another reason not to return to the old method. Staff stop practicing the manual process. Documentation moves into the new platform. Historical records accumulate there. Customers adapt. Budgets shift. Expertise leaves.

Eventually the organization still has a choice in theory.

In practice, the choice has acquired a price large enough to function like constraint.

This is not unique to AI. Infrastructure has always created lock-in. The machine age simply makes it easier for decision infrastructure to become invisible because the dependency lives partly in workflow and cognition rather than concrete machinery.

A person can retain control over each click while losing control over whether clicking remains optional.

That should count.

None of this requires removing humans from consequential systems. It requires taking human authority seriously enough to specify it.

Can the person say no?

Can they see the basis of the recommendation?

Can they request more information?

Can they slow the process?

Can they escalate disagreement?

Will the institution punish them for frequently using the override it proudly advertises?

Can the affected person appeal to someone with genuinely different authority?

Does the reviewer know when the system is outside the conditions in which it performs well?

Can the automated action be reversed?

Can the system itself be paused, rolled back, or replaced when the pattern of errors changes?

These questions describe control much better than the existence of a button.

Reversibility deserves special attention because humans are terrible at being perfectly right on the first attempt and surprisingly good at building systems that assume they should be.

A reversible decision can tolerate uncertainty. An irreversible one cannot. The same confidence level should mean different things when the consequence is a draft email versus a medical intervention, a temporary account hold versus permanent deletion, a suggested route versus control of a moving vehicle.

We often talk about AI safety as if the central question were whether a model is intelligent enough to be dangerous. A more ordinary question is whether the surrounding system lets people recover from mistakes.

Control is frequently less about preventing every error than about retaining the ability to notice and correct one before it becomes final.

Rollback is one form of control that interfaces rarely celebrate because rollback admits that authority includes undoing what authority previously approved.

Organizations are often comfortable with approval and strangely uncomfortable with reversal.

Approval fits the story of competence.

Rollback records that the system, the person, or both were wrong.

But a process that cannot reverse a bad automated action may be less controlled than a process that makes a few more errors and repairs them quickly. The error rate alone does not describe governance. The time between error and recovery matters. The cost of recovery matters. Whether the harmed person can trigger recovery matters.

The ability to stop should therefore exist at more than one timescale.

Stop this action.

Pause this class of actions.

Return to the previous model or policy.

Disable the automation while evidence is reviewed.

End the arrangement altogether.

A giant red emergency button is not enough if ordinary operators are punished for touching it, nobody remembers the fallback process, or the system can technically be stopped only after the consequence has become irreversible.

Control needs latency appropriate to consequence.

That is also how humans manage themselves.

People do not maintain control over their lives by consciously determining every action. They use defaults, rituals, commitments, alarms, budgets, calendars, passwords, friends, laws, and architecture. We deliberately constrain future choice because future choice is unreliable.

Someone who sets an automatic transfer into savings is giving up moment-to-moment control in order to gain control over a longer horizon.

Someone who removes an addictive app from a phone is reducing immediate options in order to protect a chosen intention.

Someone who gives a friend their car keys is voluntarily surrendering local control because they do not trust the future state in which the keys might be used.

Someone who signs a contract binds future behavior because the present self has decided that freedom to reconsider later would undermine a more important commitment.

Human control is therefore not identical to maximum optionality.

Sometimes control means building a system that makes certain impulses harder to obey.

This matters in the AI debate because “keep the human in control” can be interpreted too literally, as though good design always means giving the person more buttons. More buttons can create more opportunities to make inconsistent, impulsive, biased, or poorly informed decisions. A well-designed automated constraint can protect a human intention better than constant manual choice.

The important question is whose intention the constraint serves.

A navigation system that reroutes around traffic is acting on the driver’s goal. A dark pattern that makes cancellation difficult is acting against the user’s goal while preserving the appearance of choice. Both are interfaces. Both may involve automation. The moral difference lies partly in alignment between the person’s purposes and the system’s incentives.

Control is relational.

A system can make you more capable while making you more dependent. It can reduce your cognitive load while reducing your understanding. It can give you more options while shaping which options appear. It can protect you from error while making it harder to notice when the protection itself becomes the error.

This is why trust matters so much.

People do not inspect the internal mechanisms of every system they use. They could not. Civilization is a network of justified and unjustified reliance. I drink water without testing each glass. I board a plane without reviewing the maintenance records. I use software without reading the source code. I take medicine whose manufacturing process I could not reproduce. Trust is not the absence of intelligence. It is one of the ways intelligence operates under conditions of finite time.

The problem is not reliance.

The problem is uncalibrated reliance.

Research on algorithm aversion and algorithm appreciation captures two sides of this calibration problem. Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons, and Cade Massey showed that people can abandon algorithms after watching them make mistakes even when those algorithms outperform human forecasters. Jennifer Logg, Julia Minson, and Don Moore later showed situations in which people gave more weight to advice they believed came from an algorithm than to advice from another person. Taken together, the results are more interesting than either stereotype. Humans are not simply anti-algorithm or pro-algorithm. We shift our trust depending on context, comparison, expertise, visibility of error, and what we think the system is doing.

We can resent a machine for being imperfect and surrender to one because it looks objective.

Both reactions can reduce control.

A person who refuses useful automation because it once made a visible error can become less capable. A person who follows automation because its score feels mathematically authoritative can become less capable in a different direction. Control requires the harder middle: knowing what kind of confidence the system has earned.

That confidence should not be static.

Systems change. Data changes. Environments change. People change their goals. A model updated last month may behave differently from the one a policy was written around last year. A recommender trained to maximize engagement can become misaligned with a person who decides they want to spend less time engaged. A workplace tool introduced as assistance can gradually become the place where performance is measured. A convenience becomes infrastructure; infrastructure becomes dependence; dependence becomes governance.

This introduces a control problem that has nothing to do with whether the person is attentive enough.

The thing being controlled can change beneath the approval.

An organization may test one model, write procedures around it, train staff, and approve its use. Months later the model is updated. The interface remains familiar. The product name remains the same. The human reviewer follows the same policy. Yet the distribution of errors, the style of recommendations, or the conditions under which the system becomes uncertain may have shifted.

The old approval survives visually.

Does it survive substantively?

Control requires some relationship between authority and version.

If the thing changes materially, governance has to notice.

That does not mean every software update needs a board meeting. It means “we approved the system” cannot be a permanent sentence when the system is not permanent.

There should be a way to know what changed, compare behavior where it matters, and decide whether the earlier mandate still fits.

Otherwise control becomes grandfathered consent.

We agreed once.

Therefore whatever this becomes remains authorized.

Human relationships would recognize the absurdity immediately. Permission to do one thing under one set of conditions does not automatically become permission for every future variation. Technical systems can obscure that intuition because change arrives as maintenance.

Control can leak away without a dramatic moment of surrender.

You agree to one default at a time.

This is true far beyond software.

Habits work the same way. The first repetition feels chosen. The hundredth arrives before deliberation. Institutions work the same way. A temporary procedure acquires forms, staff, precedent, and budget until reversing it becomes more expensive than maintaining it. Language works the same way. A phrase becomes the standard way to describe a problem and begins determining which solutions sound reasonable.

The book’s recurring idea—humans contain scripts—is not an argument that control is impossible. It is an argument that control is episodic.

We are most in control at the moments when we examine which scripts deserve to continue.

That is a humbler form of agency than the fantasy of a sovereign self issuing commands from outside causation. It is also more useful.

You do not need to be the uncaused cause of your behavior to change the environment that shapes it.

You do not need perfect introspection to ask another person what they see.

You do not need to manually perform every task to decide which tasks should remain yours.

You do not need to understand every parameter in a model to demand evidence about when it fails.

You do not need to control every step to retain veto power over the outcome.

Human control is often second-order control: control over the conditions under which first-order behavior will later occur.

We choose the alarm because we know the sleeping person will not choose to wake.

We choose the checklist because the busy person will forget.

We choose the guardrail because the distracted driver may drift.

We choose the rate limit because the excited user may click again.

We choose the review process because the first decision-maker may be wrong.

The person who builds these constraints is not admitting defeat. They are designing around the fact that a person is not a perfectly consistent controller.

The same standard should apply to AI systems.

A good human-machine arrangement should not depend on a mythic reviewer who is always alert, fully informed, unpressured, technically fluent, and willing to override the machine at exactly the right moment. If that person existed, many control problems would disappear. The actual person will be tired sometimes. They will trust a system that has been right all week. They will rush. They will misunderstand a confidence score. They will be influenced by the order in which evidence is presented. They may worry about disagreeing with a model their organization spent millions to deploy.

Design for that person.

Build pauses where the consequence warrants them. Make disagreement ordinary rather than punitive. Show uncertainty instead of laundering it into a single crisp answer. Separate recommendation from authority. Preserve logs that let people reconstruct what happened. Make reversible actions reversible in practice, not merely in a policy document. Create an appeal route that does not just feed the same model the same inputs and call the second output independent review.

Most important, be explicit about who owns the goal.

A system that perfectly optimizes the wrong goal is not under human control just because a person configured it once.

Goals drift.

Metrics replace intentions. A support team wants customers helped; the dashboard counts tickets closed. A newsroom wants readers informed; the platform counts attention. A school wants students to learn; the system counts assignments submitted. A hospital wants patients healthier; the workflow counts throughput. A company wants good judgment; the performance system counts adherence.

Once the metric becomes easier to see than the purpose, people begin serving the representation.

Machines are very good at representations.

So are bureaucracies.

Humans are not immune. We love a number that tells us the elevator is coming.

The deeper form of control is the ability to remember what the number was for.

That is why the story “I am in control” should not be discarded. We need it. Responsibility without any belief in agency becomes resignation. A person who believes nothing they do matters has little reason to deliberate, promise, repair, or resist.

But control becomes more believable when we shrink it to human scale.

You do not control the whole system.

You may control a decision point.

You may control whether you enter.

You may control whether you leave.

You may control the terms under which something acts for you.

You may control whether a recommendation becomes an action.

You may control whether an error can be corrected.

You may control whether the system can be paused when its behavior changes.

You may control whether dependence remains reversible.

You may control which value the system is supposed to optimize, and whether that value still deserves authority tomorrow.

This is enough to matter.

It is also enough to lose.

A machine does not have to overpower a person for the person to lose control. The person can give control away because the automation is useful, one small delegation at a time, until the system becomes difficult to question. The danger is rarely a red-eyed robot seizing the wheel. It is a green button that has worked so many times nobody remembers what happens when you refuse to press it.

The checkbox at the beginning of this book asked for a declaration: I’m not a robot.

The more interesting control is not proving that statement to the machine.

It is retaining the ability to tell the machine no—and having the surrounding system treat the answer as something more than another button press.
