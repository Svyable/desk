# The Version of Me Other People Use

There are people who know you by a version of you that no longer exists.

A former teacher remembers the student who was seventeen. A childhood friend can call up a joke you stopped making twenty years ago. A sibling has access to a catalog of selves you would not introduce at work. A former colleague may think of you as the person who once mishandled a project you have spent the rest of your career learning how not to mishandle. Someone who met you during the worst month of your life may carry that month as if it were a representative sample.

None of these people are necessarily wrong.

They are using cached copies.

Human beings have always lived among these partial versions of one another. Reputation is older than writing. A village does not need a database to remember who keeps promises, who starts fights, who can repair a roof, who cheats at cards, who should not be trusted with a secret, or who once did something generous at exactly the moment generosity was expensive.

The self has therefore never belonged entirely to the person living it.

There is the version from the inside and the versions in circulation.

We spend a surprising amount of life trying to manage the gap.

I am not like that anymore.

That was taken out of context.

You do not know me.

Ask anyone who really knows me.

I can explain.

All of these sentences appeal to a deeper self behind the observed one. Sometimes the appeal is justified. Sometimes it is a request to discount inconvenient evidence. Usually it contains both possibilities.

The internet did not invent the problem. It gave the cached copies excellent storage.

A photograph that would once have disappeared into a drawer can remain searchable. An opinion offered to a small audience can be encountered years later by a stranger. A professional profile becomes a compressed public autobiography. A platform remembers purchases, locations, searches, viewing habits, clicks, pauses, contacts, device characteristics, and the timing of behavior with a patience no acquaintance possesses.

Then systems begin to infer.

This is where the version of you other people use stops being only a social story and becomes infrastructure.

A person can be represented by a score, a segment, a risk category, a recommendation profile, a fraud probability, an audience label, a likelihood to churn, a predicted lifetime value, an eligibility status, a ranking, or an invisible set of features that never appear to the person in ordinary language.

The model does not need to know you.

It needs to know enough about people statistically like the data-version of you to make a useful prediction.

That sentence describes one of the most consequential changes in modern identity. For most of history, being misjudged required a judge who had at least encountered some story about you. Now an institution can act on a representation assembled from signals neither party would recognize as biography.

You are not merely what you say about yourself.

You are what systems can infer from traces.

The Federal Trade Commission described an early version of this world in its 2014 report on data brokers. The report documented companies combining information from online and offline sources and deriving categories or inferences about consumers who often did not know the brokers existed. The underlying industry has changed enormously since then, but the structural point remains: information gathered for one context can be assembled into a profile used in another, and the profile may become more operationally important than the person’s own description.

That is a strange kind of social power.

You can disagree with a person’s opinion of you.

It is harder to disagree with a feature vector.

The feature vector does not feel insulted. It does not need to defend itself. It may not even have a sentence you can contest.

This is one reason explanation matters so much when automated systems make consequential decisions. U.S. credit law, for example, requires creditors to provide specific reasons for adverse actions. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has emphasized that the use of complex algorithms does not create an exemption from that duty. A lender cannot simply say, in effect, the model knows. The person affected is owed a reason accurate enough to make the decision intelligible and contestable.

The legal rule is narrower than a general right to understand every machine that affects you, but it contains an important design principle.

If an institution acts against a person, the institution should not be allowed to make its own opacity the person’s problem.

The CFPB’s guidance makes this concrete. A creditor cannot satisfy the obligation merely by saying an applicant failed an internal standard or missed a qualifying score. The reason has to relate to factors actually considered or scored. That does not make the entire model transparent. It does something more practical: it creates a surface on which correction can begin.

Maybe the information was wrong.

Maybe it belonged to someone else.

Maybe the factor is accurate and the person can change it.

Maybe the factor is accurate and the institution is using it in a way the law does not permit.

Without a reason, none of those possibilities has an entry point.

That requirement contains a moral idea larger than credit.

A person should not be trapped by a version of themselves they cannot inspect.

Inspection alone is not enough.

A perfectly visible profile can still be tyrannical if the person has no way to correct a factual error, challenge an inference, supply context, or ask whether the profile belongs in this decision at all.

Transparency can become theater too.

Here are the 387 variables we used.

Here is the confidence score.

Here is the model card.

Here is the page explaining that the system is statistical and may make mistakes.

All of that can be useful and still leave the affected person with no meaningful move.

The deeper right is contestability.

Not a right to win every contest. A right to have one.

This sounds obvious. It becomes difficult as predictive systems improve.

Suppose an inference is useful precisely because it notices patterns no human reviewer would have noticed. The model combines hundreds of weak signals. None is decisive. Together they correlate with an outcome. The institution values the model because the pattern is predictive.

Now the person asks why.

A traditional explanation wants a cause in ordinary language. The system may only have a contribution pattern.

The distance between those two things is not just a technical problem. It is an identity problem. A person lives narratively: I did this because of that. An institution increasingly lives statistically: people with these attributes behave this way often enough to change the expected value of the decision.

The institution can be rational in aggregate and still feel insane from inside an individual life.

This is the world in which the sentence “you don’t know me” becomes simultaneously true and insufficient.

The system may not know you.

It may not need to.

Prediction and understanding are not the same accomplishment.

A weather model does not need to know what rain means to a wedding in order to predict rain. A recommender does not need to understand why a song mattered during a breakup in order to predict that you will play it again. A fraud system does not need a theory of your character to decide that a transaction resembles other fraudulent transactions. A hiring model does not need to understand your ambitions if its job is merely to rank the probability of some proxy for job performance.

The danger begins when predictive usefulness is mistaken for ontological truth.

You clicked, therefore you like.

You paused, therefore you cared.

You bought, therefore you prefer.

You stayed, therefore you were satisfied.

You left, therefore you were not.

You resemble this group, therefore you are this kind of person.

Every inference collapses context.

Humans do this too. We have to. No relationship could survive if every action required a complete theory of the person who performed it. We use shortcuts. We update. We generalize. We sometimes mistake one event for a trait and sometimes excuse a trait as one event.

The difference is scale and persistence.

A friend’s mistaken impression may soften after dinner. A database does not get embarrassed. A scoring system does not wake up at three in the morning and wonder whether it judged you unfairly. It updates if the data and rules tell it to update.

This can make machine judgment feel cruel even when no cruelty exists.

There is nobody inside the score enjoying your exclusion.

That can make the exclusion harder, not easier, to argue with.

Human social life at least offers some messy channels for repair. You can apologize. Explain. Demonstrate change. Recruit a mutual friend. Wait. Perform differently long enough for the old story to become less useful. None of these methods is guaranteed. People can remain unfair forever. But human reputation has traditionally been negotiated through evidence plus relationship.

Machine reputation can become negotiated through data access.

Can the system see the change?

Can the feature update?

Does the model give recent evidence enough weight?

Is there a correction mechanism?

Does the institution care about a fact the data structure was never designed to represent?

Can a person submit context without being forced to turn their life into a form field?

There is another difference between a reputation and an operational profile.

A reputation describes you to someone.

An operational profile can change what happens to you.

That distinction sounds small and is enormous.

A recommender predicts what you will watch and then chooses what you are offered. A fraud system predicts whether a transaction is suspicious and then determines whether the transaction proceeds. A risk score can influence how much scrutiny a person receives, which changes how much adverse evidence is generated, which can influence the next score. A customer profile can determine which service path a person enters, and the service path can affect whether the customer stays.

The representation does not merely sit beside reality.

It can participate in producing the reality from which later predictions are made.

This is where a profile becomes performative.

The system says, this person is unlikely to respond.

So the person receives fewer opportunities to respond.

The system says, this account is risky.

So the account is subjected to more friction and scrutiny.

The system says, this customer is high value.

So the customer reaches better support and becomes more likely to remain high value.

The system says, this student is struggling.

That could trigger useful help. It could also lower expectations in ways that change what the student is offered.

The prediction may begin as a description and end as part of the cause.

That does not make predictive systems illegitimate. It means evaluation must ask more than whether the first prediction was accurate. We also have to ask what the institution did because it believed the prediction.

A map that changes the road is a different kind of map.

These questions will matter more as agents begin acting on our behalf.

A personal software agent needs a model of what we want. It will know preferences, constraints, recurring tasks, spending limits, communication style, commitments, relationships, perhaps health or travel information, and the difference between things we usually do and things we never want done automatically. To be useful, it will need a version of us.

The better the version, the more useful the agent.

The better the version, the stranger the relationship may become.

Imagine an agent that has learned your purchasing patterns so well that it can reorder ordinary goods, negotiate subscriptions, schedule appointments, answer routine messages, and reject offers it predicts you will dislike. At first this feels like relief. It is executing the stable parts of you.

Then you change.

You decide to spend differently. You stop eating something. You become interested in a subject you used to mock. You want to talk to someone you have been avoiding. You are tired of a style that has represented you for years. You do not want the statistically most likely next version of your life.

The agent’s excellence becomes friction.

It knows you too well in the wrong tense.

This is not merely a software bug. It reveals something about human identity we often miss: a self is partly a claim against its own history.

I know what I usually do.

I am not doing that now.

That sentence may be one of the most important forms of freedom available to a predictable creature.

We already experience small versions of it with recommendation systems. You watch several videos about a temporary interest and the platform builds a world around the interest. You buy a gift for someone else and advertisements decide it is your new hobby. You play one song repeatedly because a child in the house loves it and your profile absorbs the child into your musical identity. You search for symptoms on behalf of a parent and an advertising system learns a version of you that is not exactly false and not exactly yours.

The problem is not that the inference failed.

The problem is that behavior is underdetermined.

One action can belong to many stories.

The click is real. The reason is hidden.

Chapter 6 explored our shaky confidence in our own explanations. This chapter adds an uncomfortable symmetry: outside systems can predict our behavior without possessing our explanations at all.

The internal story can be wrong and the external prediction can be right.

Neither therefore owns the person.

This becomes clearer when we think about audiences.

Sociologists and media researchers have long studied the way people present different aspects of themselves in different contexts. Erving Goffman’s work on self-presentation predates social media by decades, but the basic observation survives every platform migration: people are not identical performances everywhere. The self at a funeral is not the self at a basketball game. The language used with a childhood friend is not necessarily the language used with a client. This does not make one version fake. Context is part of meaning.

Networked communication makes context harder to keep separate. Alice Marwick and danah boyd used the phrase “context collapse” to describe, among other things, the way multiple audiences can converge around the same online performance. A joke intended for friends becomes legible to coworkers, strangers, family members, future employers, and people encountering it years later under entirely different conditions.

The platform turns many rooms into one room.

People respond by inventing techniques for audience management: private accounts, close-friends lists, pseudonyms, professional profiles, disappearing posts, separate channels, careful ambiguity, relentless personal branding.

The desire underneath these techniques is not necessarily deception.

It is contextual integrity.

I want this version of me to exist here.

The machine age complicates that desire because systems do not merely observe the version we deliberately present. They combine it with behavioral traces and external data to produce versions we never performed.

A profile can therefore become more confident than a person.

The person says, I’m not sure what I want.

The system says, people with your history choose this.

The person says, that was a phase.

The system says, the phase predicts conversion.

The person says, I was buying for someone else.

The system says, the purchase still belongs to this account.

The person says, I have changed.

The system says, show me a sufficient number of new observations.

There is something comic in this until the profile controls access to something important.

Then identity becomes administration.

This is why debates about privacy are often really debates about power over interpretation. Privacy is not only secrecy. It is also some ability to influence which facts travel, which contexts they enter, and which conclusions they are allowed to support.

A fact can be accurate and still be unfairly used.

Your location at one moment can be real. Your purchase can be real. Your old post can be real. Your medical search can be real. Your debt can be real. The inference made by combining them may still be inappropriate to the decision at hand.

People understand this intuitively because we object when other humans collapse contexts too aggressively.

“You cannot judge my parenting from how I behave at work.”

“You cannot judge my work from one political opinion.”

“You cannot judge my health from what I bought at a grocery store.”

“You cannot judge my current relationship from an old photograph.”

These are not arguments against facts. They are arguments against jurisdiction.

Which version of me has authority here?

Artificial intelligence turns that question into a design choice.

Models need variables. Institutions need decision rules. Agents need memories. Personalization needs profiles. Fraud detection needs patterns. Every useful system creates some representation of the person.

The ethical question is not whether representation can be eliminated.

It cannot.

The question is whether the representation remains corrigible.

Can the person see enough of it to recognize a serious error?

Can they challenge the inference?

Can they reset a preference without pretending their history never happened?

Can they distinguish “I usually want this” from “always do this for me”?

Can they maintain separate contexts rather than allowing one unified profile to govern every domain?

Can a system forget something because the person has a legitimate interest in no longer being organized around it?

Can it remember something important even when the event is statistically rare?

That last problem is easy to miss.

Human lives contain low-frequency facts with high moral weight.

A person may usually spend freely but have one absolute rule about gambling. They may usually accept calendar invitations but never during a religious observance. They may generally enjoy surprise travel but have one medical constraint. They may have communicated casually for years but want a single relationship handled with unusual care.

A model trained on frequency can underweight the exception.

A person often defines themselves by exceptions.

I do many things, but I do not do that.

This is another place where identity exceeds prediction.

The version of you that is easiest to model is the version composed of repeated behavior. Character, however, is often revealed by what a person refuses despite repetition elsewhere.

That sounds grander than it needs to. A dietary restriction is a refusal. A budget is a refusal. A promise is a refusal made in advance. A password policy is a refusal. A boundary in a relationship is a refusal. These rules may appear rarely in the data precisely because they are working.

An agent that learns only from observed behavior can miss the invisible structure that prevented other behavior from occurring.

Absence is difficult data.

The person knows, I never did that because I decided not to.

The system may know only, no examples observed.

This is why the next generation of personal agents cannot be built only from passive surveillance if we want them to represent people rather than merely predict them. They will need explicit instruction, editable memory, durable constraints, and some hierarchy between preferences and commitments.

A preference is: I usually like aisle seats.

A commitment is: do not spend from this account without asking.

A preference is: shorten routine emails.

A commitment is: never send condolences automatically.

A preference is: book the earliest reasonable flight.

A commitment is: do not schedule over my daughter’s birthday.

A system that treats all of these as weighted tendencies will misunderstand the person.

Humans do not consist only of probabilities.

We also consist of rules we have chosen to make less probabilistic.

Of course, we violate our own rules. That has been one of the themes of this book. A promise does not prove behavior. A stated value can become a story told after the fact. People are inconsistent, suggestible, forgetful, and excellent at moving goalposts.

But the fact that commitments are imperfect does not make them reducible to observed frequency.

A person’s stated intention is data of a different kind.

It is prospective rather than merely descriptive.

It says not only who I have been but who I am trying to become.

The version of us other people use often lacks this tense.

Reputation is backward-looking. Credit history is backward-looking. Recommendation history is backward-looking. Search history is backward-looking. A résumé is mostly backward-looking. Even a predictive model uses the past to estimate the future.

A human being can also make a declaration whose main evidence lies ahead.

I am going to stop.

I am going to learn.

I am going to show up.

I am going to be different next time.

These statements can be empty. They can also reorganize a life.

The external world has a rational reason to distrust them. Anybody can claim future virtue. Institutions need evidence. Friends who have heard the promise before may be wise to wait. Lenders should not ignore history because a borrower announces a new era. Employers should not erase every documented failure because an applicant says they have changed.

The tension cannot be eliminated.

A person deserves room to change.

Other people deserve protection from changes that exist only as claims.

Civilization has built strange compromises around this tension: probation, expungement, rehabilitation, references, statutes of limitation, bankruptcy, credit aging, licenses that can be restored, records that can be sealed, professional discipline with paths back, social forgiveness that may or may not arrive.

These are systems for letting the past matter without allowing it to own the future absolutely.

Machine identity will need equivalent concepts.

Not everything should be permanent because permanence is cheap.

That sentence reverses an old scarcity. For most of human history, forgetting happened naturally. Memory required maintenance. Records decayed. Witnesses died. Communities changed. Distance created practical anonymity. The moral problem was often how to remember enough.

Digital systems changed the economics. Storage became easier than forgetting. Replication became easier than recall by a particular human. The moral problem now includes how to stop a cheap memory from becoming an eternal judgment.

Artificial intelligence adds inference to storage.

The system may not need to remember the embarrassing sentence if it can infer the trait from twenty other traces.

So deletion alone cannot solve the identity problem.

The deeper issue is contestability: whether a person can encounter the operational version of themselves and have any meaningful way to say, this representation is wrong, stale, irrelevant, or being used outside the context that gave it meaning.

There is a second problem after correction: propagation.

A bad fact can be fixed in one database and remain alive everywhere it traveled.

A stale address copied into a risk system becomes evidence of inconsistency. A mistaken identity match produces a fraud flag. The flag is exported as a risk signal. Another system never sees the original mistake; it sees only that a trusted system considered the person risky.

The error acquires institutional ancestry.

Eventually nobody owns it.

System A says it received the signal from System B.

System B says the signal was derived from vendor data.

The vendor says it aggregates multiple sources.

The person is asked to correct a conclusion whose birthplace cannot be located.

This is what makes data lineage a human issue rather than merely a database issue.

If a representation can travel, the ability to correct it has to travel too.

Otherwise the institution has built permanent memory without permanent responsibility.

That right will never be absolute. A person should not be able to edit a fraud record into innocence or rewrite a contractual history because the past is inconvenient. Other parties have interests too.

But there is a large moral distance between “you cannot erase reality” and “every inference any system makes about you deserves permanent authority.”

We should not confuse them.

The version of me other people use will always exist.

This is part of being social.

The danger is not representation. The danger is representation without friction, explanation, revision, or context.

A healthy society lets reputations form and also lets people answer them. A healthy institution keeps records and also provides correction. A healthy relationship remembers and also notices change. A healthy personal agent learns patterns and also treats explicit human commitments as something more than noisy observations.

The self is not a private database with one canonical record.

There is no master copy.

There is the person, changing through time, and many partial models held by friends, families, employers, governments, platforms, institutions, strangers, and machines.

Some models will predict the person better than the person predicts themselves.

Some will be wrong in ways the person cannot detect.

Some will preserve an old truth long after its usefulness has expired.

Some will see a pattern the person has been avoiding.

Some will flatter. Some will punish. Some will protect.

No single one becomes the person by being accurate.

That is the error to resist.

A model of me can be useful without being me.

A reputation about me can be deserved without being complete.

A memory of me can be true without being current.

A prediction about me can be statistically excellent and still fail at the exact moment I decide not to behave like my history.

The human claim is not that nobody can know us.

It is that no representation earns permanent jurisdiction merely by predicting well.

The strange future ahead may be one in which a software agent can answer routine questions about you more consistently than you can. What foods do I order? Which flights do I prefer? How formal are my emails? What price range do I usually choose? Which meetings can be declined? Which subscriptions are wasteful? The agent may know because consistency is exactly what machines are good at extracting from history.

And then somebody will ask a different question.

Who do you want to be now?

That question cannot be answered by the cache alone.
