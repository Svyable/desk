# Robustness Pass — Chapters 21 and 22

This note records the closing September 4, 2026 robustness pass for *I’m Not a Robot*. The revisions strengthen the distinction between synthetic social cues and the structure of the relationship behind them, then add a constraint to the book’s central phrase “revision under responsibility”: revision requires power, and responsibility should be assigned with attention to who actually had meaningful control.

## Chapter 21 — I Will Know the Difference

The chapter remains anchored in Joseph Weizenbaum’s 1966 ELIZA paper, Clifford Nass and Youngme Moon’s work on social responses to computers, and Nicholas Epley, Adam Waytz, and John T. Cacioppo’s theory of anthropomorphism. Those sources support the narrow proposition that people can respond socially to machines and other nonhuman agents without establishing anything about machine consciousness.

The revision adds three governance distinctions.

First, disclosure can become stale. A user may know they are interacting with AI while remaining unaware that the provider materially changed memory, incentives, advertising, retention, personality, or other terms that shape the relationship. The phrase “consent drift” in the manuscript is a conceptual label, not a claim that one established legal doctrine uses that term.

Second, the chapter now treats exit as part of relationship governance. If years of remembered interaction exist only inside one provider, leaving can carry autobiographical and emotional switching costs in addition to ordinary software switching costs. Portability is therefore presented as a potential defense against attachment-based lock-in, not as a claim that every intimate system can or should export a complete relationship history.

Third, the chapter rejects the binary “human relationships are real, machine relationships are fake.” Human relationships can also be manipulative, transactional, coercive, professionally bounded, or one-sided. The more useful analysis separates reciprocity, independence, consent, incentives, authority, accountability, data control, and exit.

The EU AI Act discussion remains limited to the specific transparency landscape already cited in the main research brief. It should not be read as claiming that current law already resolves companion-system governance, portability, or every form of synthetic intimacy.

Falsification pressure: the attachment-and-lock-in argument should be weakened if long-term companion-system users show negligible switching costs, little dependence on retained interaction history, and no meaningful behavioral response to provider-controlled changes in system personality or incentives. The dimensional relationship framework should also remain open to evidence that some categories can be governed adequately by simpler disclosure rules.

## Chapter 22 — I’m Not a Robot

The revision adds an explicit inequality constraint to “revision under responsibility.” The book no longer treats noticing a script as sufficient evidence that a person could realistically change it. Time, money, safety, health, legal rights, bargaining power, social support, institutional appeal, and access to alternatives can all affect whether revision is practically available.

This is a normative synthesis rather than a single empirical claim. It is intended to prevent the book’s agency argument from collapsing structural constraint into individual failure.

The chapter now states a proportional principle: responsibility should track meaningful control. The manuscript does not claim a formula exists for assigning responsibility. It argues that authority over goals, process, information, timing, exit, and repair should matter when deciding how much responsibility belongs to an individual operator versus upstream designers, managers, institutions, or policymakers.

The revision also makes institutional revision explicit. Appeals, policy change, model reevaluation, metric retirement, collective bargaining, professional norms, and legal change are examples of mechanisms through which groups can revise scripts that individuals cannot meaningfully alter alone. The phrase “That was us. It is no longer the instruction.” is an extension of the book’s recurring personal line into collective responsibility; it is not meant to erase continuing obligations created under the old rule.

Falsification pressure: the structural constraint argument should be weakened if, in a given domain, individuals consistently demonstrate effective ability to revise behavior despite strong economic, legal, or institutional dependence and without meaningful differences in available resources or exit options. Conversely, evidence of concentrated decision power with diffuse downstream liability would strengthen the chapter’s claim that responsibility should move upstream when local control is ceremonial.

## Closing editorial guardrail

The book’s final claim is not that humans are special because they can revise while machines cannot. The manuscript deliberately avoids turning revision into another capability moat. The claim is about the form of human social accountability developed across the book: people inherit scripts, can sometimes contest them, can be constrained from contesting them, and remain entangled in obligations created by earlier actions and institutions.

The closing chapter should therefore resist three flattering readings:

- noticing a pattern does not prove freedom from it;
- having a nominal choice does not prove meaningful control;
- being responsible does not mean being solely responsible.

The final standard remains modest: describe the actual human and institutional arrangement accurately enough that neither capability nor blame is assigned by mythology.
