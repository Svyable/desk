## Thesis

Cable bacteria force a revision in scale: metabolism need not be confined to one cell or even to molecular diffusion distance. These multicellular filaments separate oxidation from reduction by centimeters, conduct electrons through an internal fiber network, reshape sediment chemistry, and turn mud into something closer to a distributed electrochemical device. The book asks what changes when an organism's physiology is spatially larger than the environments microbiology traditionally assigned to a cell.

## Reader promise

The reader will leave able to distinguish the measured phenomenon from its metaphors. Cable bacteria really do conduct electronic current over centimeter distances. That does not make them tiny copper wires, nervous systems, power grids, or evidence for mystical planetary electricity. Their stranger importance is specific: they physically couple distant redox reactions, making one end of a filament chemically dependent on conditions thousands of cell lengths away.

## Evidence discipline

1. **Verified / documented:** centimeter-scale electrical coupling in sediment; cable-bacterium filaments spanning redox zones; direct current measurements; conductive periplasmic fibers; sulfur-ligated nickel associated with conductivity; measurable effects on sulfur, oxygen, iron and pH chemistry.
2. **Credible but incomplete / model-dependent:** exact charge-transport mechanism inside nickel-bearing proteins; how commonly ecological partners transfer electrons to cable bacteria; global quantitative importance for element cycling; evolutionary pathways into multicellular electrical division of labor.
3. **Disputed / not established:** cable bacteria as universal sediment conductors; technological claims implying immediate scalable devices; analogies to neurons or intelligence; strong climate-mitigation claims from local methane effects without system-level budgets.
4. **Speculative synthesis:** cable bacteria as evidence that biological individuality can be organized around an electrical circuit rather than local cellular completeness, and that some ecosystems contain physiological structures whose meaningful scale is centimeters rather than micrometers.
