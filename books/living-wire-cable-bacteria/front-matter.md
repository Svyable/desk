# Living Wire: Cable Bacteria and the Electric Seafloor

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a kind of fact that arrives wearing the wrong costume. In 2010, researchers looking at marine sediment reported that oxygen at the surface seemed to be chemically coupled to sulfide more than a centimeter below it. The response was too fast for diffusion. Something was carrying charge through the mud.

At first, even the right observation invited the wrong mechanism. Minerals, soluble shuttles, microbial nanowires, communities passing electrons hand to hand: all were plausible. Two years later the conductor had a body. Long bacterial filaments crossed the chemically empty zone between sulfide below and oxygen above. When the filaments were cut, the electrical coupling collapsed.

This book is about that body and the scale problem it creates.

Microbiology trained us to imagine metabolism as local. A cell takes in an electron donor, finds an electron acceptor, and closes its accounting within microscopic distance. Cable bacteria cheat that picture. Cells buried in anoxic sediment can oxidize sulfide while cells near the surface reduce oxygen, with electrons conducted along a continuous multicellular filament. The organism's useful chemistry is spatially distributed. Its parts live in different chemical worlds and remain metabolically joined.

The metaphors are irresistible: wire, cable, grid, battery. They are useful until they start doing the thinking. Cable bacteria are not copper conductors miniaturized by nature. Their current is small. Their environment is wet, chemically messy, and alive. Their conductive fibers are proteins; their unusual chemistry involves nickel; their ecology is entangled with sulfur, iron, oxygen, carbon, phosphorus, arsenic, and other microbes. The right question is not whether nature invented an electrical utility company. It is what biology becomes once electron transport can extend thousands of cell lengths.

Four evidentiary categories govern the argument. Direct measurements and replicated observations stay separate from plausible but incomplete mechanism. Ecological extrapolations stay separate from global budgets. Technological possibilities stay separate from devices that actually work. Speculation about biological individuality is labeled as speculation.

The mud is strange enough without assistance.
