# Fail-Safe Biology

The conductive fibers do not run as one fragile strand. They form a parallel network, and at cell-to-cell junctions a conspicuous cartwheel-like structure links them.

That topology matters because a multicellular conductor has a reliability problem. A filament crossing millimeters or centimeters of sediment cannot afford to become electrically useless every time one microscopic pathway is damaged. Parallel fibers create redundancy. Junction architecture allows current to be redistributed rather than relying on a single uninterrupted thread.

Calling this 'fail-safe' should not imply intentional engineering. Natural selection does not hold design reviews. But the functional resemblance is real enough to study: redundant pathways can preserve conduction after local damage, and a networked geometry changes the relationship between component failure and whole-filament failure.

The organism therefore solves two different scale problems. First, it transports electrons far beyond a single cell. Second, it maintains electrical continuity across repeated cellular boundaries. Those are not the same challenge. A good conductor that stopped at every septum would still fail biologically.

This is where the cable analogy becomes productive again. Engineered long-distance systems spend enormous effort on connections. The bulk material may be excellent while interfaces dominate failure. Cable bacteria faced their own interface problem and evolved a structure that makes the multicellular boundary electrically traversable.

We do not yet know the complete evolutionary sequence that produced this architecture. It would be a mistake to narrate the finished system backward as inevitable. But once the structure exists, its logic is visible: long-range metabolism depends not only on conductive material but on the continuity rules of a multicellular body.
