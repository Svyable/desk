# Meet the Cable

In 2012 the conductor acquired anatomy.

Christian Pfeffer and colleagues found long multicellular filaments belonging to the Desulfobulbaceae in the same sediment zones where long-distance electron transport occurred. The filaments were not decorative residents of an electrically active habitat. Their abundance tracked the phenomenon, their length matched the relevant spatial scale, and their internal structure contained longitudinal features consistent with electron transport.

The name came later and stuck immediately: cable bacteria.

The metaphor is good because the organism is physically long and electrically continuous. It is dangerous because it smuggles in expectations from engineered wire. Copper does not eat sulfide. Copper does not position one end in an electron-donor zone and the other near oxygen. Copper does not divide labor among thousands of living cells while repairing, growing, moving, and reproducing. A cable bacterium is not a wire with metabolism attached. The wire and the metabolism are one evolved object.

A filament may contain thousands of cells arranged end to end. In active sediment it can span the distance between deeper sulfide and surface oxygen, allowing the chemistry of respiration to be separated spatially. This means that a cell's immediate neighborhood no longer defines the complete metabolic opportunity available to it. A cell deep in anoxia can participate in an oxygen-dependent process because another region of the filament reaches the oxygen.

That statement is easy to say and hard to absorb. The ordinary bacterial cell is often treated as a complete metabolic unit. Cable bacteria make that assumption conditional. Individual cells remain real biological entities, but some of the filament's essential energetic work is distributed across the collective body.

The organism did not solve oxygen limitation by transporting oxygen downward. It solved it by transporting electrons upward.

That reversal is the trick.
