# Current Without Copper

For years the strongest case for cable-bacterium conduction was indirect. The sediments behaved electrically; cutting filaments broke the effect; the filaments contained longitudinal structures. The obvious next question was brutally literal: can current be measured through one?

In 2019 researchers reported direct electrical measurements on intact filaments as long as 10.1 millimeters. Nanoampere currents passed through them. The conductive structures were traced to parallel fibers in the cell envelope, with reported conductivity reaching values remarkable for a biological material. Exposure to air degraded conductance while vacuum did not, supporting electronic rather than ionic charge transport through the fibers.

That distinction matters. A wet biological filament is full of ions. Without careful controls, apparent conductivity can be mistaken for ordinary ionic movement through moisture. The measurements moved the field from a geochemical inference about electrical coupling toward a material claim: the organism contains structures that themselves conduct electrons over macroscopic distances by microbial standards.

The scale inversion is worth lingering on. A centimeter is negligible in electrical engineering and extravagant in protein-based biology. Nature did not discover a better copper wire. It discovered a conductor good enough to connect chemically separated habitats inside a living body.

'Good enough' is the more interesting category. Evolution does not optimize conductivity in isolation. The fibers must be grown, repaired, embedded in cells, integrated across junctions, and powered by chemistry available in mud. A material that looks mediocre beside metal may be extraordinary once the construction constraints include being alive.
