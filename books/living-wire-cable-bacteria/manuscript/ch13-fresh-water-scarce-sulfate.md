# Fresh Water, Scarce Sulfate

Cable bacteria were discovered in marine sediment, where sulfate is abundant enough to support vigorous sulfur cycling. Freshwater should have been a less comfortable home.

Yet freshwater sediments also support cable bacteria, sometimes at substantial densities. That result forced a second look at what the organisms were doing to their own resource landscape. In experiments published in 2020, active cable bacteria raised sulfate concentrations and stimulated sulfate reduction rates roughly 4.5-fold without a corresponding change in the abundance or community composition of sulfate reducers. The interpretation was not that cable bacteria created sulfur from nothing. Their electrogenic metabolism recycled sulfur compounds and altered retention in ways that made scarce sulfate more available to the existing community.

This is an ecological feedback rather than a simple appetite. The organism consumes reduced sulfur and, through oxidation, mineral dissolution and electrically influenced ion transport, can help regenerate the oxidized sulfur species that sulfate reducers need. One guild's metabolism changes the limiting substrate available to another.

The freshwater case matters because it breaks an easy environmental assumption. Cable bacteria are not merely specialists exploiting an already generous marine sulfur cycle. Their activity can participate in maintaining a cryptic sulfur cycle where the external sulfate pool is small.

It also warns against treating geochemical inventory as metabolic destiny. A system with little standing sulfate may still sustain substantial sulfate reduction if the same sulfur is recycled rapidly enough.

Ecology often hides behind concentration. Cable bacteria make flux harder to ignore.
