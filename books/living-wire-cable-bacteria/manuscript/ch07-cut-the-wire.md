# Cut the Wire

There are experiments that become famous because the apparatus is enormous. This one deserves to be remembered because somebody dragged a thin wire through mud.

If long bacterial filaments carried the current, severing them should break the circuit. The intervention did not need to know the molecular conductor or read a genome. It needed only to destroy continuity while disturbing as little else as possible.

After horizontal cutting, the characteristic long-distance coupling collapsed: oxygen consumption changed, the pH signature weakened, and the broad suboxic zone contracted. The result did not by itself prove every detail of the cable-bacterium model, but it made a diffuse mineral or soluble-shuttle explanation much harder to sustain as the primary carrier.

The experiment is epistemically useful because it attacks architecture rather than correlation. Finding cable bacteria wherever electrical signatures appear could still leave room for a passenger organism thriving in the same chemistry. Cutting asks whether the long object is functionally necessary on the timescale of the response.

Later work would directly measure currents through isolated filaments and identify the conductive fibers. But the cut was already doing something powerful: converting an ecological association into a causal perturbation.

There is a lesson here for anomaly research more generally, though it should not be inflated into a slogan. Weird phenomena become tractable when competing explanations have different break points. What can be cut? What can be shielded? What should survive replacement of the matrix? What changes faster than a proposed mechanism allows?

The cable bacteria story advanced because researchers found the place where one hypothesis could be physically broken.

They cut there.
