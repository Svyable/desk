# Mud That Answered Too Fast

The first surprise was not an organism. It was a timing problem.

In sediment from Aarhus Bay, oxygen disappeared near the surface while sulfide was consumed deeper down, across a gap where neither reactant should have been able to meet the other quickly. When Lars Peter Nielsen and colleagues changed the oxygen concentration in the overlying water, sulfide more than a centimeter below responded in less than an hour. Diffusion could not comfortably explain that speed. The sediment behaved as if a circuit had been opened and closed.

The 2010 Nature paper was careful. It did not yet know the conductor. The team proposed a collection of possible microbial and mineral pathways: bacterial nanowires, pyrite, soluble electron shuttles, outer-membrane cytochromes. The secure result was narrower and better. Oxidation and reduction were spatially separated, yet mass balances and microsensor profiles indicated that electric current coupled them. In the experiments, more than forty percent of sediment oxygen consumption could be associated with electrons arriving from the anoxic zone.

That number matters because it prevents the phenomenon from being filed as a laboratory curiosity. The current was not a faint side channel discovered by heroic instrumentation after everything important had already happened. It was participating materially in sediment metabolism.

The deeper conceptual trouble came from scale. Biochemistry teaches electron transport as a sequence of local handoffs across molecular distances. Even extracellular electron transfer, already established in bacteria such as Geobacter and Shewanella, had trained researchers to think in micrometers, surfaces, particles, and nearby partners. Here the chemistry on one side of an apparently inactive sediment interval was changing in response to conditions on the other side.

The mud answered too fast because something inside it had made distance metabolically cheap.

That was the observation. Almost everything else—including the organism—still had to be found.
