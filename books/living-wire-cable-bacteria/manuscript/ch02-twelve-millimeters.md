# Twelve Millimeters

Twelve millimeters is an absurd distance in microbial physiology. It is ordinary to a human thumb and enormous to a bacterium.

The Aarhus sediment profiles contained an oxic layer at the top, sulfidic sediment below, and between them a suboxic zone depleted in the obvious reactants. Conventional explanations had to move molecules across that interval. The measured response instead suggested that charge was moving while the chemistry stayed separated. Oxygen reduction occurred near the surface. Sulfide oxidation occurred deeper down. The missing connection was electrical.

The distinction sounds semantic until one draws the bookkeeping. Diffusion moves matter. Electrical conduction moves charge. If the donor and acceptor must physically approach one another, distance throttles metabolism. If electrons can travel while the reactants remain where they are, the geometry changes. A chemically unfavorable gap becomes bridgeable.

This was not yet proof of cable bacteria. It was a geochemical inference, and that limitation is important. Extraordinary-looking measurements often acquire a satisfying mechanism too quickly in retrospect. In 2010 the current was inferred from coupled oxygen and sulfide dynamics, pH structure, and mass balance. The carriers remained unidentified.

That uncertainty improved the science because it generated discriminating experiments. If mineral grains made the pathway, replacing sediment with nonconductive material should matter. If a dissolved shuttle carried electrons, physical severing should not instantly break a long conductor. If living filaments were required, filters and cuts could reveal their dimensions.

Twelve millimeters became less a measurement than a demand. Whatever the mechanism was, it had to operate coherently across a distance vastly larger than a single bacterial cell. The explanation would have to be not merely biochemical but architectural.
