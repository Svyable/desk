# Anode Below, Cathode Above

The simplest way to understand a cable bacterium is as a biological redox bridge.

At depth, sulfide is oxidized. Near the surface, oxygen is reduced. Between those locations runs the filament. The chemistry resembles an electrochemical cell stretched vertically through sediment, except the conductor is alive and the boundaries move as oxygen, sulfide, pH, and microbial activity change.

The lower cells occupy what electrochemists would recognize as an anodic region: electrons are liberated from reduced sulfur. The upper cells occupy a cathodic region: electrons are consumed by oxygen reduction. The voltage difference is not imposed by a power supply. It emerges from the chemical disequilibrium between the two environments.

This framing explains why the organism transforms more than sulfur. Moving electrons without moving an equal package of atoms requires compensating ionic fluxes. Proton production and consumption shift pH. Iron sulfides can dissolve. Minerals precipitate elsewhere. The cable changes the sediment because an electrical circuit cannot be inserted into an electrolyte without consequences.

Calling the system a battery is tempting. It is also incomplete. A battery is built to discharge stored chemical potential through a designed path. Cable bacteria grow inside an already disequilibrated environment and continuously harvest a gradient that the larger ecosystem helps maintain. Their 'electrodes' are zones of living metabolism rather than metal surfaces.

Still, the analogy earns something. It makes visible a fact that conventional microbial diagrams hide: the oxidation half-reaction and reduction half-reaction do not have to occur in the same microscopic neighborhood. Once a filament can conduct charge efficiently enough, respiration becomes a spatially distributed machine.

The sediment is not merely where the organism lives. It is part of the circuit geometry.
