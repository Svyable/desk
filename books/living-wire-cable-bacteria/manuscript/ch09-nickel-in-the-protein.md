# Nickel in the Protein

The wire turned out to contain a metal, but not the one a human engineer would have guessed.

In 2021, spectroscopy and chemical imaging tied cable-bacterium conductivity to a sulfur-ligated nickel group inside the conductive protein core. Removing nickel or changing its oxidation state reduced conductivity. The fibers also appeared to possess an insulating protein shell around the conductive core.

The result did not close the mechanism. How charge moves through the nickel-bearing protein architecture over centimeter distances remains an active question. But it substantially narrowed the mystery. The conductor was not an unspecified biological 'nanowire.' It had a recurring chemical feature whose perturbation changed electrical performance.

Nickel is already familiar in biology. Enzymes use it in active sites. What is unusual here is the role suggested by the cable: not a localized catalytic center performing chemistry in one protein pocket, but part of a structure supporting long-range conduction through a multicellular organism.

The phrase 'living wire' becomes less metaphorical at this point, but it should also become more precise. The filament is not homogeneous. It contains a network of specialized fibers. Those fibers contain protein architecture. The protein contains a metal-bearing motif. Conductivity depends on chemistry at that nested scale.

A centimeter-long electrical phenomenon therefore resolves downward into molecular composition without disappearing. That is the pleasure of the case. Explanation does not make the strange fact ordinary. It shows how much architecture was required to make the fact possible.
