# One Organism, Ten Thousand Cells

Multicellularity is usually introduced with differentiation: muscle cell, neuron, gut, leaf. Cable bacteria complicate that sequence because their multicellularity is first encountered as electrical continuity.

A filament can contain thousands of cells yet behave as a single electrochemical span. Surface cells have access to oxygen but not much sulfide. Deeper cells have access to sulfide but not oxygen. The filament makes those asymmetries complementary. Electrons liberated by oxidation below travel along the body and are consumed by reduction reactions nearer the surface.

This is not merely a crowd of identical cells cooperating in the same patch. The useful environmental resources are separated in space. The organism survives by being long enough to inhabit both worlds at once.

That makes body length a metabolic parameter. Too short, and the filament cannot bridge the redox gap. Too badly oriented, and the necessary chemistry falls outside reach. Too discontinuous, and current stops. In a cable bacterium, geometry is physiology.

The phrase “one organism” should still be handled carefully. Microbial individuality is not settled by a metaphor. Filaments grow, divide, and contain repeated cellular compartments. But electrically, the filament possesses functions no isolated cell can reproduce. The relevant unit depends on the question being asked.

For gene expression, one cell may matter. For electron flow across sediment, the filament matters. For geochemistry, the entire population of filaments may matter. Nature has no obligation to choose the scale most convenient for our nouns.

Cable bacteria are useful because they expose that mismatch brutally. Ten thousand cells can be counted one by one and still form a physiological object whose essential reaction cannot be understood one cell at a time.
