# The pH Signature

Before cable bacteria were visible, they left a chemical handwriting sample.

Active sediments develop a characteristic pH structure: alkalinization near the oxygen-consuming surface zone and acidification deeper where sulfide is oxidized. Those gradients are consequences of spatially separated half-reactions. The organism does not simply remove sulfide and oxygen. It rearranges proton chemistry across distance.

That matters because pH controls what minerals dissolve, what precipitates, what ions remain mobile, and what neighboring microbes experience. Long-distance electron transport therefore has a second-order reach larger than the filament itself. A bacterial body conducts charge; the resulting electrochemistry remodels the sediment around it.

The pH profile also became one of the field's practical signatures. Researchers could use microsensors to look for the distinctive combination of oxygen, sulfide and pH zones associated with electrogenic sulfur oxidation. But a signature is not an identity card. Similar-looking chemistry can arise through other processes, and cable-bacterium abundance, microscopy and molecular identification remain important when assigning mechanism.

This is a recurring discipline in the book: a proxy becomes useful precisely when its limits stay visible. The pH peak helped reveal the electrical process. It should not be promoted into proof wherever it appears.

Still, the chemical fingerprint is striking because it turns the filament inside out. The organism may be microscopically thin, but its metabolism leaves centimeter-scale gradients. You can miss the body and still measure what the body has done to the mud.
