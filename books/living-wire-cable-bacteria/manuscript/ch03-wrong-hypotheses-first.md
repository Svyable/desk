# Wrong Hypotheses First

Good discoveries are often remembered backward. Once the cable bacterium is visible in the photograph, the earlier uncertainty can look like delay. It was not. The wrong hypotheses were part of the instrument.

The 2010 work had already shown that known rates of molecular diffusion could not comfortably account for the coupling between oxygen near the sediment-water interface and sulfide deeper below. But several electrically plausible mechanisms remained. Conductive minerals could form a granular network. Soluble redox molecules could shuttle electrons. Separate microbes could cooperate through extracellular structures. A mixed system could combine all three.

Each possibility implied a different vulnerability. A mineral network should depend on the sediment matrix. A soluble shuttle should survive a narrow mechanical cut. A filamentous biological conductor should have a characteristic diameter, should appear where current appears, and should fail when its continuity is broken.

The experiments that followed were almost rude in their simplicity. Researchers inserted barriers with different pore sizes. They replaced portions of sediment with nonconductive glass microspheres. They drew a thin wire horizontally through active sediment, physically cutting anything long and biological that crossed its path. The long-distance electrical signal collapsed after cutting. Glass beads did not abolish it. Filters narrowed the dimensions of whatever had to pass.

This is one reason the cable-bacteria story is more useful than its headline. “Bacteria conduct electricity” is memorable but scientifically cheap. The stronger story is a chain of alternatives being forced to make different predictions. The final explanation did not become credible because it sounded futuristic. It became credible because the mud behaved differently when the supposed conductor was severed.

Wrong hypotheses were not embarrassment. They supplied the knife.
