# 2 — Deseret

The bureaucratic center of this story was not a ship. It was a test organization in Utah.

Deseret Test Center operated out of Fort Douglas and planned Project 112's chemical and biological vulnerability work. The geography is useful because it breaks the movie version immediately. A secret shipboard test in the Pacific begins, administratively, with desks, test plans, meteorology, logistics, agent handling, sampling doctrine, service coordination and somebody deciding what constitutes a useful answer.

Project 112 was larger than SHAD. Official VA history describes land- and sea-based tests conducted from 1962 into the early 1970s under the Deseret Test Center. Land tests examined behavior of agents under different environmental and climatic conditions. Shipboard tests examined the vulnerability of warships and defensive procedures. The distinction matters. SHAD was not a free-floating code name for every Cold War chemical or biological experiment, and every disturbing military exposure story from the period should not be swept into it.

That discipline pays off because the actual inventory is strange enough.

The later official record lists biological warfare agents including *Coxiella burnetii*, *Francisella tularensis* and staphylococcal enterotoxin B, as well as chemical agents including sarin, VX, tabun and soman across Project 112 testing. Individual tests also used biological simulants and tracers. The word *simulant* is where sloppy histories often begin. It does not mean imaginary. It means a material selected to stand in for another threat in some relevant experimental dimension. Whether a simulant was harmless, low-risk, or inadequately understood is a separate question that must be answered material by material and year by year.

Likewise, the presence of sarin somewhere in the Project 112 program does not license a sentence saying every SHAD sailor was exposed to sarin. A program inventory is not an individual exposure history.

Deseret's job was to turn military questions into experiments. How far does an aerosol penetrate? What does wind do to the cloud? Can a ship maintain a protected envelope? Where should samplers be placed? Does a mask work under operational conditions? How effective is decontamination? These questions are technically legitimate. They are also exactly the kind of questions that create ethical ambiguity when the platform under test is crewed.

Modern research ethics language can make the past look cleaner than it was. We imagine a bright border: on one side, research subjects; on the other, personnel doing their jobs. Operational test culture often blurs that border. If a sailor remains at his station while a ship's vulnerability is tested, is he a subject? A participant? A crew member? A technician? If a nasal swab is taken from him to validate a mechanical sampler, the category becomes harder to evade.

The category mattered because categories determine paperwork.

A research subject belongs to one moral and administrative system. A servicemember executing an operational test belongs to another. A biological sample can move a person from one category toward the other without anyone formally saying that it has happened.

This is one reason the later record hunt matters so much. Deseret Test Center closed in 1973. Decades later, when veterans and VA needed exposure information, the original organizational container was gone. Defense health officials describe the problem plainly: classified information was not completely cataloged or located in one facility, and investigators had to search forty-year-old records held by different services in different places.

An institution can disappear before its consequences do.

That sentence is easy to make melodramatic. The files themselves are more interesting. A closed test center leaves behind reports whose original purpose was to preserve operational knowledge. The military wanted to remember what a cloud did to a ship. It did not necessarily build the archive to remember what happened to Seaman Jones at frame 214 of the experiment.

The later medical question therefore collides with the original engineering ontology. Engineers index systems by test condition. Medicine indexes consequences by person. Intelligence and classification index information by sensitivity and need to know. Veterans benefits index claims by service connection. Four filing systems can describe the same afternoon without being able to join their tables.

Project SHAD is what happens when, forty years later, society tries to perform that join.
