# Recontact With Reality

A pilot who flies by instruments is not less connected to reality than a pilot staring out the window.

The instruments are what make reality visible under conditions where unaided senses fail. Attitude, altitude, airspeed, heading, engine state, navigation: each gauge or display compresses physical processes into signals a human can use.

The discipline is not to reject instruments.

It is to cross-check them.

A single instrument can fail. Sensors can freeze, disagree, drift, or produce values that look plausible. Experienced crews are trained to compare sources and ask whether the indications cohere with one another and with what the aircraft is doing.

This is recontact with reality: using representations while preserving independent routes to the thing represented.

Every domain in this book needs an equivalent.

A company has financial statements. Recontact may mean talking to customers, watching operations, examining raw transactions, visiting suppliers, reading support tickets, or testing products directly.

A government has aggregate statistics. Recontact may mean field offices, surveys, audits, local reporting, public hearings, case review, and independent research.

A model has benchmark scores. Recontact may mean real deployment, adversarial tests, independent evaluation, monitoring, and outcome data.

The key is independence.

If every reality check is generated by the same system, the organization has built a hall of mirrors.

Imagine a retailer that measures customer satisfaction through an email survey sent only to customers who completed purchases. The company also tracks repeat purchases among those customers. Both metrics improve.

Meanwhile people who abandoned purchases because of a confusing checkout never enter either dataset.

Two measures agree because they share the same blind spot.

An independent route would sample abandoned sessions, observe usability tests, monitor complaints, or compare conversion across alternate flows.

Redundancy only helps when failure modes differ.

This is why diversified evidence matters more than simply more data.

Ten dashboards built from the same transactional database do not provide ten perspectives.

An interview, physical inspection, alternative dataset, outside audit, controlled experiment, and historical comparison may each carry less volume while adding greater epistemic independence.

Good institutions design disagreement among sensors.

This idea can be formalized in engineering but is equally valuable in management. If a supply-chain risk model says a supplier is healthy, procurement may still examine delivery variability, financial statements, ownership, geographic concentration, worker turnover, quality drift, and local intelligence.

The measures do not have to agree.

Disagreement tells you where to investigate.

Organizations often experience disagreement as a problem to be reconciled quickly. The meeting seeks one number.

This instinct destroys information.

If finance sees margin improvement while customers report declining quality, the tension should survive long enough to find the mechanism. If employee engagement rises while voluntary exits rise, do not average the story. If a model predicts low risk while experienced operators are worried, ask why.

Conflicting representations are diagnostic assets.

This suggests a practical rule: preserve the anomaly until it is explained, not until it is outvoted.

Anomaly does not mean truth. A single vivid story can mislead. A respected expert can have a bad intuition. Customer complaints can overrepresent extremes. Field staff can resist change for self-interested reasons.

The point is not to privilege qualitative evidence.

It is to prevent aggregate evidence from making contrary observations disappear by default.

Ethnography is especially useful as a recontact method because it slows interpretation.

A researcher sits with people in context rather than asking them to choose among predefined answers. The method can reveal unanticipated variables: who else participates in a purchase, which workarounds are normal, what people are embarrassed to say in formal surveys, which physical constraints shape behavior.

The strength is discovery.

The weakness is generalization.

A few households cannot establish population prevalence. This is why ethnography and quantitative research work well together. One finds candidate mechanisms; the other tests distribution.

Management often wants one method to do both jobs.

It asks a survey to discover what nobody thought to ask or asks a focus group to estimate market size.

Methodological fit is another abstraction discipline.

Fieldwork has an organizational analogue: gemba, management by walking around, ride-alongs, shadowing, call listening, customer visits. These practices are vulnerable to executive theater. The factory cleans before the visit. Managers select friendly customers. employees behave differently around senior leaders.

Yet imperfect contact can still be better than none if leaders know the distortion.

The point is not to “see the truth” in an afternoon.

It is to acquire sensory evidence the dashboard cannot provide.

A chief executive who hears five support calls may not know the frequency of the problems. She may understand their texture in a way a ticket category did not convey.

Texture matters when designing interventions.

The same principle applies to investment. Financial statements and market prices provide abstraction. Recontact can include reading contracts, visiting facilities, speaking with customers and competitors, testing the product, examining hiring, and understanding incentives.

None guarantees superior returns.

The discipline matters because valuation models are most dangerous when they become self-contained.

A spreadsheet can make almost any acquisition attractive with assumptions that sound individually plausible. Recontact asks whether customers behave as the model assumes, whether switching costs are real, whether the product is loved or tolerated, whether capital expenditure can actually be deferred, whether the management team has earned credibility.

Numbers should send you somewhere.

Science institutionalizes recontact through replication and prediction. Engineering uses testing and failure analysis. Law uses adversarial process. Journalism uses source verification. Medicine uses examination and diagnostics. Democracy uses elections, protest, public comment, and investigative oversight.

These institutions differ, but all create channels through which reality can contradict an official representation.

When those channels weaken, abstraction risk rises.

Authoritarian systems are especially vulnerable because bad news becomes career-threatening. Local officials learn to improve reports. Central leaders receive information shaped by what subordinates believe is safe to say.

The state becomes informationally blind while appearing administratively precise.

Companies can reproduce the pattern without political terror.

A charismatic founder punishes dissent. A sales leader refuses bad forecasts. A board prefers smooth guidance. Managers learn to sand the edges before information travels upward.

Soon everybody is surprised at once.

Psychological safety has economic value because it affects data quality.

If people cannot report uncertainty, the organization has corrupted its sensors.

This is a more concrete reason to care about culture than vague appeals to happiness. Candor determines whether weak signals enter decisions.

The challenge is preventing candor from becoming endless debate.

Organizations need closure. A decision must eventually be made.

This creates a two-stage discipline: widen before deciding, narrow after deciding.

Before a consequential decision, seek counterevidence, alternative models, local knowledge, and failure modes. After deciding, coordinate execution unless new evidence crosses a threshold that justifies reopening.

Confusing the stages creates dysfunction.

A team that narrows too early becomes dogmatic. A team that never narrows becomes paralyzed.

Good leaders know when dissent is exploration and when dissent is sabotage of a settled choice.

The threshold should depend on reversibility.

If a decision is easily reversible, extensive analysis can be waste. launch a small test. If a decision commits years of capital, changes safety exposure, or creates irreversible harm, wider recontact is worth the delay.

Reversibility is an epistemic asset because it lets action generate information.

This is why startups can sometimes learn faster than incumbents. A small company has fewer commitments and can test cheaply. A large company has more data but more cost attached to changing course.

Knowledge is not only information volume.

It is the ability to update behavior.

This distinction helps explain why institutions can become “data driven” and still fail to learn. They collect enormous evidence but use it mainly to justify existing plans.

Learning requires a decision that can move.

A metric should have an associated action. A forecast should have a condition. A postmortem should change a process. A customer study should alter a product hypothesis.

Otherwise evidence becomes decoration.

Decision journals are useful because they preserve the pre-decision state. Write down what you believe, the key uncertainties, expected outcomes, and signals that would change your mind.

Later, compare.

This protects against hindsight and narrative repair.

Humans are talented at making the past look inevitable. The losing branch disappears. A founder remembers always believing the pivot was necessary. An investor remembers skepticism before a crash. A manager remembers warning about a risk after the risk materializes.

Written forecasts expose memory's editing.

They also improve character.

A person who expects to review decisions later has an incentive to distinguish confidence from performance. You can make a good decision and get a bad outcome. You can make a bad decision and get lucky.

This separation reduces superstition.

Organizations need it because outcome-based cultures encourage risk hiding. If every failed experiment damages a career, employees will choose experiments whose success can be guaranteed or politically reframed.

Learning becomes impossible.

The answer is not to celebrate failure indiscriminately. Failure that teaches nothing is still failure. Negligence is not experimentation.

The standard is whether uncertainty was explicit and information gained.

A good experiment has a question.

A good pilot has a decision attached.

A good postmortem identifies mechanism rather than merely assigning blame.

These are recontact technologies.

Markets provide another. A customer declining to buy is reality contact. A competitor taking share is reality contact. A bond spread widening is reality contact. Profit and loss can cut through elaborate stories.

But markets are not final arbiters of every value.

A public good may be socially valuable and commercially unprofitable. Pollution costs can sit outside the firm's accounts. A monopoly can earn profits while making customers worse off.

Recontact itself needs several maps.

This is the mature end of the argument.

There is no pure unmediated reality available to institutions. Every observation arrives through senses, instruments, language, categories, and incentives.

“Go see for yourself” does not make the observer neutral.

The goal is not to escape abstraction.

It is to create a network of abstractions whose errors are not perfectly correlated.

A map plus a compass. A dashboard plus raw cases. A model plus adversarial testing. A survey plus observation. A financial statement plus cash. A strategy plus a market test. A reputation plus direct evidence.

Truth at scale is an ensemble.

This has a character requirement: willingness to let lower-status evidence defeat higher-status theory.

The spreadsheet may be elegant. The customer may still hate the product.

The forecast may be rigorous. The machine may still be making a new noise.

The strategy may have board approval. The supplier may still be unable to deliver.

Reality has no obligation to respect the hierarchy that produced the abstraction.

Institutions that remember this can use abstraction aggressively without becoming brittle.

They can model, measure, optimize, and automate because they preserve escape routes.

They do not confuse humility with indecision.

Humility is the decision to keep a door open for evidence.
