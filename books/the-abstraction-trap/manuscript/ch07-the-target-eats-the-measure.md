# The Target Eats the Measure

In a well-run organization, a measure begins its life humbly.

Someone notices that an outcome matters but cannot be observed everywhere at once. A proxy is chosen. Defect rate stands in for manufacturing quality. Test scores stand in for some part of learning. call time stands in for service efficiency. arrests stand in for policing activity. publication count stands in for research productivity. monthly active users stand in for product adoption.

At first the proxy may be genuinely informative.

Then people learn it matters.

The British economist Charles Goodhart is associated with a compact warning: when a measure becomes a target, its relationship with the underlying objective tends to deteriorate. Social scientists had identified related patterns in other domains, including Donald Campbell's observation that quantitative indicators used for social decision-making become vulnerable to corruption pressures.

The point is not mystical. Incentives redirect effort toward the measured surface.

Suppose a school district is worried that children are not learning to read. Standardized assessments reveal wide differences among schools. Leaders begin publishing scores and directing resources toward low-performing campuses. Teachers get clearer feedback. Some schools improve.

Then the scores acquire stakes.

School reputations, bonuses, interventions, enrollment, and careers become attached to the result. The test no longer sits outside the educational process. It enters lesson planning, scheduling, student selection, test preparation, discipline, and sometimes cheating. Subjects not emphasized by the test lose time. Teachers may focus on students near proficiency thresholds because those students can change the metric most efficiently.

The test can remain statistically reliable while the institution changes what the score means.

This is the central trick of target corruption. The number does not have to become false. Its informational value can weaken because behavior has reorganized around it.

The pattern is easiest to ridicule when incentives are crude. Stories about factories producing useless heavy chandeliers or tiny nails under Soviet production quotas have circulated for decades, sometimes in versions difficult to verify. The enduring appeal of such tales comes from their intuitive structure: reward the count, get countable junk; reward weight, get heavy junk.

Real organizations rarely design targets so foolishly. They build sophisticated metrics with safeguards. The problem survives because the world is more complicated than any reward function.

A call center that wants faster service tracks average handle time. Employees learn to end calls quickly. Customer satisfaction falls. Management adds satisfaction. Employees discover that asking happy customers to complete surveys raises scores. Management randomizes surveys. Workers learn which issues are likely to damage both metrics and transfer them.

Every correction creates a new surface.

This does not mean measurement inevitably fails. Targets can work very well when the objective is narrow, the task is stable, and manipulation is costly or obvious. A factory can track dimensions within tolerance. A warehouse can count on-time shipments. A laboratory can calibrate an instrument against a standard. A pilot can maintain an assigned altitude.

The abstraction trap deepens when the objective is multidimensional and the proxy becomes consequential.

Sales is a classic example because the result seems easy to define. A salesperson is supposed to sell. Pay commission on revenue.

Soon the organization discovers that not all revenue is equal. Some customers require steep discounts. Some churn quickly. Some create heavy support costs. Some do not pay. Some deals pull engineering resources away from the product. Compensation evolves: revenue plus margin, perhaps collections, renewals, strategic products, multi-year contracts.

The plan becomes more accurate and less legible.

At some point incentive designers face a choice. Keep adding terms until the formula resembles the whole business, or accept that managerial judgment must fill the gap.

The dream of a perfect target is the dream of management without managers.

This dream is especially attractive to large systems because judgment is expensive, inconsistent, and politically risky. A formula can be audited. An administrator can say, “The score determined the outcome.” Discretion requires someone to own the choice.

Metrics therefore do legal and emotional work as well as informational work. They create a shield against accusation.

College admissions, credit underwriting, employee ratings, medical triage, public procurement, and insurance all use standardized measures partly because cases must be handled consistently and decisions defended. The alternative—pure intuition—is vulnerable to favoritism and bias.

The answer cannot be “use no metrics.”

The more serious question is where to preserve discretion, how to audit it, and how to prevent the proxy from becoming the objective.

One useful distinction is between measures used for learning and measures used for reward.

A factory may track a defect metric so engineers can identify causes. If the metric is immediately tied to punishment, workers may become reluctant to report defects. The same information system that could support learning now suppresses the data it needs.

Safety cultures face this problem constantly.

Near-miss reporting is valuable because it captures weak signals before catastrophe. But if reporting a near miss automatically damages a team's performance rating, silence becomes rational. A low incident count may then indicate either excellent safety or excellent concealment.

The metric's sign reverses.

Healthcare contains similar ambiguity. A hospital with higher reported complication rates could be worse, or it could treat sicker patients, measure more carefully, and report more honestly. Risk adjustment can improve comparisons, but each adjustment introduces assumptions. A system that rewards low mortality without considering patient selection might encourage avoidance of high-risk cases.

The more consequential the target, the more carefully its causal pathway must be understood.

This is where simplistic invocations of Goodhart's law can become lazy. “Metrics get gamed” is not an excuse to abandon accountability. If a public agency performs poorly, managers should not defend themselves by saying reality is too rich to measure. Complexity can become camouflage for incompetence.

The challenge is to design measurement systems that are difficult to satisfy without improving the thing that matters.

That usually means combining several methods and examining process, not only outcomes.

A school can use assessments alongside classroom observation, graduation patterns, attendance, student work, and later outcomes. A company can pair revenue with customer retention and qualitative account review. A hospital can examine outcomes, adherence to evidence-based processes, case mix, patient experience, and safety reports.

Multiple measures reduce the leverage of any single proxy, though they also increase complexity.

Another defense is to keep some metrics diagnostic rather than promotional.

Once an organization publishes a ranking, people begin managing the ranking. Internal measures can sometimes remain more informative because they are used to ask questions rather than allocate prestige. The best operators often watch dozens of indicators without pretending all deserve targets.

A metric can be a smoke alarm without becoming an employee's bonus.

This distinction is routinely violated because executive attention itself functions as a reward. If the chief executive asks about a number every Monday, the organization learns that number's importance even if no formal incentive exists.

Attention is compensation.

The recursive nature of targets becomes obvious in digital platforms. A team wants users to find value in a product. “Engagement” is adopted as a proxy: sessions, time spent, posts, clicks, messages, daily activity.

The metric can be useful. A product nobody uses is unlikely to be valuable.

But once growth teams optimize engagement, the product changes. Notifications become more salient. feeds become more personalized. streaks, badges, autoplay, and recommendations pull users back. The system becomes better at producing the behavior that validates the engagement metric.

Now high engagement can mean strong value, habit, social necessity, anxiety, compulsion, or some mixture.

The proxy has become an intervention.

Consumer psychology matters because people do not approach these systems as detached optimizers. Variable rewards, social comparison, defaults, loss aversion, and present bias can shape behavior. A metric based on repeated use can therefore be increased by making leaving unpleasant rather than making staying valuable.

This distinction is economically important.

Customer lifetime value can rise because customers are delighted or because cancellation is difficult. Retention can reflect loyalty or lock-in. Higher revenue per user can come from greater utility or greater extraction. A dashboard may look identical while the long-term business differs profoundly.

Trust is the hidden variable.

Companies that optimize proxies aggressively often consume trust as an unpriced input. Dark patterns, surprise fees, misleading defaults, manufactured urgency, and endless retention friction can improve immediate conversion while teaching customers to approach the company defensively.

The metric captures the harvest and misses soil depletion.

Politics has even stronger incentives because measures become weapons in public argument. A government may celebrate low unemployment, opponents point to labor-force participation or wages, advocates cite GDP growth, critics cite inequality, one side counts border encounters, another counts removals, one side cites crime rates, another cites victimization surveys.

Every measure has a denominator, definition, and time window.

This does not mean the truth is unknowable. It means political actors select abstractions that support narratives. Citizens must learn to ask boring questions: Compared with what period? Adjusted for population? Nominal or real? Reported incidents or survey estimates? Stock or flow? Mean or median? Per capita or aggregate?

Democracy benefits from statistical literacy because rhetoric loves unlabelled denominators.

Markets create their own target problems. Public companies know analysts watch quarterly earnings. Management can cut discretionary spending, adjust working capital, time transactions, repurchase shares, or emphasize alternative performance measures. Many actions are legitimate. Some improve the business. Others improve the reported surface.

The separation is often visible only later.

Financial regulation responds by defining official measures, requiring disclosure, and constraining accounting choices. Firms then adapt within the rules. Regulators revise the rules. The cycle repeats.

Regulation is partly a contest over representations.

Capital ratios are a vivid example. Banks need enough loss-absorbing resources. A ratio turns this need into a manageable standard. But the denominator depends on how assets are weighted for risk. If different assets receive different regulatory treatment, banks have an incentive to reshape portfolios around the weights.

A safety measure becomes a design constraint for the system it measures.

This is not necessarily bad. That is what regulation intends. The problem arises when the risk weights and actual risk diverge, or when exposures migrate toward forms treated favorably by the abstraction.

The same dynamic appears in environmental regulation. Limit a pollutant measured at a particular point and firms may change process, location, timing, or chemistry. The result can be genuine improvement, substitution into another harm, or creative compliance.

Targets reveal ingenuity.

One of the most important managerial questions is therefore: If we make this number important, what will smart people do to it?

The word smart matters. Systems often fail because designers imagine passive subjects. Employees, companies, students, traders, hospitals, and citizens are adaptive. They learn rules. They identify thresholds. They discover loopholes. They reframe behavior.

A target is a game announcement.

Game design offers useful intuition. A well-designed game aligns scoring with the activity that makes play interesting. If players can maximize points through a boring exploit, the design is broken even if nobody cheats. The exploit is information about the rules.

Organizations should treat gaming the same way.

When employees find a way to hit the metric without serving the objective, moral condemnation may be less useful than redesign. The exploit reveals where the representation has detached from the purpose.

Of course, fraud remains fraud. People can lie, conceal, and steal. But systems that require saintly resistance to badly designed incentives are not well designed.

Culture exists partly to handle what contracts cannot specify.

A strong professional norm can discourage metric gaming even when it is possible. Doctors, engineers, auditors, scientists, judges, and other professions develop ethical codes because formal incentives cannot cover every case. The profession asks members to honor the object behind the measure.

This is one reason erosion of professional identity can be dangerous. If workers experience themselves only as agents maximizing a score, the unmeasured obligation loses defenders.

A nurse who sees care as more than throughput will notice harm the scheduling system misses. An engineer who identifies with safety may resist a deadline. An auditor who values independence may challenge a profitable client. These are not sentimental extras. They are governance mechanisms for incomplete contracts.

Character is a control system.

That idea can sound old-fashioned in a world enamored with incentive design, but no organization has enough metrics to encode every obligation. Rules run out at the edge. The person remains.

The mature organization therefore uses targets with modesty. It defines what the measure is for. It watches for behavioral adaptation. It separates diagnostic metrics from reward metrics where possible. It samples raw cases. It rotates or revises measures as the environment changes. It asks what is getting worse while the target improves.

Most importantly, it keeps the underlying purpose linguistically alive.

A sales target exists to build valuable customer relationships that produce revenue, not to produce booked revenue by any means. A safety target exists to reduce harm, not to reduce reports. A productivity metric exists to increase useful output, not visible busyness. A school assessment exists to understand learning, not to manufacture scores.

When the purpose disappears from language, the proxy is free to become the mission.

This is the moment the target eats the measure.

The number survives. It may improve beautifully.

And the thing it once helped us see slips out of frame.
