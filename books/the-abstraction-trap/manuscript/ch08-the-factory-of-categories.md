# The Factory of Categories

In the middle of the twentieth century, consumer markets learned to see people in new ways.

A household could be described by income, age, location, family size, occupation, education, race, religion, and purchasing behavior. Magazines assembled audiences. television networks assembled audiences. Retailers assembled neighborhoods. Pollsters assembled electorates. Advertising agencies learned to translate social life into segments that could be bought and sold.

The category became an economic object.

Segmentation solved a real problem. A company cannot design, price, and distribute every product for every person as if each buyer were unique. It needs groups. A grocer needs to decide what belongs on the shelf. An automaker needs to decide which models to build. A media company needs to tell advertisers who is watching. A bank needs to estimate risk. A political campaign needs to allocate time.

Grouping makes choice possible at scale.

The trouble begins when the group stops being treated as a convenient lens and starts being treated as a person.

“Suburban mothers,” “Gen Z,” “high-net-worth investors,” “value shoppers,” “creators,” “early adopters,” “soccer fans,” “working class,” “luxury consumers”: each label can reveal patterns while hiding enormous variation. The category earns power because it is compact enough to travel through an organization. A product manager can put it in a brief. A chief executive can say it in a meeting. A media planner can buy it.

Human complexity becomes operational.

The anthropology of consumption complicates the familiar economic picture of a buyer choosing among utilities and prices. People use goods to participate in social worlds. Clothing marks affiliation and distance. Food carries memory, religion, class, health, aspiration, and care. Cars signal adulthood, independence, competence, taste, or practical necessity depending on context. Phones are tools and status objects and social infrastructure at once.

A product category can therefore become a social category.

Consider sneakers. A shoe can be evaluated for cushioning, durability, fit, traction, and price. It can also carry associations with sport, music, neighborhood, fashion, celebrity, scarcity, authenticity, and collection. The physical object has not become unreal. Its material function coexists with a symbolic system that changes willingness to pay.

Marketers call this brand value. Anthropologists might describe a richer field of meaning. Financial statements eventually convert some of it into margins.

The abstraction trap appears when management assumes it created the meaning it merely entered.

Communities often appropriate products for uses and identities companies did not anticipate. A workwear garment becomes fashion. A cheap car becomes a subculture. A software tool built for one profession becomes infrastructure for another. A children's toy becomes a collector market.

Meaning is negotiated, not delivered in a box.

The consumer is therefore not just the endpoint of a value chain. The consumer helps produce the product's social value.

This complicates the idea of targeting. A company identifies a segment, designs messages for it, and then measures response. If the campaign succeeds, more people in the category buy. Their visible adoption can attract similar people. The segment becomes more real because it was targeted.

Marketing creates some of the pattern it later reports.

Fashion makes this recursive loop obvious. A style is identified as emerging. Retailers stock it. influencers wear it. magazines and feeds display it. Consumers encounter it repeatedly and begin to see it as current. Sales rise. The sales data confirm the trend.

The prediction becomes distribution.

Trend forecasting can therefore be both descriptive and causal. This is not manipulation in a simple sense. People retain agency, competing styles exist, and many promoted trends fail. But attention is scarce. Representations of what is popular affect what people see, which affects what can become popular.

Charts create audiences.

The music industry learned this long before recommendation algorithms. Radio play, sales charts, label promotion, touring, retail placement, and social imitation reinforced one another. A song that enters rotation becomes more familiar; familiarity can increase preference; preference supports more rotation. The chart purports to measure popularity while participating in its production.

Digital platforms industrialized the loop.

A recommendation system predicts what a user might click or watch. The recommendation changes what the user encounters. The click becomes new training data. The model learns from a world partly curated by the previous model.

This is recursive classification at machine speed.

The system can become very good at predicting behavior inside the environment it has shaped. That does not necessarily mean it has discovered a person's stable preferences. It may have discovered which preferences are easiest to activate under the platform's design.

This distinction matters for consumer autonomy.

Suppose a user watches one video about home renovation. The platform recommends more. She watches several because they are there. Soon the system classifies her as strongly interested in renovation and fills the feed accordingly. Her apparent preference intensifies because alternatives receive less exposure.

The person and model co-train each other.

Sometimes this is delightful. A recommendation system can help someone discover music, books, tools, communities, or knowledge she would never have found. Personalization can reduce search costs dramatically.

Sometimes it narrows.

A category inferred from past behavior can become a channel that makes future behavior more predictable. The platform benefits from predictability because predictable behavior is easier to monetize. The user may benefit from relevance while losing serendipity.

This is not a uniquely digital problem. Neighborhoods, schools, professions, clubs, and social networks have always shaped exposure. The difference is scale, speed, and optimization.

Algorithmic systems can measure response continuously and alter the environment for millions of people at once.

The commercial dream is the segment of one: perfect personalization. Each customer receives the message, price, product, and timing most likely to generate action.

In principle this could reduce waste. Why show irrelevant advertisements? Why stock products nobody nearby wants? Why send the same offer to people with different needs?

Yet perfect personalization contains a philosophical contradiction. To treat someone as an individual, the system must still represent that individual through categories and probabilities. The “segment of one” is not the person. It is a model of the person.

The model is built from traces.

Purchase history, search, location, device, time, social graph, inferred interests, response patterns, and similarities to others become a statistical portrait. The portrait may predict behavior better than a demographic category, but it remains a reduction.

What it omits is often circumstance.

A consumer buys baby formula. The model infers a parent. Perhaps the consumer is an uncle. A person searches for cancer treatments. The model infers illness. Perhaps she is a medical student. A user spends hours on a topic. The system infers affinity. Perhaps the user is researching something she detests.

Behavior does not come with its own interpretation.

Ethnographic research is valuable because it can recover context. Researchers visit homes, watch routines, ask people to show how they use products, and listen for contradictions between stated preference and actual practice. The method is slow and vulnerable to its own biases, but it can reveal the category error hidden in large datasets.

A company may think it sells a drill; customers are trying to hang shelves. It may think it sells financial software; customers are trying to avoid embarrassment at tax time. It may think a feature is unused because nobody values it; users may value it but be unable to find it.

Observation reopens the frame.

This is why strong consumer organizations treat quantitative and qualitative research as complements. Large data tells them where something is happening. Fieldwork can tell them what kind of thing it is.

The two methods fail differently.

A focus group can be dominated by articulate participants. Interviews can elicit post-hoc explanations. Ethnographers can overgeneralize vivid cases. Surveys can force respondents into categories that do not match how they think. Behavioral data can measure action without motive.

Triangulation is not methodological politeness. It is a defense against abstraction traps.

The category factory extends beyond marketing into finance. Credit models sort borrowers by predicted risk. Insurance models group people by expected loss. Wealth managers classify clients by risk tolerance. E-commerce systems identify likely fraud. Each classification affects price and access.

The stakes make category design political.

A lender may discover that a variable predicts default. Should it be used? What if the variable proxies for protected characteristics, neighborhood inequality, or historical exclusion? Predictive usefulness does not settle legitimacy.

The same problem appears in employment. A model may identify traits correlated with successful incumbents. If the existing workforce reflects past hiring bias, the model can convert history into a selection rule.

The abstraction inherits the world.

This is one of the most important misconceptions about data-driven systems: that data arrives before society. Data is society after collection. It reflects who had access, which behaviors were recorded, which outcomes were labeled, and which institutions defined success.

A model trained on historical choices can be a highly accurate summary of historical choices.

That does not tell us whether those choices should continue.

Consumer categories also shape self-conception. People adopt market labels because labels offer social shorthand. “Runner,” “gamer,” “minimalist,” “founder,” “wellness,” “outdoors,” “luxury,” “maker.” Once an identity is claimed, purchases can become investments in consistency.

The market supplies props for the self.

This is not necessarily shallow. Material culture has always helped people express roles and belonging. Uniforms, jewelry, tools, religious objects, household goods, and clothing carry social meaning across history.

What is new is the industrial precision with which identity can be monitored and addressed.

A platform can infer a transition before friends know: pregnancy, relocation, job change, breakup, new hobby, financial stress. Retailers and advertisers compete to arrive at the moment identity is unsettled because habits are unusually open to change.

Life transitions are commercial opportunities because categories are being rewritten.

The ethical question is not simply privacy. It is how much power a system should have to act on an inferred identity the person has not chosen to disclose.

Prediction can feel invasive even when no secret database contains a literal statement. The inference itself is the representation.

This problem will grow as models become better. Crude classification leaves room for obvious error. Highly accurate classification can create stronger pressure to accept the model's view.

A person may hear, “People like you usually choose this.” The phrase sounds helpful. It also narrows the imagination.

Strategy encounters the same issue at the market level. Companies define industries because they need competitors. A railroad sees other railroads. A newspaper sees other newspapers. A camera company sees other camera companies. The category simplifies strategic analysis.

Then an entrant arrives from outside the category.

Airlines compete with rail on time. social media competes with entertainment, news, games, and conversation for attention. Smartphone cameras compete with dedicated cameras not by becoming better cameras first, but by being present and connected. Cloud software competes with internal IT, consulting, hardware, and labor.

The market boundary was an abstraction.

Disruption often looks surprising because incumbents defend the category rather than the customer problem.

This is not because managers are stupid. Categories organize reporting, expertise, regulation, investor expectations, and professional identity. Leaving the category can mean entering a business the organization is not built to understand.

The abstraction has assets attached to it.

A newspaper company is not only a belief that it sells news. It owns printing presses, distribution systems, advertising relationships, newsroom routines, labor agreements, and a cost structure. When digital distribution changes the economics, the old category remains physically embedded.

This is why strategic redefinition is so difficult. Changing the noun changes the assets that matter.

The best companies periodically ask questions that sound semantic and turn out to be economic: What business are we in? Who is the customer? What problem are they solving? Which alternatives belong in the comparison? What behavior would make our current segmentation obsolete?

These questions are attempts to loosen categories before reality does it violently.

The same discipline helps individuals. People can become trapped by professional categories—lawyer, engineer, executive, academic, entrepreneur—long after the role stops fitting. Status, networks, sunk costs, and identity make the label sticky.

A career category can be useful. It tells the market something about capabilities. It can also become a prison when the person confuses the signal with the self.

The category factory operates inside us too.

We simplify other people because social life demands it. We simplify ourselves because coherence is psychologically comfortable. The danger is not having identities. It is granting the category veto power over new evidence.

A consumer can surprise the algorithm. A company can leave its industry. A worker can learn a different craft. A customer segment can dissolve. A trend can fail despite promotion.

Reality retains an escape route.

Good research looks for that route deliberately. It seeks the person who does not fit the persona, the customer who uses the product “wrong,” the market entrant that violates the category, the behavior the model cannot explain.

Anomalies are expensive because they make planning harder.

They are also where new categories come from.

Every segmentation system was once a claim that certain differences mattered more than others. When the world changes, the hierarchy of differences changes too.

The factory never closes.

The question is whether we keep mistaking its output for nature.
