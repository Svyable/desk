# The Abstraction Trap

**How Useful Simplifications Become Dangerous Realities**

Sven Hard Benson

A map is useful because it omits almost everything. A balance sheet is useful for the same reason. So is a musical score, a probability, a job title, a credit rating, a gross-domestic-product figure, an organizational chart, a market price, a model benchmark, and the little red number on an app icon. Civilization scales by turning thick reality into thinner symbols that can travel farther than the circumstances from which they came.

The danger begins when the symbol returns to govern the reality it compressed.

This book is about that return trip. It is about abstractions that start as aids to judgment and end as substitutes for judgment; categories that begin by describing people and later train people to fit the category; markets whose prices change the behavior that produces the next price; metrics that create the performance they claim merely to record; models that make institutions legible while making inconvenient facts disappear.

The argument is not anti-measurement, anti-model, or anti-technology. The opposite. Abstraction is among the great human inventions. Without it there is no mathematics worth the name, no modern science, no large organization, no double-entry bookkeeping, no statistics, no maps of useful scale, no insurance pool, no complex supply chain, no symphony that survives its first performance, no software stack, and no computer that can do more than imitate a very patient switchboard operator.

The question is how to keep a useful simplification from becoming a dangerous reality.

The answer, I will argue, is not to demand perfect representations. Perfect representations are mostly useless. A map at one-to-one scale is the territory again. The discipline is to know what an abstraction leaves out, to notice when incentives begin reshaping themselves around it, and to preserve routes back to the world the abstraction was built to serve.

The chapters that follow move from cartography and music into accounting, consumer psychology, finance, aviation, war, platforms, artificial intelligence, institutions, science, and character. The recurring structure is recursive: we build a representation of the world; we act on the representation; the action changes the world; then we mistake the altered world for confirmation that the representation was right all along.

Sometimes it was. Sometimes we have merely taught reality to answer in the language of our model.

That difference is where judgment lives.
