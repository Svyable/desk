# The State's Simplified Citizen

A government cannot govern people it cannot identify.

The sentence sounds authoritarian until one considers the ordinary benefits of being legible to a state. A birth certificate can establish identity. A street address lets emergency services find a home. A land registry can protect ownership against a stronger neighbor. A census can help allocate representation and public resources. A tax identifier lets obligations and benefits follow a person across years.

Legibility is not merely control. It can be a form of protection.

It is also an abstraction.

The state encounters a person through categories: resident, citizen, taxpayer, dependent, veteran, employee, employer, student, disabled person, beneficiary, property owner, voter, offender, patient, migrant. Each category exists because a law or program needs a boundary.

Real lives cross boundaries more easily than administrative systems do.

A person may care for an elderly parent without being formally recognized as a caregiver. A worker may have several jobs and fit no employer's model of a stable employee. A family may move between households. An informal business may be economically real while legally invisible. A person may qualify for help under one program's definition and not another's.

The citizen experiences one life.

The state experiences records.

This gap explains much of bureaucratic frustration. A person says, “You already have this information.” The agency says, “That is a different system.” Both statements can be true.

Administrative categories are built for jurisdiction, law, privacy, funding, and historical technology. The abstraction is fragmented because authority is fragmented.

Modern states have repeatedly tried to solve this through standardization.

Censuses classify populations. postal systems standardize addresses. statistical agencies define industries, occupations, inflation baskets, employment status, and national accounts. Public-health agencies define cases. education systems define enrollment and completion. welfare programs define income and household.

These definitions make policy measurable.

They also create edge cases that are not edge cases to the people living them.

Consider unemployment. A person who wants work, is available, and has recently searched is treated differently in labor statistics from someone who has stopped searching. The distinction is methodologically meaningful. It prevents the unemployment rate from becoming a vague count of everyone without a job.

But public debate often treats the headline rate as if it described all labor-market distress.

The statistic's careful definition disappears in circulation.

This is a recurring pattern. Technical institutions preserve nuance in documentation; political systems distribute the number without the documentation.

A measure with a precise scope becomes a broad narrative.

Inflation has similar problems. A consumer-price index measures changes in prices across a defined basket and methodology. No household buys the exact average basket. A renter in a fast-growing city, a retiree with high medical spending, and a family driving long distances can experience different personal inflation.

The official measure can be correct while an individual's experience is different.

Public trust suffers when institutions respond to that difference by treating personal experience as ignorance.

The better explanation is multiscale truth.

An aggregate statistic answers a population question. It does not invalidate local variation.

This principle becomes politically explosive around poverty. Governments need thresholds to determine eligibility and track conditions. But poverty is not a switch that flips when income crosses a line. Housing cost, household composition, disability, debt, geography, childcare, transport, and informal support all affect lived material security.

The threshold is necessary for administration and artificial in experience.

Small differences around the line can therefore produce large differences in benefits.

These cliffs change behavior. A worker may rationally avoid additional income if it triggers loss of health coverage, childcare assistance, or other benefits worth more than the raise. Policymakers then redesign phase-outs to reduce cliffs, creating more complex formulas.

The abstraction produces incentives, and incentives force the abstraction to evolve.

Tax systems are recursive machines of this kind. Legislatures define income, deductions, credits, gains, businesses, dependents, and residence. Individuals and companies organize transactions around the definitions. Authorities respond with new rules. Tax advisers emerge to navigate the map.

A legal category becomes an industry.

This is not proof that taxation is uniquely distortive. Any rule consequential enough will attract adaptation. The same happens with environmental permits, financial regulation, zoning, immigration rules, procurement, and education funding.

Policy design is game design among strategic actors.

The state must therefore predict how people will respond to the representation used to govern them.

Simplification becomes especially dangerous when categories carry stigma or force.

A risk score used to allocate inspections, policing, or benefits can concentrate institutional attention on people already well represented in historical data. More attention produces more records. More records can reinforce the score.

The state's eye trains on its own trace.

This is a known problem in predictive systems. Observed events depend partly on where institutions look.

If police patrol one area more heavily, they may detect more low-level offenses there even if underlying behavior is similar elsewhere. A model trained on recorded enforcement outcomes can interpret concentrated observation as concentrated propensity.

The data is not fabricated.

It is generated by a process.

The distinction between data and generating process is essential to responsible social science.

Administrative records are seductive because they are large. Millions of rows feel more objective than a survey. But scale does not remove selection.

Who appears in the data? Who does not? Which events create a record? Which behaviors stay private? Which categories were available to the clerk? Which incentives shaped reporting?

A database is a fossil record of institutions.

Historical censuses demonstrate this vividly. Categories of race, occupation, household, and identity have changed across time because the social and political systems creating them changed. Researchers using old data must understand the definitions rather than projecting current categories backward.

The same warning applies to future analysts studying us.

Today's neat columns will look culturally specific tomorrow.

Identity politics in the broad sense—conflict over which identities deserve recognition—often emerges from this administrative reality. Recognition can unlock rights, statistics, funding, and visibility. If a group is not represented in official categories, its needs may be difficult to demonstrate.

Classification can become a route to justice.

It can also freeze fluid identities into boxes.

A census asks for a category because aggregate counting requires consistency. A person may experience identity as mixed, contextual, or changing. The state needs a box; the citizen may need several or none.

There is no purely technical solution because the choice expresses public values.

Privacy adds another tension. Better government service often requires combining data. A person would prefer not to submit the same information to five agencies. Fraud detection improves when records can be compared. Public-health response improves with timely data.

But central legibility creates surveillance risk.

The more complete the state representation of a person, the more damage misuse, breach, political change, or error can cause.

Good governance therefore needs deliberate illegibility.

Data minimization, access controls, purpose limits, deletion rules, warrants, due process, and separation among systems are not merely technical inefficiencies. They can be constitutional friction.

The state should not know everything it can know.

This principle has a business analogue. A company can collect enormous behavioral data because storage is cheap. The fact that data might improve personalization does not settle whether collection is legitimate.

Information creates option value for the collector and exposure for the subject.

The asymmetry matters.

A complete representation can be powerful even when each individual datum seems harmless.

This is why identification systems deserve special care. A universal identifier can reduce administrative friction and improve service. It can also make linkage across domains trivial.

Societies choose different balances depending on history, law, institutional trust, and capacity.

The abstraction is never merely a database schema.

Citizenship itself is a high-stakes category. Two people can live on the same street, work similar jobs, pay similar taxes, and face different rights because one possesses a legal status the other does not.

Borders convert geography into membership.

States need boundaries to exercise jurisdiction. Yet human mobility constantly produces cases that strain them: refugees, dual citizens, temporary workers, undocumented residents, stateless people, children born across systems.

The category determines whether movement is travel, migration, return, asylum, or violation.

Words become legal events.

This is why political rhetoric over migration is so powerful. The chosen noun—migrant, refugee, illegal immigrant, asylum seeker, worker, invader—does not merely describe. It activates moral and legal frames.

The abstraction recruits policy.

Humanistic social science adds value by putting lived experience beside administrative category.

A household can be studied as a benefit unit and as a network of care. A border can be studied as a legal boundary and as a place where families, trade, ecology, and culture cross. An unemployed person can be studied as a labor-force status and as a biography.

Neither view cancels the other.

Governance needs both.

The state cannot administer by biography alone. A benefits officer cannot negotiate the tax code individually with every citizen. Rules need generality.

But generality should preserve appeals for cases where the category fails.

Due process is an anti-abstraction institution.

It says that a classification with consequences can be challenged. Evidence can be heard. The person is not identical to the record.

Courts perform this function imperfectly and expensively, but the principle is profound.

A rule-based society needs a route from rule back to case.

Administrative systems can build smaller versions: human review, ombuds offices, hardship exceptions, transparent criteria, error correction, and documented reasons.

These mechanisms look inefficient until the system makes a consequential mistake.

Then they are civilization.

The risk of automated government is not simply that machines will be biased. Humans and paper systems can be biased too. The deeper risk is that automation will make categories faster and harder to contest.

A wrong clerk may be persuaded. A wrong system can replicate the error across millions of cases.

Scale turns a small abstraction error into policy.

The opportunity is symmetrical. Good automation can reduce arbitrary variation, process benefits faster, detect inconsistent treatment, and free staff for difficult cases.

Technology can make the state more humane if it handles routine legibility while preserving human routes for exception.

The design question is where the citizen can reenter the system as a person.

This is the question every large institution should ask.

Where can the employee be more than a performance score? Where can the patient be more than a risk category? Where can the customer be more than a segment? Where can the borrower be more than a credit model?

Scale requires simplification.

Dignity requires that simplification not be final.

A government that knows nothing about its citizens cannot serve them well.

A government that believes its records contain the whole citizen cannot serve them wisely.

The mature state lives inside that tension.
