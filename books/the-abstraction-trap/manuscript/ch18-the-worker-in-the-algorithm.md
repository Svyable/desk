# The Worker in the Algorithm

Frederick Winslow Taylor carried a stopwatch into the factory.

The image endures because it captures an old managerial dream: if work can be decomposed, timed, compared, and standardized, productivity can be improved scientifically rather than left to habit and bargaining.

The dream was not absurd.

Industrial work contained enormous variation. Methods differed by worker. Tools were poorly arranged. managers often lacked basic knowledge of how tasks were actually performed. Measurement could reveal waste and make training more systematic.

But the stopwatch did more than observe labor.

It changed the relationship between the worker and the job.

Once a task had a standard time, management could define normal performance. Pay, staffing, scheduling, and discipline could attach to the number. Knowledge that had belonged partly to workers moved into process design.

The abstraction redistributed power.

This is a recurring feature of management technology. A representation of work can make the work easier to coordinate while making the worker more replaceable.

Assembly lines, scripts, standard operating procedures, enterprise software, workflow systems, route optimization, call-center dashboards, warehouse scanners, and gig platforms all continue the project in different forms.

Each turns embodied practice into legible steps.

Legibility can improve fairness. A worker no longer depends entirely on a foreman's whim. Clear standards can expose discrimination. Training becomes portable. safety procedures become consistent. Customers receive more predictable service.

Legibility can also intensify surveillance.

A warehouse system can know how many items a worker picks, how long each movement takes, and how much idle time appears between scans. A call center can track handle time, after-call work, quality scores, schedule adherence, and sentiment. A delivery platform can record route, acceptance, completion, customer rating, and location.

The manager's eye becomes software.

This changes the psychology of work even when no human supervisor is watching.

People respond to the representation they believe judges them.

A worker who knows idle time is tracked may hesitate to use the bathroom. A representative who knows every second counts may cut off a difficult customer. A driver who fears a rating may tolerate abusive behavior.

The metric becomes ambient authority.

Traditional management at least made authority visible. A supervisor gave an order and could, in principle, be challenged. Algorithmic management can make the rule harder to locate.

“The system assigned it.”

“The score fell.”

“The route updated.”

“The account was deactivated.”

Responsibility migrates into the abstraction.

This is one reason appeals matter so much in automated labor systems. A classification can have material consequences: fewer tasks, lower pay, discipline, termination.

If the worker cannot understand or challenge the representation, management has gained power without corresponding explanation.

The issue is not whether algorithms should manage work. In many domains software can schedule more efficiently than humans, match supply and demand, detect unsafe patterns, and reduce favoritism.

The question is what kind of discretion remains.

A hospital scheduling system may optimize staffing based on predicted demand. Nurses still know which patients are unusually difficult. A delivery route may minimize travel time while a driver knows one building's elevator is broken. A warehouse standard may capture average movement while ignoring a recurring equipment problem.

Local knowledge survives automation.

The institution must decide whether local knowledge can alter the plan or only be recorded as noncompliance.

This is an old conflict between scientific management and craft.

Craft is not merely freedom. It is accumulated judgment about exceptions.

A master electrician recognizes when a wall, load, material, or prior repair makes the standard approach dangerous. An experienced machinist hears a change in a machine before a sensor crosses a threshold. A nurse sees that a patient “doesn't look right” before a vital sign formally deteriorates.

These observations are abstractions too, built from experience.

They are less legible because the practitioner may struggle to state the exact rule.

Organizations often treat tacit knowledge as inefficient because it cannot be easily transferred. Sometimes that is correct. A system dependent on one veteran employee is fragile.

The solution, however, is not always to replace tacit knowledge with procedure. It may be to design better ways for tacit knowledge to inform procedure.

The line worker becomes a sensor for the process.

Lean manufacturing traditions made this idea central in different forms: problems should surface rather than be hidden, and workers close to the process should participate in improvement.

The managerial significance is profound.

A worker is not merely a unit of execution. The worker can be part of the organization's learning system.

Metric-heavy management can destroy this role if every deviation is punished.

Suppose a production target is aggressive. An operator notices a strange vibration and pauses the line. If the organization celebrates the pause as responsible investigation, information travels. If the worker is penalized for lost output and the machine usually survives, the next operator learns silence.

Culture updates faster than policy.

This is why evidence-based motivation cannot be reduced to reward formulas.

People care about pay, but also autonomy, competence, fairness, social belonging, status, meaning, predictability, and whether effort appears connected to results. External incentives interact with these motives rather than replacing them.

A compensation system can therefore raise measured output while lowering discretionary effort, learning, or cooperation.

The organization gains what it bought and loses what it assumed was free.

Teamwork is especially vulnerable because individual metrics struggle to capture shared production.

A sales representative closes the deal, but product built it, marketing generated awareness, legal negotiated terms, support protects renewal, finance manages credit, and operations delivers.

Assign all credit to the visible closer and the organization may teach employees to hoard opportunity.

Assign credit too diffusely and individual contribution becomes invisible.

There is no perfect formula because cooperation produces joint value.

This is one reason culture matters economically. Norms can coordinate behavior that contracts cannot efficiently price.

A colleague helps because helping is what good colleagues do, not because a point system awards 0.7 units of collaboration.

Attempts to measure every prosocial behavior can make the culture strangely transactional.

The abstraction crowding out the norm.

Gig platforms push this logic toward a boundary case. A driver, courier, seller, or freelancer may experience work through an interface that combines market, manager, payment system, reputation score, and rulebook.

The app presents itself as neutral matching infrastructure.

Yet platform design determines which information each side sees, how prices are set or suggested, which jobs are offered, how cancellations are penalized, how ratings matter, and how disputes are resolved.

The interface is an employment constitution even when the legal category says contractor.

This is why classification battles over employee versus contractor matter so much. The label determines rights and obligations that the lived relationship may only partially resemble.

Law must compress a continuum of control into categories.

Companies and workers then organize around the category.

Again the abstraction becomes causal.

Remote work created another measurement crisis. Managers accustomed to seeing employees in offices lost a familiar proxy for effort: presence.

Some organizations responded with outcome measures. Others adopted monitoring software. Employees debated productivity, collaboration, culture, autonomy, and fairness.

The disagreement revealed how little “work” had ever been directly observed.

Office presence was always an abstraction.

A person at a desk can be highly productive or perform busyness. A remote worker can produce excellent work or disappear. The old representation felt natural because everyone had grown used to it.

Changing the environment exposed the proxy.

Knowledge work makes measurement especially hard because output is lumpy and interdependent. A software engineer may spend three days understanding a problem and ten minutes fixing it. A researcher may fail repeatedly before finding something valuable. A manager may spend an hour resolving a conflict that saves months of dysfunction.

Activity counts can punish the thinking that creates the value.

Artificial intelligence will intensify this because visible production becomes cheaper.

Lines of code, documents, analyses, emails, designs, and presentations can multiply without proportional growth in useful outcomes.

If organizations continue measuring knowledge workers through volume, AI will create spectacular metric improvement.

It may also create noise.

The scarce capability shifts toward choosing problems, verifying output, integrating context, coordinating people, and accepting responsibility.

These qualities are difficult to count.

Management will be tempted to build new proxies.

How many model outputs were accepted? How quickly were tasks completed? What percentage was automated? Which employee uses AI most?

Some metrics may help during transition.

The trap is rewarding adoption rather than value.

A worker who automates the wrong process can generate negative productivity more efficiently.

The deeper labor question is who benefits when abstraction improves productivity.

Scientific management historically created surplus through more coordinated production. The distribution of that surplus depended on bargaining power, labor markets, institutions, ownership, and politics.

Automation has the same structure.

A technology can make a worker ten times more productive while wages barely move if labor is easy to replace and capital captures the gain. Another technology can raise wages if it complements scarce skill. Productivity is not a moral allocation rule.

The abstraction of “labor productivity” says output per unit of labor input increased.

It does not say who became richer.

This matters for culture because people evaluate systems through fairness, not only aggregate efficiency.

A company that uses automation to remove tedious work, share gains, and create better jobs may gain trust. A company that intensifies monitoring and eliminates autonomy may achieve similar output statistics with a very different workforce.

The dashboard cannot settle which institution has been built.

Management ultimately requires a theory of the human being.

Is the worker primarily a cost to minimize, an agent to incentivize, a source of local knowledge, a professional with norms, a member of a community, an owner of human capital, or a person with purposes outside work?

Every operating system answers, even when management never states the theory.

The answer appears in what gets measured.

A system that tracks only output will eventually discover that output has hidden inputs: health, trust, skill, cooperation, attention, pride.

These can be consumed faster than the metric notices.

The worker in the algorithm is therefore not merely someone being measured.

The worker is part of a loop. She reads the metric, changes behavior, teaches colleagues, invents workarounds, chooses whether to report problems, updates effort, and decides whether the institution deserves trust.

Management represents the worker.

The worker models management back.

The quality of the organization emerges between those two abstractions.
