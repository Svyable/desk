## Thesis

Cold War sensory-deprivation research began partly as an attempt to understand coercion and vigilance, then discovered something stranger and more durable: ordinary perception is not a camera feed but an active bargain with patterned stimulation. When the world is made monotonous enough, cognition does not simply go blank. It manufactures, drifts, slows, hallucinates, and becomes easier to steer. Intelligence agencies noticed the coercive possibilities; psychologists discovered a boundary condition of mind. The book asks what survived replication, what was exaggerated into “brainwashing,” what crossed ethical lines, and what later interrogation systems inherited from the research.

## Evidence classes

1. **Verified/declassified/documented:** McGill perceptual-deprivation experiments; U.S. intelligence interest in isolation and coercion; MKULTRA/ARTICHOKE records; documented Cameron subproject; congressional and inspector-general records.
2. **Credible but incomplete:** institutional funding chains, researcher knowledge of intelligence sponsorship, specific pathways from laboratory findings into interrogation doctrine where documentary links are partial.
3. **Disputed/unconfirmed:** strong claims of reliable personality erasure, programmable mind control, or operationally dependable “brainwashing.”
4. **Speculative synthesis:** deprivation research is best understood as discovering the brain’s dependence on structured environmental prediction, not a secret switch for mind control.

## Defeat condition

If the historical record shows no meaningful intelligence connection to the sensory-deprivation research, or if later controlled work shows that the characteristic perceptual/cognitive effects were artifacts of a narrow early protocol, the central collision weakens substantially.
