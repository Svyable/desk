# Direct Evidence Is Expensive

Every field says it wants direct evidence until the invoice arrives.

Kola consumed decades, custom machinery, hundreds of workers, repeated drilling failures and a national-scale institution to obtain a twelve-kilometre transect through one patch of crust. The result is almost comically small compared with the size of a continent.

That ratio explains why indirect methods dominate Earth science. Seismic networks can sample whole regions. satellites can map gravity and magnetism globally. Electrical and magnetotelluric surveys cover areas no drilling campaign could touch. Drilling is not the replacement for inference; it is the calibration tax.

The same economics appears in other frontier sciences. A sample-return mission is vastly more expensive than orbital spectroscopy. A biopsy is more invasive than imaging. Physical access to a classified artifact is rarer than testimony about it. Direct evidence is expensive because reality is inconveniently located.

This makes the few places where direct access exists disproportionately valuable.

Kola is one. Ocean drilling is another. Ice cores, mine shafts and deep underground laboratories are related examples. Each turns a volume normally known through proxy into material that can be measured in ways the remote model did not anticipate.

But the expense creates a temptation to overgeneralize. If only one hole reaches a given depth, every unusual result may be treated as universal because there is no equally deep control site. The remedy is not to distrust the hole. It is to replicate the access architecture.

Modern ultradeep drilling may finally make that possible in more geological settings. A network of wells beyond ten kilometres—crystalline shields, sedimentary basins, active margins, stable interiors—would transform Kola from a singular anecdote into one point in a comparative science.

That is the future Kola never had.

The next revolution in deep-Earth knowledge may come not from drilling one deeper hole, but from having enough deep holes to discover which surprises were local.
