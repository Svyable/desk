# The Boundary That Wasn't There

The deepest insult Kola delivered to a clean crustal diagram was not that a boundary vanished. It was that the line on the seismic section survived while the story attached to it became harder to tell.

SG-3 drilled through roughly seven kilometres of Proterozoic rocks of the Pechenga structure and another five kilometres of Archean basement. Before the hole, a strong seismic change near seven kilometres invited a familiar interpretation: the crust was changing composition with depth, perhaps toward the kind of simple granite-to-basalt transition that made an excellent line in a textbook cross-section.

The drill did cross from Proterozoic into Archean rocks near that depth. What it did not find was a clean basaltic lower crust waiting beneath the granitic upper crust. More awkwardly, the seismic behavior did not map neatly onto rock names.

Ninel Pavlenkova's 1992 synthesis is careful on this point. Wide-angle seismic work still showed a subhorizontal boundary near seven kilometres, but she called its origin uncertain. Core observations associated the boundary with higher porosity, velocity anisotropy and changes in pressure. A separate velocity inversion began around 4.5 kilometres, where changes in fissuring and fluid regime were conspicuous.

That 4.5-kilometre interval became even less abstract when geophysicists lowered instruments into the hole. Vertical seismic profiles recorded from roughly 2.2 to 6 kilometres crossed the Luchlompolsky fault zone. Later shear-wave analysis found anisotropy of about four to five percent, locally rising to roughly ten percent in the fault zone. Two physical explanations could fit the splitting: aligned hornblende in the metamorphic fabric, or aligned cracks. The waveform was real. Its geological cause was not uniquely encoded in it.

This is where the popular version of Kola usually becomes too tidy. It says scientists expected basalt, drilled, discovered fractured water-bearing granite instead, and learned that the seismic boundary had been caused by water. The first half is useful shorthand. The last step is too confident.

Lev Vernik and colleagues tested twelve cores from depths spanning the well at confining pressures up to 100 MPa, both dry and water-saturated, then compared those laboratory velocities with sonic logs and vertical seismic profiles. Their 1994 analysis argued that the velocity inversion below about 4.3 kilometres was most likely explained by variations in bulk chemical composition together with foliation-induced anisotropy. They explicitly reviewed, rather than simply adopted, the competing proposal that enhanced porosity, microfracturing and free water caused the low-velocity layer.

That disagreement improves the Kola story. The hole did not replace one confident cartoon with another. It supplied enough direct material to make several mechanisms testable against one another.

By the 1992 Kola seismic campaign, the experiment had become almost recursive: use a borehole drilled to correct remote geophysics as the receiver for more geophysics. Two vertical seismic profiles sampled the well every 25 metres between about 2.2 and 6 kilometres while a 38-kilometre surface reflection line crossed the region. The resulting work resolved dipping lithologic contacts, a major shear zone and deeper subhorizontal reflectors. Some reflections could be tied to actual layering. Others were better associated with faults, cataclastic fabrics, anisotropy or structures whose physical origin remained disputed.

A seismic reflector, in other words, is an observation before it is a geological noun.

Velocity depends on the elastic properties of the rock frame, mineral composition and orientation, cracks, pore space, pressure, saturation and temperature. Close cracks and a rock generally stiffens. Add fluid and P- and S-waves respond differently. Align hornblende or mica and the measured velocity depends on direction. Put a fault zone through the section and a large change can occur gradually enough that the reflection coefficient remains modest. Several different underground arrangements can therefore cast similar shadows at the surface.

Kola made that non-uniqueness physical. A piece of core could be put under pressure in a laboratory. A sonic tool could measure the wall of the hole. A surface source could send energy down to a receiver in the borehole. Those measurements did not always collapse onto one explanation, which is precisely why the experiment mattered.

There is a temptation to call this a failure of seismic imaging. It is closer to a calibration of ambition. Seismology had correctly detected structure that mattered. Drilling showed that assigning a single lithology to that structure could be much less secure than detecting the structure itself.

The distinction travels well beyond the Kola Peninsula. Resource exploration, geothermal projects, carbon storage and deep waste disposal all begin by inferring inaccessible geometry from indirect measurements. A reflector interpreted as a seal can contain a fractured zone. A velocity anomaly can partly reflect fabric rather than a new rock type. A basement described as crystalline can still contain connected fluids. The operational question is rarely whether the geophysical image is 'right.' It is which parts of the interpretation remain right when a second kind of measurement arrives.

SG-3 was unusually good at producing that second measurement, and unusually bad at letting it stay simple.

Near seven kilometres, the line survived.

What changed was what anyone could honestly say the line meant.
