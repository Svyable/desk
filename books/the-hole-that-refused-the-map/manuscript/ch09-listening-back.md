# Listening Back Into the Hole

Once the hole existed, seismology could be turned around.

Instead of using only surface receivers to infer the crust, researchers could put instruments in the borehole and compare downhole measurements with surface reflection profiles. Vertical seismic profiling made the well a calibration line through the inverse problem.

The Kola-92 international experiment is important for this reason. By the early 1990s the Soviet project was politically and financially collapsing, but the borehole had become too scientifically valuable to remain a national object. Russian, British, American, Norwegian and other researchers used the site to study crustal reflectivity with direct geological control.

The results complicated the layer cake further. Reflections could be associated with dipping compositional units, shear zones and fluid-filled structures. The crust was reflective because it contained contrasts, but contrast did not mean one universal kind of boundary.

This is what calibration does when it works. It does not necessarily make the remote instrument simpler. It teaches the instrument how many different physical things can look alike.

Kola therefore changed the meaning of a seismic image without making seismic imaging obsolete. That is a mature relationship between direct and indirect evidence. The direct measurement constrains interpretation; the remote method supplies regional context the borehole can never provide.

One hole cannot map a continent. One seismic line cannot tell you exactly what every reflector is.

Together they can embarrass each other's assumptions.

That is a good experimental arrangement.
