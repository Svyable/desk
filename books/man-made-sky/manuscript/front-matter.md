# MAN-MADE SKY

*The year humans learned to manufacture a radiation belt*

Sven Hardy Benson

This is a book about a secret experiment that worked.

That sentence is dangerous because it makes nearby fantasies feel cheaper. They are not. Project Argus is useful precisely because its evidentiary chain is unusually good: named operation, known dates, known launch system, known physical prediction, purpose-built instrumentation, measured artificial radiation belts, later scientific publication. The astonishing thing is documented. It therefore gives no evidentiary credit to a different astonishing thing merely because both involve governments and the sky.

The book keeps four evidence classes separate. **Documented** means primary records, official histories, contemporaneous scientific measurements, or strong archival reconstruction establish the proposition. **Credible but incomplete** means serious reporting, participant recollection, or technically coherent reconstruction exists but an important primary link remains missing. **Disputed** means the proposition has identifiable advocates but lacks independent confirmation or faces substantial contrary evidence. **Conjecture** means the book is making a synthesis or asking a question that the evidence has not answered.

Nuclear detonations in near-Earth space are documented. Artificial radiation belts are documented. Project Argus's basic experimental purpose and measured effects are documented. The larger claim that Cold War states were willing to treat portions of the geophysical environment as an experimental medium is an inference from those facts, not a claim that every proposed environmental manipulation was attempted. Claims about present secret radiation-belt engineering, weather control, UAP, or hidden planetary weapons do not inherit Argus's evidence.

The governing prohibition is simple: **an extraordinary precedent changes the prior on what institutions might attempt; it does not change the evidence for what they actually did in another case.**
