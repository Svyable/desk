# Borrowed Senses

*When Intelligence Agencies Built Machines Out of Animals*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a photograph in the CIA museum of a camera made small enough to ride on a pigeon. There is a Navy program, still operating, in which dolphins find objects that electronic sonar has trouble finding in cluttered water. There was a CIA experiment that put a microphone, transmitter, and antenna on a cat and discovered the part of the system nobody had engineered: the cat.

These stories are usually told as Cold War comedy. That is a mistake. The comedy is real, but it hides the engineering problem.

An animal arrives with sensors we did not design, locomotion we did not build, camouflage we did not invent, and a nervous system whose internal representation of the world we only partly understand. Intelligence agencies noticed. So did militaries. Long before autonomy became a software category, they were trying to recruit autonomous organisms into technical systems.

This book is about what happened when they tried.

It is not a catalog of spy-animal curiosities. It is an investigation of a recurring bargain: borrow a biological capability that exceeds the machine, then discover that the capability cannot be separated cleanly from the living thing that owns it. A dolphin's biosonar comes with a dolphin. A pigeon's navigation comes with a pigeon. A cat's unobtrusiveness comes with appetites, fear, attention, fatigue, preference, and the ancient feline conviction that your priorities are negotiable.

The evidence is uneven, and the unevenness matters. Four classes are kept separate throughout this manuscript.

**Verified, declassified, or documented facts** include programs and capabilities established in official records, released archival material, or strong primary scientific literature. The CIA publicly documents the Acoustikitty experiment and its termination without operational deployment. The Agency displays and describes its pigeon camera. The U.S. Navy openly describes its Marine Mammal Program and the operational roles of bottlenose dolphins and California sea lions.

**Credible but incomplete reporting or testimony** includes participant recollections, institutional histories, and technically plausible accounts for which important operational records remain unavailable. These can establish what a participant says happened. They do not automatically establish the event independently.

**Disputed allegations** include claims that military dolphins were trained as offensive weapons, stories about the death of the CIA's instrumented cat, and other tales whose repetition has often outrun their documentary ancestry. These claims are treated as provenance problems before they are treated as events.

**Speculative synthesis** is mine. The central synthesis is that animal intelligence programs expose a limit in the usual history of computing and autonomy: some of the earliest useful autonomous sensing systems were not computers approximating animals but institutions trying to wrap technical missions around animals that already possessed the autonomy. That interpretation may prove useful. It is not a declassified finding.

There is another trap here. A strange documented program can become collateral evidence for every stranger story adjacent to it. It should not. The fact that the CIA implanted a listening system in a cat does not authenticate every story about intelligence experimentation on animals. The fact that the Navy has operational dolphins does not prove claims about armed dolphins. Reality is under no obligation to become more permissive merely because one improbable thing turned out to be true.

The opposite error is just as bad. Ridicule can make a documented program disappear in plain sight. A pigeon camera sounds ridiculous until you compare its altitude with a reconnaissance aircraft. A dolphin mine detector sounds quaint until the Navy tells you that in some shallow, cluttered environments the animal still finds objects that are difficult for electronic sonar. The relevant question is not whether the program makes a good anecdote. It is whether the organism solves a real sensing, mobility, concealment, or recovery problem.

That is where this book begins: not with a joke about a cat, but with a system diagram that left out the most important component.
