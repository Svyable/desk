# BORROWED SENSES

## How Intelligence Agencies Learned to Recruit Biology Before They Could Build It

Sven Hardy Benson

## 1. The Dolphin Found It in Thirty Seconds

There is a category error hiding inside the phrase *military technology*.

We picture titanium. Silicon. Gallium nitride. A clean room somewhere with people wearing white suits and speaking in acronyms. The weapon, sensor, vehicle or intelligence platform begins as an engineered object and eventually acquires enough capability to enter the world.

Sometimes the Navy used a dolphin.

During RIMPAC in 2018, a bottlenose dolphin from the U.S. Navy Marine Mammal Program was sent into a simulated minefield off Southern California. The animal entered the water, searched, and surfaced within thirty seconds reporting a detection. Its handler sent it back to verify. The dolphin returned, received a marking device, carried it down, placed it near the target, and surfaced. Divers subsequently confirmed the mark.

This sounds quaint until you say exactly what happened.

A biological system performed underwater target search, acoustic interrogation, classification, navigation, decision, two-way communication, reacquisition and precision localization in a cluttered environment. The system was reusable. It ran on fish. It had been manufactured by evolution.

The Navy's own current account is unusually candid. Bottlenose dolphins possess a sonar capability that remains extraordinarily difficult to duplicate in the environments where mines become hardest to find. The program says dolphins can locate objects that electronic sonar struggles with, including mines buried beneath the seafloor. California sea lions contribute low-light vision, directional underwater hearing, deep-diving ability and trainability. The program dates its work to 1959 and says more than a dozen marine mammal species—as well as sharks, rays, turtles and marine birds—were once tested before the operational system narrowed to dolphins and sea lions.

That is not a sideshow in the history of sensing.

It is a clue.

The twentieth century is usually told as the century in which machines acquired senses. Radar gave machines something like distant touch. Sonar gave them ears underwater. Infrared gave them vision beyond red. Satellites gave states eyes above borders. Computers eventually learned to classify what the sensors collected.

Running beside that story is a stranger one. Whenever the engineered sensor was not good enough, intelligence and military organizations sometimes went shopping in evolution's catalog.

The CIA built a camera small enough for a pigeon to carry over a target. This was not simply because pigeons were available. Aircraft such as the U-2 and A-12 operated at enormous altitude. CORONA satellites were higher still. A pigeon could cross the target hundreds of feet above it while looking like exactly what it was: a pigeon. The agency's Office of Research and Development built a miniature camera with an interval mechanism, battery-powered film advance and harness. The bird supplied the flight control, navigation, propulsion, obstacle avoidance, recovery system and camouflage.

Read that sentence again without the bird joke.

The camera was the easy part.

The pigeon was the autonomous vehicle.

Parts of those missions remain classified. That matters. A released museum artifact proves an instrument existed. It does not prove every story subsequently attached to the instrument, and it certainly does not give us permission to fill the classified space with imagination. This book will not do that. Secrecy is evidence about access, not a blank check for capability.

But enough is public to reveal a pattern.

In 2003, at Umm Qasr during the Iraq war, the Navy paired bottlenose dolphins with REMUS unmanned underwater vehicles and human explosive-ordnance teams. REMUS did what machines did well: methodically survey large areas without tiring. Dolphins were then used to reacquire and discriminate potential mine targets. Humans handled demolition. This was not animal versus machine. It was a division of cognitive labor among species and artifacts.

That phrase is going to bother people.

Good.

We have spent decades defining intelligence in ways that flatter the human brain and, more recently, the digital computer. Yet an operational system does not care which component gets philosophical credit. It cares whether the mine gets found.

A dolphin's echolocation is not intelligence in the same sense as writing a sonnet. A pigeon's homing is not reconnaissance analysis. A sea lion attaching a recovery line is not strategic planning. Confusing these things would be sentimental nonsense.

But the reverse mistake is just as bad: treating evolved perception and behavior as dumb peripherals because they occur inside an animal.

The animal programs force a more useful decomposition.

What is the sensor?

What extracts a feature?

What recognizes the target?

What navigates?

What decides whether the evidence crosses threshold?

What communicates confidence?

What learns from correction?

What part is the animal, what part is the trainer, what part is the instrument, and what part exists only in the relationship between them?

Once you ask those questions, the military animal stops being a curiosity and becomes an early biohybrid system.

Not a cyborg in the movie sense. Something more interesting: a system assembled across incompatible kinds of intelligence.

The dolphin did not need to understand mine warfare.

The handler did not need to echolocate.

The mine marker did not need to think.

Together they could do something none of the components could do alone.

That may be the oldest lesson in this book and the one most relevant to the newest technology we have.

We keep asking when machines will become intelligent enough to replace us.

States have been solving a different problem for a very long time.

They ask what can sense the thing we cannot sense, go where we cannot go, discriminate what our instruments cannot discriminate, and return with an answer we can use.

Sometimes the answer had feathers.

Sometimes it had whiskers.

Sometimes it breathed through a hole in the top of its head.
