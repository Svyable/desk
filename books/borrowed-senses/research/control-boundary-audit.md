# Control-boundary audit — Borrowed Senses

## Bounded editorial problem

Chapter 1 has a strong line: “The pigeon was the autonomous vehicle.” It is memorable, but the public CIA record supplies the counterexample that should keep the book from romanticizing biological autonomy. CIA’s own Pigeon Camera museum page says the pigeon cameras were ultimately ineffective because the birds had trouble flying over the exact locations the Agency needed photographed. The animal supplied propulsion, homing, concealment, obstacle avoidance, and a flight path; it did not supply arbitrary waypoint control.

That failure is not an embarrassment to the book’s thesis. It is one of the best pieces of evidence for the more precise version of it: borrowing an evolved capability means borrowing its control limits too.

## Primary / authoritative sources checked

### CIA — Pigeon Camera

https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/artifact/pigeon-camera/

CIA documents the miniature camera, timed/interval photography, battery-powered film advance, low-altitude imagery advantage, and continued classification of mission details. Crucially, the same page says the system was ultimately ineffective because birds had difficulty flying over the exact locations CIA wanted imaged.

**Manuscript consequence:** preserve the camera/autonomy inversion, but Chapter 3 should distinguish homing autonomy from programmable navigation. “Autonomous vehicle” is defensible only if the next move is to ask what kind of autonomy and under whose control.

### CIA — Natural Spies: Animals in Espionage

https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/natural-spies-animals-in-espionage/

CIA’s public history describes pigeons carrying miniature cameras and emphasizes concealment and the detail available from imagery collected hundreds of feet above a target. This is useful institutional history, but it is not independent evidence of operational success and should not be counted separately from the museum account when assessing efficacy.

### Office of Naval Research — Men, Mammals, and Machines

https://www.onr.navy.mil/media-center/news-releases/men-mammals-and-machines

The Umm Qasr account gives the book a much better control architecture than the opening presently extracts from it. REMUS systematically surveyed the channel; sonar images were processed; dolphins inspected potential targets and reported back; human teams handled demolition. The source explicitly describes the machine as strong at repetitive wide-area survey and dolphins as useful for reacquisition/discrimination.

**Manuscript consequence:** “division of cognitive labor” should become a reconstructed information path rather than a metaphor. Map where sensing, preprocessing, classification, navigation, communication, and action occurred, and identify which claims come from Navy/ONR participants rather than independent performance evaluation.

### NIWC Pacific — Marine Mammal Program

https://www.niwcpacific.navy.mil/About/Departments/Intelligence-Surveillance-and-Reconnaissance/Marine-Mammal-Program/

The current Navy program page says dolphins find mines and other objects difficult for electronic sonar, especially in shallow/cluttered environments, and says present technology remains unable to match all animal missions. Treat this as an authoritative description of the Navy program and its own capability claims, not as a neutral head-to-head benchmark.

## Strongest counterargument to the book

The phrase “intelligence agencies recruited biology because evolution beat machines” can quietly turn a mixed historical record into a victory lap for animals. The pigeon program is a direct warning: a biological platform can possess extraordinary navigation and still fail the mission because the military requirement is not “go home”; it is “go home by this exact route, over this exact target, at a useful geometry, reliably enough to plan around.”

Likewise, Navy statements that dolphins outperform electronic sonar in difficult conditions establish the program’s stated rationale, not a complete comparative performance curve. Selection effects matter: programs publish demonstrations and institutional histories more readily than failed trials, marginal missions, animal refusals, retraining burdens, weather limits, handler dependence, logistics, welfare costs, or cases where a machine quietly became good enough.

The strongest conventional alternative is therefore not that animals were gimmicks. It is that their apparent superiority was task-specific and system-dependent: useful only inside a large human training/logistics apparatus, and often traded against poor programmability, uncertain repeatability, biological variability, and limited mission transfer.

## Evidence ladder for later chapters

Do not let these propositions collapse into one another:

1. the animal possesses an unusual sensory or navigational capability;
2. trainers can elicit a relevant behavior in controlled conditions;
3. the behavior can be coupled to an instrument or reporting protocol;
4. the integrated system performs the operational task reliably;
5. it outperforms the contemporary engineered alternative on a specified metric;
6. the advantage survives total-system costs, logistics, failure modes, and deployment constraints;
7. the biological component is eventually replaced because engineering catches up rather than because doctrine, budgets, ethics, or mission requirements change.

Each case should stop at the highest rung actually supported.

## Quantitative research requirement

For pigeon reconnaissance, recover mission/trial records sufficient to estimate target-overflight success, useful-frame yield, navigation error, release geometry, recovery rate, and weather/training constraints. For marine mammals, seek controlled or operationally relevant comparisons reporting probability of detection/classification, false alarms, search rate, localization error, bottom/clutter conditions, buried-versus-proud targets, handler/training burden, endurance, and reacquisition performance. Avoid writing “better than sonar” as though sonar were one instrument or “machines caught up” as though replacement had one cause.

## Narrative opportunity

The pigeon failure should probably arrive soon after “The camera was the easy part.” It changes the meaning of the line rather than merely qualifying it. The camera may indeed have been the easy part; the hard part was persuading an animal whose navigation had been optimized for returning home to satisfy a reconnaissance planner’s demand for an exact intermediate path.

That gives Chapter 3 a live problem: the CIA did not merely need a bird that knew where to go. It needed a bird that could be wrong in precisely the way a machine is allowed to be commanded.

The same question can recur at Umm Qasr with its meaning changed. There, the Navy did not ask one component to do everything. REMUS surveyed, people processed, dolphins reacquired/discriminated, and humans demolished. The operational success may have come not from finding the universally superior intelligence but from assigning each component the part of the problem it could actually control.

## Publication hold

Do not promote CIA/Navy institutional claims into neutral comparative-performance claims until primary technical evaluations or independent performance literature support the relevant metric. Do not describe pigeon reconnaissance as operationally successful merely because the camera existed or imagery was possible. Preserve the CIA’s own “ultimately ineffective” finding as counterevidence.
