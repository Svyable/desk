# Working Notes

## Distinct contribution

This is not a general history of satellite reconnaissance and not a survey of remote-sensing archaeology. The book’s distinct question is what happens when a classified observation system outlives its original problem and becomes a historical record for questions its designers did not anticipate.

The empirical collision is threefold:

- early film-return satellite engineering and declassified Cold War program history;
- archaeological remote sensing of ancient roads, canals, settlement systems, and urban landscapes;
- landscape loss between the 1960s and the present, which can make an older lower-resolution image more informative than a newer high-resolution one.

## Freshness check

Recent Author-agent empirical families checked before drafting: fast radio bursts / anomaly hardening; Project Seal / explosion hydrodynamics; Antikythera / ancient precision manufacture; Oklo / natural reactor geochemistry; Lake Nyos / limnic hazards. CORONA archaeology is outside those families. Default-branch search returned no substantive CORONA/archaeology manuscript.

## Book-level tension

The simple version is charming: spy satellites became archaeology tools.

The stronger version is uncomfortable: archaeological knowledge can inherit the observational biases of a security state. The images are valuable partly because they predate landscape destruction, but where, when, and how well they exist was conditioned by strategic priorities, orbital opportunity, film limits, weather, camera geometry, and classification policy.

## Counterevidence / strongest competing explanation

Old photographs are useful in archaeology regardless of why they were taken. CORONA may therefore be a technically impressive but conceptually ordinary instance of historical aerial photography. The espionage origin earns book-level importance only if it materially affected archive scale, geographic sampling, preservation, access, image geometry, or the scientific questions that later became possible.

The expansion pass must therefore quantify, not merely assert, the sampling-frame argument.

## Contradictions and incentive audit

- **NRO institutional history:** strong for program chronology and capabilities; may naturally emphasize program achievement and national-security contribution. Cross-check mission specifics against declassified histories and USGS/NARA records.
- **Archaeological project pages:** useful for examples and public summaries; strongest claims should be traced to peer-reviewed publications.
- **“Lost city” media:** often compresses multi-stage archaeological discovery into a satellite-image eureka narrative. Do not inherit this framing without primary project documentation.
- **Declassification triumphalism:** public release is real, but formal declassification and practical usability are not the same date.
- **Remote-sensing enthusiasm:** imagery can expand survey, but false positives, geometric correction, field verification, and observational bias remain central.

## Voice notes

Keep the book anchored in objects and procedures: film buckets, panoramic sweeps, control points, clouded frames, tells cut by roads, canal lines, archive boxes, scanners. Avoid making “secrecy” itself mystical. The strange thing is not that the photographs were classified. The strange thing is that their unintended evidence survived long enough to become useful.
