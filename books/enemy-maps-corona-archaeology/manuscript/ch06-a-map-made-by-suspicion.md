# A Map Made by Suspicion

No global archive is actually global. It has edges, priorities, weather, budgets, blind spots, and somebody deciding what deserves another pass.

CORONA is often described as nearly worldwide coverage, and that is true enough to explain its later scientific value. It is not true enough to ignore how that coverage was produced. Collection followed strategic interest. The Soviet Union, China, the Middle East, military corridors, border regions, and other areas of concern drew attention for reasons that had nothing to do with archaeology.

That gives the archive a hidden sampling frame. Archaeologists working in regions heavily photographed during the Cold War may inherit rich time slices that colleagues elsewhere do not. Repeated coverage can make one landscape unusually legible across seasons or years. Sparse coverage can make another look archaeologically quiet when the real difference lies in the history of American intelligence priorities.

The problem is familiar in another form. Archaeological surveys also have sampling frames: roads, permissions, fields that can be walked, landowners who cooperate, security conditions, budgets, vegetation, the location of previous excavations. CORONA does not abolish those biases. It adds its own.

Cloud makes the point brutally. USGS metadata warns that a substantial portion of declassified imagery contains significant cloud cover. A satellite may have passed over the right place on the right day and still preserved almost nothing useful from the ground. The archive can be dense in mission records and empty in archaeological information.

A map made by suspicion can still become a map of antiquity. But the reason the photograph exists belongs in the interpretation. Otherwise later scholarship risks mistaking the reach of a twentieth-century security apparatus for an unbiased survey of the ancient world.
