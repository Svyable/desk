# Distortion Is Part of the Evidence

A CORONA photograph is not a map laid flat by nature. It is an image produced by a moving satellite, a panoramic camera, a curved Earth, terrain relief, film transport, scan geometry, and later digitization. The photograph has to be argued into geographic shape.

That technical nuisance is one of the best protections against mystical thinking about remote sensing. The image does not arrive as truth. It arrives as measurement with a history.

Panoramic cameras sweep across a wide field of view. Features near different parts of the frame are displaced in ways that become significant when a researcher tries to overlay the photograph on modern mapping data. Terrain adds more displacement. Film can shrink or deform. Scanning can introduce another layer. Orthorectification attempts to model these effects and anchor the image to known control points and elevation data.

Casana and Cothren’s archaeological work on CORONA emphasized that correcting these distortions was not a cosmetic step. If the goal is to trace a canal, compare settlement distributions, derive elevation, or measure change, geometric reliability matters.

This chapter belongs in the book because technical correction is epistemology made visible. Every historical source has distortion, but photographs tempt us to forget that. CORONA makes the problem literal enough to measure.

The same discipline should apply to interpretation. A dark line can be an ancient route, a drainage feature, a modern track, an image artifact, or a boundary created by later cultivation. Confidence should rise when independent evidence converges and fall when it does not.

The most useful photographs are not the ones that seem to need no explanation. They are the ones whose errors can be characterized well enough that the remaining signal becomes trustworthy.
