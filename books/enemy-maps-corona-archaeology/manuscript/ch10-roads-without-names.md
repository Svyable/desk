# Roads Without Names

Some ancient roads survive without paving, milestones, or documents. What remains is use written into the ground.

Across northern Mesopotamia, shallow linear depressions known as hollow ways mark routes repeatedly traveled by people and animals. From the surface they can be difficult to recognize as parts of a larger system. From above, especially in older imagery taken before modern land-use changes blurred them, their geometry becomes harder to ignore.

Jason Ur and other researchers used declassified CORONA photography to map thousands of kilometers of these route traces. Their appearance often depends on subtle differences in moisture and vegetation: a slight depression collects water, plants respond, and a former path becomes a darker line in a photograph. The road is visible partly because it stopped being a road.

This is a good example of why satellite archaeology cannot be reduced to resolution. The important signal is relational. A single dark line may have many causes. Repeated lines radiating from settlements, connecting sites, crossing fields in persistent patterns, and matching known topography can support a much richer interpretation.

The roads also force a change in archaeological scale. Excavation tends to privilege places where people stopped: houses, temples, workshops, graves, fortifications. Hollow ways preserve movement between those places. They are the negative architecture of settlement systems.

CORONA became useful because its 1960s view caught many of these traces before later field reorganization weakened them. The archive therefore preserves not merely objects but connectivity.

That is one of the recurring pleasures of this material. A system designed to look for large, contemporary strategic features proved unusually good at recovering ancient infrastructure that was almost too ordinary to become monumental.
