# Cities Before the Bulldozers

Archaeology often meets destruction after the fact. A road cuts through a mound. A suburb covers a field system. A reservoir reaches its operating level. The phrase “site preservation” can remain abstract until the baseline is gone.

CORONA offers baselines.

Early imagery across parts of the Near East captures many places before late twentieth-century development accelerated. In some frames, the value lies less in discovering an unknown site than in seeing a known one before its margins disappeared. Modern roads had not yet cut across it. New irrigation had not blurred older channels. Expanding towns had not consumed the low-relief edges that are easy to miss from ground level.

The early Islamic city of al-Raqqa became one useful demonstration. Researchers used CORONA imagery to study topography and the surrounding cultural landscape in a place where later change complicated direct observation. The photographs did not replace archaeology on the ground; they supplied a dated spatial condition against which later transformations could be read.

This shifts the question from “what is there?” to “what was still visible then?” Landscapes are processes. Ancient features decay after abandonment, and modern land use adds another layer of erasure. A feature can survive for centuries and then become difficult to recognize within a few decades.

Mechanized leveling, dams, deep plowing, road building, quarrying, urbanization, and industrial agriculture can alter broad areas within a generation. CORONA makes some of that recent transformation measurable.

A before-image does not save a site. It saves evidence that the site once looked different. Sometimes that is the only remaining way to recover its former shape.
