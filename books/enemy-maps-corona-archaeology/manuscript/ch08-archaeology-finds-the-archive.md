# Archaeology Finds the Archive

Archaeologists did not need convincing that old photographs could matter. Aerial photography had been exposing ancient field systems, buried walls, roads, and settlement patterns for decades. What CORONA added was age, scale, and coverage in places where modern development was moving faster than archaeological documentation.

Near Eastern researchers adopted the imagery quickly because the timing was unusually fortunate. Many photographs captured landscapes in the 1960s before large reservoirs, highway construction, mechanized agriculture, suburban growth, quarrying, and other changes transformed them. The image did not show antiquity directly. It showed a modern landscape in which antiquity was still easier to see.

That difference sounds small until a tell has been cut by roads, a canal trace leveled, or an ancient trackway absorbed into plowed fields. A higher-resolution modern satellite image can be less archaeologically informative than a blurrier photograph taken before the feature disappeared.

Jesse Casana, Jackson Cothren, Tuna Kalayci, Jason Ur, and others helped turn CORONA from a specialist curiosity into a reproducible archaeological tool. Their work did not depend on the glamour of espionage. It depended on orthorectification, georeferencing, stereo interpretation, careful comparison with field evidence, and the willingness to treat image artifacts as possible errors rather than discoveries.

The resulting studies changed the scale at which some ancient landscapes could be discussed. Routes could be mapped across regions rather than around excavated sites. Canal systems could be followed beyond the monument that first drew attention. Settlement patterns could be compared with water, soils, and communications. Archaeology gained not merely more sites but more relationships.

That distinction is central. A satellite is seductive because it seems to reveal hidden things. Its better contribution is often less theatrical: it makes spatial structure visible. CORONA’s strongest archaeological achievements are not sensational lost-city announcements. They are networks, distributions, before-images, and the patient recovery of context.
