# HEXAGON Opens Another Door

CORONA is not the end of the archive. It is the first doorway.

Later declassification releases opened imagery from KH-7 and KH-9 systems. For archaeologists, the KH-9 HEXAGON archive is especially important because it extends high-resolution historical coverage into the 1970s and early 1980s. USGS describes hundreds of thousands of declassified scenes from the program, while recent archaeological work has begun to build practical workflows around them.

Emily Hammer, Mackinley FitzPatrick, and Jason Ur have argued that HEXAGON can complement CORONA rather than merely replace it. The later imagery offers another historical slice, often with different resolution, coverage, and image characteristics. That allows researchers to observe change between declassified systems before modern commercial satellite archives begin.

The chronology matters. A landscape photographed by CORONA in the 1960s, by HEXAGON in the 1970s, by Landsat over subsequent decades, and by modern high-resolution systems later can be treated as a sequence rather than a single frozen comparison. Archaeological visibility becomes something that can be watched disappear, persist, or reappear under different land-use conditions.

This also complicates the clean narrative in which one secret program accidentally rescued the past. What exists is a layered state archive produced by successive technical systems, each with different purposes and constraints. Declassification occurs in waves. Digitization occurs unevenly. Some film becomes easy to download; other material remains difficult to discover or process.

The deeper lesson is institutional. Data can outlive the bureaucratic reason for their creation. When that happens, continuity of preservation and public access determines whether technical capability becomes historical memory or simply decays in storage.

CORONA teaches us to value the before-image. HEXAGON makes the archive temporal.
