# The Landscape That No Longer Exists

An old satellite image can become more valuable after the ground gets worse.

This is the inversion at the center of CORONA archaeology. Newer sensors are generally better instruments. They can have finer resolution, multispectral bands, easier geolocation, and near-instant access. Yet a technically superior image taken after a landscape has been altered may contain less archaeological information than an older, imperfect photograph.

The comparison is easiest to see where modern agriculture has reorganized fields. Deep plowing can flatten low earthworks and spread material. Irrigation can redraw drainage. Bulldozing can erase subtle relief. Expanding roads and towns fragment continuous patterns into disconnected remnants. A modern image records these changes accurately. Accuracy is not the same as historical usefulness.

CORONA becomes a time machine only in this modest sense: it returns us to an earlier visible surface. It does not reconstruct what lay underground in 1967. It does not restore destroyed deposits. It does not show a Bronze Age landscape untouched by later centuries. It shows a twentieth-century landscape in which some older traces remained more legible than they do now.

That qualification matters. “Preserved landscape” is shorthand, not literal preservation. Every CORONA frame already contained thousands of years of reuse, erosion, construction, abandonment, cultivation, and memory. The image is not antiquity before disturbance. It is one interruption in a very long disturbance history.

Still, one interruption can be enough. Archaeology often advances by identifying differences between observations. If a feature appears clearly in 1967 and has vanished by 2005, that difference is evidence. It can guide archival research, field survey, excavation priorities, heritage assessment, or simply documentation of loss.

The photograph cannot stop the bulldozer. It can make forgetting harder.
