# The Bias in the Sky

Every archive has a politics of survival. CORONA adds a politics of looking.

Its cameras did not wander randomly. Collection priorities reflected strategic concerns, technical opportunity, and mission planning. The resulting archive is broad enough to feel comprehensive while still being patterned by the interests of the state that created it.

That matters when scholars count what they can see. Suppose one region has repeated high-quality coverage from favorable seasons while another has sparse, cloudy, or poorly scanned imagery. A map of detected archaeological features may partly reflect ancient settlement and partly reflect twentieth-century observation density. Without an image-availability model, those causes can blur.

The problem is not unique to CORONA. Museum collections, excavation records, colonial surveys, rescue archaeology, and modern field projects all produce structured absences. What is distinctive here is that the sampling bias comes from an unrelated purpose. Archaeological visibility is being inherited from Cold War attention.

This creates a methodological obligation. Coverage, mission date, season, cloud, image quality, resolution, camera type, and processing history belong beside the archaeological interpretation. A feature count without observation conditions may be precise and still misleading.

There is also a conceptual reward. Once the bias is acknowledged, the archive becomes evidence about two landscapes at once: the physical landscape on the film and the geopolitical landscape that determined why the film was exposed there.

A photograph of an ancient road is never only an ancient road. It is also a record of who had the power, money, and reason to photograph that ground from orbit in 1967.
