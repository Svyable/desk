# February 1995

In February 1995, hundreds of thousands of old satellite photographs entered a different kind of public life.

President Bill Clinton’s Executive Order 12951 directed the declassification of imagery from early American photo-reconnaissance programs including CORONA, ARGON, and LANYARD. The National Archives became responsible for original film holdings and public viewing access. The U.S. Geological Survey received duplicate film to support scientific use and distribution.

The scale was unusual. USGS later described more than 800,000 images in the first release and more than 990,000 photographs across early declassification releases. Material collected from 1959 onward suddenly extended the public record of Earth’s surface back before Landsat began operations in 1972.

A legal release, however, does not make a difficult archive easy. Practical use required cataloguing, scanning, metadata, affordable distribution, software, and methods for correcting the geometry of panoramic cameras. A photograph can be public and still remain inconvenient enough that only specialists can work with it.

That inconvenience shaped archaeology’s adoption of CORONA. Researchers had to learn how to turn a distorted strip of film into a geographically reliable surface before they could measure ancient roads or compare a 1960s landscape with modern development. The scientific afterlife depended as much on mundane infrastructure as on the executive order itself.

There is a tempting celebratory version of the story in which secrecy ends and knowledge begins. It is too clean. Civilian scholars had lost decades of possible access, and landscapes changed during those decades. Declassification did not restore what had been destroyed. It revealed that photographs of some of those vanished conditions had survived.

The image had not changed. Access had. That distinction is the hinge of the book.
