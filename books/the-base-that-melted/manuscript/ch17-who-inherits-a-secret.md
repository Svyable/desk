# Who Inherits a Secret

Secrets have owners.

Liabilities have heirs.

That is the Camp Century problem after the drama is stripped away.

The Army that built the base no longer needs it. Project Iceworm never became an operational force. The Cold War ended. Thule became Pituffik Space Base. Greenland's political status evolved. Environmental law changed. Climate science changed. The physical remains stayed in the ice.

Who is responsible for a site after the mission that justified it has vanished?

The legal answer depends on agreements, jurisdictions and negotiated obligations. The political answer is harder because responsibility contains more than legal liability. The United States created the installation. Denmark was the sovereign power under whose defense agreement it operated. Greenland bears the place and the environmental inheritance.

Climate change makes the triangle dynamic.

If the debris remains safely buried for another century, the practical burden may be monitoring.

If pathways change, the burden could become remediation.

If the hazard is delayed beyond current institutions, the burden becomes preservation of knowledge itself.

That last obligation is underrated.

A future government cannot manage a site it has forgotten.

Maps, inventories, models, sample archives, radar surveys and institutional memory are part of containment. They do not physically isolate contaminants, but they preserve the ability to act before uncertainty becomes crisis.

Camp Century's basal sediment teaches the same lesson from the other direction. Forgotten material can become scientifically precious if preserved. Forgotten waste can become politically dangerous for the same reason: the physical object outlives the meaning attached to its label.

The challenge is to keep meaning synchronized with matter.

Modern governments are not designed for this elegantly. Elections run on years. Budgets run annually. Senior officials rotate. Agencies reorganize. Records migrate. Long-lived infrastructure creates obligations measured in decades or centuries.

The mismatch creates what might be called temporal offloading.

A decision looks cheap because part of its cost matures after the deciding institution's horizon.

Abandonment is especially vulnerable to this. Closing a facility ends operating expenses now. Complete remediation may cost more now. Leaving material in place transfers some risk into the future, where discount rates make it appear smaller.

Sometimes that is rational. In-situ containment can be safer than excavation. Disturbing contaminated material can create new exposure pathways. Remote Arctic work is dangerous and expensive. The correct answer is not always “dig it all up.”

The correct answer is to admit that leaving material in place is an active management strategy, not the absence of one.

It requires assumptions.

Those assumptions need monitoring.

Monitoring needs ownership.

Ownership needs institutional continuity.

Camp Century did not originally receive that governance chain because burial was treated as closure.

The climate program retrofits one.

This is the more general lesson for classified infrastructure. Secrecy can be temporary while environmental consequences are not. Classification systems need sunset procedures for physical responsibility, not only documentary disclosure.

When a program closes, someone should inherit the site ledger.

Where is everything?

What materials remain?

Which assumptions make the site safe?

Which measurements would reveal those assumptions failing?

Who has authority to act?

What information can be declassified without compromising current security?

What must be preserved even if nobody expects to need it for fifty years?

These are mundane questions. They are also how a civilization proves it understands time.

The old Camp Century story was about strategic surprise.

Hide enough missiles and the enemy cannot destroy them all.

The modern Camp Century story is about preventing surprise from our own past.

Do not let the buried site become unknown again.

That reversal is almost perfect.

The original system sought uncertainty as protection.

The inherited system needs measurement as protection.

Secrecy and stewardship optimize in opposite directions.

A weapons program benefits when adversaries know little.

A cleanup program benefits when successors know as much as possible.

The transition between those modes is rarely designed. It should be.

Camp Century shows what happens when it is not: historians, glaciologists, archivists, environmental scientists and diplomats reconstruct the object after the fact.

They have done that reconstruction remarkably well.

The remaining uncertainty is not evidence of failure. Some uncertainty is irreducible. The goal is not omniscience.

The goal is a future in which nobody can plausibly say, We had no idea that was there.
