# The 2016 Alarm

Some scientific papers are remembered for a number.

Some are remembered for a mechanism.

The Camp Century paper published in *Geophysical Research Letters* in August 2016 was remembered as a headline.

A secret nuclear base under Greenland might melt out.

That version was not invented from nothing. William Colgan and colleagues explicitly argued that under a high-emissions climate scenario, Camp Century could eventually shift from net accumulation toward net ablation, undermining the assumption that the buried installation and its wastes would remain sequestered indefinitely. They estimated that physical, chemical, biological and radiological wastes could ultimately be remobilized.

The words were strong because the underlying governance problem was strong.

A Cold War base had been abandoned under one climate expectation. Climate change was modifying that expectation. The political agreement that had permitted the base had never been designed to answer who should manage its environmental legacy under a future warmer regime.

This is climate change at its most legally awkward: not merely adding a new hazard but reopening an old contract.

The paper also arrived at the perfect media moment. Greenland had become one of the iconic visual objects of climate change. Satellite imagery showed outlet glaciers retreating. Surface melt events were receiving global attention. Sea-level projections placed the ice sheet at the center of long-term coastal risk. Add a buried American military base and the story became irresistible.

A good public story compresses.

A good scientific analysis often expands.

The compression here removed three distinctions.

First, Camp Century is not the Greenland Ice Sheet.

Second, future exposure of waste is not the same as waste currently reaching the environment.

Third, a modeled transition under a particular scenario is not an observed event with a fixed date.

These distinctions were available in the paper. They did not always survive the retelling.

This is not unique to climate science. Risk communication has a structural asymmetry. A sentence with uncertainty is longer than the headline without it. “Could eventually” loses against “will.” A scenario becomes a forecast. A high-end pathway becomes “scientists say.” An environmental mechanism becomes an image.

The image in this case was magnificent: a lost nuclear city emerging from a melting glacier.

The actual Camp Century problem was almost the opposite.

The danger was not that buildings would suddenly rise intact from the ice like a submarine surfacing. The structures were already deformed and crushed. The relevant concern was that a long-term shift in mass balance and hydrology could bring meltwater into contact with debris and contaminants, mobilizing material through water rather than unveiling a preserved city.

Water is less photogenic than ruins.

It is often more important.

The 2016 study estimated legacy waste categories using historical information and modeled the climatic threshold at which continued burial might eventually reverse. The authors emphasized political implications because climate change could transform an apparently settled Cold War site into a transboundary environmental responsibility involving the United States, Denmark and Greenland.

That framing was prescient.

It triggered exactly the response a useful risk paper should want: better field data.

The Danish Government, in agreement with the Government of Greenland, established the Camp Century Climate Monitoring Programme. The program was led by the Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland. In 2017 researchers returned to the site, installed automated climate stations, drilled 175 meters of ice core, and collected nearly 100 kilometers of ice-penetrating radar data.

The old secret base had generated a new transparent measurement system.

There is something satisfying about that symmetry.

A project once shaped by strategic opacity became a site with satellite-transmitted climate measurements available through a public portal.

But epistemology should not be made sentimental either. Monitoring programs have budgets, assumptions and finite horizons. They do not erase political disputes. They convert some uncertainties into observations.

That conversion is enough to change a story.

The radar work helped map the debris field. The climate stations improved local estimates of snowfall, temperature and melt. Firn modeling asked not only whether the surface might melt more often, but how deeply meltwater would penetrate before refreezing.

This is the sort of detail that reverses headlines.

A warmer summer does not mean water descends tens of meters through cold porous firn. Meltwater can refreeze near the surface, releasing latent heat and altering firn structure. Repeated melt can eventually create ice layers and new hydrological pathways, but the depth and persistence depend on local conditions.

The relevant distance at Camp Century is not from the surface to zero.

It is from the surface to the buried debris.

By the late 2010s, radar indicated that the main debris field was more than thirty meters below the surface. Continued accumulation was expected to push it deeper relative to the surface over coming decades under the modeled scenarios. That geometry matters enormously.

The difference between one meter of meltwater infiltration and thirty meters of depth is the difference between a climate signal and an exposure pathway.

This is why site-specific science often disappoints ideologues.

Global warming can be robustly established while a local feared consequence becomes less likely.

A single local correction does not falsify the global process.

Nor does the global process guarantee the local consequence.

The distinction should be ordinary. In public arguments it is frequently treated as betrayal.

Camp Century offers an unusually clean example because the same research community contains both the warning and the correction. Colgan, a coauthor of the 2016 paper, later led the monitoring program whose results narrowed the near-term concern.

That is not hypocrisy.

It is what evidence looks like when researchers are allowed to learn.

Science communication often rewards the first result more than the correction because novelty has asymmetrical value. “Base may melt out” is news. “New local measurements indicate it probably will not before 2100” is maintenance of knowledge.

But maintenance is where credibility lives.

The 2016 alarm deserves neither ridicule nor permanent promotion to prophecy.

It exposed a dependency that had been hidden inside the phrase “preserved for eternity.”

The safe burial of Camp Century was not a fact about the waste.

It was a fact about the climate regime.

Once that is understood, monitoring becomes mandatory even if the answer for the current century is reassuring.

The paper also raised a deeper political question that the later correction does not remove.

Suppose a site will remain isolated until 2100.

Then what?

Governments are bad at obligations that mature after the people making the decision are dead.

A century is long enough for agencies to reorganize, treaties to be reinterpreted, jurisdictions to change and archives to scatter. Environmental liability becomes an intergenerational relay race in which each runner inherits a map drawn by strangers.

Camp Century demonstrates that delay is not disappearance.

The 2016 paper compressed that lesson into a climate scenario.

The monitoring program expanded it back into physics.

Both are needed.

One tells us why to look.

The other tells us what is there.
