# Front Matter

## The Base That Melted
### Camp Century, Project Iceworm, and the Secret Under Greenland

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There is a particular kind of confidence that only appears when a civilization has just learned to move mountains with bulldozers and split atoms on schedule.

The United States had that confidence in Greenland.

The premise was almost embarrassingly literal. Snow falls. Snow becomes firn. Firn becomes ice. Dig a trench, put a building inside it, roof the trench, let winter erase the construction scars, and the base disappears into the white. If the roof deforms, reinforce it. If the trench narrows, shave it back. If fuel is expensive to haul over the ice, bring a nuclear reactor. If fixed missile silos are too easy for an enemy to target, put the missiles on rails and move them through a buried network so large that the Soviet Union can never know which launch sites are occupied.

This was not science fiction. Parts of it were built.

Camp Century operated inside the Greenland Ice Sheet. Soldiers slept there. Scientists worked there. A portable nuclear reactor made electricity and heat. Engineers measured how the tunnels behaved. Glaciologists drilled until they reached the bottom of the ice and then kept going into the dirt beneath it.

The giant missile network was not built.

That distinction matters because Camp Century attracts two opposite forms of bad storytelling. The first turns a documented but unrealized strategic concept into a completed secret arsenal. The second responds to exaggeration by flattening the place into an innocent research camp whose military context was incidental. Neither survives contact with the record.

The public research was real. The engineering was real. The nuclear reactor was real. Project Iceworm was real. The full missile force was not.

The ice also had its own vote.

It moved.

That sounds trivial because glaciers are famous for moving. It was not trivial to people attempting to build streets inside one. A tunnel is a demand that the surrounding material hold still in a particular shape. Ice can agree for a while. Then it begins to remember gravity.

Camp Century is therefore a story about a design premise colliding with a medium. But that failure is only the first act.

The Army's ice-core work became more durable than the Army's architecture. The core helped scientists reconstruct past climate. The basal sediment beneath it disappeared into institutional storage, was rediscovered decades later, and became evidence that the landscape under northwestern Greenland had once supported vegetation when the ice sheet was much smaller or absent there. A military project premised on using ice as permanent strategic cover helped produce evidence that Greenland's ice is not permanent on geological timescales.

Then the abandoned base itself re-entered the climate story.

A 2016 paper modeled a future in which warming could turn Camp Century from frozen archive into environmental liability by eventually mobilizing wastes left under the ice. Headlines had an irresistible shape: secret nuclear base, climate change, buried contamination, melting ice. The shape was good enough to travel faster than the uncertainty.

Field measurements later made the near-term picture less cinematic. Updated modeling released through the Danish-Greenlandic monitoring program concluded that the buried debris is unlikely to emerge or be reached by surface meltwater before 2100 under the modeled scenarios. The base has been pushed deeper by continuing snowfall and ice flow. That does not erase the waste. It changes the calendar.

This book keeps both results.

The point is not to catch science changing its mind. Science is supposed to change its mind when better local measurements arrive. The interesting object is the migration of certainty: from engineers who thought accumulation would bury the base indefinitely, to climate modelers who warned of eventual remobilization, to site-specific monitoring that narrowed the century-scale risk, all while Greenland as a whole continued losing ice.

Camp Century rewards anyone willing to keep two clocks running at once.

One is the human clock: budgets, administrations, treaties, careers, deployments, classified compartments, cleanup decisions, public explanations.

The other belongs to ice.

Ice has no respect for fiscal years. It records volcanoes, isotopes, dust, temperature, snowfall and atmospheric chemistry while human institutions rename themselves and forget where they stored the samples. It deforms buildings after the engineers leave. It preserves debris until conditions change. It can turn a secret into an archaeological layer.

The title of this book is deliberately unfair. Camp Century has not simply melted out. Current evidence says it remains buried and is likely to remain so through this century. The title refers to the premise that did melt: the idea that the ice could be treated as permanent, obedient infrastructure.

It could not.

The most useful question in this story is not, What did the government hide?

It is harder than that.

What happens when the thing doing the hiding is alive on a timescale longer than the secret?
