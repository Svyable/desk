# Build Nothing You Cannot Abandon

The easy lesson from Camp Century is that the Army should not have tried to build a missile network in moving ice.

That lesson is true and nearly useless.

The harder lesson is that every ambitious technology should be designed with its abandonment already visible.

Not merely its failure.

Its abandonment.

Failure engineering asks what happens when a component breaks, a power source trips, a tunnel deforms, a sensor lies, a missile misfires.

Abandonment engineering asks what happens when the institution stops caring.

Who turns it off?

What has to be removed?

What can safely remain?

How is the site marked for people who will never meet the builders?

Which assumptions keep the leftovers safe?

How will those assumptions be measured after the original operators are gone?

Camp Century had an answer to the first set of questions. It improvised the second.

The improvisation was understandable. The ice looked like a repository that managed itself. The central reactor could be removed. The rest could be buried by accumulation. The site was remote. The program was over.

That logic worked long enough to become history.

Then climate science changed the horizon.

The deeper principle is not environmentalist. It is systems engineering.

Every system exists inside a larger system whose variables it does not control.

Iceworm depended on ice dynamics.

Camp Century's waste isolation depended on climate.

PM-2A depended on logistics and specialist labor.

The climate archive depended on freezer custodians nobody in 1966 could name.

The modern monitoring program depends on governments continuing to fund attention.

There is no autonomous object.

This is where the book ends because Camp Century has become less a Cold War curiosity than a template.

We are building long-lived technical systems everywhere.

Carbon storage sites whose integrity must be understood for generations.

Nuclear repositories with warning horizons longer than languages usually remain stable.

Orbital constellations that leave debris after companies fail.

Deep-sea infrastructure in places where inspection is expensive.

AI data centers and transmission infrastructure whose useful life may be shorter than their environmental footprint.

Geoengineering proposals that deliberately manipulate planetary systems.

Biotechnology repositories containing organisms and sequences future institutions may interpret differently.

None of these is Camp Century.

All share the temporal mismatch.

Builders optimize for the mission.

The future inherits the residue.

A mature engineering culture would make abandonment part of feasibility from the beginning. Not a final environmental-impact appendix. A primary design variable.

What does this system become when its reason for existing disappears?

Project Iceworm failed before it had to answer at full scale.

In one sense, Greenland got lucky.

There is no 130,000-square-kilometer abandoned missile network under the ice.

Camp Century is enough.

Enough to show what happens when architecture is embedded in a medium that keeps moving.

Enough to leave a waste legacy.

Enough to generate diplomatic questions decades later.

Enough to produce an ice core that transformed climate science.

Enough to preserve basal sediment capable of revising our understanding of Greenland's past.

Enough to be rediscovered by radar.

The place is a museum of unintended afterlives.

The missiles were never deployed, yet Iceworm survives in political history.

The tunnels collapsed, yet they survive in radar.

The base closed, yet it survives in environmental governance.

The core was old science, yet its dirt became new science.

The waste was buried, yet the assumption behind burial had to be reopened.

That is why “build nothing you cannot abandon” is not a slogan against building.

It is an argument for taking the future seriously enough to build better.

A civilization capable of planning a hidden missile network under Greenland should also be capable of planning the last day of the network.

We are often better at beginnings because beginnings have sponsors.

Endings have custodians.

The sponsor gets the ribbon cutting, the mission patch, the budget justification and the career credit.

The custodian gets a corroded valve thirty years later.

The institutional reward system therefore overproduces beginnings and underfunds endings.

Camp Century makes the imbalance visible because the ending was delegated to snow.

Snow accepted the assignment for a while.

Then a warmer world changed the terms.

Current measurements suggest the site will remain buried through 2100. That is good news. It is not absolution. It means the civilization that inherited Camp Century has time.

Time is only valuable if used.

Preserve the records.

Maintain the monitoring.

Keep the evidence classes separate.

Do not turn a real secret program into a mythical deployed arsenal.

Do not turn a real climate risk into a fake deadline.

Do not turn a reassuring near-term model into a claim of permanent safety.

Do not turn a military science program into a morality tale in which good knowledge cleanses bad strategy.

Do not turn ancient vegetation into a simplistic forecast.

The discipline required is almost embarrassingly old-fashioned.

Say what is known.

Say who says it.

Say what would change the conclusion.

Keep the sample.

Keep the map.

Keep watching.

Camp Century was built in a place that looked empty.

It was built inside a material that looked permanent.

It was attached to a future that looked urgent.

All three appearances were temporary.

The Greenland Ice Sheet is not a blank page. It is a moving archive.

The United States wrote a base into it.

Science later read something else.
