# Public inference audit — 2026-09-16

## Scope

This is one bounded research pass on Chapters 4–7 of *The Woodpecker*. It tests the manuscript's strongest narrative claim in that run: that the Soviet system could be globally observable while its purpose remained only partly legible outside classified channels.

The useful question is not whether civilians heard the signal. They plainly did. It is whether the surviving contemporaneous public record shows a real interval in which observers could measure the interference, infer Soviet origin, and seriously propose over-the-horizon radar without possessing the internal system history later available after the Soviet collapse.

## What the current manuscript gets right

The existing sequence is strong when it separates existence from purpose. The line "The machine was secret. The side effect was international" works because the historical record contains a public dispute over the side effect years before the system's internal history became accessible.

The manuscript should keep its warning against inventing official surprise. Civilian discovery and state knowledge were not synchronized. A public listener could be uncertain about purpose while Western technical intelligence already knew substantially more.

## Contemporary public record

### 1. By September 1979 the interference problem was public, international, and already attached to an OTH-radar hypothesis

John Edwards's September 12, 1979 *Washington Post* article reported that the signal had disrupted shortwave, maritime, aviation, amateur, military, business, government, and other radio communications; that U.S. operators had asked the FCC to investigate; and that the FCC and State Department had raised the interference with Soviet officials. The article also reported that more than a dozen other nations had pressed Moscow for relief. Most important for this book, it did **not** present the signal as an unknowable mystery: it described over-the-horizon missile-warning radar as the more plausible technical explanation while still treating purpose as unresolved.

Source: John Edwards, “Silencing the Russian Woodpecker,” *The Washington Post*, September 12, 1979. https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1979/09/12/silencing-the-russian-woodpecker/2434d721-d7ad-4fe6-a94a-41ff37c77a78/

**Claim limit:** this is contemporaneous journalism, not the underlying FCC case file. It is strong evidence for what a technically literate public reader could encounter in 1979; it is not sufficient evidence for exact transmitter power, exact source coordinates, or the FCC's complete internal confidence state.

### 2. The Woodpecker had become a spectrum-governance problem visible in the U.S. Congressional Record

The November 15, 1979 *Congressional Record* reproduced an October 24 *Christian Science Monitor* article on the Woodpecker during the World Administrative Radio Conference in Geneva. The item is useful less as technical proof than as dated evidence that the signal had crossed from hobbyist nuisance into international spectrum politics. It places amateur concern, treaty/radio-regulation arguments, and the Geneva conference in the same contemporary record.

Source: *Congressional Record*, November 15, 1979, Extensions of Remarks, p. 32809, reproducing Richard F. Barrett, “Ham W6CFK Calling Moscow: Please Silence Woodpecker,” *Christian Science Monitor*, October 24, 1979. https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1979-pt25/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1979-pt25-2-3.pdf

**Claim limit:** congressional reproduction does not convert every statement in the newspaper article into a congressional or FCC factual finding. Use it to establish contemporaneous salience and public framing, not Duga performance.

### 3. The conventional explanation was technically ordinary enough to be evaluated before the Woodpecker appeared

Headrick and Skolnik's 1974 *Proceedings of the IEEE* review, “Over-the-Horizon Radar in the HF Band,” predates the famous 1976 Woodpecker interference. That chronology matters. OTH radar was not an exotic explanation invented after the signal appeared; it was an established engineering field with known propagation advantages and known clutter/noise difficulties.

Source: J. M. Headrick and M. I. Skolnik, “Over-the-Horizon Radar in the HF Band,” *Proceedings of the IEEE* 62(6), 664–673 (1974), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1109/PROC.1974.9506

A later technical retrospective by Headrick describes HF OTH work as having demonstrated long-range nuclear-burst and missile-launch detection by 1956 and emphasizes the central engineering difficulties of target-to-clutter and signal-to-noise ratio. That is useful counterpressure against a manuscript tendency to make Duga's physical principle sound stranger than it was to radar engineers.

Source: J. M. Headrick, “Applications of high-frequency radar,” *Radio Science* 33(4), 1045–1054 (1998). https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/98RS01013

## Strongest counterargument to the book's framing

“Observable but illegible” can become too dramatic if it implies that the Woodpecker's purpose remained broadly opaque for years. The 1979 public record already carried a technically plausible OTH-radar explanation, and OTH radar itself was mature enough to have a substantial engineering literature before 1976. The system may therefore have been **institutionally illegible in detail** rather than **functionally illegible in category**.

That distinction improves the thesis rather than defeating it. A better formulation is:

> The public signal made the category of machine increasingly inferable; what remained difficult to recover from the signal alone was the system's exact architecture, performance, organizational role, failure modes, and strategic value.

This is more precise than treating “purpose” as one binary secret. It also gives Chapters 4–7 a genuine progression: nuisance → measurement → category inference → limits of inference.

## Recommended later manuscript move

Do not add another paragraph explaining secrecy in the abstract. Replace part of the current Chapter 4–7 repetition with one dated sequence built from the 1976–1979 public record:

- listeners encounter interference;
- regulators and governments treat it as an international spectrum problem;
- technical observers can already recognize OTH radar as a plausible category;
- the remaining unknowns are the consequential ones: which system, where, how well, under whose command, and with what strategic role.

That sequence would make “observable, not legible” earned rather than announced repeatedly.

## Research still required before prose change

1. Obtain the underlying FCC interference/direction-finding correspondence and any State Department demarches referenced by contemporary reporting.
2. Obtain ITU/IFRB/WARC records rather than relying on newspaper descriptions of international complaints.
3. Recover pre-1976 U.S. intelligence records so the book can compare the classified knowledge state with the public one without guessing.
4. Recover the original 1988 FCC technical characterization before publishing exact PRI, bandwidth, frequency-range, or transmission-duration figures.
5. Separate evidence for Soviet origin, source geography, Duga-family attribution, and achieved missile-warning performance. They are different claims.

## Editorial consequence

The best next prose revision is not “make the mystery bigger.” It is to let the mystery shrink in public as evidence accumulates. That is more interesting and more defensible. The reader should watch a strange noise become a plausible radar before watching the remaining questions resist the same treatment.
