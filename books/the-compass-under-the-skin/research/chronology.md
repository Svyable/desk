# Chronology — The Compass Under the Skin

- **1950s–1960s:** controlled orientation work establishes that migratory birds can use geomagnetic information; Wolfgang Wiltschko’s robin experiments become a foundation for avian magnetic-compass research.
- **1970s:** behavioral work clarifies that the avian compass behaves primarily as an inclination compass rather than a simple north/south polarity detector. Radical-pair ideas begin entering magnetoreception theory.
- **1978:** Klaus Schulten and colleagues develop radical-pair concepts that later become central to chemical magnetoreception hypotheses.
- **1980s–1990s:** light-dependence, wavelength effects, magnetic-pulse experiments, trigeminal manipulations and candidate magnetite structures generate the idea that compass and map functions may rely on different channels.
- **1995:** Wiltschko and Wiltschko report that European-robin orientation is affected by light wavelength and by a magnetic pulse, evidence compatible with more than one magnetic process but not proof of any receptor.
- **2000s:** cryptochrome becomes the leading molecular family for radical-pair compass hypotheses; neurobiological work links magnetic orientation to visual processing regions in migratory birds.
- **2012:** Treiber and colleagues show that prominent iron-rich cell clusters in the pigeon upper beak are macrophages, not the magnetosensitive neurons they had been interpreted to be. A visually persuasive anatomical candidate collapses without collapsing the behavior.
- **2014:** Engels and colleagues report double-blinded experiments in which anthropogenic electromagnetic noise disrupts magnetic compass orientation in European robins; grounded aluminum screening restores orientation and deliberately introduced broadband noise disrupts it again.
- **2015:** Brothers and Lohmann report evidence consistent with geomagnetic imprinting and magnetic navigation in sea-turtle natal homing.
- **2018:** Mouritsen’s *Nature* review frames long-distance navigation as a multisensory, multiscale problem rather than a single-cue trick.
- **2019:** Wang and colleagues report alpha-band EEG changes to selected rotations of Earth-strength magnetic fields in humans. The paper argues for transduction, not conscious navigation.
- **2020:** a garden-warbler experiment applying oscillating magnetic fields locally to the eyes reports a null result and argues against the most ambitious simple retinal radical-pair image model.
- **2021:** Xu and colleagues demonstrate magnetic sensitivity in purified CRY4 from European robins and stronger sensitivity than homologous CRY4 from chicken and pigeon under their in-vitro conditions.
- **2022:** targeted 75–85 MHz RF fields are reported to disrupt night-migratory songbird compass orientation, adding mechanistic pressure on radical-pair models; a separate free-flight magnetic-pulse study in European robins reports no navigation effect, constraining strong particle-map interpretations.
- **2024:** theoretical work shows how tightly bound radical pairs in cryptochrome could display Earth-strength magnetosensitivity under asymmetric recombination conditions via a quantum-Zeno-related mechanism; this expands physical plausibility without establishing the in-vivo receptor.
- **2026:** the central empirical situation remains asymmetric: magnetic behavior is well established in many animals, while receptor identity, signal transduction, and the division of labor among magnetic channels remain active research problems.
