# Two Electrons in a Protein

The radical-pair mechanism sounds extravagant until it is described correctly.

A chemical bond breaks or an electron transfers. Two radicals appear, each carrying an unpaired electron. Those electron spins begin in a correlated state. The pair can interconvert between spin configurations, and because different configurations can favor different reaction products, anything that changes the spin dynamics can change chemistry.

A magnetic field is one of those things.

The trick is scale. Earth’s field is tiny compared with the fields used in most laboratory magnetic-resonance equipment. Thermal noise in a warm wet cell is enormous by comparison with the energy associated with aligning a single electron magnetic moment to the geomagnetic field. If the proposed sensor were a conventional energy detector, the idea would be in trouble immediately.

Radical-pair chemistry does not ask the cell to measure that energy directly. It asks whether weak magnetic interactions can alter the time evolution of a non-equilibrium spin system before the radicals recombine or react. The relevant comparison is not simply field energy versus kT. It is the competition among spin precession, hyperfine interactions, exchange and dipolar coupling, relaxation and chemical reaction rates.

That distinction is where popular explanations often go wrong. “Quantum effects survive in the warm wet brain” makes for a headline but hides the actual claim. The proposed compass does not require a bird to maintain a macroscopic quantum superposition across its brain. It requires molecular-scale spin correlations to influence reaction yields during a chemical lifetime. Spin chemistry laboratories already study such effects. The biological question is whether evolution has built a receptor around them.

Cryptochromes are attractive because they contain the ingredients. They are flavoproteins related to photolyases. Blue-light absorption can change the redox state of their flavin adenine dinucleotide cofactor. Electron transfer along a chain of tryptophan residues can generate radical pairs. Depending on geometry and kinetics, magnetic-field direction can influence product yields.

For a compass, molecular orientation matters. If every candidate molecule tumbles freely and averages every direction, the field can change reaction rates without supplying a stable bearing. A directional sensor needs anisotropy: some property of the molecule and its placement must preserve orientation relative to the cell and ultimately the animal.

This requirement points naturally toward ordered proteins in a membrane or other cellular structure, which is one reason retinal localization matters. A field-dependent chemical reaction in a test tube is a magnetic sensor in the broadest sense. A compass requires spatial organization.

Then comes amplification. The magnetic effect on reaction yield can be small. Biology is capable of amplifying small molecular events—vision begins with single photons under extreme conditions—but analogies do not do the engineering for us. The cryptochrome signal has to reach a biochemical pathway, alter neuronal activity and remain distinguishable from ordinary fluctuations in light and metabolism.

The radiofrequency experiments became powerful precisely because they interrogate this spin physics. Weak oscillating magnetic fields at particular frequencies can, in some radical-pair models, perturb spin dynamics far more than a simple mechanical magnetite model would predict. If behavior is disrupted in frequency and intensity regimes that match radical-pair calculations, that is mechanistic evidence rather than merely another correlation.

But the chain must remain visible. Model prediction. Applied field. Behavioral effect. Replication. Alternative physical explanations. Receptor candidate. Neural pathway.

Skip enough links and the bird becomes a mascot for quantum mechanics.

Keep them, and something better happens. A biological mystery turns into an engineering problem with failure modes.
