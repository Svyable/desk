# Cryptochrome Four

By 2021 the cryptochrome story had acquired something it had badly needed: a molecule from a migratory bird that could be purified, manipulated and asked directly whether a magnetic field changed its chemistry.

The protein was cryptochrome 4 from the European robin.

Jingjing Xu and a large interdisciplinary team compared CRY4 from the robin with homologous proteins from chicken and pigeon. In vitro, the robin protein showed magnetic sensitivity in its photochemistry, and under the reported conditions it was more sensitive than the versions from the two non-migratory species. Site-specific mutations helped trace the electron-transfer pathway through a chain of tryptophans and showed how successive radical pairs contributed to the magnetic response.

This is a genuinely remarkable result. It is also easy to overstate.

A magnetically sensitive protein is not automatically the magnetoreceptor used by the animal. The experiment established a physical capability and linked that capability to molecular structure. It did not remove CRY4 from the robin retina and show that the bird lost its compass. It did not restore CRY4 and restore orientation. It did not demonstrate the entire transduction chain from spin chemistry to neuronal firing to behavior.

The distinction is not pedantry. Sensory biology is full of molecules that possess properties the organism does not use for the function a researcher initially imagined.

CRY4 is nevertheless unusually well placed. It is expressed in avian retina. Work before and around the 2021 result had localized it to retinal cell populations and investigated its protein interactions. Its expression pattern in migratory birds has been discussed in relation to season and time of day. Unlike some other cryptochrome candidates, it can stably bind the flavin cofactor needed for the proposed photochemistry. These pieces do not prove receptor identity, but they reduce the number of coincidences one must tolerate to dismiss the hypothesis entirely.

The comparative result is particularly seductive. A migratory robin’s CRY4 being more magnetically sensitive than chicken CRY4 feels almost like evolution signing the paper.

But comparisons between three species are not an evolutionary proof. Pigeons navigate impressively despite being classified differently from a night-migratory robin. Migration is not the only ecological context in which magnetic information matters. Protein preparation, redox state and assay conditions matter. A robust evolutionary claim would need broader phylogenetic sampling and careful connection between molecular variation and measured behavioral dependence.

The right way to treat the 2021 paper is stronger than skepticism and weaker than coronation. It moved cryptochrome from a plausible family of magnetically interesting photoreceptors toward a chemically demonstrated candidate with species-relevant properties.

That changed what a critic had to explain.

Before, one could say that no avian cryptochrome had been shown to respond to weak magnetic fields in a way relevant to the compass. Afterward, that objection became harder to sustain. The remaining questions migrated downstream. Does the same radical chemistry occur in the living retina under physiological illumination and redox conditions? Is CRY4’s orientation fixed appropriately? What biochemical partner receives the change in state? Which neuron carries the signal? How does the brain transform a field-dependent reaction yield into a heading?

There is a tendency in unresolved science to treat “we still have questions” as evidence that nothing happened. That is as bad as treating a strong paper as final proof.

The useful measure of progress is whether the unknowns become more specific.

CRY4 did that. It turned “perhaps some light-sensitive molecule” into a protein with a structure, an electron-transfer chain, mutations, measured spectra and a magnetic response.

Now the animal has somewhere concrete to hide the rest of the trick.
