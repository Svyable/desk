# More Than One Magnetic Sense

The phrase “magnetic sense” may be doing too much work.

A bird can need heading information, positional information, calibration and error correction. Those functions need not arrive through one receptor any more than every mechanical sensation in a human arrives through one kind of nerve ending.

Once that possibility is allowed, several stubborn facts line up more naturally.

The avian compass is light dependent and behaves as an inclination compass. Radical-pair chemistry in retinal cryptochromes has properties that fit those constraints. Some magnetic-pulse and trigeminal results, by contrast, look more compatible with field intensity or map information than with the light-dependent compass. The historically proposed beak receptor failed as anatomy, but the functional distinction between compass and map survived.

This does not prove two magnetic senses. It shows why insisting on one can generate false contradictions.

Suppose a robin uses a retinal radical-pair system to estimate field axis. That channel could answer a directional question continuously during nocturnal flight. Now suppose a second receptor—perhaps particle based, perhaps not—responds to field intensity or a different geomagnetic variable and reaches the brain through a nonvisual pathway. That channel could contribute to learned or inherited spatial information. A pulse might disrupt the second channel without changing the first. Darkness might disrupt the first without erasing the second. A trigeminal lesion could impair map use while leaving cage compass orientation intact.

The model earns attention because it predicts dissociations.

It also makes evolutionary sense without requiring a grand design. Different magnetic tasks could have been recruited at different times from different molecular machinery. Cryptochrome already existed as a photoreceptive/circadian protein family. Iron-handling and mechanosensory pathways already existed. Evolution is unusually willing to build a new instrument from old parts if the signal is useful.

There is no guarantee that different taxa converged on the same parts. Sea turtles, insects, fish and birds inhabit radically different sensory environments. A night-migratory songbird may benefit from a compass tightly coupled to the eye. A marine animal navigating in darkness or turbid water may exploit other architectures. Magnetotactic bacteria solve an entirely different problem with chains of magnetic crystals and whole-cell alignment.

Calling all of this “magnetoreception” is functionally useful but mechanistically dangerous.

The analogy is electroreception. Weak electric fields are biologically detectable, but the relevant organs and ecological uses differ among lineages. A common physical variable does not require a common receptor.

The practical consequence for research is that experiments need to name the task before naming the mechanism. Is the animal choosing a migratory bearing? Detecting a change in intensity? Correcting a displacement? Recognizing a natal region? Learning an association? Showing a neural response with no observable behavior?

Those are not interchangeable endpoints.

This framework also protects against overinterpreting the eventual discovery of a receptor. If CRY4 is causally established as the avian inclination compass, that would be a major solution, not the end of magnetoreception. It would not automatically explain turtle maps, pulse effects, trigeminal signals or human polarity-sensitive EEG responses.

A solved compass could coexist with an unsolved map.

Science likes singular nouns because singular nouns fit diagrams. The animal has no obligation to cooperate.
