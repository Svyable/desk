# The Compass Under the Skin

*Magnetoreception and the Sense We Cannot Point To*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a peculiar kind of scientific embarrassment that arrives when an animal can plainly do something and nobody can agree on what part of its body is doing it.

Magnetoreception lives in that embarrassment.

The old version of the argument asked whether animals could sense Earth’s magnetic field at all. For many species, that question is no longer the useful one. Birds change orientation when experimenters change magnetic fields. Sea turtles respond to magnetic signatures associated with different geographic regions. The field is weak, but the behavior is not imaginary.

Then the certainty thins out. A bird does not have a little compass capsule behind its eye. The famous iron-rich structures once offered as the magnetic receptor in pigeon beaks turned out, under better anatomy, to be macrophages. A protein called cryptochrome 4 from a migratory robin really is magnetically sensitive in a test tube, which is extraordinary, but a purified protein is not yet a sense organ. Weak radiofrequency fields can disrupt avian compass behavior in some carefully controlled experiments, a result that makes radical-pair chemistry much harder to dismiss, but other experiments push against the cleanest version of that model. Humans may show a neural response to rotations of an Earth-strength magnetic field without possessing anything like a conscious ability to point north.

This book is not an attempt to rescue mystery by making the science vague. It is an attempt to keep the layers of the science apart long enough to see the actual mystery.

Behavior is one layer. Physical transduction is another. Receptor anatomy is another. Neural representation is another. Conscious perception is another. Ecological use is another. Magnetoreception is difficult partly because results at one layer keep getting recruited as proof at another.

The most interesting possibility is not that somebody finally finds the magnetic organ. It is that the question itself is too singular. A migrating animal may need a compass and a map, and those are different jobs. Direction can come from the angle of magnetic field lines. Position can come partly from gradients in field intensity and inclination. One channel could be light dependent and retinal. Another could involve magnetic particles and a different nerve. Both could be corrected by stars, odor, landmarks and experience.

If that turns out to be true, the failure to find “the” magnetoreceptor will look less like a fifty-year delay and more like a clue we kept trying to simplify away.
