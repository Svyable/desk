# What Radiofrequency Disruption Can Prove

A good perturbation does more than make an animal fail. It fails in a way that discriminates among mechanisms.

This is why radiofrequency experiments became central to the cryptochrome argument. A weak oscillating field can be designed around the predicted spin dynamics of radical pairs. If a particular frequency range disrupts magnetic orientation at field strengths too small to plausibly act through heating or ordinary nerve stimulation, the effect starts to look like spectroscopy performed on an intact animal.

That is the aspiration. The reality is harder.

Different radical pairs have different hyperfine interactions. Different lifetimes create different frequency sensitivities. Molecular motion, exchange coupling, relaxation and the radical’s chemical identity all matter. A behavioral assay integrates all of those hidden variables and adds a bird on top. A positive result can eliminate classes of models, but it rarely points to one molecular structure with the precision of an electron-paramagnetic-resonance spectrum.

The 2022 work using broadband fields around 75–85 MHz was especially interesting because the range had been chosen with radical-pair calculations in mind. Night-migratory songbirds lost magnetic-compass orientation under the applied field. The authors argued that the result was consistent with a flavin-based radical pair containing substantial hyperfine interactions, as expected for a flavin–tryptophan pair.

“Consistent with” is doing important work.

The experiment did not reach into the retina and label the reacting radicals. It asked whether an intact behavior responded to a perturbation whose frequency content had mechanistic meaning. That is a more informative result than simply discovering that some arbitrary electromagnetic field makes birds confused, but it remains an inference across levels.

There is counterpressure. A 2020 experiment on garden warblers applied oscillating magnetic fields locally to the eyes and found no compass disruption. Its authors argued that the result ruled out the radical-pair model in its most ambitious form, especially the version in which retinal cryptochromes create a magnetic image that should have been destroyed by the applied local field.

That negative result is not a footnote to be hidden because it complicates the quantum story. It asks a serious anatomical question. If the eye is the transducer and the applied field is sufficient where the relevant molecules reside, why did the compass survive?

Possible answers exist: field geometry, retinal coverage, frequency, radical identity, signal redundancy, species differences or incorrect assumptions about which eye regions matter. Some may prove correct. But each answer must be converted into a prospective prediction before it becomes more than an escape hatch.

This is the productive form of controversy. The mechanism becomes narrower as experiments get more specific.

Radiofrequency disruption can establish that a sensory system is sensitive to oscillating fields under defined conditions. Frequency dependence can make some physical mechanisms more plausible and others less so. Combined with light dependence and cryptochrome chemistry, it can create a convergent case for radical-pair involvement.

It cannot, by itself, prove CRY4 is the receptor. It cannot prove the bird sees a magnetic pattern. It cannot prove that every magnetoreceptive animal uses the same chemistry. It cannot be generalized to human health effects without evidence from humans.

Those limits do not weaken the result. They make it useful.

The field advances when an experiment hurts one version of the story without being allowed to kill the entire subject. Magnetoreception has survived enough failed receptor stories that this distinction should be institutional memory by now.

A negative local-eye experiment is not evidence that birds cannot detect magnetic fields. A positive 80 MHz experiment is not evidence that the receptor mystery is over.

Together, they are the shape of a mechanism being cornered.
