# What the Alpha Drop Means

An alpha-band drop is not north.

That is the easiest way to keep the 2019 human result in proportion.

Electroencephalography does not read thoughts. Alpha power changes under many conditions: shifts of attention, sensory processing, eye closure, cognitive state. A stimulus-locked decrease can show that the brain treated an event as biologically meaningful without telling us what the event represented or whether the participant could use it.

The magnetic result becomes interesting because of selectivity. The reported response did not occur for every field rotation. Geometry and polarity mattered. That makes a simple “the coils made a noise” explanation less satisfying if the controls truly matched everything except the magnetic variable. It also creates a mechanistic puzzle. A classic radical-pair inclination compass is expected to be largely insensitive to magnetic polarity. A polarity-sensitive human response would point elsewhere.

The authors proposed a ferromagnetic transduction element such as biogenic magnetite as a viable class of explanation. That is physically plausible and historically dangerous.

Dangerous because the pigeon-beak episode should have cured the field of mistaking “magnetite could do this” for “this tissue is the receptor.” Humans contain magnetic particles from biological and environmental sources. The existence of magnetite in tissue is not enough. A human receptor claim would require reproducible anatomy, controlled contamination, innervation and perturbation.

There is another possibility: the alpha response may be real but vestigial. Evolution does not delete every circuit the moment it stops becoming behaviorally obvious. A sensory pathway can lose access to conscious decision while retaining subcortical or cortical responses. That would make humans useful for studying what remains after ecological function decays.

Or the result may be narrower still: a subset of participants may carry a response that varies with local developmental history, magnetic environment or anatomy. The original study reported substantial individual variability. A population average can conceal a biologically real minority or manufacture an apparently clean effect from flexible analysis. Independent preregistered work has to decide which.

This is why the human question should be divided into four claims.

First: can human tissue physically transduce Earth-strength magnetic changes?

Second: does that transduction produce repeatable neural activity?

Third: can humans consciously discriminate magnetic stimuli?

Fourth: does magnetic information improve navigation or behavior outside the laboratory?

Each step is stronger than the previous one. Evidence does not automatically climb the ladder.

The same discipline applies to animal research, but humans make the error easier to see because we can ask them what they experienced. In the 2019 chamber, participants were not reporting a magnetic percept corresponding to the EEG effect. The brain apparently did something the person did not know how to name.

That is not mystical. Most neural processing is like that.

The fascination comes from scale. Earth’s field is part of the ordinary physical environment, as constant in human evolutionary history as gravity and daylight cycles. Many other animals use it. It would be surprising if no vertebrate ancestor of ours ever transduced it. It would also be unsurprising if primates came to rely so heavily on vision, landmarks and cultural navigation that any magnetic channel atrophied into uselessness.

The alpha drop therefore sits in the correct place for an unfinished sense: downstream from physics, upstream from perception.

Whether anything useful connects the two remains open.
