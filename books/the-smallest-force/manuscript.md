# THE SMALLEST FORCE

## When a mind moves a random number

Sven Hardy Benson

> Evidence rule: a statistical deviation is not a force until the experiment earns the noun.

# 1 — THE MACHINE THAT DOES ALMOST NOTHING

The machine has no personality.

That is why they chose it.

No spoon to bend. No compass needle to twitch. No sealed envelope waiting for a clairvoyant noun. No laboratory assistant deciding whether a table moved. A box produces bits. Zero. One. Zero. Zero. One. If the box is behaving, nobody should be able to tell you what comes next, and over enough trials neither digit should win by much.

Then somebody sits beside it and tries to make one of them win.

This may be the smallest extraordinary claim ever designed.

It is also, in a peculiar way, one of the largest.

Because if intention alone can bias a properly characterized physical random process—even by an amount too small to notice without millions of trials—then the interesting thing is not that somebody has learned an unusually inefficient way to flip coins. The interesting thing is that the inventory of physical causation is incomplete.

That sentence is expensive. It should be.

For decades, experiments grouped under micro-psychokinesis, micro-PK, mind–matter interaction, or anomalous perturbation have asked versions of this question. Electronic random-number generators became attractive precisely because they seemed to remove the theatrical weaknesses of older psychokinesis demonstrations. The output could be counted automatically. The target could be specified in advance. Control runs could be collected. Nobody needed to judge whether a metal object looked slightly more bent after an evening with a gifted subject.

The elegance is real.

So is the trap.

A random-number experiment turns an extraordinary physical claim into a statistical claim. That is progress only if the statistics cannot quietly absorb all the things the apparatus was supposed to remove: optional stopping, selection, device drift, multiple endpoints, experimenter degrees of freedom, participant heterogeneity, publication bias, calibration choices, unnoticed correlations, and the oldest seduction in anomaly research—the conversion of a small unexplained remainder into a named phenomenon.

The history is therefore stranger than either camp usually permits.

The United States Army asked the National Research Council to examine unconventional human-performance claims. The resulting 1988 report devoted serious space to parapsychological techniques and random-number-generator experiments. Its conclusion was not that the subject had been too ridiculous to inspect. It was that the committee found no scientific justification, on the evidence it reviewed, for psychokinesis as a useful phenomenon. That distinction matters. Ridicule did not do the rejecting. An evaluation did.

Later, the Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research laboratory accumulated a very large experimental record in which operators attempted to shift random-event-generator output. Advocates reported tiny cumulative deviations. A later three-laboratory replication did not reproduce the primary effect. Defenders subsequently argued that the original database was highly heterogeneous and disproportionately influenced by a small number of participants, meaning the replication had been designed around the wrong model of the alleged effect.

That defense is interesting for a reason that has almost nothing to do with whether it saves psychokinesis.

It changes the claim.

If an effect exists uniformly enough to be a general human capacity, one experimental architecture follows. If it exists only in rare individuals, another follows. If it depends on experimenter, feedback, intention, novelty, emotional state, device type, or an interaction nobody can specify prospectively, then each rescue creates a smaller target and a larger model-selection problem.

The effect can become more elusive while the theory becomes more accommodating.

That is a dangerous bargain.

The 2006 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis by Holger Bösch, Fiona Steinkamp, and Emil Boller surveyed hundreds of random-number-generator studies. It found a very small overall effect, but the authors argued that the pattern could in principle be explained by publication bias. This is exactly the kind of result that anomaly culture handles badly because it refuses to be a verdict. A small aggregate departure is not nothing. It is also not permission to call the departure psychokinesis.

The noun is the problem.

Suppose a million-trial experiment produces a deviation whose nominal probability under a specified null model is small. What has happened?

At least five possibilities are immediately alive.

The physical generator may not match the statistical model. The analysis pipeline may have admitted degrees of freedom not represented in the final p-value. The published experiment may be one survivor from a larger invisible population. The deviation may be an ordinary low-probability event. Or some interaction not contained in the conventional model may exist.

Only the last category even opens the door to psychokinesis, and it is still much larger than psychokinesis.

This book lives inside that gap.

Not because the claim is obviously true. Not because it is obviously foolish. Because micro-PK is an unusually sharp case of a general epistemic failure: **we name residuals before we understand their denominator**.

A radar return becomes a craft. A symptom cluster becomes a weapon. A statistical excess becomes a mental force. Sometimes the noun eventually proves useful. Sometimes it merely hardens uncertainty into an object that subsequent investigators inherit as though somebody already demonstrated the thing itself.

Random-number experiments give us a chance to watch that hardening happen almost one bit at a time.

## A force should travel through a causal ladder

For the purposes of this book, psychokinesis will not mean `a statistically unusual dataset collected while somebody intended an outcome`.

That definition has already smuggled the conclusion into the observation.

A useful causal ladder is harder:

**intention → physical interaction → altered generator state or event probability → recorded bitstream → prespecified statistic → independently reproducible deviation.**

Every arrow matters.

A failure at the last arrow can mean the phenomenon is not stable. A failure between generator and bitstream can mean instrumentation. A failure between bitstream and statistic can mean analysis. A failure between intention and physical interaction is the central unsolved mechanism claim.

Calling the final statistic `mind–matter interaction` leaps backward across the entire ladder.

The leap may someday be justified.

It has not been justified by vocabulary.

## The engineering temptation

There is another reason this history deserves reconstruction now.

Randomness has changed.

Early electronic coin-flippers were experimental apparatus. Today randomness is infrastructure. Cryptography, secure communications, simulations, randomized protocols, lotteries, sampling systems and quantum information all depend on generators whose behavior must be understood under increasingly explicit threat models. Modern device-independent quantum-randomness work goes so far as to certify unpredictability from Bell-test correlations under stated physical assumptions.

That creates an exquisite collision.

Parapsychology asked whether intention could bias randomness.

Modern information science asks how randomness can be certified against an adversary.

These literatures barely speak to each other, yet they are making claims about the same vulnerable noun: random.

If a mind can perturb a physical RNG, the claim should eventually be expressible as an adversarial model. Which generator architectures are susceptible? What coupling variable changes? Does distance matter? Shielding? Feedback? Entropy source? Does the effect survive blinded remote operation? Can an experimenter who does not know the target direction produce it? Does it survive a cryptographically committed protocol? Does it affect raw physical events or only postprocessed output? Can a preregistered adversarial test distinguish the proposed interaction from device bias at useful error rates?

These are not skeptical tricks.

They are what it means to turn an anomaly into engineering.

And if proponents cannot say what experiment would make the effect fail, the smallest force has become too flexible to measure.

## Four boxes, not one

This manuscript will keep four evidence classes visible.

**1. Verified / documented.** Governments and academic laboratories did investigate psychokinesis and RNG effects. The National Research Council evaluated the literature. PEAR collected a substantial experimental database. Multi-laboratory replication attempts occurred. Peer-reviewed meta-analysis exists. Those facts do not depend on psychokinesis being real.

**2. Credible but incomplete.** Some published datasets report deviations from specified chance models. Their existence is documented; what generated those deviations remains contested. Individual experimenters may have observed patterns they reasonably considered anomalous without possessing a decisive causal explanation.

**3. Disputed allegations.** Claims that particular people can reliably influence physical random systems, that operational military systems were affected psychokinetically, or that classified programs established robust mind-over-matter capabilities require evidence beyond program interest, anecdotes, patents, memoirs, or statistical labels.

**4. Speculative synthesis.** This book will test several ideas of its own: the **residual-naming error**, the **elastic-effect problem**, the **causal-ladder test**, and the possibility that modern randomness certification supplies anomaly research with a much harsher and cleaner experimental language. These are analytical tools, not discoveries.

The rule is simple.

A weird number earns investigation.

It does not automatically earn a force.

# Architecture

1. **The Machine That Does Almost Nothing** — why the smallest extraordinary claim may carry the largest physical implication.
2. **Before the Bits** — dice, falling objects, séances, macro-PK, fraud controls, and why investigators wanted an automatic witness.
3. **Electronic Coin Flippers** — Helmut Schmidt, early RNG designs, and the migration from spectacle to statistics.
4. **The Army Asks** — why military institutions repeatedly inspect low-probability, high-payoff human-performance claims.
5. **What the NRC Actually Rejected** — reconstructing the 1988 evaluation without turning it into either a debunking slogan or an endorsement by attention.
6. **Princeton’s Tiny Excess** — PEAR’s apparatus, operators, databases, controls, and the difference between cumulative significance and useful effect size.
7. **Two People in the Dataset** — participant heterogeneity, outliers, and what happens when a universal claim retreats into exceptional performers.
8. **Replication Is a Model Test** — the three-laboratory replication and the arguments made after it failed its primary target.
9. **The File Drawer Has Physics in It** — publication bias, selective reporting, optionality, and why literature structure can manufacture apparent forces.
10. **A Random Machine Is Still a Machine** — entropy sources, pseudorandomness, drift, autocorrelation, calibration, electronics, and environmental coupling.
11. **What Does Intention Touch?** — mechanism proposals and the discipline of refusing quantum vocabulary as a substitute for a coupling model.
12. **Randomness Became Infrastructure** — cryptography, QRNGs, Bell tests, entropy certification, and the modern adversarial conception of random.
13. **Build the Hostile Experiment** — preregistration, cryptographic commitment, remote targets, independent hardware, raw-data publication, sequential analysis, and red-team design.
14. **The Operationality Gap** — even a real microscopic effect may be useless for signaling, sabotage, intelligence, or control.
15. **The Elastic Effect** — experimenter effects, decline effects, exceptional subjects, feedback dependence, and theories that grow whenever evidence shrinks.
16. **What Would Change Physics?** — the minimum demonstration that would force serious revision rather than another meta-analysis.
17. **What Would Kill the Claim?** — explicit defeat conditions and why a science needs ways to lose.
18. **The Smallest Force** — what remains after testimony, significance, mechanism, and utility are separated.

# Defeat conditions

The book’s strongest speculative claim—that modern adversarial randomness methods can materially sharpen the micro-PK question—should be narrowed or abandoned if those methods do not map onto the physical RNGs or hypotheses actually used in the strongest experiments.

The `elastic-effect problem` should be abandoned if preregistered independent experiments can prospectively identify high-performing individuals or conditions and reproduce direction, magnitude, and boundary conditions without post-hoc subgrouping.

A robust psychokinetic interpretation would become substantially more credible if independent laboratories, using independently built and characterized generators, cryptographically committed protocols, blinded target assignment, public raw data, fixed analyses, and adequate power repeatedly produced the same directional effect and demonstrated that known physical/environmental couplings could not account for it.

Conversely, if increasingly adversarial protocols drive the aggregate effect toward zero while explanatory exceptions multiply, the rational conclusion is not that consciousness has become shy. It is that the evidentiary object was probably created upstream of the proposed force.
