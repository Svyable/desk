# The Last Witnesses

*What the Universe Forgets, and What We Are Here to Keep*

Sven Hardy Benson

This book begins with a conditional result in cosmology and ends with a human choice.

The conditional result is severe. In a universe whose late-time acceleration is driven forever by a positive cosmological constant, observers in the remote future lose access to much of the evidence that made modern cosmology possible. Gravitationally unbound galaxies recede beyond useful observation and ultimately beyond future causal contact. The cosmic microwave background continues to redshift. Stellar processing makes clean primordial chemical evidence harder to recover. A sufficiently late civilization could be more technologically capable than ours and still inherit a poorer cosmological record.

The condition matters. Dark energy is not understood at the level the name suggests. ΛCDM remains an extraordinarily successful baseline model, but recent measurements, including results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, have kept open the possibility that the late-time acceleration is not exactly a cosmological constant. The far future therefore belongs in the category of model-dependent projection, not observed fact.

The underlying reality is less fragile: observation has an epoch. Sources change. Signals redshift. samples are processed. transients pass. Horizons can limit causal access. Science does not study an eternal display case. It reconstructs history from whatever traces remain available to the observer.

That makes record-making physically interesting. A civilization can take evidence available during one epoch and carry a representation of it into another. A spectrum can outlive the star that produced it. A map of the microwave background can remain after the local sky stops offering the same measurement. An archive can give later minds evidence their immediate environment no longer contains.

Nothing here requires humanity to be cosmically chosen. The book argues the opposite. A witness is not a king. We happened to arrive while a remarkably rich set of clues is still legible, and we happen to possess machinery capable of copying some of them forward. Any obligation follows from values we already have—curiosity, continuity, scientific honesty, and concern for future knowers—not from a purpose written into the universe.

Throughout, four kinds of claim are kept separate: direct observation; inference within well-tested present models; extrapolation into regimes we have not observed; and philosophical synthesis. The distinctions are part of the argument. A record worth sending forward should make it possible for future readers to disagree with us.

The goal is not to make cosmology immortal.

It is to notice that the sky has a date.
