# What Deserves to Survive Us

Once you decide to preserve something for the future, the argument moves immediately from physics to taste.

What deserves the space?

The question sounds easier when storage is cheap. Save everything. Build another data center. Add another disk. Push another copy to the cloud.

Cheap storage postpones selection. It does not abolish it.

Attention remains finite. Maintenance remains finite. Verification remains finite. Energy remains finite. Every archive becomes a graveyard if nobody knows what matters inside it.

Deep time makes selection unavoidable because a compact core is more likely to survive than an undifferentiated civilization dump.

So what goes in?

The first answer should be evidence whose source is uniquely time-dependent.

The Hubble flow as observed across a large external-galaxy population.

The cosmic microwave background in frequency and polarization.

Primordial abundance measurements from minimally processed environments.

Large-scale structure across cosmic time.

Gravitational-wave observations that carry information about compact objects and expansion.

Long-baseline astronomical records.

Rare transient events.

Physical samples whose environments may not remain accessible.

The criterion is not importance in today’s prestige hierarchy. It is irrecoverability plus future discriminatory power.

Would losing this observation remove a route by which later minds can distinguish competing histories?

If yes, preserve strongly.

The second category is the decoding scaffold.

An archive full of cosmology that cannot explain its own units is a polished stone.

So preserve mathematics, physical constants, atomic spectra, methods of timekeeping, coordinate systems, detector principles, uncertainty conventions, and the chain from local reproducible experiments to remote astronomical inference.

This part may matter more than some of the cosmological data.

A future mind with the key can interpret imperfect evidence.

Without the key, perfect evidence becomes texture.

The third category is error.

Not every error. The consequential ones.

Preserve the failed models that were genuinely competitive. Preserve calibration shifts. Preserve the Hubble tension. Preserve the lithium problem. Preserve the development of dark-energy constraints. Preserve cases where famous results were corrected or retracted.

A future reader should know that our scientific record was produced by fallible institutions capable of self-correction sometimes and self-deception sometimes.

This is not confession for its own sake.

It is trust infrastructure.

The fourth category is method.

How did we know?

Theories can be rediscovered. Methods can too, but method explains the evidence. Distance ladders. Standard candles and rulers. Spectroscopy. Interferometry. Statistical inference. Detector calibration. Survey selection. Foreground subtraction. Error propagation. Model comparison.

A future civilization may use different mathematics or statistics, but understanding our route lets them strip away our conventions and recover the encounter with nature.

The fifth category is context about the observer.

Who were we?

Not a complete history of humanity inside the cosmology core. Enough to interpret bias.

Where was Earth?

What was the atmosphere like?

Which wavelengths reached the ground?

What technologies did we have?

Which institutions built instruments?

How were timestamps defined?

What computational approximations were necessary?

Which parts of the sky were inaccessible or undersampled?

The observer is part of the apparatus.

Preserve the apparatus.

Then the list becomes morally harder.

Should the archive contain human culture?

Of course some archive should.

Whether it belongs in the same core is another question.

A species that sends only cosmological measurements would be scientifically serious and existentially weird. We measured the universe but did not say what music sounded like. We preserved dark-energy likelihoods and forgot children’s drawings. We recorded the age of recombination and omitted the experience of rain.

The Voyager record understood this intuitively. Its contents were not a journal supplement. They were an attempt at portraiture.

A civilizational deep-time archive should include culture.

But culture creates governance problems science data does not. Who selects the music? Which languages? Whose histories? Which religions? Which atrocities? Which jokes? Which ordinary lives? A selection presented as “humanity” will always contain power.

The only honest answer is plurality.

Many cultural archives.

Many curators.

Overlapping records.

No single canonical portrait pretending to be the species.

The scientific core can be more standardized because hydrogen is less politically contested than history, though scientific institutions are not culture-free either.

Separate the governance layers.

This also protects the scientific archive from becoming hostage to cultural canon wars. Future cosmology should not disappear because one century rejects another century’s museum choices.

What deserves to survive us is not the same as what deserves to be centralized.

There is a second trap in selection: present importance is a bad predictor of future value.

Old weather logs, business records, photographs, and mundane specimens become scientific resources because they capture baselines. The cosmic archive should therefore include random and representative sampling, not only curated “important” objects.

Preserve ordinary galaxies, not just quasars and gravitational lenses.

Preserve quiet sky, not just transients.

Preserve null observations.

Preserve failed targets.

Preserve calibration fields.

Future theories may find structure in what we considered background.

This is the archival equivalent of conserving biodiversity rather than only charismatic species.

A panda gets a poster.

A soil microbe may run the ecosystem.

The same bias exists in data.

The spectacular observation gets the press release. The boring reference dataset may be what makes the spectacular observation interpretable.

So selection should include anti-selection: deliberately preserve some material without a current hypothesis.

Random samples are a defense against our imagination.

This is a powerful principle beyond astronomy. Natural-history museums increasingly recognize that specimens collected for one reason can later be used to measure contaminants, genetic change, disease, or climate effects not anticipated by the collector. The future value came from having a sample not optimized for the future question.

Cosmological archives should do the same.

Keep some sky simply because it was sky.

There is also the question of scale.

The full scientific enterprise may produce exabytes, zettabytes, and whatever names follow. Deep-time physical replication cannot assume infinite growth. The compact archive must compress.

Compression is judgment.

Lossy compression is stronger judgment.

A future-proof archive should therefore make the loss visible. Document which dimensions were compressed, which thresholds applied, what dynamic range was discarded, and what representative raw data survive for comparison.

Never let a compact product masquerade as the original observation.

A map is not the territory, yes.

The archive should include the legend saying how much territory the map threw away.

Physical samples deserve special treatment because they can answer questions no digital representation anticipates.

Meteorites.

Lunar material.

Asteroid samples.

Cometary material if returned.

Terrestrial geological cores.

Ice where it can be preserved.

Biological specimens under appropriate governance.

Manufactured reference materials from instruments and standards.

A future analyzer may detect isotopes, defects, contaminants, or molecular structures we cannot measure today.

The material contains high-dimensional information we do not know how to encode.

Keep some unprocessed.

This is the Apollo lesson again.

Do not spend every sample with today’s instrument.

The same principle applies to bandwidth. If a mission can transmit only a processed subset from a distant probe, onboard choices become permanent. Future spacecraft design should consider preserving high-value raw observation locally even when full transmission is impossible, allowing later retrieval if practical.

That is a niche engineering recommendation now and could become important in a more capable Solar System civilization.

The archive should also preserve maps of what was never sampled.

Unknown is data about the archive.

If a sky region was missing because the Milky Way obscured it, mark the zone. If a detector saturated, mark the hole. If an instrument failed during a period, preserve the failure. If an ocean sample was never collected, do not interpolate and forget.

The future needs absence distinguished from zero.

Database designers know this as the difference between NULL and 0.

Civilizations forget it constantly.

We turn “no record” into “nothing happened.”

A deep-time archive should be fanatical about the difference.

What deserves to survive us may also include the tools for making new records.

Not just knowledge, but capability.

How to build a spectrometer from basic principles.

How to establish atomic time.

How to make a telescope.

How to measure parallax.

How to fabricate detectors.

How to preserve samples without contamination.

A future civilization that has lost technological continuity may find an archive before it regains every capability needed to verify the contents. A ladder from basic materials and experiments toward precision measurement could accelerate recovery.

This risks turning the archive into an encyclopedia of technology and making the core enormous.

Layer again.

A compact physical primer.

A larger technical library.

Pointers and inheritance.

Civilization does not need one object to do every job.

There is a final selection I would make that has almost no data volume.

Preserve questions.

Not only answers and anomalies.

Questions.

What is dark matter?

What is dark energy?

Did inflation occur, and if so what was its mechanism?

Why is there more matter than antimatter in our observable universe?

How should quantum mechanics and gravity be reconciled?

Are fundamental constants truly constant?

Does proton decay occur?

What is the neutrino mass ordering and absolute scale?

Are there additional light relics?

Is there life elsewhere?

Is intelligence common?

Which observations would change our minds?

A question tells the future where our map ended.

It also prevents the archive from performing false completion.

We were not done.

That fact may be the most accurate summary of our science.

Every century likes to imagine it is near a conceptual closure because the solved problems are visible and the unsolved ones have not yet been named. Then a new instrument opens a band and reality becomes impolite again.

Preserve the open edges.

A future mind may live in an evidence-poor universe and still possess theories we cannot imagine. Our unanswered questions could look childish. Fine. They will at least know which walls we touched.

What deserves to survive us?

Not us, exactly.

Not a flattering portrait.

Not a final doctrine.

The strongest record is a set of encounters with reality, accompanied by the machinery needed to doubt them.

The galaxy spectrum.

The microwave map.

The isotope ratio.

The detector diagram.

The uncertainty.

The failed fit.

The question mark.

And somewhere, because we were not instruments ourselves, enough ordinary human material to explain why anyone bothered.

We looked up.

We noticed the window.

We kept a copy.
