# Evidence Has a Half-Life

A half-life is a precise thing in nuclear physics and a dangerous metaphor everywhere else.

I am going to use it anyway.

Radioactive decay gives us a clean statistical law: after one half-life, half the nuclei in a large population remain undecayed on average. Evidence does not behave that neatly. There is no universal clock after which half of a civilization’s knowledge becomes false or inaccessible. Some inscriptions survive millennia. Some digital files die before the laptop that created them. Some astronomical signals improve with time because the baseline grows. Others pass once.

Still, evidence has a half-life in the ordinary sense that access degrades.

Witnesses die.

Samples are consumed.

Species go extinct.

Sites erode.

Media fail.

Languages change.

Institutions forget why a folder matters.

Sources move beyond reach.

The important point is not that everything disappears. The important point is that evidence requires maintenance or favorable physics to remain usable.

We understand this poorly because knowledge feels abstract after it enters our heads. The boiling point of water, the orbit of Neptune, the structure of DNA—these appear to belong to a realm of facts detached from fragile storage. But every route by which another person learns those facts is physical. Neurons. Ink. Magnetic domains. Flash memory. Fiber links. Radio signals. Protein sequences. Stone grooves.

There is no disembodied library.

Information must be instantiated.

That statement sounds like philosophy until a hard drive fails.

Then it becomes customer support.

The cosmic-horizon problem is one extreme case of physical access changing. The source itself becomes causally unavailable. But most evidence half-lives are shorter and more embarrassing. Scientific data management already loses material through ordinary neglect. Research groups disband. Personal drives disappear. Proprietary software stops running. Web links rot. Documentation assumes knowledge no longer common. Formats become unsupported. A paper survives while the code and intermediate data vanish.

The polished result may remain readable for centuries while the ability to interrogate it disappears in decades.

That is a kind of epistemic mummification.

The claim survives. The organs are gone.

Reproducibility discussions usually focus on present trust. Can another team rerun the analysis now? Did the result depend on undocumented choices? Are the data and code available? Those are immediate concerns and sufficient reason for better practice.

Deep time adds another criterion.

Can a stranger reconstruct the evidentiary chain after everyone involved is dead?

Not run the exact software in a museum emulator. Understand what happened.

That requires documentation at multiple levels because executable preservation alone is brittle. A container image may preserve dependencies for years or decades. A virtual machine may extend the period. Eventually the surrounding hardware architecture, operating assumptions, cryptographic signatures, and institutional context can become alien. The durable layer is conceptual explanation tied to physical references.

This is why the oldest scientific records that remain useful tend to be legible without preserving the original apparatus.

Ancient eclipse reports do not include executable code. They include enough date, place, and description to be compared with celestial mechanics. Tycho’s planetary observations remain historically important without a functioning duplicate of every instrument because the measurement conventions can be reconstructed. Old photographic plates are especially valuable because the physical image itself can be remeasured by new equipment.

The record stayed close to the phenomenon.

Modern digital science often moves in the opposite direction.

We create deep pipelines in which raw detector output is transformed through calibration, filtering, model fitting, machine learning, and database systems before a human sees a result. The pipeline can extract extraordinary precision. It can also produce dependency stacks so deep that no one person understands the whole route.

This is not a condemnation of complexity.

A radio telescope array or cosmological survey cannot be reduced to a notebook and a ruler.

The response is architectural: identify which layers must remain intelligible independently of transient software ecosystems.

For a future cosmic archive, I would preserve at least four representations of any foundational observation.

First, the physical description: what interacted with what. Photons at these frequencies entered a detector of this material and geometry. A voltage or count resulted. A clock and pointing system attached time and direction. Keep the story close to physics.

Second, representative primary data: enough actual measurements that the future can see the signal in its least interpreted practical form.

Third, the canonical analysis: the methods by which our era extracted the published result, including code where possible.

Fourth, a compact mathematical summary: equations, dimensionless quantities, uncertainties, and cross-checks designed to survive even if the richer layers are lost.

The layers fail differently.

That is the idea.

A robust archive should not bet everything on one mode of legibility.

We already know how to do this in safety engineering. Aircraft have redundant systems. Spacecraft include safe modes. Data centers replicate across failure domains. Financial systems reconcile independent ledgers. The novelty is applying the same seriousness to civilizational memory.

Evidence preservation is a reliability problem.

Reliability engineering begins by refusing the phrase “it should be fine.”

What are the failure modes?

Bit rot.

Media obsolescence.

Physical destruction.

Unauthorized alteration.

Authorized alteration that turns out to be wrong.

Loss of keys.

Loss of language.

Loss of units.

Loss of provenance.

Loss of context.

Institutional collapse.

Civilizational collapse.

Species change.

Planetary loss.

Cosmic source loss.

The list quickly becomes absurd, which is useful. Not every layer should defend against every failure mode. A research archive designed to last one hundred years is different from a restart archive designed to survive a planetary catastrophe. A physical deep-time marker is different again.

The mistake is pretending one object solves all three.

The Voyager Golden Record is a beautiful example of both ambition and limitation. Launched in 1977 aboard Voyager 1 and 2, the record contains sounds, images, music, greetings, and scientific information intended as a message from Earth to any finder. Its designers included diagrams meant to communicate playback and locate the Solar System using pulsars.

The record is not a serious archive of human science.

It was not meant to be.

It is a bottle in an ocean large enough to make oceans embarrassed.

Its value for this book lies in the design challenge. How do you communicate without assuming language? How do you indicate units? How do you show that grooves should be played? How do you locate a sender when stars move and pulsars evolve? The exercise exposes the hidden context behind every ordinary document.

A page says “three meters.”

The future asks: what is a meter?

We answer with a definition tied to the speed of light and the second.

The future asks: what is a second?

We answer with a specified transition frequency of cesium-133.

Now we have moved from social convention toward reproducible physics.

This is the right direction for deep time.

Dimensionless constants are even better because they do not depend on unit conventions. Ratios of frequencies, masses, or lengths can provide anchors. Prime numbers and geometry can establish mathematical structure, though mathematics still requires a representational bridge. Atomic spectra can connect symbols to physical processes available to any technologically capable reader made from similar physics.

The archive should teach its own measurement language.

That is more important than choosing indestructible stone.

A perfectly preserved unknown script is still a problem.

The Rosetta Stone mattered not because stone is immortal but because it contained overlapping texts in scripts that permitted decipherment. Redundancy of meaning turned durability into access.

A cosmic Rosetta archive should do the same thing across scientific representations.

Show a hydrogen spectral line as measured in the laboratory.

Define the frequency numerically in an internal unit system.

Show the same line shifted in galaxy spectra.

Connect redshift to wavelength ratios.

Show distance indicators independently.

Show the population relation.

Let each part teach the next.

Self-description is not one preface page. It is a network of mutually constraining examples.

There is another half-life we should name: the half-life of trust.

Records do not remain credible merely because they remain intact.

Future societies may distrust institutions associated with the archive. Regimes use archives to legitimize themselves. Religions preserve texts. Corporations preserve favorable records and destroy embarrassing ones. States classify and redact. Scientific organizations are not immune to power, fashion, or error.

A deep-time scientific archive should be designed for readers who suspect manipulation.

This sounds paranoid until you remember that we are the suspicious future readers of many historical records.

We ask who wrote it.

Why?

Who paid?

What was omitted?

Was the document altered?

Do independent sources agree?

Can physical evidence check the claim?

Future readers should apply the same questions to us.

The archive earns trust through multiplicity and falsifiability, not branding.

Independent observatories. Different nations. Different instruments. Raw observations that encode shared celestial events. Cross-signatures if cryptography survives. Physical samples. Repeated measurements over time. Contradictory analyses retained rather than erased.

A conspiracy can forge a file.

It becomes harder to forge an entire coherent universe recorded independently across media and institutions.

Harder is not impossible.

Science rarely offers impossible-to-fake certainty.

It offers converging constraints.

The half-life of context may be the most dangerous because it can decay invisibly. A dataset loads. The columns have names. The values look reasonable. But a future analyst does not know that one field changed meaning in version 3.2 or that a set of observations used a different calibration source after a hardware replacement.

Anyone who has inherited a long-lived spreadsheet knows the species of disaster.

Deep time simply removes the person you would ask.

The cure is boring and therefore likely to be neglected: data dictionaries, version histories, calibration lineage, immutable release snapshots, machine-readable metadata, and prose written for someone outside the immediate team.

Boring infrastructure is a form of respect for strangers.

There is no photograph of a good schema migration ceremony. No child dreams of becoming a metadata curator because the universe depends on it. Yet our ability to carry knowledge through technological eras will depend at least as much on curators as on discoverers.

This book began with telescopes and has ended up praising librarians.

That feels correct.

Astronomers extend perception across space.

Archivists extend testimony across time.

The cosmic witness species needs both.

We also need to stop treating preservation as a final step performed after “the real science” is done. For irreproducible evidence, preservation is part of measurement design. Decide in advance which products must remain recoverable. Document transformations while the people who understand them are still available. Export to open standards before software becomes archaeology. Create physically independent copies before institutions become fragile.

The timing matters because archival quality decays immediately after project completion.

People leave.

Memory goes first.

This is the shortest half-life in the room.

A scientist can often explain an undocumented calibration choice five minutes after making it. Five years later the choice may look mysterious even to the same scientist. Fifty years later it becomes an inference. Five hundred years later it becomes a legend if anyone cares.

Capture explanation near the event.

That principle scales from a laboratory notebook to cosmology.

There is a temptation to solve deep time with artificial intelligence: train models on the entire archive, preserve the model, let it explain the past forever. That may become useful as one layer. It should never be the only one.

Models compress.

Compression loses detail. Model behavior can be opaque. Training data lineage can vanish. A future system may reproduce our conclusions while being unable to distinguish what came from observation and what came from statistical interpolation. Worse, a preserved conversational model can create a persuasive illusion of continuity while hallucinating the dead.

The archive must remain upstream of the storyteller.

Future intelligence can build its own storyteller.

Give it evidence.

There may eventually be computational methods capable of near-perfect semantic preservation across changing media. Great. Use them. Then preserve simpler representations beneath them because every sophisticated layer expands the failure surface.

Civilization should learn from spacecraft.

The clever system gets a safe mode.

The safe mode gets a physical manual.

The manual gets a diagram.

The diagram gets redundancy.

This is not elegance.

It is survival.

Evidence has no universal half-life, but every piece has a vulnerability profile. Some depends on a source that will never recur. Some depends on a medium that will fail. Some depends on a language. Some depends on an institution. Some depends on a cosmological epoch.

Our job is not to make evidence eternal.

That is probably impossible.

Our job is to notice which decay processes we can interrupt.

A photon from the early universe arrives once and is absorbed by a detector.

We cannot preserve the photon.

We can preserve what it taught the detector to say.

Then preserve enough that someone else can decide whether the detector was telling the truth.
