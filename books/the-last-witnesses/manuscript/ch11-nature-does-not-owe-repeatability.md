# Nature Does Not Owe Repeatability

The phrase “repeat the experiment” carries moral force in science.

It should.

A result that appears only once in one laboratory, depends on one team’s private judgment, and disappears when others try to reproduce it deserves suspicion. Replication is one of the ways science separates a stubborn external world from enthusiasm.

Cosmology has always complicated the slogan.

We have one observable universe.

We cannot rerun its initial conditions. We cannot switch off dark matter, wait thirteen billion years, and compare the result. We cannot create a second cosmic microwave background with a different baryon density. We cannot ask the Big Bang to happen again after lunch under controlled conditions.

So cosmology repeats differently.

It repeats methods across independent slices of one universe.

Different supernovae.

Different sky regions.

Different instruments.

Different physical probes.

The CMB and baryon acoustic oscillations constrain related cosmological structure through different observations. Gravitational lensing measures mass through its effect on light. Galaxy clustering supplies another view. Distance ladders use overlapping methods. When different routes converge, confidence grows.

Repeatability becomes triangulation.

This is not second-rate science. It is science adapted to a historical object.

Geology works similarly. Evolutionary biology cannot rerun the Cambrian. Paleontology cannot recreate a dinosaur lineage at planetary scale. Climate science has one Earth and one historical emissions trajectory. Researchers use natural experiments, models, independent proxies, comparative cases, and predictions about new observations.

The demand remains the same in spirit: expose claims to reality in more than one way.

The far-future end-of-cosmology problem reveals a boundary.

Nature does not owe us an unlimited supply of independent ways.

A probe can disappear.

A source population can vanish from view.

A pristine sample can be chemically processed.

A baseline can pass.

The ability to repeat through independent observation can shrink.

That does not make the past measurement unscientific. It makes preservation more consequential.

We already accept this with singular events.

When LIGO detected gravitational waves from a binary black hole merger in September 2015, that particular merger could not be rerun. Confidence came from two detectors seeing a consistent signal, waveform agreement with general relativity, instrumental analysis, and later detections of other mergers. The event was singular; the phenomenon became repeatable as a class.

When a rare supernova occurs, astronomers race to observe it across wavelengths because the exact event is unrepeatable. Data coordination matters. Delay cannot be fixed with a better telescope after the transient fades.

This mindset should extend to cosmological epochs.

We are in a transient class called “the present observable universe.”

The duration is vast. The principle is the same.

There is a peculiar asymmetry between theory and observation here. A theory can be copied nearly perfectly as symbolic information. General relativity written in 1915 can be transmitted to a future civilization in equations, and they can explore its consequences with tools Einstein never imagined. An observation cannot be regenerated from the equation alone because the observation includes contingent history.

This makes data more perishable than law.

Scientists often behave institutionally as if the reverse were true. We preserve papers—the theory and conclusions—more reliably than the full observational substrate. Journals are curated. Citations are permanent-ish. Raw data may sit on project infrastructure with a shorter funding horizon.

The incentives are understandable.

Papers are compact.

Data is expensive.

But for an unrepeatable universe, the hierarchy may be backward.

A future civilization can rediscover general relativity if the physical world still permits the relevant experiments. It cannot rediscover the spectrum of a supernova whose photons passed its location ten billion years earlier.

The observation is the scarce resource.

This does not mean preserve data instead of theory.

It means theory is easier to regenerate.

That insight can guide triage when preservation resources are limited.

Ask not merely: how important is this result?

Ask: how replaceable is this evidence?

A routine laboratory calibration that any future lab can redo may deserve less deep-time priority than a mediocre photograph of a unique transient. The photograph can never be improved after the event except computationally. The calibration can.

There are exceptions. Some laboratory materials or machines may become impossible to reproduce because industrial capabilities disappear. A measurement tied to a lost isotope source may become unique. The criterion remains replaceability, not disciplinary prestige.

Nature also does not owe us clean separations among causes.

Cosmological inference is hard because many parameters can mimic one another in limited datasets. Degeneracies are broken by combining probes. Remove a probe and the parameter space opens.

This gives us a technical way to think about evidence loss.

Which future measurements become degenerate when a present probe disappears?

Suppose no CMB is available. What combinations of cosmological parameters could future local observations constrain? Suppose there are no external galaxies. What can stellar dynamics reveal about vacuum energy? Suppose primordial abundances are unavailable. What early-universe histories remain observationally distinguishable?

These are not merely philosophical questions. They can be modeled.

A research program could quantify the information content of cosmological observables as a function of cosmic time under different dark-energy scenarios. Fisher matrices, Bayesian forecasts, simulated future skies, and causal-horizon calculations could estimate which parameters become unidentifiable and when. The details would depend on assumptions about future technology, but even idealized observers could reveal fundamental limits.

That would turn “the universe forgets” from an essay line into a map of epistemic erosion.

I would like to see that map.

Not because trillions of years need a policy memo, but because the exercise would clarify what makes present cosmology possible. Remove one pillar at a time and watch which inferences collapse.

The method resembles lesion studies in neuroscience or fault injection in engineering. Break a component deliberately in the model and learn what the system depended on.

Take away the CMB.

What do we lose?

Take away high-redshift galaxies.

What do we lose?

Take away pristine deuterium.

What do we lose?

Take away standard candles.

What do we lose?

Take away general relativity as an assumed framework.

What do we lose?

The result would likely reveal that some “known facts” are supported more delicately than popular summaries imply, while others are massively overdetermined.

That is useful now.

It helps identify which observations deserve archival emphasis.

It also helps identify what future observers might independently recover, reducing the temptation to preserve everything as equally sacred.

A good archive should focus on irrecoverable complementarity.

If future local physics can derive a fact robustly, we do not need to devote absurd resources to transmitting our statement of it. If a piece of historical evidence breaks a degeneracy they cannot otherwise break, it is precious.

This is the same principle used in data compression, except the objective function is future inference rather than file size.

Preserve information that changes the answer.

There is an analogy to medical diagnosis. A patient’s symptoms can fit several conditions. One test separates them. The test is valuable not because it contains many bits but because it changes the posterior probabilities sharply.

Cosmological evidence should be ranked similarly.

Which archived measurements would most change a future island-universe cosmologist’s posterior over global histories?

The Hubble flow would be near the top.

The CMB would be near the top.

Primordial abundance evidence would matter.

Large-scale structure and baryon acoustic features would matter.

Time dilation of distant transients would support expansion.

Independent age measurements would constrain chronology.

A set of mutually reinforcing observations could do far more than a gigantic undifferentiated dump.

This suggests that a cosmic archive should be designed by adversarial reconstruction.

Give a team a simulated future sky stripped of disappearing evidence. Let them construct the best cosmology they can.

Then add one archived dataset from our era.

What changes?

Add another.

What becomes identifiable?

Which records are redundant?

Which compact summaries preserve most of the discriminatory power?

Which context is necessary to avoid misinterpretation?

The project could be done today as a scientific and archival exercise.

It would also be fun, which is not a trivial advantage. Civilizations are more likely to preserve practices that people find intellectually alive than duties presented as solemn burdens to hypothetical descendants.

Turn the archive into a recurring challenge.

Could you rediscover the Big Bang from this package?

Could you infer expansion from these observations without being told the conclusion?

Could you distinguish ΛCDM from a static local model?

Could you detect where the archive is incomplete?

If the answer is no, improve it.

This treats future legibility as something testable now rather than an act of faith.

There is another reason repeatability matters culturally.

Science survives political pressure partly because claims can, in principle, escape the institution that made them. A government laboratory can report a value, but another laboratory can measure it. A famous professor can assert a result, but a student can examine the data. This dispersal of epistemic authority is imperfect and often violated, but it is foundational.

Deep-time preservation risks recentralizing authority.

If future observers cannot repeat the cosmological measurement, whoever controls the ancient archive controls access to the claim.

That is dangerous.

The solution is duplication before the natural repeatability disappears.

Many archives.

Many custodians.

Open decoding standards.

Physical references.

Public checksums.

Competing copies whose differences are visible.

The future should not need permission from one priestly institution to inspect the ancient sky.

This is not just democratic sentiment. It is error control.

Independent archives can reveal tampering and drift.

Imagine that over a million years one lineage updates a canonical cosmology package while another preserves immutable snapshots. Differences become auditable. A third lineage stores physical etched summaries. A fourth stores high-volume digital data. None is complete. Together they make corruption harder.

The architecture resembles distributed version control more than a national monument.

That feels appropriately modern and surprisingly ancient. Manuscript traditions survived because copies dispersed. Differences among copies sometimes help scholars reconstruct older readings. Redundancy created textual criticism.

A cosmic archive may need scientific criticism across copies.

Which version is oldest?

Which values changed?

Which changes reflect better observation?

Which are corruption?

Can external physics adjudicate?

The record becomes a living population rather than a single sacred object.

Nature does not owe repeatability.

Civilization can manufacture some.

We cannot repeat the vanished galaxy, but we can preserve its spectrum in independent archives.

We cannot rerun recombination, but we can preserve independent CMB maps from multiple missions.

We cannot remake the primordial gas cloud after it is processed, but we can preserve measurements and, where possible, physical samples of local primordial-like material.

Artificial repeatability is weaker than natural repeatability because copies share ancestry. A systematic error in the original measurement propagates to every copy. That is why multiple instruments and methods matter before preservation.

The ideal archive captures independent encounters with the phenomenon.

COBE, WMAP, Planck, and ground-based CMB experiments do not merely duplicate one dataset. They provide partially independent measurement lineages.

Different supernova surveys do the same.

Different galaxy surveys.

Different gravitational-wave detectors.

Different abundance measurements.

Preserve the plurality.

A future reader should be able to see when multiple machines agreed on the sky.

There is a final humility in the repeatability problem.

We sometimes talk about the scientific method as though it were a universal algorithm any intelligence would discover. Perhaps the broad logic of testing models against evidence is convergent. But specific norms—publication, replication, error bars, peer review, open data—are cultural technologies evolved under our constraints.

A future intelligence may organize knowledge differently.

Our archive should therefore transmit evidence in forms that do not require loyalty to our institutions.

They can ignore our journals.

They can hate our notation.

They can replace our statistics.

They can mock our cosmological priors.

If the observations are preserved with enough context, none of that matters.

Reality can survive the culture that measured it.

That is the standard.

Nature will not repeat the universe for us.

We should make sure we do not force the future to take our word for it.
