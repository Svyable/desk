# Dark Energy Is Still on Trial

A book about the far future of the universe should be suspicious of any sentence beginning “the universe will.”

The future tense is doing theoretical work.

We observe a past light cone and a local present. We infer laws. We fit models. Then we extend them beyond the region and time directly tested. This is not a flaw. Prediction is what makes physical theory useful. But the farther the extrapolation, the more important it becomes to remember which assumptions are riding along.

The classic end-of-cosmology argument rides on dark energy behaving like a cosmological constant.

That assumption has been reasonable.

It is not sacred.

The acceleration of cosmic expansion is one of the strongest observational discoveries of modern cosmology. Multiple probes support a universe whose late-time expansion history cannot be explained by ordinary matter alone within general relativity. In the standard ΛCDM model, the extra component is Λ, the cosmological constant. It behaves like a constant vacuum-energy density with equation-of-state parameter w = -1.

ΛCDM has been annoyingly successful.

The model is simple enough to fit on a mug and powerful enough to describe a remarkable range of observations. Planck’s final cosmological analysis found strong consistency with a spatially flat six-parameter ΛCDM model. Large-scale structure, baryon acoustic oscillations, supernovae, and other probes have made the framework the default language of cosmology.

The success creates two opposite bad instincts.

One is to treat Λ as final truth.

The other is to treat any tension as proof the model is dead.

Frontier physics lives between those impulses.

The theoretical problem with Λ has never gone away. Quantum field theory gives us reasons to expect vacuum contributions, but understanding why the observed effective cosmological constant is so small compared with naive estimates is one of physics’ most famous unsolved problems. The numerical gap is so grotesque that it has become a ritual example of theory failing to explain a measured scale.

Calling it “dark energy” does not solve the problem.

It gives the ignorance a name.

In 2024 and 2025, DESI made the ignorance more interesting. The experiment uses spectra of millions of galaxies and quasars to build a three-dimensional map of large-scale structure. Baryon acoustic oscillations provide a standard ruler across cosmic time. When DESI measurements are combined with CMB and supernova datasets, fits allowing dark energy to evolve with time have shown a preference over a strict cosmological constant at a level significant enough to provoke serious attention but not sufficient to declare discovery.

By April 2026, DESI had completed observations for its originally planned five-year survey, reporting more than forty-seven million observed galaxies and quasars and continuing into an extended program. The full five-year cosmological analysis remained a major future checkpoint.

This is exactly the kind of moment an archive should preserve honestly.

Something may be happening.

Or not.

The distinction has enormous consequences for this book.

If dark energy is truly constant and remains dominant forever, accelerated expansion continues in the familiar asymptotic way. A cosmological event horizon persists. Distant unbound galaxies disappear from future contact. The CMB redshifts away. The island-universe picture becomes a reasonable extrapolation.

If dark energy evolves, several things could happen depending on how.

It could weaken enough that acceleration changes character.

Its equation of state could vary while remaining negative enough to sustain a horizon.

A scalar field could roll toward a different vacuum state.

Gravity itself could differ from general relativity on cosmological scales.

Some models can produce future deceleration. Some can produce catastrophic expansion. Some can lead to recollapse under appropriate dynamics. Many are tightly constrained. Some are theoretically ugly. None should be promoted simply because they make a better story.

The point is not to enumerate endings.

The point is that the evidentiary future depends on live physics.

This is good news for the thesis because it prevents it from becoming prophecy.

The universe may or may not erase the particular evidence in the particular way Krauss and Scherrer calculated. What we know now is that observability is dynamic and that plausible cosmological models can create severe future information loss. That is enough to motivate preservation without pretending the forecast is certain.

In fact, uncertainty strengthens the preservation case.

If dark energy’s behavior is unsettled, then our current era is especially valuable because we are measuring the transition while the evidence is rich. DESI, supernova surveys, CMB experiments, weak-lensing surveys, standard sirens, and future missions are not merely determining one parameter. They are deciding which cosmic future is physically credible.

The data should outlive the argument.

This is where our scientific culture is at its best when it behaves well. DESI releases data. Collaborations publish methods. Independent teams combine datasets differently and argue about supernova compilations, calibration, parameterizations, and priors. The disagreement is not a failure to communicate. It is the process by which a hint is either turned into a discovery or dissolved.

A future archive should preserve the hint before history knows what it was.

Suppose the full DESI dataset eventually returns dark energy closer to constant and the current preference fades. The 2025 excitement will look like a transient statistical or systematic wrinkle. Preserve it anyway. Future scientists should see that the model survived pressure rather than descending untested.

Suppose the evidence strengthens and cosmology undergoes a genuine shift. Preserve the before-state even more carefully. The path from ΛCDM dominance to a dynamical model will become a case study in how scientific frameworks change under pressure.

The archive should never back-edit surprise.

There is a specific archival danger around model-dependent derived products. A survey may publish distance measurements or parameter constraints optimized for ΛCDM and common extensions. If future theory changes radically, those compressed results may not retain all the information needed for a different interpretation.

Preserve upstream products.

This is not a novel recommendation, but dark energy makes the reason vivid. The physics most likely to surprise us is exactly the physics that current compression schemes are least likely to anticipate.

A future theorist may want to test a model we would consider bizarre. Give them galaxy positions, redshifts, selection functions, covariance, calibration, and enough pipeline detail to rebuild the observable. Do not give them only w0 and wa.

The names of current parameterizations should not become the bars of a future cage.

There is a broader philosophical error hidden in all far-future writing: confusing extrapolation with observation because the equation is stable on the page.

Take exponential expansion under constant Λ. Once the differential equation is written, evolving it forward feels certain. The algebra does not hesitate. The graph runs smoothly toward absurd times.

But physical confidence is not the same as mathematical determinism inside the model.

The model may be incomplete.

Proton decay is a similar example. Grand unified theories can predict it, and experimental lower bounds on proton lifetime are enormous, but we have not observed proton decay. Writing the far future as though every proton definitely decays on a specific timescale would overstate the evidence.

Black-hole evaporation is another. Hawking radiation is among the most compelling semiclassical results in theoretical physics, yet the full quantum-gravity story remains incomplete and direct observational confirmation is unavailable for astrophysical black holes.

Far-future physics is a stack of extrapolations.

Good writing should show the stack.

Established local physics at the bottom.

Well-tested cosmological model above.

Less-tested asymptotic regime above that.

Speculative civilization behavior on top.

The higher we climb, the less load the sentence should carry.

This book has tried to keep one foot on the ground by making the human recommendation useful even if the top layers change.

Preserve unique evidence.

That remains sensible if dark energy is constant.

It remains sensible if dark energy evolves.

It remains sensible if the universe eventually recollapses.

It remains sensible if future physics reveals relics that make cosmology easier than expected.

The archive may prove unnecessary for cosmology and still be useful science.

That asymmetry is important.

A well-maintained astronomical archive has present value for reanalysis, long-baseline studies, calibration, education, historical research, and new theories. We do not need to charge the entire cost to hypothetical trillion-year descendants.

Deep time is a design stress test, not the sole customer.

The same logic protects the project from becoming a boondoggle. If someone proposes a billion-dollar cosmic archive whose only value appears after the Sun is dead, ask harder questions. If the same investment improves present scientific preservation, open standards, distributed access, and off-world resilience while also serving deep time, the case is stronger.

Cosmic vanity should not become a procurement category.

There is another live uncertainty worth preserving: the Hubble tension.

Measurements that infer the current expansion rate from early-universe observations under ΛCDM have differed from some local distance-ladder determinations. The tension has persisted through years of methodological improvement and debate. Alternatives involving early dark energy or other new physics have been explored. So have systematic explanations.

This is relevant because it reminds us that even the recent expansion history is not perfectly closed.

A future archive should include the independent routes.

If the tension resolves through a calibration issue, preserve the issue.

If it reveals new physics, preserve the pre-discovery state.

If it remains unresolved for decades, preserve that too.

Cosmology is not one clean number handed down by Planck.

It is a network of measurements whose agreements and tensions contain information.

The universe is still on the stand.

Dark energy is one of the witnesses and possibly one of the suspects.

We do not know what it is.

That ignorance should change our emotional relationship with the far future. Instead of imagining one inevitable cosmic ending, imagine a set of conditional futures being narrowed by observation. We are living during the trial.

That makes contemporary cosmology less like reading the last chapter and more like taking testimony.

DESI’s galaxies are testimony.

Supernovae are testimony.

CMB anisotropies are testimony.

Weak lensing is testimony.

Gravitational-wave standard sirens may become stronger testimony.

Each constrains a different piece of the expansion history.

The most valuable thing we can preserve is not the verdict.

It is the transcript.

A verdict can be wrong.

A sufficiently rich transcript lets the future retry the case.

This should be the guiding rule for frontier physics archives generally. Do not preserve only the theory that won in our century. Preserve the high-quality observations that gave theories something to win against.

Quantum gravity may rewrite our understanding of spacetime.

New dark-matter physics may change structure formation.

Neutrino physics may alter cosmological inferences.

Unknown fields may matter.

Future theoretical frameworks may make our parameter names look parochial.

The photons, spectra, timings, maps, and physical samples will remain closer to the world we encountered.

Closer, not pure.

Every measurement is theory-laden at some level.

But there is a difference between a calibrated detector stream and a sentence announcing the fate of the universe.

Preserve nearer the detector.

Then preserve the sentence too, dated and labeled.

“I think this is where the universe is going” is valuable historical information when it is attached to “and this is what I saw.”

Dark energy is still on trial.

That should not weaken our willingness to witness.

It should sharpen it.
