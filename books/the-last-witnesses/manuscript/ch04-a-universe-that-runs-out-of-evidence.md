# A Universe That Runs Out of Evidence

The end of cosmology is not the end of the universe.

That distinction is the reason the idea is interesting.

Most stories about the far future are stories about objects. Stars burn out. Galaxies age. Black holes evaporate if Hawking’s semiclassical picture survives into the regimes where we extrapolate it. Matter may decay if protons turn out not to be perfectly stable. Temperatures fall. Useful free energy becomes harder to find. These stories are thermodynamic and astrophysical. They ask what remains.

The end-of-cosmology argument asks a different question.

What can whatever remains still know?

Under the simplest version of ΛCDM, the answer changes dramatically with time. Vacuum energy associated with a positive cosmological constant does not dilute as space expands. Matter density falls roughly with the inverse cube of the scale factor. Radiation falls faster. Eventually the constant term dominates more completely, and the expansion approaches an exponential form characteristic of de Sitter space.

The mathematics is elegant.

The observational consequences are rude.

Structures that are not gravitationally bound to our local region recede. Their emitted light becomes ever more redshifted. There is a cosmological event horizon: events sufficiently far away can occur without any signal from them ever reaching a local observer in the infinite future. The accessible universe does not merely become faint. It becomes causally restricted.

Krauss and Scherrer took that familiar physics and asked what a future astronomer would infer from the surviving evidence.

The answer was close to historical parody.

The local gravitationally bound remnant would look like an island of stars embedded in darkness. External Hubble-flow galaxies, the very objects Edwin Hubble used to establish expansion, would no longer be observable. The cosmic microwave background would be redshifted to extraordinarily long wavelengths and diluted until local plasma effects and the de Sitter thermal environment create fundamental obstacles. Primordial deuterium would be processed by stars. The three observational pillars that made the hot Big Bang compelling—expansion, the microwave background, and primordial nucleosynthesis—would no longer present themselves in the same clean way.

A very old civilization could inherit a very young-looking cosmology.

That is the insult.

We like to imagine knowledge as monotonic. Later observers should know at least what earlier observers knew, plus whatever has been discovered since. Technology improves. Theory improves. The long future belongs to more capable minds.

But knowledge is not only a function of capability.

It is capability multiplied by available evidence.

That multiplication sign is not a proposed quantitative law. It is a reminder of dependency. Improve the observer while degrading the evidence enough and the inference can get worse.

This is easy to see outside physics. A modern historian has vastly better analytical tools than a medieval chronicler. That does not mean the historian can recover the name of every person who lived in a village whose records burned. A forensic laboratory can detect chemicals at exquisite concentrations. It cannot sample a bloodstain that was washed away three centuries ago. A linguist can analyze sound with software no ancient grammarian possessed. The software does not restore a language that left no recording and no transcription.

Advanced inference does not abolish missing data.

Cosmology is historical science at the largest scale. It reconstructs an unrepeatable past from a present state containing relics. The more distinct relics survive, the more the reconstruction can be constrained. Remove them and multiple histories can become compatible with what remains.

The classic far-future scenario is therefore an example of epistemic entropy, though the phrase should be handled cautiously. Physical entropy has a precise thermodynamic meaning. “Epistemic entropy” here is metaphorical: accessible evidence gets poorer, not because truth itself becomes disordered but because observational distinctions are lost.

The universe can become less self-describing.

There is something almost offensive about that idea because physics trains us to expect laws to be universal. The laws do not become less universal. The equations do not forget. What changes is the boundary condition available to a local observer.

A future physicist might derive general relativity perfectly and still not know the global history of spacetime.

Law is not history.

This is one of the deepest underappreciated realities in science. We often speak as if complete laws plus enough intelligence imply complete explanation. They do not. Differential equations require initial or boundary conditions. A dynamical law can tell you how states relate without telling you which state actually occurred. Quantum theory can assign amplitudes without supplying the classical history you happened to inhabit. Thermodynamics constrains transitions without preserving a film of every molecular collision.

The universe is lawful without being self-archiving.

Human beings know this intuitively in other domains. The laws of mechanics do not tell you who dropped the glass. The laws of chemistry do not tell you which meal was cooked in a kitchen last Tuesday. The laws of genetics do not uniquely reconstruct every extinct organism. History requires traces.

Cosmic history does too.

This is why the disappearance of the microwave background matters so much in the thought experiment. The CMB is not just one more astronomical source. It is an all-sky relic with a nearly perfect blackbody spectrum and tiny anisotropies encoding conditions in the early universe. Its existence linked a theoretical hot beginning to direct observation. Its temperature and angular fluctuations became precision tools. Its polarization carries additional information about reionization, density perturbations, and potentially primordial physics.

A future observer without that relic does not merely lose a picture.

They lose a calibration point for the universe’s youth.

Likewise, the recession of galaxies does more than remove scenery. The Hubble-Lemaître relation gives large-scale evidence that the universe is not static. If all visible galaxies belong to one gravitationally bound remnant, local motions no longer trace global expansion in the familiar way. A future astronomer can be surrounded by excellent stars and have almost no Hubble diagram.

And primordial nucleosynthesis is vulnerable for a different reason. Stars are chemical engines. They alter the elemental composition of gas. Deuterium, in particular, is fragile and generally destroyed in stellar interiors rather than produced in large quantities by ordinary stellar nucleosynthesis. Measuring deuterium in relatively pristine environments gives cosmologists a handle on primordial abundance. As the universe ages and gas cycles through stars, clean samples become harder to find.

Each line of evidence fails differently.

That is important because it shows the problem is not a single technological obstacle that one heroic instrument solves. The far future changes the source population, the radiation background, and the chemical record.

The crime scene is not merely getting dark.

It is being remodeled.

There are possible surviving clues. Krauss and Scherrer did not claim that all physical inference becomes impossible. A sufficiently sophisticated civilization might infer that its stellar system had an earlier history. It might understand that stars require finite ages. It might use local dynamics, black holes, element abundances, or exotic relics. New physics could provide observables we do not know to preserve. Artificial archives from earlier eras could transform the problem completely.

That last possibility is the hinge.

A natural universe can run out of accessible evidence while an intelligent subsystem carries evidence forward.

Nothing in physics forbids a record from outliving the phenomenon it records. The universe already contains such records naturally. Meteorites preserve early Solar System chemistry. Fossils preserve organisms. The CMB itself is a record. The interesting step is that life can manufacture secondary records—copies of copies, with interpretation attached.

A photograph of a fossil can outlive the fossil.

A spectrum can outlive the star.

A cosmological catalog can outlive the galaxies in it.

This is not immortality. Every storage medium is physical and therefore subject to damage, entropy, collision, radiation, decay, neglect, and whatever far-future processes dominate. Records need redundancy, repair, migration, and energy. There is no magical exemption for information carved by intelligent hands.

But intelligence changes the game because it can actively refresh records.

DNA does this biologically. Organisms do not preserve one original molecule across generations; they copy sequences, with errors and correction machinery. Human culture does something similar. We do not possess Homer’s first manuscript. We possess a transmission lineage. The persistence belongs to the pattern, not the substrate.

A cosmic archive should probably be thought about the same way.

The naive deep-time archive is a bunker: choose a durable material, inscribe the truth, put it somewhere geologically quiet, and hope. The better model may be a living lineage of records distributed across habitats and periodically recopied into contemporary media while retaining provenance.

This creates a different problem.

A record that survives by copying can drift.

Anyone who has played the children’s game of passing a whispered sentence along a line knows the danger. Cultural traditions mutate. Texts are edited. Databases are cleaned. Units are changed. File formats are converted. Software is ported. Each migration can preserve access while introducing distortion.

Scientific archives therefore need error detection in the broadest sense.

Checksums are the trivial layer. They can tell you whether bits changed unexpectedly. They cannot tell you whether a calibration assumption was silently rewritten or whether a future curator “improved” the dataset by removing an anomaly that later turns out to matter.

Redundant independent descriptions help. Preserve raw measurements and derived products. Preserve code and prose explanations. Preserve units with physical definitions. Preserve multiple representations. Preserve disagreement. Preserve hashes and signed lineage if cryptographic conventions remain intelligible, but do not depend on cryptography alone. Preserve mathematical relationships that let future readers cross-check meaning against physics.

The challenge is to make corruption detectable even when institutions change.

This is not an exotic cosmic problem. It is a present archival problem stretched until its hidden assumptions show.

A government archive assumes a government. A cloud archive assumes electricity, networks, corporate continuity, and compatible machines. An academic repository assumes institutions with budgets and enough social stability to maintain them. A paper book assumes readers who understand the language and a physical environment where paper lasts.

Deep time strips away assumptions one by one.

That is why the cosmological argument becomes unexpectedly political.

The universe may create horizons, but humans create shorter horizons constantly.

A laboratory closes and data becomes inaccessible. A nation falls and records are dispersed. A proprietary format outlives the company that controlled it. A war destroys a library. A scientific field moves on and stops training people who understand an old instrument. The astrophysical horizon may be trillions of years away while the institutional horizon is Friday.

If we cannot preserve cosmology for fifty years, talk of preserving it for fifty billion is theater.

The long view should discipline the short one.

Start by making today’s records less fragile.

Open formats. Multiple geographic copies. Clear provenance. Publicly documented methods where security and privacy do not prohibit them. Migration plans. Institutional succession. Raw data retention policies based on future uniqueness rather than immediate publication value. Periodic reconstruction exercises in which independent teams try to reproduce a result from the archive alone.

That last test interests me most.

An archive should occasionally be forced to prove it is an archive.

Take a cosmological result whose original team is unavailable. Give a competent group only the preserved materials. Can they recover the published inference? Can they identify where judgments entered? Can they reproduce plots? Can they discover which software dependencies were implicit? If not, the archive contains artifacts rather than knowledge.

Now imagine the same test after a million years.

The language has changed beyond recognition. Units have changed. The species may have changed. The planet may not be the same home. The local sky is different. There may be no institutional memory connecting the archive to the civilization that made it.

What survives then?

Mathematics is the obvious bridge, but even mathematical notation is conventional. Physical constants and dimensionless ratios help. Atomic spectra provide common reference points if atomic physics remains recognizable. Geometry can encode relationships. Star maps are bad deep-time anchors because stars move. Pulsars have been proposed as coordinates, but they spin down and die. The periodic table is physically grounded, though isotopic composition changes and symbol choices are arbitrary.

The point is not to solve deep-time communication here.

The point is that the universe running out of natural evidence gives artificial evidence a specification.

It has to be durable enough to matter.

It has to be self-describing enough to decode.

It has to be evidentiary enough to challenge rather than command.

It has to expose uncertainty.

It has to survive copying.

It has to be distributed enough that one catastrophe does not erase it.

It has to be small enough in its essential core that many copies are plausible, while preserving richer layers where resources allow.

And it has to remain connected to observation.

Without the last requirement, we produce scripture.

The far-future cosmologist should be able to say: these ancestors claimed the universe contained external galaxies in recession. Here are their sky coordinates in their epoch. Here are images at several wavelengths. Here are spectra showing redshifted lines. Here is their method for defining redshift. Here are local atomic transitions they used as references. Here are independent observations from different instruments and institutions. Here are their uncertainty estimates. Here are later replications. Here are the predictions their model made and the observations that followed.

The archive does not ask for belief.

It offers cross-examination.

That may be the most human thing we could send forward.

We are a species capable of lying, mythmaking, propaganda, self-deception, and sincere error. Any future intelligence receiving a message from us should distrust us on principle. A credible archive anticipates that distrust and gives the recipient tools to work around the messenger.

Science at its best already does this.

The paper says what was done so another group can try to break it. The dataset is shared so another method can be applied. The error bars acknowledge that measurement is not revelation. Peer disagreement is not a flaw in the system; it is one of the ways the system resists becoming a priesthood.

If cosmology ends naturally in some far future, a scientific archive could keep part of it open artificially.

That does not defeat the horizon.

It defeats one consequence of arriving late.

The distinction is worth preserving. We cannot mail future observers fresh photons from galaxies that have crossed out of causal reach. We can mail measurements made when the photons were available. The record will be finite, sampled, biased by our instruments and interests, and contaminated by our mistakes.

It will still contain facts their sky no longer does.

That is enough to change what can be known.

A universe can run out of evidence without running out of truth.

An archive is one way truth can leave a trail after nature stops leaving the same one.
