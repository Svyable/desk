*What the Universe Forgets, and What We Are Here to Keep.*

The working thesis is that cosmology has an expiration date. If cosmic acceleration persists, the universe does not merely become colder and emptier. It becomes harder to understand. Distant galaxies cross an observational horizon. The microwave afterglow of the Big Bang redshifts toward practical invisibility. Some of the evidence from which we reconstructed cosmic history becomes unavailable to future observers who may be every bit as intelligent as we are.

That makes our era stranger than the usual story of human insignificance allows. We are not at the center of the universe, and there is no evidence that the universe was made for us. But we may occupy an unusually information-rich interval: late enough for heavy elements, planets, instruments, and minds; early enough that the sky still carries evidence of origins on almost every scale. The human role explored here is therefore neither cosmic royalty nor cosmic irrelevance. It is custodianship under uncertainty. We are witnesses to evidence that nature itself may not preserve indefinitely.

The book treats that claim carefully. The far-future argument depends on the behavior of dark energy, which remains an open empirical question. Recent DESI results have strengthened hints that dark energy may evolve rather than remain a cosmological constant. So the book distinguishes what is observed now, what follows under standard ΛCDM, what changes under dynamical-dark-energy models, and what is philosophical inference rather than physics.
