# Research Brief — Republic of Companies

## Thesis under test

*Republic of Companies* uses one deliberately contestable company in every American decade from the 1770s through the 2020s to tell a second history of the United States: the history of the organizations that changed how Americans coordinated capital, labor, production, transportation, information, consumption, computation, and infrastructure.

The selection criterion is **importance, not admiration**. The winner need not be the decade's largest company, most valuable security, most innovative laboratory, most durable corporation, or most benevolent employer. The working test is whether the company changed the operating system of American life strongly enough that competitors had to imitate it, governments had to accommodate or regulate it, workers had to organize around it, customers changed their expectations because of it, or later industries inherited its organizational method.

The one-company rule is a narrative constraint, not a claim that history is objectively rankable. Every chapter therefore keeps a serious runner-up and a falsification question visible. A selection is strongest when a reasonable reader can reject the winner without needing to reject the facts.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. primary laws, court decisions, regulatory filings, congressional records, patents, company reports, contemporaneous correspondence, and archival documents;
2. national museums, the Library of Congress, National Park Service, Smithsonian, Federal Reserve, university archives, and other institutional historical collections;
3. peer-reviewed economic and business-history research and major scholarly books;
4. carefully sourced company histories for dates, product chronology, and internal decisions, with company claims treated as interested evidence rather than neutral interpretation;
5. high-quality journalism for events and contemporary consequences not otherwise documented in primary sources.

Company-origin myths are treated cautiously. The book avoids claiming a firm "invented" a technology when the historical record instead supports commercialization, integration, standardization, or scale. The recurring argument is usually organizational rather than heroic: the important company made a capability repeatable.

## Selection method and falsification rules

For each decade, ask five questions:

- **Coordination:** Did the company make a new kind of economic coordination practical at scale?
- **Inheritance:** Did later companies copy the method even outside the original industry?
- **Dependency:** Did customers, workers, suppliers, governments, or competitors reorganize around the company or its system?
- **Spillover:** Did the company's effects materially escape its product category and alter institutions, geography, labor, law, or expectations?
- **Counterfactual:** If the company had disappeared at the beginning of the decade, would the same organizational change likely have arrived at roughly the same speed and shape through rivals?

A winner should lose if a runner-up has stronger evidence on most of those dimensions. The book does not use market capitalization, revenue, employment, patent count, or founder fame as automatic proxies for importance.

## Chapter 1 — 1770s — British East India Company

**Core claim:** The most consequential company in the American 1770s was a British chartered corporation because Parliament's effort to improve the company's tea economics helped turn corporate privilege into a concrete object of colonial resistance.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Parliament of Great Britain, Tea Act 1773 (13 Geo. III c.44), for the statutory framework allowing East India Company tea to move directly to the colonies while retaining the Townshend duty.
- Journals and papers surrounding the Boston tea crisis in the Massachusetts Historical Society and Library of Congress collections, especially Thomas Hutchinson and colonial merchant correspondence.
- UK Parliament historical materials on the East India Company and Tea Act.
- National Archives (U.S.) and Library of Congress materials on the Continental Congress and the revolutionary chronology after the Coercive/Intolerable Acts.
- H. V. Bowen, *The Business of Empire: The East India Company and Imperial Britain, 1756–1833*, for the company's fiscal and political entanglement with the British state.

**Runner-up:** Local merchant houses and insurers in Boston, Philadelphia, and New York.

**Falsification note:** Do not claim the Tea Act was simply a tax increase; in important respects it was designed to make legal East India Company tea cheaper and commercially more competitive. Do not claim the company "caused" the American Revolution. The corporate episode belongs inside a much older constitutional and imperial conflict.

## Chapter 2 — 1780s — Bank of North America

**Core claim:** The Bank of North America demonstrated that a young republic could pool private capital in a durable institution and use bank credit to support public capacity before the federal constitutional system was fully stabilized.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Journals of the Continental Congress, May 1781 and December 1781, for Robert Morris's bank proposal and congressional charter.
- Library of Congress, Robert Morris papers, for wartime finance and the institutional context.
- Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia historical materials on early American banking.
- Pennsylvania legislative records surrounding the 1785 repeal and 1787 restoration of the state charter.
- Edwin J. Perkins, *American Public Finance and Financial Services, 1700–1815*, for the wider colonial and early-national credit system.

**Runner-up:** The First Bank of the United States, chartered in 1791, which is chronologically a stronger 1790s institution than an 1780s company.

**Falsification note:** The bank did not solve the Confederation's fiscal weakness and should not be described as a modern central bank. Its importance is demonstration and institutional precedent.

## Chapter 3 — 1790s — Almy, Brown & Slater

**Core claim:** The Pawtucket enterprise around Samuel Slater turned imported British textile knowledge into a repeatable American factory system and changed the organization of work around water-powered machinery.

Primary and institutional trail:

- National Park Service, Blackstone River Valley National Historical Park and Slater Mill materials, for the Pawtucket mill, machinery, labor, and chronology.
- Smithsonian National Museum of American History textile-manufacturing collections.
- David J. Jeremy, *Transatlantic Industrial Revolution*, for British technical knowledge transfer and Slater's place within a broader movement.
- U.S. Patent Office / Library of Congress materials on early American manufacturing policy and the 1790 patent regime.
- Economic histories of New England textiles and cotton supply for the connection between northern industrialization and slave-grown southern cotton.

**Runner-up:** First Bank of the United States; Eli Whitney's cotton-gin enterprise and patent.

**Falsification note:** Slater was not the sole founder of American industrialization and did not arrive in an industrial vacuum. Do not detach Rhode Island textiles from the expanding slave cotton economy that supplied raw material.

## Chapter 4 — 1800s — E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Company

**Core claim:** DuPont made technically disciplined process manufacturing a durable American industrial capability in a strategically important product.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Hagley Museum and Library, Eleutherian Mills / du Pont family collections, for founding documents, manufacturing methods, accidents, workforce, and early company growth.
- DuPont corporate historical timeline for chronology, checked against Hagley archival evidence.
- National Park Service and Delaware historical materials on the Brandywine industrial corridor.
- Antoine Lavoisier / French powder-administration scholarship for E. I. du Pont's technical training.
- War Department procurement and War of 1812 histories for the strategic demand context.

**Runner-up:** Early turnpike corporations and maritime insurers.

**Falsification note:** Gunpowder was old technology and DuPont did not invent it. The claim is about quality control, process knowledge, accumulated industrial learning, and durable organization.

## Chapter 5 — 1810s — American Fur Company

**Core claim:** John Jacob Astor's company demonstrated how a private commercial network could extend American economic presence across enormous distances ahead of dense settlement or administration.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Library of Congress and New-York Historical Society Astor papers and fur-trade collections.
- National Park Service, Fort Union Trading Post National Historic Site and Astoria / Pacific Northwest historical materials.
- U.S. statutes governing foreign participation in the fur trade after the War of 1812.
- Hiram Martin Chittenden, *The American Fur Trade of the Far West*, used cautiously as an older synthesis.
- Modern scholarship centering Indigenous nations as active commercial and political participants rather than treating the West as vacant territory.

**Runner-up:** Early steamboat enterprises, especially those built around Fulton/Livingston interests.

**Falsification note:** Do not describe the company as "opening empty land." Indigenous nations already governed, traded, traveled, and named the territory. The firm helped redirect and intensify existing commercial systems under expanding U.S. power.

## Chapter 6 — 1820s — Merrimack Manufacturing Company

**Core claim:** The Lowell system expanded the factory from a building into a planned urban and labor environment integrating capital, waterpower, housing, production, and management.

Primary and institutional trail:

- National Park Service, Lowell National Historical Park, for the canal system, mills, boardinghouses, labor force, and industrial chronology.
- Center for Lowell History / University of Massachusetts Lowell collections, including mill-worker writings and corporate records.
- *Lowell Offering* and Lowell Female Labor Reform Association materials for worker perspectives.
- Thomas Dublin, *Women at Work: The Transformation of Work and Community in Lowell, Massachusetts, 1826–1860*.
- Economic histories of the Boston Associates and Waltham-Lowell system.

**Runner-up:** Erie Canal–related corporations and early insurance companies.

**Falsification note:** Do not romanticize the "mill girls" as proof of benign industrialization or flatten them into victims. Wage opportunity, paternalist control, long hours, organization, and later immigrant labor all belong together.

## Chapter 7 — 1830s — Baltimore & Ohio Railroad

**Core claim:** The B&O helped establish the railroad as a new kind of American corporation: capital-intensive, geographically distributed, publicly privileged, managerially complex, and capable of making its own geography.

Primary and institutional trail:

- B&O Railroad Museum archives and corporate chronology.
- Maryland State Archives, 1827 B&O charter and related legislative materials.
- Library of Congress railroad maps and early engineering materials.
- Alfred D. Chandler Jr., *The Visible Hand*, for railroads' role in the rise of professional management.
- Historical research on railroad finance, eminent-domain powers, accounting, and common-carrier obligations.

**Runner-up:** Pennsylvania Main Line enterprises and canal companies.

**Falsification note:** Do not claim the B&O invented the railroad. Its importance is organizational precedent in the United States and the demonstration of a privately financed common-carrier system with public consequences.

## Chapter 8 — 1840s — Magnetic Telegraph Company

**Core claim:** Commercial telegraphy separated the speed of information from the speed of transportation and made the communications network a distinct business system.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Library of Congress, Samuel F. B. Morse papers, including the Washington–Baltimore demonstration and commercialization correspondence.
- Smithsonian National Museum of American History telegraph collections.
- Congressional appropriation records for the 1843 experimental telegraph line.
- U.S. patent litigation and Supreme Court material concerning Morse's claims.
- Richard R. John, *Network Nation*, for the political and institutional history of American communications networks.

**Runner-up:** Adams Express and other express companies that coordinated information and parcels alongside railroads.

**Falsification note:** Morse was one participant in an international field of electrical-telegraph invention. The chapter claims commercial network significance, not solitary invention.

## Chapter 9 — 1850s — Singer Manufacturing Company

**Core claim:** Singer turned an expensive durable technology into a mass consumer system through direct selling, service, branding, patent coordination, and installment finance.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Smithsonian National Museum of American History sewing-machine collections.
- U.S. patent records and litigation surrounding Elias Howe, Singer, and other sewing-machine manufacturers.
- Adam Mossoff, scholarship on the 1856 Sewing Machine Combination / patent pool.
- Company catalogues, advertisements, and international branch records in major museum and archival collections.
- Economic histories of installment selling and durable-goods consumer credit.

**Runner-up:** American Express; Pennsylvania Railroad.

**Falsification note:** Singer did not invent the sewing machine. Credit itself was ancient. The selection rests on industrializing sales, service, financing, and global distribution around the machine.

## Chapter 10 — 1860s — Union Pacific Railroad

**Core claim:** Union Pacific made explicit the American public-private development bargain: federal loans and land were used to induce a private corporation to build strategic national infrastructure ahead of mature demand.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Pacific Railway Act of 1862 and 1864 amendments, Library of Congress / Congress.gov.
- National Park Service, Golden Spike National Historical Park.
- Union Pacific historical records and annual reports.
- Congressional Crédit Mobilier investigations and House historical materials.
- Modern scholarship on Chinese and Irish railroad labor, Indigenous dispossession, buffalo destruction, land grants, and western settlement.

**Runner-up:** Western Union, which consolidated telegraphy into a national network during the decade.

**Falsification note:** Do not describe federal land as unused or vacant. The railroad accelerated settlement and U.S. military/administrative reach through territories already inhabited and governed by Indigenous nations. Do not reduce the completed railroad to Crédit Mobilier corruption; the infrastructure and the abuse were both real.

## Chapter 11 — 1870s — Standard Oil

**Core claim:** Standard Oil forced Americans to confront the possibility that genuine efficiency, vertical integration, low consumer prices, bargaining power, and suppression of competition could coexist inside the same company.

Primary and institutional trail:

- *Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States*, 221 U.S. 1 (1911), for the later judicial record on consolidation and conduct.
- Ida M. Tarbell, *The History of the Standard Oil Company*, read as influential investigative history with disclosed personal and Progressive-era context.
- Rockefeller Archive Center collections.
- Pennsylvania oil-region and railroad records on rebates, transport, and the South Improvement Company controversy.
- Ron Chernow, *Titan*, and Alfred Chandler's business history as secondary synthesis.

**Runner-up:** Pennsylvania Railroad; Western Union.

**Falsification note:** Avoid the simplistic claim that Standard's position came only from predation. Process efficiency, byproduct recovery, scale, and reliable distribution mattered materially. Avoid the opposite simplification that rebates and coercive tactics were merely neutral volume discounts.

## Chapter 12 — 1880s — American Telephone & Telegraph / Bell System

**Core claim:** The telephone made network effects a central corporate problem: users benefited from integration while the owner of integration acquired market power.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Smithsonian and Library of Congress Bell telephone collections and patent materials.
- AT&T corporate archives / Bell System historical reports for network buildout and long-distance chronology.
- FCC historical materials on telephone regulation and universal service.
- Kingsbury Commitment (1913) and later Communications Act context used to show the political consequences of the earlier architecture.
- Richard R. John and telecommunications-history scholarship on network competition and interconnection.

**Runner-up:** Edison electric-light companies and Westinghouse.

**Falsification note:** AT&T's mature regulated monopoly belongs mostly to later decades. The 1880s chapter is about the network architecture and the formation of long-distance AT&T, not a claim that the final Bell System already existed in full.

## Chapter 13 — 1890s — Sears, Roebuck and Company

**Core claim:** Sears used the postal and railroad networks to turn a catalog into a remote interface to national inventory, reducing information and geographic disadvantages for rural consumers.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Library of Congress and Smithsonian digitized Sears catalog collections.
- Sears corporate records and historical catalogues in the Chicago History Museum.
- U.S. Postal Service histories of Rural Free Delivery and later Parcel Post, with chronology kept distinct.
- Boris Emmet and John E. Jeuck, *Catalogues and Counters: A History of Sears, Roebuck and Company*.
- Rural economic histories on local merchandising, mail order, consumer prices, and Populist-era market integration.

**Runner-up:** Montgomery Ward, which preceded Sears in mail order; General Electric.

**Falsification note:** Sears did not invent mail order, and Parcel Post did not begin until 1913. The 1890s claim rests on scale, assortment, price transparency, and the integration of catalog, fulfillment, rail, and mail.

## Chapter 14 — 1900s — United States Steel

**Core claim:** U.S. Steel marked a step change in financial capitalism by demonstrating that Wall Street could manufacture an industrial giant through merger and make a billion-dollar corporation into a tradable financial object.

Primary and institutional trail:

- U.S. Steel first annual reports and 1901 capitalization materials.
- *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 251 U.S. 417 (1920), for the federal antitrust case and market record.
- Library of Congress and Hagley collections on Carnegie Steel, Federal Steel, J. P. Morgan, and early U.S. Steel.
- Naomi Lamoreaux, *The Great Merger Movement in American Business, 1895–1904*.
- David Nasaw, *Andrew Carnegie*, and financial histories of J. P. Morgan.

**Runner-up:** General Electric; American Tobacco; International Harvester.

**Falsification note:** Financial consolidation did not make U.S. Steel permanently the most innovative steel producer. Bethlehem Steel and others challenged it. The chapter treats scale as an organizational achievement with potential inertia, not as proof of permanent superiority.

## Chapter 15 — 1910s — Ford Motor Company

**Core claim:** Ford joined moving assembly, standardized product design, productivity, price reduction, and unusually high wages into a production system that became a model for mass industrial society.

Primary and institutional trail:

- The Henry Ford / Benson Ford Research Center records on Highland Park, Model T production, the moving assembly line, and the five-dollar day.
- Library of Congress photographs and Progressive-era labor materials.
- Ford Motor Company historical production records, checked against independent scholarship.
- Stephen Meyer, *The Five Dollar Day*.
- David Hounshell, *From the American System to Mass Production, 1800–1932*.

**Runner-up:** General Electric; Bell System; Armour/Swift in meatpacking and industrial flow.

**Falsification note:** Ford did not invent every element of the assembly line, and the five-dollar day was not simply altruism or a macroeconomic plan to let workers buy cars. Turnover reduction, labor control, productivity, and paternalist surveillance are central to the decision.

## Chapter 16 — 1920s — General Motors

**Core claim:** Under Alfred Sloan, GM made decentralized divisional management, financial control, brand segmentation, consumer finance, styling, and planned product change into a coherent alternative to Ford's single-product system.

Primary and institutional trail:

- General Motors Heritage Center / corporate archives for product and organization chronology.
- Alfred P. Sloan Jr., *My Years with General Motors*, treated as a participant memoir rather than neutral history.
- GMAC historical materials for consumer finance.
- Alfred D. Chandler Jr., *Strategy and Structure*.
- Automobile-industry sales, registration, and production data from U.S. government statistical compilations.

**Runner-up:** RCA, for radio broadcasting and the national advertising culture; General Electric.

**Falsification note:** Avoid using "planned obsolescence" as a claim that GM simply engineered early mechanical failure. The 1920s strategy relied heavily on styling, annual model change, price ladders, trade-ins, and psychological obsolescence.

## Chapter 17 — 1930s — Procter & Gamble

**Core claim:** P&G institutionalized brand management and made consumer attention, market research, and sponsored broadcasting part of the internal operating structure of a large corporation.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Neil McElroy's 1931 brand-management memorandum, reproduced and discussed in Harvard Business School and marketing-history collections.
- Procter & Gamble corporate archives and histories of Ivory, radio sponsorship, and consumer research.
- Library of Congress radio collections and FCC records on commercial broadcasting.
- Marketing-history scholarship on soap operas and national packaged-goods brands.
- New Deal labor and communications statutes for the changing regulatory environment.

**Runner-up:** RCA/NBC; DuPont; General Motors.

**Falsification note:** P&G did not invent branding or advertising. The claim is organizational: the company made responsibility for a brand a manager's job and integrated research, product, promotion, and sales around that unit.

## Chapter 18 — 1940s — Boeing

**Core claim:** Boeing's wartime aircraft programs show how federal procurement could turn complex engineering, mass production, supplier coordination, labor mobilization, and national strategy into one permanent industrial capability.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Boeing historical archives for B-17, B-29, wartime production, and plant chronology.
- National Museum of the U.S. Air Force and Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum B-29 materials.
- U.S. Army Air Forces procurement and production histories.
- National Archives materials on Executive Order 8802 and defense-industry employment.
- Wartime Production Board and military-industrial mobilization histories.

**Runner-up:** General Motors, whose wartime conversion was enormous; DuPont for explosives, materials, and Manhattan Project involvement.

**Falsification note:** The B-29 was a multi-company, government-directed program. Do not imply Boeing built every airframe or component. The bombing of Japanese cities and atomic missions belong to the history because industrial capability enabled military choices; the chapter does not claim the manufacturer alone determined policy.

## Chapter 19 — 1950s — McDonald's

**Core claim:** McDonald's turned service standardization into a replicable operating unit and made franchising, site control, supply specifications, training, and roadside branding into a system other service industries copied.

Primary and institutional trail:

- McDonald's corporate history for the brothers' 1948 Speedee Service System, Ray Kroc's 1954 visit, Des Plaines opening in 1955, and 1961 acquisition; company-origin material checked against independent histories.
- Smithsonian fast-food and roadside-America collections.
- Federal Highway Administration history of the 1956 Interstate Highway Act.
- John F. Love, *McDonald's: Behind the Arches*, for franchise and real-estate development.
- Labor and food-system scholarship for the consequences of standardized fast-food work and supply chains.

**Runner-up:** IBM; Disney; Boeing.

**Falsification note:** The brothers created the Speedee system before Kroc. Kroc's importance was scaling and governing the franchise system, not inventing fast food.

## Chapter 20 — 1960s — IBM

**Core claim:** System/360 made compatibility a strategic promise and changed the computer from a succession of isolated machines into a platform around which customer software, skills, peripherals, and business processes could accumulate.

Primary and institutional trail:

- IBM, "The IBM System/360," historical archive: launch April 7, 1964, common architecture, replacement of five existing product lines, and the company's roughly $5 billion program commitment.
- IBM Thomas J. Watson Jr. historical archive for executive sponsorship and risk.
- Computer History Museum System/360 collections.
- Frederick P. Brooks Jr., *The Mythical Man-Month*, for lessons from OS/360 development.
- U.S. Department of Justice 1969 IBM antitrust complaint and later case record for market-power context.

**Runner-up:** Boeing, AT&T, Fairchild Semiconductor, Xerox.

**Falsification note:** Treat IBM's own "first platform" language as company interpretation. The defensible historical claim is that System/360 was a landmark compatible commercial computer family whose architecture reshaped enterprise computing.

## Chapter 21 — 1970s — Intel

**Core claim:** Intel helped turn the programmable microprocessor into a general commercial component, pushing computing out of institutional rooms and into products other companies could design.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Intel historical archive on the Busicom project, 4004 announcement (November 1971), 8008, 8080, 1103 DRAM, EPROM, and early financial chronology.
- Computer History Museum microprocessor collections.
- Smithsonian integrated-circuit and personal-computer collections.
- Gordon Moore, "Cramming More Components onto Integrated Circuits" (1965), for the observation later called Moore's Law.
- Semiconductor labor/environment histories for fabrication work and Silicon Valley contamination.

**Runner-up:** FedEx; Exxon; Apple late in the decade.

**Falsification note:** The "first microprocessor" label depends on definitions and competing contemporary projects. The chapter explicitly does not rest the selection on a single-first claim; it rests on commercialization of a programmable processor platform and its descendants.

## Chapter 22 — 1980s — Microsoft

**Core claim:** Microsoft's decisive move was retaining the ability to license its operating system beyond IBM, allowing software economics and PC compatibility to move strategic power away from the box maker toward the portable platform layer.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Computer History Museum, IBM PC and MS-DOS timeline and collections.
- IBM PC technical reference and 1981 launch materials.
- Microsoft corporate archive / early histories for the acquisition of 86-DOS rights and DOS licensing chronology.
- Seattle Computer Products records and litigation histories.
- Federal antitrust records from the 1990s used only as downstream evidence of platform power built in the 1980s.

**Runner-up:** Apple; Walmart; General Electric.

**Falsification note:** Avoid the folklore that IBM simply "gave away" DOS. IBM had rational speed-to-market reasons, and the PC market's eventual architecture was not obvious in 1980. The important fact is Microsoft's nonexclusive licensing position as compatible PCs proliferated.

## Chapter 23 — 1990s — Walmart

**Core claim:** Walmart made the discount store a node in a national information and logistics network, using distribution centers, a private fleet, scanner data, satellite communications, supplier integration, and purchasing scale to change retail and manufacturing.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Walmart corporate history: by 1990 the company described itself as America's largest retailer; 1987 private satellite network; 1990s international expansion; 1997 first $100 billion sales year.
- Walmart annual reports from the 1980s and 1990s for stores, sales, distribution, technology, and employment.
- U.S. Census Bureau retail and trade data for sector context.
- Emek Basker and other peer-reviewed economic research on Walmart's effects on prices, employment, and local retail.
- David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson research on import competition for the broader China/import context; do not attribute the entire globalization shock to Walmart.

**Runner-up:** Microsoft; Intel; Netscape/AOL; FedEx.

**Falsification note:** Cross-docking, barcodes, discounting, and global sourcing were not Walmart inventions. The claim is integration and scale. Consumer price benefits and pressure on workers/suppliers/communities must both remain visible.

## Chapter 24 — 2000s — Google

**Core claim:** Google converted search intent into a self-service computational advertising market, changing who could buy an audience and teaching business to value clicks, keywords, conversion, and measurable acquisition.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Google historical / Ads materials: AdWords launched in 2000; cost-per-click search advertising introduced in 2002; modern Google Ads auction documentation for the continuing relevance/quality-plus-bid logic.
- Google 2004 Form S-1 for business-model, advertising, search, and auction descriptions near the end of the early growth period.
- U.S. Department of Justice search and advertising antitrust records for later evidence on defaults, market structure, and platform power.
- Stanford / Page and Brin original search research for technical background on PageRank, without confusing search invention with advertising invention.
- Overture/GoTo histories for the prior development of pay-per-click search advertising.

**Runner-up:** Amazon; Apple late in the decade; Facebook.

**Falsification note:** Google did not invent pay-per-click advertising or the auctioning of search placement. Its importance lies in combining search quality, self-service advertising, automated auctions, relevance, and enormous query volume into a durable economic engine.

## Chapter 25 — 2010s — Amazon

**Core claim:** Amazon fused marketplace governance, Prime, fulfillment, logistics, cloud infrastructure, data, and subscription into an operating layer other consumers and businesses increasingly used rather than merely shopped at.

Primary and institutional trail:

- Amazon annual reports and shareholder letters for Prime, Marketplace, fulfillment, advertising, and capital-investment chronology.
- AWS first-party history: S3 launched March 14, 2006; EC2 began in August 2006; later EC2 and AWS histories for the expansion of rented computing infrastructure.
- Amazon SEC filings for AWS segment economics after separate disclosure began.
- Federal Trade Commission and state antitrust complaints / litigation for marketplace and platform-power allegations, treated as allegations unless adjudicated.
- Labor, injury, warehousing, and logistics research from OSHA, NLRB records, academic labor economists, and reputable investigative reporting.

**Runner-up:** Apple; Facebook/Meta; Google.

**Falsification note:** The underlying trends toward e-commerce and cloud computing would have existed without Amazon. The selection rests on the unusually strong convergence of retail, marketplace, fulfillment, subscription, logistics, and cloud inside one reinforcing system.

## Chapter 26 — 2020s — NVIDIA (provisional)

**Core claim:** Accelerated computing became a scarce industrial input to the generative-AI boom, and NVIDIA converted decades of GPU architecture, CUDA software, networking, and systems work into the platform rival AI labs, cloud firms, enterprises, and governments increasingly planned around.

Primary and institutional trail:

- NVIDIA CUDA Programming Guide: CUDA introduced in 2006 to enable non-graphics computational workloads on GPUs.
- NVIDIA historical timeline and contemporaneous materials on 2012 AlexNet / ImageNet, checked against the original Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton paper.
- Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton, "ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2012).
- NVIDIA Hopper/H100 launch materials: H100 announced March 22, 2022; full-production announcement September 20, 2022; Transformer Engine and NVLink designed around large AI workloads.
- NVIDIA Blackwell launch and architecture materials, 2024–2025.
- NVIDIA, February 25, 2026 fiscal-year results: fiscal 2026 revenue $215.9 billion, up 65%; quarterly Data Center revenue $62.3 billion in Q4.
- NVIDIA Vera Rubin materials, January–July 2026: Rubin architecture entered production; by May/July the company described a supply network spanning 350+ factory sites in 30 countries and a rack-scale platform integrating GPU, CPU, NVLink, networking, storage processing, and software.
- U.S. Department of Commerce / Bureau of Industry and Security export-control rules for advanced computing products and China-related restrictions.
- CHIPS and Science Act / Commerce Department materials for U.S. semiconductor industrial policy.
- TSMC filings and supply-chain research for the manufacturing dependency beneath NVIDIA's fabless design model.

**Runner-ups:** OpenAI, Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta, TSMC, SpaceX.

**Falsification note:** This is deliberately an unfinished verdict as of September 2026. OpenAI may prove more consequential if the application/model layer captures the durable power; Microsoft or the hyperscalers may capture the stack; TSMC may look more fundamental in retrospect; custom accelerators, model efficiency, open standards, or an architectural break could erode NVIDIA's position. The chapter should be revised before release if the decade's evidence changes materially.

## Cross-cutting counter-evidence the manuscript must preserve

- **Government is not background.** Charters, land grants, procurement, patents, currency, highways, spectrum, antitrust, trade policy, research funding, and export controls repeatedly create the field on which the private company operates.
- **Importance is not invention.** Several winners commercialized or integrated technologies developed by others. The book should prefer "made repeatable," "standardized," "scaled," or "made into a platform" when "invented" is too strong.
- **Consumer benefit does not erase market power.** Standard Oil, AT&T, Walmart, Google, Amazon, and other selected companies often produced lower prices, better service, or wider access while creating dependency or competition concerns.
- **Labor is part of the operating system.** Slater's child/family labor, Lowell mill women, railroad construction crews, industrial workers, telephone operators, assembly-line employees, fast-food workers, warehouse labor, and semiconductor/data-center supply chains should remain inside the causal account rather than as obligatory end-of-chapter caveats.
- **Geography is political.** Fur trading and railroads operated through Indigenous territories; highways and suburban retail changed land use; cloud and AI infrastructure depend on local grids, water, tax policy, and community consent.
- **Technology does not imply inevitability.** Rival architectures existed in telegraphy, telephony, automobiles, computing, semiconductors, operating systems, retail, search, cloud, and AI. The winner often looks inevitable only after standards, installed bases, capital, and law have accumulated around it.

## Editorial rule

A surprising winner is welcome. An indefensible winner is not.

The book's purpose is not to make readers agree with all twenty-six selections. It is to leave them with a more demanding definition of corporate importance: the company mattered most when the rest of the country had to reorganize around what it had learned how to do.
