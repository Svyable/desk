# Credit Before Country

In May 1781, before the United States had won the war that would secure its independence, Robert Morris asked Congress to approve a bank.

The sequence matters. The country did not first become stable and then build financial institutions appropriate to its new condition. It tried to build a financial institution because stability was nowhere near guaranteed. The Continental Army needed supplies. The public finances were disordered. Congress had no independent power to levy national taxes under the Articles of Confederation. Paper currency had depreciated catastrophically. State governments, foreign lenders, merchants, tax collectors, soldiers, and creditors were tied together by promises whose value depended on a political experiment that was still at war.

Morris, a Philadelphia merchant and financier serving as Superintendent of Finance, proposed the Bank of North America as one instrument for making those promises more credible. Congress approved his plan on May 26, 1781. The bank was organized around private subscribed capital, including funds connected to French loans, and received a congressional charter later that year. Pennsylvania also chartered it. It opened in Philadelphia in January 1782 while the Revolution was still unresolved. In the history of American business, the institution is often described as the first nationally chartered commercial bank or the first bank incorporated by the new government. In the history of the republic, its importance is even more basic: it was an early attempt to manufacture public capacity out of a private balance sheet.

This is why the Bank of North America belongs to the 1780s. Other enterprises of the decade sold more visible goods. Merchants still dominated much of American commerce, and partnerships rather than corporations remained the ordinary legal form of business. But the bank demonstrated a principle that would become foundational to American development: if a project was too large, too long-lived, or too uncertain for any one person’s fortune, an institution could gather capital from many owners, create transferable claims, extend credit, and continue operating beyond the immediate transactions of its founders.

That sounds normal now because modern economies are built from such abstractions. In 1781, the normal thing was the crisis.

The Revolution had exposed the difference between political resolve and financial capacity. Congress could authorize an army, but authorization did not feed a soldier. It could issue paper, but printing did not make the paper retain purchasing power. The phrase “not worth a Continental” survived because the Continental currency’s collapse became part of popular memory. States had their own fiscal needs and currencies. Tax collection was weak and fragmented. Foreign credit mattered. Private merchants supplied governments that could be slow, uncertain, or unable to pay. The young nation had declared rights in universal language and financed many of its obligations through improvised instruments whose value was anything but universal.

Morris was not inventing banking. European commercial societies had centuries of experience with institutions that accepted deposits, discounted bills, transferred funds, and supported public borrowing. Nor was he approaching the problem as a neutral technician. He was a wealthy merchant with extensive commercial interests and a strong belief that restoring public credit required giving men of property reason to trust the government. Critics worried, with reason, that the bank could concentrate economic power and reward insiders. The institution was controversial almost as soon as it existed. Pennsylvania would revoke its state charter in 1785 before restoring it in 1787 after another political fight.

Those disputes are not side notes. They are the chapter.

The bank’s design sat directly on the boundary Americans had just fought over in the 1770s. A government was granting privileges to a corporation because the government needed capabilities the corporation could help provide. The East India Company had made that relationship look dangerous. The Bank of North America made it look necessary. The distance between those two judgments would occupy American politics for generations.

The bank could issue notes, take deposits, and make loans. Its paper circulated because people believed the bank had assets and would redeem its obligations. That confidence could not be ordered into existence. It had to be produced through governance, reserves, reputation, and the quality of the bank’s borrowers. In that sense, the bank sold an unusual product: confidence in promises. A farmer could understand land. A merchant could inspect a cargo. A metal coin had a physical existence people had learned to treat as value. Bank credit required people to accept that an institutional ledger and a printed note could carry purchasing power because a corporation stood behind them.

The Revolution had already forced Americans to live inside a world of paper claims. The bank tried to make some of those claims more dependable.

Its relationship with government was especially important. Morris wanted the bank to support public finance, and the bank did make loans that helped the Confederation government meet obligations. This did not magically solve the fiscal weakness of Congress. The deeper constitutional problem remained: a national government unable to reliably raise its own revenue could not become creditworthy merely by opening an account. But the bank provided a working demonstration of how private capital and public needs could be joined. That example mattered when Alexander Hamilton later designed the First Bank of the United States under the new Constitution.

The Bank of North America also forces a distinction between money and credit that tends to disappear in ordinary life. Money is what settles a transaction. Credit lets the transaction happen before settlement. A government fighting a war needs both. So does a business waiting for goods to cross an ocean, a farmer between planting and harvest, or an industrial company building equipment before revenues arrive. The ability to move resources through time is one of the quiet technologies of economic growth. The institution that can persuade savers to surrender present control of capital and borrowers to promise future repayment becomes an organizer of activity it does not itself physically perform.

This is corporate power in a form less visible than a monopoly on tea. The bank did not need to own farms to affect agriculture. It did not need to operate ships to affect trade. It did not need to manufacture guns to affect war finance. By deciding whose promises were credible and on what terms, it could influence activity distributed throughout the economy.

The politics followed naturally. Who gets credit? Who backs the bank? Who bears losses? Who benefits from scarcity? What does the public receive in return for a charter? Can a corporation created through public authority use that privilege for private gain? Are wealthy creditors a stabilizing constituency with reason to support the government, or an elite able to bend the government toward their interests? These questions were asked in different language in the 1780s. They have never stopped being asked.

The Bank of North America did not dominate the United States the way later national financial institutions would. Its scale was modest by modern standards, its geographic reach limited, and its political position unstable. That makes it easy to underestimate if importance is measured by the size of an enterprise at its peak. The more useful comparison is institutional. Before the bank, the new republic’s financial system depended heavily on individual merchants, public offices, specie, foreign credit, and circulating paper of uncertain quality. After it, Americans had a concrete domestic example of an incorporated bank that could pool capital and create a private mechanism for supporting public credit.

Once a society sees such a mechanism work, arguments shift. The question is no longer whether the thing can exist. The question becomes who controls it and under what constitutional authority.

Hamilton would take that argument national in the 1790s. His First Bank of the United States, chartered in 1791, was larger, more politically consequential, and more deeply connected to his broader fiscal program. A perfectly defensible book could award the 1790s to that bank. This one does not, because the 1790s contain another transformation that moved out of finance and into the organization of work itself. But Hamilton’s bank cannot be understood without the 1780s experiment that preceded it. The Bank of North America was the prototype America built while the machinery of nationhood was still unfinished.

The institution also helps correct a popular story about capitalism and government in the early United States. In the simplified version, political freedom creates a protected private sphere, entrepreneurs enter it, and markets then develop largely on their own. The actual sequence is filled with charters, statutes, debts, public contracts, legal privileges, monetary policy, land rules, and institutional improvisation. Markets require rules about what counts as property, which promises courts will enforce, what money is, who can create a bank, and what happens when borrowers fail. Early American capitalism did not emerge outside government. It emerged through repeated political decisions about the forms economic organization could take.

The Bank of North America was one of those decisions.

Its controversies reveal how uncertain the corporate form still was. Today incorporation is usually administrative. Entrepreneurs choose a state, file documents, pay a fee, and create a legal entity without persuading a legislature that their particular business deserves a public charter. In the eighteenth century, incorporation could look more like a special grant. Because the privilege was selective, the choice of who received it was inherently political. Critics could plausibly ask why one set of investors deserved legal advantages unavailable to ordinary partnerships.

This concern was amplified by republican suspicion of concentrated power. The Revolution had been fought against a political order associated with inherited privilege and distant authority. A corporation with a special charter, wealthy subscribers, and a role in public finance could look uncomfortably aristocratic. Pennsylvania politics around the bank became a struggle over precisely these fears. The revocation of the charter in 1785 showed that corporate existence was not yet insulated from democratic politics. The restoration two years later showed that the institution’s supporters could make an equally powerful case about commerce, credit, and property rights.

The fight established another recurring American pattern: economic institutions become difficult to discuss only as economic institutions once people build political identities around them. Banks are never merely banks when voters believe they embody a theory of the republic.

Andrew Jackson’s later war against the Second Bank of the United States would make that pattern famous. The conflict was already visible here in miniature. Supporters saw organized credit as civilization, stability, and development. Opponents saw concentrated financial power, special privilege, and a threat to equality. Both interpretations could contain truth because a bank can simultaneously expand economic possibility and distribute power unequally.

This double character recurs in nearly every company ahead. The B&O will make distance cheaper and help transform Baltimore while imposing new forms of industrial discipline and political influence. Standard Oil will reduce costs and improve the reliability of refined petroleum while using tactics that helped make monopoly a national political issue. AT&T will create a remarkably integrated communications network while making monopoly part of its theory of efficiency. Amazon will make infrastructure available to tiny firms on a credit card while becoming infrastructure other firms can struggle to avoid.

The most important companies are rarely important because their effects point in one moral direction.

For the Bank of North America, the central achievement was the conversion of trust into an institution. That achievement required credibility among people with capital, and credibility in turn required governance that often privileged their concerns. Morris believed public credit was a national necessity. He was right in the limited sense that a government unable to borrow on reliable terms is constrained in everything from war to infrastructure. He was also helping establish a system in which creditors would possess leverage over public choices. There is no clean way to keep the capacity while discarding every consequence of the structure that creates it.

The 1780s were full of evidence that the Confederation’s existing arrangements were inadequate. States quarreled over commerce. Debts remained contentious. Veterans and farmers faced economic hardship. Shays’ Rebellion in Massachusetts in 1786 and 1787 became, for nationalists, evidence of dangerous weakness and disorder, though the grievances of the rebels were rooted in concrete tax and debt pressures rather than some generalized hostility to republican government. The Constitutional Convention met in 1787. The Constitution gave the new federal government fiscal powers the Confederation had lacked.

The bank did not cause the Constitution. It belonged to the same decade of institutional experimentation. Americans were discovering that independence was not self-executing. A declaration could create political legitimacy in principle. It could not create a customs system, reliable taxation, a national currency, liquid credit markets, or institutions capable of coordinating a large republic.

Companies would become part of that coordination.

The Bank of North America’s most revealing feature may be how quickly it became both indispensable and suspect. That combination is a sign of institutional importance. People rarely fight bitterly over things that do not matter. Within a few years of opening, the bank had supporters arguing that its credit was useful to commerce and public finance and opponents arguing that its privileges threatened republican equality. The corporation had become a place where Americans could see competing theories of freedom: freedom as protection from concentrated power, and freedom as access to the organized capital needed to act.

The country never resolved the choice because it was not truly a choice. It wanted both.

That contradiction would shape the American financial system: a recurrent desire for abundant credit without creditor domination, stable banks without monopolistic privilege, national coordination without excessive centralization, and government support without political favoritism. Crises would expose the tradeoffs. Reforms would rearrange them. New institutions would claim to have solved the old problem and then produce a new version.

The Bank of North America survives today as a historical ancestor inside a financial genealogy far removed from its original scale. Its modern descendants are not what make it the company of the 1780s. What matters is the moment when it opened its doors in wartime Philadelphia and embodied an idea the republic urgently needed: capital could be organized through a durable institution, and that institution could lend power to a government that did not possess enough power on its own.

The United States had not yet completed the political architecture that would make national credit more secure. The bank arrived first.

That ordering is worth remembering whenever American business history is told as though companies simply appeared after government established the rules. In one of the republic’s earliest and most important cases, the company and the government were improvising each other at the same time.

By the end of the decade, Americans had a Constitution designed partly in response to the weaknesses exposed by war and confederation. They had a stronger federal government, a live argument over finance, and a growing awareness that economic independence required more than severing imperial ties.

They also had another problem. The country imported manufactured goods it increasingly wanted to make for itself. British textile technology was guarded. Skilled mechanics were discouraged from carrying certain knowledge abroad. The young republic had capital and ambition, but much of the machinery of industrialization was still somewhere else.

In the next decade, one man would cross the Atlantic without blueprints and reconstruct a manufacturing system from memory. The company that formed around him would change what Americans meant by work.
