# Republic of Companies

The Most Important Company of Every American Decade—and the Stories That Built a Nation

Sven Hardy Benson

America turns 250 in 2026 with a familiar set of stories about itself. We know the presidents, wars, elections, amendments, movements, inventions, crashes, and recoveries. We know the canonical images: a declaration on parchment, a spike driven into a railroad tie, an assembly line, a mushroom cloud, a moon landing, a personal computer, a search box, a smartphone. They are useful images. They are also incomplete.

Running beside the political history of the United States is another history made of organizations. Some were tiny when their consequential decisions were made. Some became the largest enterprises the world had yet seen. Some disappeared. Some survived long enough to become background infrastructure. A few became so powerful that Americans had to invent new laws, agencies, unions, financing systems, and political vocabularies simply to live with what those companies had created.

This book tells that second history through an intentionally unreasonable device: one company for every decade.

The word *important* is doing more work here than it usually admits. This is not a ranking of the best-run companies, the most valuable stocks, the nicest employers, the most ingenious founders, or the brands people remember most fondly. Several winners in these pages inflicted harms that cannot be separated from the systems that made them powerful. Several depended heavily on government support while presenting themselves as triumphs of private enterprise. Several grew by exploiting workers, excluding competitors, taking land, bending regulation, or arranging the market so that the apparent freedom of choice concealed a much narrower set of real options.

Importance is not admiration.

The test is different. For each decade, I asked which company most changed the operating system of American life. Which firm made a new kind of coordination ordinary? Which one altered how people worked, bought, borrowed, moved, communicated, or understood the possible scale of an organization? Which company created a pattern that competitors had to copy, governments had to regulate, workers had to negotiate with, and later generations would inherit without necessarily remembering where it came from?

That criterion produces some obvious winners and several strange ones. Ford belongs in the 1910s because the moving assembly line and the five-dollar day helped turn mass production into a social system rather than merely a manufacturing technique. IBM belongs in the 1960s because System/360 made compatibility itself a strategic promise and turned computing into a platform. Microsoft belongs in the 1980s because retaining the right to license an operating system to the makers of IBM-compatible computers proved more consequential than building the computers. Amazon belongs in the 2010s not simply because it sold an astonishing number of packages, but because its cloud business made computing infrastructure something a young company could rent by the hour instead of finance in advance.

Other choices require an argument. The first chapter names the British East India Company as the most important company of the 1770s. It was not American. That is precisely the point. Before the United States had a mature corporate economy of its own, a state-backed corporation on the other side of the Atlantic became the object around which colonists argued about monopoly, taxation, representation, and political power. Tea from one corporation ended up in Boston Harbor. America’s company story begins with a company Americans did not want to obey.

The 1780s belong here to the Bank of North America, one of the young republic’s earliest experiments in concentrating private capital for public purposes. The 1790s belong to the partnership behind Samuel Slater’s mill, where British manufacturing knowledge crossed the Atlantic in a man’s memory and became machinery, wages, schedules, and a new industrial discipline. The nineteenth century then accelerates through powder, fur, textile cities, railroads, telegraph wires, installment payments, transcontinental infrastructure, oil trusts, telephone networks, and mail-order catalogs. By 1901, the United States could produce a corporation capitalized above a billion dollars. Half a century later, the organizational methods pioneered in business had become inseparable from military mobilization, suburban life, franchising, and the administration of a consumer republic.

The later chapters may feel closer to home because many of their products remain on desks and in pockets. That familiarity can make the change harder to see. A search box looks trivial compared with a railroad. A cloud-computing console has none of the physical drama of a steel mill. A graphics processor is a rectangle of silicon hidden inside a server rack. Yet these companies reorganized activity on a continental and then planetary scale. Google converted intent into an auction. Amazon converted fixed computing infrastructure into a variable operating expense. NVIDIA spent years turning a chip designed to draw pixels into a programmable parallel-computing platform, then found itself supplying a scarce input to the artificial-intelligence boom.

The final choice is necessarily provisional. The 2020s are not over. NVIDIA could still prove to be the wrong winner. OpenAI, Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, SpaceX, TSMC, or a company not yet important enough to appear obvious could look more consequential from the 2030s. The uncertainty is a feature rather than an embarrassment. Every earlier chapter is written with the knowledge that the ending already happened. The last chapter does not have that luxury. It lets us feel, briefly, what every decade in this book felt like while people were actually living through it: crowded with rival futures, none yet certified by hindsight.

The one-company rule also creates losers, and some of those losers will annoy readers. That is useful. There is no objective instrument that can prove Sears mattered more than General Electric in the 1890s, or that Walmart mattered more than Netscape in the 1990s. The constraint forces a judgment, and judgments reveal the theory underneath them. I have tried to make the theory visible enough that readers can disagree intelligently. A short list of serious runners-up appears in the back matter. The argument is stronger when a different selection can be made without changing the facts.

There is another reason to tell American history through companies at the 250-year mark. Corporate power is often discussed as though it sits outside the republic, a private realm governed by markets while the public realm is governed by politics. The historical record is messier. The companies in this book repeatedly cross that boundary. Congress chartered, subsidized, regulated, prosecuted, purchased from, protected, dismantled, or depended upon them. Courts defined their rights. Wars expanded some and destroyed others. Public roads, schools, patents, research spending, postal networks, land grants, currency systems, and procurement contracts made private scale possible. At the same time, companies built infrastructure government did not yet know how to build, developed management systems public institutions later borrowed, and created new facts on the ground that politics then had to catch up with.

The United States is not a company. It has never been one. But for 250 years, Americans have repeatedly delegated astonishing pieces of daily life to companies and then argued, usually after the delegation became difficult to reverse, about the terms of that bargain.

That is the republic inside this book: not a nation ruled by corporations, and not a nation independent of them, but a country that has again and again discovered what kind of society it was becoming by looking at the companies it could no longer ignore.
