# The Company America Hated

The most important company in America in the 1770s was not American.

That is an awkward way to begin a book about 250 years of American companies, which is part of why it is the right place to begin. In the decade when the United States declared itself into existence, the corporate form was not yet the familiar private container into which almost any commercial ambition could be poured. Large chartered companies were stranger and more political creatures. They were created through acts of sovereign authority. They could receive monopolies, govern distant territories, field military forces, collect revenue, and become instruments of state policy while remaining enterprises with investors and accounts. The British East India Company was the largest and most consequential example available to the colonists who would soon become Americans. By 1773 it had also become, quite literally, cargo.

On the evening of December 16 that year, men moved through Boston toward Griffin’s Wharf. Some had disguised themselves in ways meant to evoke Mohawk identity, though the disguises were neither uniform nor especially convincing. They boarded three merchant ships—the *Dartmouth*, *Eleanor*, and *Beaver*—and opened hundreds of chests of East India Company tea. The contents went into the harbor. The destruction was orderly enough that the participants did not simply sack the vessels; the target was the tea. They were attacking a commodity, but they were also attacking the arrangement behind it: Parliament, imperial taxation, monopoly privilege, and a company whose financial problems had been turned into a problem for people an ocean away.

The Boston Tea Party can be made so familiar that its peculiar corporate architecture disappears. Schoolbook versions often compress the episode into a clean sentence about taxation without representation. That principle mattered enormously, but it does not explain why the thing floating in the water was tea belonging to one particular corporation. The Tea Act of 1773 was designed in part to help the East India Company move a vast surplus of tea and improve its financial position. It allowed the company to export tea directly to the American colonies under conditions that could make legally imported tea cheaper even while retaining the existing parliamentary duty. The law therefore did not simply make tea expensive. In an important sense it could make the company’s tea more commercially competitive. That was exactly what made the arrangement politically dangerous.

A tax can announce itself as coercion. A bargain is harder to refuse.

For colonial merchants who had built businesses inside the existing trading system, the East India Company’s new access threatened to cut them out. For political opponents of Parliament’s claim to tax the colonies, cheaper tea raised the possibility that consumers might accept the tax because the final price looked attractive. For smugglers and their commercial allies, a more efficient legal channel threatened an established illicit business. For ordinary colonists deciding whether to drink the tea, several motives could coexist without fitting neatly into a single patriotic category. The controversy was about constitutional authority, but it was also about distribution, monopoly, local commerce, and the political meaning of a low price produced by special privilege.

The company at the center of this dispute had begun more than a century and a half earlier. Chartered at the end of 1600, the East India Company accumulated trading privileges and territorial power gradually, then spectacularly. By the eighteenth century it was not merely a merchant sending ships between Britain and Asia. In India it had become a territorial power with armies, tax revenues, and political obligations. Its servants and shareholders could profit from an organization whose decisions affected millions of people. Its scale depended on the British state, and the British state increasingly depended on it. That entanglement helps explain why the company’s trouble in the early 1770s could draw Parliament into rearranging an Atlantic market.

The company was in trouble despite being powerful. This apparent contradiction recurs throughout the history in this book. Scale creates capabilities; it also creates obligations, political exposure, fixed costs, and expectations that smaller organizations do not carry. The East India Company’s finances were strained after territorial expansion, war, administrative costs, corruption, and a severe crisis in Bengal. At home it had accumulated tea it needed to sell. Its importance to British finance and imperial government made simply allowing it to fail politically unattractive. Parliament’s response turned corporate distress into legislation.

American resistance therefore confronted a form of power that does not fit comfortably inside the modern categories of public and private. The company was neither merely the government nor merely a business. It occupied the productive ambiguity between them. Its charter and privileges came from the state. Its investors expected returns. Its commercial network supplied goods. Its military and administrative apparatus exercised forms of rule. When colonists objected to its tea, they were reacting to a corporation whose advantages were inseparable from the state’s authority to grant them.

This is one reason the episode belongs at the front of an American corporate history rather than in a preface about Britain. The United States was born into an argument about what kinds of concentrated economic power were legitimate and how closely private enterprise should sit to political sovereignty. The argument would change form repeatedly. The Bank of the United States would provoke it. Railroads would provoke it. Standard Oil would provoke it. Utilities, banks, defense contractors, telephone networks, digital platforms, and artificial-intelligence companies would each provoke versions of it. The facts would be different, but the recurring question would be recognizable: when a company becomes important enough that the public cannot simply walk away from it, what exactly is that company?

The East India Company offered an extreme answer. It could be a merchant, tax collector, military force, territorial administrator, diplomatic actor, employer, borrower, and recipient of government rescue all at once. To eighteenth-century Americans, this was not an abstraction in corporate law. It arrived in wooden chests.

The Tea Act’s commercial design sharpened the issue because it placed consumer behavior inside the constitutional dispute. If colonists bought the cheaper tea, they could be understood as accepting Parliament’s authority to impose the retained duty. A purchase became something more than a purchase. This was politics conducted through the supply chain. Merchants who handled the tea, consignees who received it, ship captains who carried it, customs officials who processed it, and households that might drink it all occupied positions in an imperial system that resistance leaders were trying to disrupt.

This was not the first time colonial Americans had used consumption as political action. Nonimportation agreements and boycotts had already made ordinary goods into constitutional instruments. What the East India Company episode demonstrated with unusual clarity was that a company could become a political symbol because its business model embodied a contested arrangement of power. The tea itself was replaceable. The privilege was the issue.

The events in Boston were only one part of a broader colonial response. Tea shipments faced resistance in other ports. In Philadelphia and New York, pressure helped prevent tea from being landed. In Charleston, tea was landed but placed in storage rather than sold in the intended way. Boston became the defining confrontation because Massachusetts officials and the tea consignees did not yield, the ships could not simply depart without resolving the customs problem, and the standoff ended with physical destruction on a scale London could not ignore.

Parliament answered the Tea Party with the measures Americans came to call the Intolerable Acts. The punishment of Massachusetts helped convert a local confrontation into a continental political problem. The First Continental Congress met in 1774. Armed conflict followed in 1775. Independence came in 1776. No serious history should claim that one corporation caused the American Revolution; the underlying dispute over imperial authority long predated the Tea Act, and the colonies contained political, religious, commercial, and regional disagreements too large to compress into a tea shipment. But it is equally misleading to remove the company from a causal chain in which Parliament’s attempt to solve a corporate and imperial problem helped trigger one of the Revolution’s most memorable confrontations.

The East India Company also exposes a temptation that will shadow every selection in this book: the temptation to tell corporate history as a procession of ingenious people building useful things. There will be plenty of ingenious people ahead. Samuel Slater will reconstruct textile machinery from memory. Alfred Sloan will devise a way to manage a federation of automobile divisions. IBM will bet on a compatible family of computers. Intel engineers will put a general-purpose processor on a chip. Those accomplishments matter. But companies do not enter history only through invention. They matter through law, leverage, logistics, labor, political access, and the ability to make their own problems somebody else’s problem.

In the 1770s, the East India Company’s problem was tea it could not easily sell and a financial structure Britain could not comfortably ignore. Parliament designed a solution. The colonies rejected the terms. That sequence is less romantic than a story about liberty alone, and more revealing about the country that followed.

The new United States would initially remain suspicious of corporations, especially those that appeared to carry monopoly privileges. Incorporation was generally a specific public grant rather than the routine administrative act it later became. Early corporations were often justified by a public purpose: banks, bridges, canals, turnpikes, insurers, utilities. The grant of legal privileges raised the obvious question of what the public received in return. Americans had recently watched a chartered company become entangled with imperial sovereignty on a scale that made the answer impossible to ignore.

Yet the republic would also discover almost immediately that it needed institutions capable of doing things individuals and loose partnerships could not easily do. It needed to finance war debts, mobilize capital, build infrastructure, insure risk, manufacture at scale, and eventually coordinate activity across a continent. The corporate form, stripped of the East India Company’s quasi-sovereign extremity, would become one of the country’s most powerful tools for solving those problems. The distrust never vanished. Neither did the demand for scale.

This tension gives corporate history much of its movement. Americans repeatedly resist concentrated private power and then create conditions under which new concentrations form. They celebrate competition but build networks whose usefulness grows with size. They distrust monopoly but ask companies to undertake projects that require extraordinary capital. They distinguish public from private while constructing markets through patents, charters, land grants, procurement, currency, courts, highways, spectrum allocations, research funding, and tax policy. The argument is not a defect in the system. In many periods it is the system.

The East India Company is therefore less an ancestor of the American corporation than a warning posted near its birthplace. It showed what could happen when commercial scale, political privilege, and state power fused. The companies that follow in this book would rarely possess anything like its formal sovereignty. Union Pacific would not rule territories as the East India Company ruled parts of India. AT&T would not maintain a colonial army. Walmart would not collect taxes. Google would not possess a royal charter. The comparison would be absurd if made literally.

What survives is the problem of dependency. An organization can become so woven into the operation of a society that decisions made inside the company acquire consequences usually associated with public institutions. Prices, access, routes, standards, credit, speech, employment, technical architecture: each can be privately administered while publicly consequential. The more useful the system becomes, the harder exit becomes as a complete political answer.

Tea in 1773 was not yet such a system in North America, but the East India Company had already become one elsewhere. That knowledge was not invisible to colonists. British political debate about the company, reports of fortunes made in India, and controversies over imperial administration were part of the world in which Americans interpreted Parliament’s tea policy. The company represented more than a vendor. It represented the possibility that commercial privilege could become a vehicle of political rule.

There is an irony in what followed. The rebellion that targeted the company’s tea helped create a country that would become extraordinarily fertile ground for corporations. The United States eventually developed a legal and financial environment in which incorporation became far easier than an eighteenth-century royal charter, capital could be assembled at immense scale, and private companies could build systems more pervasive in daily life than the East India Company’s tea trade had ever been in the colonies. By the late nineteenth century, Americans would again fear that concentrated corporate power threatened republican government. They would invent antitrust law in response. By the twentieth, the largest corporations would coordinate armies of employees, global supply chains, scientific laboratories, mass media, consumer credit, and infrastructure. By the twenty-first, a handful of firms would operate communications and computing platforms used by billions of people.

None would be the East India Company. That distinction matters. So does the family resemblance Americans kept noticing.

The choice of the East India Company for the 1770s also clarifies the rule this book will use in every decade. The winner need not be the company with the greatest revenue generated inside the United States. It need not even be domiciled there. It must be the company that best reveals what changed in the relationship between organized capital and American life during that decade. In the 1770s, no domestic enterprise can compete with a company whose product helped turn an imperial constitutional dispute into an event remembered by children two and a half centuries later.

The tea chests at Griffin’s Wharf contained a commodity harvested and processed thousands of miles away, carried through a global commercial system, protected by laws written in London, and offered into a colonial market under terms designed partly to rescue the finances of a politically connected corporation. The men who destroyed them were not conducting a seminar in corporate governance. Their motives differed, and their world did not divide neatly into categories we now call business and government. But they understood enough about the arrangement to know where to strike.

They did not burn Parliament.

They dumped the company’s tea.

Three years later, the colonies declared independence. The new nation then faced a problem familiar to revolutions: it was easier to reject old institutions than to finance the new ones it suddenly required. Armies needed food and pay. Governments needed credit. Promises had to become money, and money had to become trust.

The next company in this story would not be hated as an emblem of foreign empire. It would be built by Americans trying to make a republic solvent enough to survive.
