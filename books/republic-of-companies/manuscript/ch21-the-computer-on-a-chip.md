# The Computer on a Chip

In 1971, Intel placed the central processing logic of a programmable computer onto a sliver of silicon small enough to disappear under a fingertip.

The device was the 4004.

Its commercial origins were almost comically modest compared with its consequences.

The chip grew out of work for Busicom, a Japanese calculator company that wanted a family of electronic calculators. Rather than build many specialized logic circuits for different functions, Intel engineers developed a more general architecture in which one programmable processor could execute instructions stored in memory.

The computer became a component.

That changed who could build one.

The history of the "first microprocessor" is more complicated than a single corporate claim. Other companies and projects were developing highly integrated processing chips around the same period, including military and calculator applications. Texas Instruments pursued related designs. Garrett AiResearch created a multi-chip processor for the F-14's Central Air Data Computer. Definitions of first vary depending on whether the requirement is single-chip, general-purpose, commercial availability, or actual deployment.

Intel's historical importance does not require winning a trivia contest.

The 4004, followed by the 8008 and especially the 8080, helped turn the microprocessor into a general commercial platform.

That platform moved computing out of rooms.

The previous chapter followed IBM as it made large computers into institutional infrastructure. System/360 taught corporations and governments to build durable systems around a common architecture.

Intel attacked the other end of the scale.

What happens when computation becomes cheap enough to embed?

The answer eventually becomes almost everything.

Calculators.

Terminals.

Industrial controls.

Cars.

Medical devices.

Cash registers.

Appliances.

Personal computers.

Network equipment.

Phones.

Sensors.

A microprocessor is less visible than a mainframe because it can disappear inside another product.

That invisibility is its power.

The semiconductor industry's basic economic engine was integration. Put more transistors onto a chip. Reduce the cost per function. Increase speed. Reduce size and power consumption. Repeat.

Gordon Moore, who co-founded Intel after working at Fairchild Semiconductor, had famously observed in 1965 that the number of components that could be economically placed on integrated circuits was increasing rapidly. The exact cadence associated with "Moore's Law" changed over time, but the broader expectation became self-reinforcing across the industry.

More transistors next generation.

More capability.

Lower cost per computation.

Design the factory, equipment, software, and product roadmap around the assumption that improvement will continue.

A prediction became an industrial planning discipline.

That discipline made semiconductors unusual.

Most commodities become more expensive as society demands higher performance.

Computing often became both better and cheaper per unit of capability.

A machine that once required a corporate budget could later fit on a desk.

Then in a pocket.

Then inside a toy.

This cost curve changed the organization of the economy because it allowed automation to enter places where a computer had previously been absurdly expensive.

Intel's role in that transition was not only fabrication.

It was architecture.

A general-purpose processor creates value because software can change what the same hardware does. The physical chip is fixed after manufacture, but instructions can make it operate as part of many different systems.

Programmability converts hardware into optionality.

That is why the microprocessor is more consequential than a specialized circuit performing one task perfectly.

A general processor can enter markets its designers did not predict.

The user supplies the future through code.

This also changes innovation economics.

If every product requires custom hardware, experimentation is expensive. Engineers must redesign circuitry for each new function.

A general processor lowers the cost of trying an idea.

Write software.

Connect memory and peripherals.

Test the system.

The microprocessor therefore transferred part of product development from hardware into software.

That shift enlarged the population capable of innovation.

The personal-computer revolution demonstrated the effect.

The Altair 8800, introduced in the mid-1970s around Intel's 8080 processor, became one of the sparks of the hobbyist computer movement. It was primitive by later standards, but it offered something radical: an individual could plausibly own a programmable computer.

Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote a BASIC interpreter for the Altair and founded the company that became Microsoft.

Apple emerged from the same broader hobbyist ecosystem using a different processor family.

The point is not that Intel single-handedly created the PC industry.

It helped make the industry's cost structure possible.

The processor became a market product available to people outside the giant institutions that had dominated computing.

This is the corporate consequence that earns Intel the decade.

IBM sold computers.

Intel sold the thing other companies could build computers around.

That position can become more powerful over time because it sits underneath brands the consumer sees.

A processor company does not need to predict every successful application if many application companies standardize on its instruction set.

The architecture accumulates software.

Software accumulates users.

Users attract developers.

Developers reinforce the architecture.

The compatibility loop begins again, one layer lower than IBM's mainframes.

Intel would exploit that loop most dramatically in the 1980s and 1990s through the x86 family and its partnership-rivalry with Microsoft inside the personal-computer ecosystem.

The 1970s built the foundation.

The decade also revealed the geography of the new economy.

Intel grew in the region south of San Francisco that would become known globally as Silicon Valley. The area combined semiconductor firms, Stanford University, defense and aerospace demand, venture capital, experienced engineers, supplier networks, and a labor market in which technical people moved among companies.

The local network accelerated knowledge transfer.

An engineer leaving one firm did not necessarily leave the region.

Knowledge walked across parking lots.

That made the industry different from older corporate systems where research was concentrated inside vertically integrated giants.

Silicon Valley's firms competed intensely while drawing from the same ecosystem.

Competition and circulation reinforced each other.

Intel itself emerged from that circulation. Moore and Robert Noyce had been leaders at Fairchild Semiconductor before leaving to found Intel in 1968. Fairchild had itself grown out of an earlier break from Shockley Semiconductor.

The pattern became legendary.

Talent leaves.

New company forms.

Capital follows.

Employees receive equity.

Success produces another generation of founders and investors.

The industry reproduced through corporate fission.

That organizational model would become nearly as important as the chips.

Intel's early business included memory products, and memory was initially central to the company. The later strategic shift away from commodity memory toward microprocessors would become one of the most studied decisions in corporate management.

That later pivot highlights a paradox.

A company can invent the technology that defines its future before understanding that the technology is its future.

Innovation does not arrive with a revenue forecast attached.

The 4004 began as a solution for a calculator customer.

The platform escaped the project.

That is a recurring feature of general technologies.

Electric motors were not important because they made one factory machine better.

They were important because they could be placed almost anywhere mechanical work was needed.

Microprocessors followed the same pattern for information work.

The chip also intensified national-security dependence on semiconductor manufacturing.

Integrated circuits had deep roots in military and aerospace procurement. As chips spread into civilian systems, the country acquired a new kind of strategic vulnerability.

An economy that embeds computation everywhere depends on the capacity to design and manufacture advanced semiconductors reliably.

That dependence would become far more visible decades later as fabrication concentrated in East Asia and governments rediscovered semiconductor industrial policy.

The 1970s began the path.

A general-purpose chip was too useful not to spread.

The environmental and labor costs deserve attention too.

Semiconductor manufacturing requires chemicals, ultra-clean production environments, water, energy, and complex waste management. Early Silicon Valley's clean-looking industrial campuses could hide hazardous exposures and groundwater contamination. Women and immigrant workers filled many fabrication and assembly jobs while engineering leadership remained much less diverse.

The digital economy did not float above industrial labor.

It depended on it.

The processor looked immaterial only because the material work had moved behind clean-room walls.

Intel's strongest runner-ups for the 1970s include Exxon, which became the largest oil company in a decade dominated by energy shocks; FedEx, which reorganized time-sensitive logistics; Southwest Airlines, which demonstrated a new operating model for air travel; and Apple, whose 1976 founding signaled the personal-computer future.

Intel wins because the microprocessor became a general input into all of them.

Oil powered the machine age.

The chip would power the information age.

The crucial corporate shift was from making a complete product to making a programmable layer underneath products.

That layer multiplied.

The 4004 contained only a few thousand transistors.

Later processors would contain billions.

The numerical difference is astonishing.

The institutional difference is larger.

Computation stopped being a destination you went to.

It became something products carried inside them.

Intel did not put a computer on every desk in the 1970s.

It helped make a future possible in which the desk itself would eventually contain several.
