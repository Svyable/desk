# The Brand Man

Neil McElroy’s memo was three pages long.

The management idea inside it would outgrow the paper.

McElroy was a young advertising manager at Procter & Gamble when, in 1931, he wrote an internal memorandum about the problem of managing competing soap brands. P&G already sold multiple products into overlapping categories. The temptation inside a large company is to manage the factory, sales force, and advertising budget from the top, treating individual products as outputs of one organization. McElroy proposed something closer to internal entrepreneurship. Each brand should have a person responsible for understanding its market, sales, advertising, weaknesses, and competitive position. That “brand man” would treat the product almost like a small business inside the larger company.

Procter & Gamble is the company of the 1930s because it helped turn the brand from a name on a package into an organizational unit.

This was a subtle revolution. General Motors had already shown that a large corporation could manage multiple divisions and brands aimed at different customers. P&G pushed similar logic into fast-moving consumer goods where the physical differences between competing products could be smaller, purchases more frequent, and emotional associations more important.

Soap is a humble object.

That made it a sophisticated marketing problem.

A consumer choosing between household products often cannot inspect the manufacturing process. She sees packaging, price, scent, texture, advertising claims, prior experience, family habit, and the reputation of a name. If multiple products clean adequately, the company must create and defend a reason to prefer one.

The brand becomes a bundle of expectations.

P&G had been building brands long before McElroy. Ivory soap, introduced in the nineteenth century, became famous through consistent product identity and national advertising. The company conducted systematic consumer research by the 1920s. It learned that making a product was only one part of the business. Understanding how people used it, talked about it, bought it, and compared it could influence what the company made next.

Consumer research turned the household into a site of corporate inquiry.

A manufacturer had traditionally known production better than consumption. It knew raw-material costs, factory yields, shipping volumes, and wholesale orders. Once goods moved through retailers, the final user could become abstract. Market research tried to close that gap. Companies interviewed consumers, tested products, studied buying behavior, and organized feedback.

The customer began becoming data.

This process would become vastly more quantitative later—scanner data, loyalty cards, credit histories, web cookies, search queries, location signals, A/B tests, and machine-learning profiles—but the managerial instinct was already present. A company can reduce uncertainty by observing users more systematically.

Observation creates better products and more effective persuasion.

The two are difficult to separate.

P&G’s brand-management system institutionalized attention. A brand manager could not assume the product deserved shelf space because it belonged to a large corporation. The manager was supposed to know the category, monitor competitors, evaluate advertising, and fight for resources. Internal competition kept products from disappearing inside bureaucracy.

This is a clever response to the central weakness of scale.

A large company has capital, factories, sales relationships, purchasing power, and research capabilities. It also has distance between decision makers and specific customers. A small company may know one product intimately because its survival depends on it. Brand management tries to give a large company many small-company obsessions without giving up large-company resources.

The cost is complexity.

Multiple brands can duplicate work. Internal managers can compete for the same customers. Advertising budgets can cannibalize one another. Headquarters must decide when two products are genuinely distinct and when the corporation is paying to fight itself. P&G accepted these costs because consumer markets were becoming segmented enough that one universal product did not capture every preference.

McElroy’s memo therefore belongs in the same family as Sloan’s decentralized GM. Both solved complexity by distributing responsibility and then coordinating it through corporate systems. The unit changed from automobile division to consumer brand. The managerial principle traveled.

The decade gave P&G another institution to master: radio.

Radio had turned the American home into a mass-media endpoint. Families could hear entertainment, news, music, advertising, and political speeches transmitted nationally. For consumer companies, this created an extraordinary possibility. A manufacturer no longer had to wait for the customer to encounter an advertisement in a newspaper or store. It could finance the entertainment itself.

P&G and other soap companies sponsored serialized daytime radio dramas aimed substantially at women managing households, a commercially important audience for soaps and household products. The connection became so durable that the genre acquired the name “soap opera.” The term outlived the advertising model that created it.

This is one of the most elegant examples of a company reshaping culture accidentally while pursuing customers deliberately.

The shows were not simply commercials stretched to dramatic length. They offered recurring characters and stories capable of building listening habits. Sponsorship placed the brand inside a relationship between audience and entertainment. The advertiser was no longer interrupting culture from outside. It was paying for the culture to exist.

Modern content marketing, sponsored podcasts, creator partnerships, branded entertainment, and free digital platforms funded by advertising all inherit the logic. Give people something they value; use their attention to support the economics of selling something else.

Google would later make this model computational. Meta would make it social. Television networks made it visual. P&G participated in an early household version where soap paid for narrative.

The 1930s were a particularly revealing decade for this model because the Great Depression had shattered household incomes and business confidence. Companies could not rely on easy prosperity. Consumers became more price-sensitive. Brands needed to justify loyalty in a market where every expenditure mattered more.

Advertising during a depression can appear irrational. Why spend money on persuasion when customers have less money to spend?

Because shrinking demand intensifies the competition for whatever demand remains.

Companies with strong balance sheets can also use downturns to preserve or increase mental availability while weaker rivals cut spending. Brand investment can therefore compound through a crisis if the company survives long enough to harvest the recognition later.

P&G’s consumer staples had another advantage: people continued washing clothes, dishes, homes, and themselves. Demand could fall or shift, but the category did not disappear as luxury purchases might. This made packaged household goods relatively resilient and gave the company room to refine a management system during economic catastrophe.

The Depression also changed the relationship between business and government. Franklin Roosevelt’s New Deal expanded federal regulation, labor rights, financial oversight, public works, and social insurance. Companies adapted to a state more willing to intervene in markets and employment. The 1935 National Labor Relations Act strengthened workers’ rights to organize. The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 established federal wage and hour rules for covered workers.

The managerial corporation was no longer negotiating only with markets.

It was negotiating with a larger administrative state.

P&G’s factories, employees, suppliers, advertisers, and retailers all existed inside this changing legal environment. The company’s brand-management achievement can seem apolitical because a soap package does not look like infrastructure or monopoly. Yet mass consumer companies depend intensely on rules governing labeling, advertising, labor, product safety, trade, taxation, and broadcasting.

The softer the product, the easier it is to miss the state behind the market.

Radio sponsorship itself depended on a federally regulated spectrum. The Communications Act of 1934 created the Federal Communications Commission, which oversaw broadcast licensing. P&G could finance programs reaching national audiences because governments had allocated and regulated the electromagnetic commons in a way that allowed commercial broadcasting networks to develop.

Once again, a private company’s innovation sat on a public architecture.

The brand manager also changed career paths inside corporations. Marketing became a route to general management because it required synthesis across research, product development, pricing, sales, advertising, and finance. McElroy himself would eventually become president of P&G and later U.S. Secretary of Defense. His career symbolized the rise of the professional manager whose expertise was organization rather than ownership.

This is a major departure from the founder-led firms earlier in the book. Rockefeller owned a significant portion of Standard Oil. Henry Ford exercised extraordinary personal control over Ford Motor Company. By the middle of the twentieth century, giant corporations increasingly depended on managers who could rise through internal systems without founding or owning meaningful shares of the enterprise.

Managerial capitalism separates control from entrepreneurial origin.

That separation can make companies more durable. A founder dies; a management system recruits a successor. It can also make companies more bureaucratic. Managers may optimize careers, budgets, and internal politics rather than long-term shareholder or customer value. The corporation develops an internal society with incentives of its own.

Brand management was one way to fight this by making someone’s career depend on a product’s performance.

The system also encouraged product proliferation. If a company can assign managers to distinct brands, it can occupy more shelf space and address narrower preferences. Retailers, however, control physical shelves. This creates a bargaining relationship between manufacturer and distributor. A strong brand can force a retailer to stock it because customers ask for the name. A strong retailer can force a manufacturer to offer better terms because the shelf controls access.

The balance of power between brands and retailers would shift repeatedly across the century.

Sears created private labels and controlled its catalog. Supermarkets created large new channels for packaged goods. Television magnified national brand advertising. Walmart later accumulated extraordinary bargaining power through scale and data. Amazon turned the shelf into a search ranking.

P&G survived these channel changes because the company treated consumer preference as an asset it could carry from one retailer to another.

If a shopper specifically wants Tide, the store needs Tide.

Tide itself arrived in 1946, after the decade covered here, and became one of P&G’s defining brands. Its success came from research into synthetic detergents and from the brand-management machinery that could launch and support a product nationally. The sequence is important. P&G’s 1930s contribution was building the organizational capability before some of its most famous products existed.

Capabilities precede hits.

This is a useful antidote to retrospective product history. We notice the successful detergent, car, computer, or chip and treat it as the cause of the company’s greatness. Often the deeper cause is a system capable of producing, recognizing, funding, and distributing multiple successful products over time.

P&G became one of the world’s great marketing companies because it institutionalized the work of caring which product won.

The company’s research practices also made the consumer part of innovation. Product teams could test performance and preferences rather than relying entirely on executive intuition. This empirical orientation resembles the A/B testing culture of modern internet companies, though the speed and data scale are incomparable. The conceptual move is the same: use observed behavior to make decisions instead of assuming the executive is the customer.

The danger is also the same. What people do in a test is not a complete account of what is good for them. Metrics optimize what they measure. Advertising can exploit insecurity or social pressure. Consumer research can become surveillance as technology makes observation more granular. The discipline of listening to customers does not settle what a company should ask customers to want.

P&G’s 1930s advertising operated in a culture saturated with gender roles. Daytime programs targeted women because household purchasing was coded as women’s responsibility. Advertisements could reinforce ideals of cleanliness, domestic competence, beauty, and social judgment that made product use feel morally loaded. A soap did not merely remove dirt; marketing could suggest it protected respectability.

Brands become powerful when they attach functional products to social anxiety.

That power deserves neither automatic condemnation nor celebration. Advertising can provide information and finance entertainment. It can also manufacture dissatisfaction. The economic achievement is precisely that the company learns to influence meaning around an object whose chemistry alone cannot explain its market share.

## The Brand Manager as an Internal Founder

McElroy’s idea solved a psychological problem inside large organizations as much as an administrative one.

A product owned by everybody can become nobody’s obsession.

The factory cares about efficient production. Sales cares about accounts. Finance cares about margins. Advertising cares about campaigns. Research cares about product performance. Each function can perform well while the brand as a whole drifts because no one person wakes up responsible for the total result.

The brand manager concentrates accountability.

That person does not literally own the brand, but the role tries to recreate some of the urgency of ownership. Sales weakness becomes the manager’s problem. A competitor’s campaign becomes the manager’s problem. Packaging, pricing, distribution, research, and advertising become pieces of one commercial argument rather than disconnected departmental outputs.

This is internal entrepreneurship with a corporate balance sheet behind it.

The model became powerful because it addressed a recurring weakness of bureaucracy: specialization can make every function locally rational while the customer experience becomes globally incoherent.

The brand manager exists to hold the whole in mind.

Modern product managers in technology companies inherit part of this logic. They often own no engineers, designers, salespeople, or infrastructure directly, yet they are expected to coordinate those functions around a product outcome. The vocabulary changed from brand to product. The organizational problem remained.

Who is responsible for the thing the customer actually experiences?

P&G gave that question a job title.

## Radio Turned Attention Into Inventory

A radio network had a finite broadcast schedule.

That schedule could be divided, sold, sponsored, and programmed. Attention therefore became inventory in a literal commercial sense: a company could purchase a recurring place in the listener’s day.

P&G’s sponsorship strategy was powerful because recurrence builds memory differently from occasional advertising. A serialized drama invites the audience to return. The brand financing the drama benefits from the habit even when the program is not a continuous product pitch.

The advertiser therefore invests in a relationship rather than a single impression.

This is structurally similar to the subscription logic later used by media platforms, except the consumer pays with attention rather than money. The sponsor subsidizes the content because the content keeps an audience available.

The more reliable the audience habit, the more valuable the surrounding commercial access becomes.

This changed advertising from a message placed beside culture into part of the financing structure of culture.

The arrangement created obvious tensions. A sponsor paying for a program might care about the content around its brand. Broadcasters cared about audience size and advertiser relationships. Creators cared about stories. Listeners wanted entertainment. None of the parties possessed identical incentives.

Commercial media was therefore born with governance problems that digital platforms would later intensify.

Who controls the content when attention finances the system?

How much influence should the sponsor have?

What happens when the material that attracts the largest audience conflicts with the reputation the advertiser wants?

P&G did not solve those questions.

Its radio strategy helped make them normal.

## The Shelf and the Mind Became Competing Bottlenecks

A packaged-goods company can win the consumer’s preference and still lose if the product is not available at the store.

A retailer can control shelf space and still lose if shoppers demand a brand it refuses to stock.

This creates two bottlenecks.

Physical distribution controls what can be bought.

Mental availability influences what shoppers ask to buy.

P&G’s brand system strengthened the second bottleneck as a defense against the first.

If Ivory or a later P&G brand became sufficiently trusted, retailers needed access to that demand. The manufacturer gained bargaining power because the consumer carried the preference into the store.

This is why brand investment can function like infrastructure for a producer.

A factory can be copied. A formula can sometimes be approximated. Shelf relationships can change. A large stock of consumer memory travels differently. It exists outside the company, distributed across households.

The brand becomes an installed base in people’s minds.

That metaphor is not perfect, but it explains why advertising can create durable economic value despite producing no physical asset on the company’s balance sheet. A familiar name lowers the cost of future persuasion.

The next purchase begins with prior memory.

P&G learned to treat that memory as something managers could cultivate deliberately.

## Why P&G, Not RCA?

The strongest rival for the 1930s is RCA.

Radio transformed mass communication, culture, politics, entertainment, and advertising. A company associated directly with the technology that put a live national voice inside the home has an obvious claim to the decade.

P&G wins for a narrower reason tied to this book’s thesis.

RCA helped build the medium.

P&G showed companies what to do with the medium once it existed.

It combined brand management, consumer research, recurring sponsored content, and professional managerial careers into a system that could survive across changing media technologies.

Radio would give way partly to television. Television would give way partly to cable, streaming, search, social feeds, and creator platforms. The specific distribution channel kept changing.

The corporate capability P&G built was more portable.

Know the consumer.

Own a differentiated promise.

Assign a manager to defend it.

Finance attention where the consumer spends time.

Measure the response.

Repeat.

That operating system would remain legible to a marketing executive decades after the radio set itself became historical furniture.

By the end of the 1930s, Europe was at war and the United States was moving toward a mobilization that would transform industry again. Consumer companies would shift production. Automobile factories would make military equipment. Chemical companies would supply materials. The federal government would become the largest customer many firms had ever seen.

The company of the 1940s would be chosen not for a household brand but for a machine whose development compressed science, production, logistics, military doctrine, and government finance into one industrial system.

Boeing would build bombers on an unprecedented scale, helping turn aerospace into a permanent American industry tied to national power.

P&G had learned to manage attention in the home.

Boeing would learn to manage complexity in the sky.
