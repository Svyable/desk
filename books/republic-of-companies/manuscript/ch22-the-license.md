# The License

IBM built the personal computer that became the standard.

Microsoft owned the operating system relationship that escaped the machine.

That difference made a company.

In 1980 and 1981, IBM was moving quickly to enter the personal-computer market. The company had enormous resources, a powerful brand, and deep experience selling large computing systems to institutions.

What it did not have was time.

The PC team in Boca Raton broke with IBM tradition by using many off-the-shelf components rather than designing every part internally. Intel supplied the processor. Other vendors supplied peripherals. Microsoft supplied software.

For the operating system, Microsoft acquired rights to 86-DOS from Seattle Computer Products, adapted it for the IBM machine, and provided IBM with PC DOS.

The crucial fact was what Microsoft kept.

It retained the ability to license its own version, MS-DOS, to other computer manufacturers.

The license became more important than the first box.

IBM's PC was a commercial success and quickly became a reference design for business computing. Software developers wrote applications for it. Peripheral makers built expansion cards and devices. Corporate buyers standardized around it. The familiar IBM name made the personal computer acceptable inside offices that might have dismissed hobbyist machines as toys.

The market wanted IBM compatibility.

IBM did not own all of compatibility.

That was the opening.

Other manufacturers began producing computers that could run the same software ecosystem. The proprietary IBM BIOS initially presented an obstacle, but compatible manufacturers used clean-room reverse engineering and other approaches to create machines that behaved enough like the IBM PC to run its software without copying protected code illegally.

Hardware compatibility spread.

MS-DOS spread with it.

This gave Microsoft a position that was unusual for an era still accustomed to thinking of the computer maker as the center of computing.

IBM sold the visible machine.

Microsoft sold the layer that could travel from machine to machine.

That layer became the standard.

The previous chapter followed Intel downward into the processor architecture beneath computers. Microsoft occupied another layer above the chip and below the application.

Operating systems coordinate hardware, files, memory, devices, programs, and user interaction. They make a computer usable enough that application developers do not need to rebuild every low-level function themselves.

The operating system is therefore an intermediary.

Intermediaries can become powerful when everyone on both sides depends on them.

Hardware makers need software.

Software developers need an installed base.

Users want machines that run the software they already know.

The operating system connects all three.

Once that loop starts, the company controlling the platform does not need to manufacture the majority of the hardware to shape the market.

That was Microsoft's strategic position.

The power came from licensing rather than exclusivity.

At first glance, licensing software broadly can look like giving away control. A company that owns a product might prefer to sell it only with its own hardware so the entire margin stays inside the company.

Microsoft did not have a hardware business to protect.

That made openness to multiple manufacturers an advantage.

Every new PC maker licensing MS-DOS expanded the potential market for DOS software.

Every new DOS application made DOS-compatible machines more useful.

Every new user made the platform more attractive to developers.

The same architecture could grow without Microsoft financing factories, inventory, dealer networks, and physical assembly at the same scale as the manufacturers.

The license was scalable because reproduction was cheap.

Software economics differ fundamentally from manufacturing economics.

Designing software can be expensive.

Copying the next unit is almost free.

That means a successful software company can spread globally without rebuilding the product physically each time.

The business model rewards standardization.

Write once.

License many times.

Improve the common code.

Collect revenue as the installed base grows.

This model would become one of the defining engines of the information economy.

The IBM relationship gave Microsoft credibility, but IBM's own success also created the ecosystem that weakened its control.

Because the PC used a relatively open architecture and widely available components, compatible manufacturers could compete on price, speed, features, and distribution. Compaq became one of the most important early examples, producing machines capable of running IBM PC software. Other companies followed.

The clone market grew.

IBM had created a standard larger than itself.

This is one of the great strategic paradoxes in corporate history.

A standard can increase the size of a market while decreasing the original creator's control over it.

IBM benefited enormously from making the PC credible and accessible to developers.

The same accessibility allowed competitors to enter.

Microsoft benefited because every compatible entrant could become a new licensee.

Competition among hardware companies strengthened the software platform.

That asymmetry became clearer over the decade.

IBM attempted to regain control with the PS/2 line and the Micro Channel Architecture in the late 1980s. The company also partnered with Microsoft on OS/2, intended as a successor to DOS for more advanced PCs.

But the installed base had become difficult to move.

Customers owned DOS applications.

Developers knew the DOS environment.

PC manufacturers had businesses built around the existing compatible architecture.

Standards acquire constituencies.

Once enough people have invested around them, changing the standard requires persuading an entire ecosystem rather than shipping a better product.

That is path dependence.

Microsoft was learning to exploit it.

Windows began as a graphical operating environment running on top of DOS. Early versions were not immediately dominant. The Macintosh offered a more integrated graphical experience. IBM and Microsoft continued working on OS/2. Other graphical systems existed.

The decisive advantage was not that Windows was obviously superior from the beginning.

It had access to the DOS installed base.

Microsoft could use one platform to build the next.

This is another reason the license mattered.

A company controlling a widely distributed layer can introduce adjacent products to customers already inside its ecosystem.

The user does not start from zero.

The developer does not start from zero.

The distribution channel does not start from zero.

Installed base becomes launch infrastructure.

That would make Windows one of the most powerful software platforms in history during the 1990s.

The foundation was laid in the 1980s through DOS licensing.

The business also changed how corporate America thought about intellectual property.

In an industrial company, value is easy to locate in the factory, inventory, machinery, and distribution network.

In software, the valuable asset may be a legal right attached to code that can be reproduced indefinitely.

The contract determines who can copy, modify, distribute, bundle, and sublicense.

A few clauses can shape an entire market.

That is what makes the title literal.

The License.

The hardware was expensive and visible.

The rights were abstract and durable.

The rights won.

This was not simply a story of Microsoft outsmarting IBM.

IBM had strong reasons for its choices. It wanted to enter the PC market quickly. It did not know exactly how large the personal-computer market would become or which layers would capture the most value. Software still looked like something attached to hardware rather than an industry that could eventually dominate hardware makers in market value and strategic importance.

Hindsight makes the mistake look obvious.

It was not obvious at the time.

That is why the case became a management parable.

The most valuable part of a new system may be the layer nobody initially treats as scarce.

IBM treated hardware design, manufacturing, and brand as central strengths.

Microsoft treated software rights as an opportunity.

As personal computers standardized, hardware became more substitutable.

The software environment became less substitutable.

Value migrated toward the layer with switching costs.

The shift also affected consumers.

Broad hardware competition drove down PC prices and accelerated innovation. Buyers could choose among manufacturers while keeping access to familiar software. Businesses could replace machines without retraining every employee or abandoning every application.

Compatibility created enormous social value.

It also concentrated platform power.

A standard operating system can become a gatekeeper because application developers design around it and users depend on it. The platform owner gains influence over technical interfaces, distribution, default software, developer relationships, and the terms under which competitors reach customers.

The same network effect that helps users also creates antitrust risk.

Microsoft would face that risk directly in the 1990s when the U.S. government challenged its conduct around Windows and the web browser market.

The later case is downstream evidence of the 1980s architecture.

You cannot have a platform antitrust problem before the platform becomes powerful enough to matter.

The DOS era built that power.

Microsoft's rise also changed the geography of the technology industry.

The company moved from Albuquerque to the Seattle area, where it became the anchor of a major technology cluster. The region already contained Boeing and a strong engineering culture. Microsoft's growth attracted talent, suppliers, investors, and later entrepreneurs. Employees who became wealthy through the company would fund new firms and institutions.

One license helped reshape a metropolitan economy.

That is how corporate ecosystems form.

A successful company does not merely create jobs inside itself.

It creates alumni.

Alumni create firms.

Wealth creates investors.

Technical labor markets deepen.

Universities and governments respond.

The local economy becomes better at producing the next company.

Seattle's later importance in software, e-commerce, and cloud computing cannot be explained by Microsoft alone.

Microsoft made the region much more capable of producing those industries.

The 1980s corporate field is crowded. Walmart was building the retail system that would dominate the next chapter. Apple helped define personal computing and graphical interfaces. GE became associated with a new model of shareholder-oriented corporate management under Jack Welch. Financial firms transformed mergers and capital markets. Cable television changed media. FedEx expanded time-sensitive logistics.

Microsoft wins because it changed where value sat inside the computer.

Intel made computation cheap enough to become a component.

IBM made the PC safe enough to become a standard business purchase.

Microsoft made the operating environment portable enough to become an industry standard independent of one manufacturer.

The decisive product was not a box.

It was permission repeated at scale.

One company could build the computer.

Another company could build a compatible one.

Both could pay Microsoft.

That is the business model that defined the decade.

The license was not paperwork around the product.

The license was the product architecture.
