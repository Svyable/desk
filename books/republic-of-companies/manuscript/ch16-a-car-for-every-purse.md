# A Car for Every Purse

General Motors looked badly designed until Alfred Sloan decided the disorder was the design.

William C. Durant had assembled GM beginning in 1908 by acquiring automobile companies and related suppliers at a pace that made the corporation large before it was coherent. Buick, Cadillac, Oldsmobile, and other operations carried their own products, managers, dealers, engineering habits, and identities. Durant was a dealmaker and promoter. He could gather companies faster than he could impose a stable system for governing them. He lost control, regained it, and lost it again.

By the early 1920s, Ford presented the opposite picture. The Model T system was focused, integrated, disciplined, and dominant. GM looked like a portfolio of accidents.

Alfred P. Sloan Jr. saw an organizational opportunity inside the mess.

He became GM’s president in 1923 and helped build a management architecture often summarized as “decentralized operations with coordinated control.” Divisions would retain substantial responsibility for products and operations while headquarters would allocate capital, set broad strategy, compare performance, and coordinate across the enterprise. The idea sounds ordinary because generations of corporations copied it.

In the 1920s, it was a way to make bigness governable.

General Motors is the company of the decade because it solved two problems Ford had made visible: how to manage variety inside one corporation and how to make consumer desire keep moving after basic affordability had been achieved.

Ford’s great achievement was standardization. Sloan’s was segmentation.

GM organized automobile brands into a price ladder, seeking a car for different incomes and stages of aspiration. Chevrolet could compete for mass-market buyers. Buick, Oldsmobile, Pontiac, and Cadillac occupied other positions as the portfolio evolved. A customer who entered the GM system at one price point could imagine moving upward without leaving the corporation.

The company was no longer selling one ideal car.

It was selling a map of the consumer’s future self.

This changed the strategic meaning of product variety. Ford treated variation primarily as manufacturing cost. GM treated carefully managed variation as demand creation. The challenge was to offer enough difference to justify choice without allowing divisions to cannibalize one another chaotically. Headquarters had to define boundaries.

This is portfolio strategy in consumer form.

The organization depended on information. A decentralized division has local knowledge and speed, but headquarters needs comparable numbers to decide where capital should go. Sloan and his colleagues developed financial controls and return measures that allowed central management to evaluate different businesses without running each factory directly. The corporation became a system for governing managers through metrics.

This was a major advance in administrative technology.

U.S. Steel had shown that finance could combine many operating companies. GM showed how management could preserve distinct operating identities while making them answerable to a common capital allocator. The conglomerates of later decades, multidivisional manufacturers, and modern holding companies would all inherit versions of the problem.

The model also changed executive work. The senior leader no longer needed to know every production detail. Headquarters set objectives, reviewed results, appointed managers, and decided investment priorities. Authority shifted from direct supervision toward systems of reporting.

Numbers became a way to manage people you could not see.

That abstraction increases scale and creates risk. A division can look healthy through chosen metrics while neglecting quality, worker safety, customer trust, or long-term capability. Managers learn which numbers headquarters rewards. The system then produces behavior around the measurement. Sloan’s model was powerful because it acknowledged this problem and tried to balance autonomy with review, but no control architecture eliminates gaming or short-termism.

Corporate governance is the art of being wrong at a distance as infrequently as possible.

GM’s second innovation was cultural as much as organizational. The automobile market was moving from first purchase toward replacement and status. A car could be mechanically functional and still feel old. Styling, color, model-year changes, accessories, and brand identity gave consumers reasons to consider something new before the old vehicle ceased to operate.

This practice is often called planned obsolescence, a phrase that can imply engineers deliberately designed products to fail. GM’s strategy was subtler. It cultivated psychological and stylistic obsolescence. The previous year’s car could still run perfectly while looking like last year’s car.

Desire acquired a depreciation schedule.

Annual model changes transformed manufacturing and marketing. Dealers gained events around new introductions. Advertising could emphasize novelty. Used-car values and trade-ins became part of the purchase calculation. Styling departments acquired strategic importance. Harley Earl, whose design work became central to GM beginning in the late 1920s, helped institutionalize automobile styling as a corporate function.

The product’s appearance was no longer decoration added after engineering.

It was one of the things management engineered demand around.

Consumer finance reinforced the system. General Motors Acceptance Corporation had been created in 1919 to finance dealers and retail customers. Installment credit reduced the cash barrier to buying a car, much as Singer’s payment plans had expanded the sewing-machine market decades earlier. The difference was scale and frequency. Automobiles were vastly more expensive, and GM’s model encouraged replacement over time.

The company therefore controlled more of the customer journey: brand, dealer relationship, trade-in, financing, and future upgrade.

A car could be purchased from future income and replaced before the loan-funded usefulness of automobile ownership had emotionally worn out.

This was mass consumer capitalism becoming sophisticated.

The 1920s are often remembered as a decade of consumer credit, advertising, radio, appliances, chain stores, and financial speculation. Household purchases increasingly depended on the expectation of continued wages. Installment debt let families enjoy goods earlier and exposed them to risk if income disappeared. The prosperity of the decade made future earnings feel dependable.

The crash at the decade’s end would reveal how much American consumption had come to depend on that confidence.

GM did not cause the Great Depression. Its success belonged to the system whose fragility the Depression exposed: industrial production capable of extraordinary output, financial markets capable of mobilizing vast capital, and households encouraged to treat tomorrow’s income as a resource available today.

Mass production had solved scarcity faster than society solved stable demand.

Companies responded by becoming experts at desire.

Advertising in the 1920s increasingly shifted from describing utility toward creating associations with status, beauty, health, romance, modernity, and social belonging. GM’s brand ladder fit this culture perfectly. The difference between Chevrolet and Cadillac could be mechanical and material, but it was also social language.

A corporation with multiple brands could speak that language in several registers at once.

This is why brand portfolios became so powerful. Procter & Gamble would later sell multiple soaps and detergents through distinct brand identities. Marriott would operate hotel brands across price segments. Technology companies would sell “pro,” “air,” “max,” and entry products that turn hardware specifications into social positioning. The portfolio lets one corporation occupy multiple identities without forcing customers to confront the common ownership behind them.

GM learned that one company could compete with itself on purpose.

The system helped it overtake Ford in U.S. automobile sales by the late 1920s. Ford eventually shut down Model T production in 1927 to retool for the Model A, an extraordinary admission that the old system had become a constraint. GM’s more flexible portfolio had turned Ford’s focus into vulnerability.

The victory was not permanent. No organizational design stays optimal as markets change. GM would later accumulate bureaucracy, labor costs, brand overlap, quality problems, and strategic inertia. Japanese automakers would challenge its manufacturing methods. The company would enter bankruptcy in 2009 and emerge after federal intervention. Sloan’s management system was not proof against decline.

That later history makes the 1920s more useful, because it shows that a system can be revolutionary in one era and burdensome in another.

Decentralization creates entrepreneurial energy when divisions are distinct. It creates duplication when coordination costs rise. Financial controls sharpen accountability when metrics reflect reality. They produce short-term behavior when metrics become targets. Brand variety expands market coverage until too many brands blur distinction.

Management innovations age like technologies.

GM’s labor system also differed from the consumer image of freedom. Assembly plants remained disciplined, dangerous industrial workplaces. The open-shop resistance to unions would eventually face the rise of the United Auto Workers in the 1930s. The famous Flint sit-down strike against GM in 1936 and 1937 forced recognition of the union and helped establish industrial unionism as a major political force.

The worker and consumer were again two views of the same corporation.

A GM employee might experience the company as foremen, line speed, wages, layoffs, and safety. A Chevrolet customer experienced choice, financing, styling, and mobility. Corporate history becomes distorted when it uses only one doorway.

The company’s size also gave it enormous influence over suppliers, dealers, towns, and national policy. Automobiles increased demand for roads and fuel. Dealership networks distributed corporate presence through local entrepreneurs. Parts suppliers organized around production schedules. Advertising supported media. Consumer finance deepened household debt markets.

GM was an ecosystem before the word became fashionable in strategy decks.

The automobile business also interacted with race and geography. Car ownership could expand personal mobility for Black Americans facing segregated public accommodations and transportation, while highways and later suburban development would contribute to urban disinvestment and segregation through public policies that often benefited white households disproportionately. The Green Book, first published in 1936, existed because automobile freedom for Black travelers came with the need to identify places where they could safely eat, sleep, buy fuel, or receive service.

A mass-market technology does not enter a society evenly.

GM sold cars into the society that existed. The cars then changed what that society could do, including its inequalities.

The company’s 1920s success also depended on petroleum, steel, rubber, glass, finance, and road construction. Ford had already shown how automobiles could reorganize complementary industries. GM expanded the effect by treating the car as a recurring consumer purchase rather than a durable machine acquired once and kept indefinitely.

Replacement cycles create industrial demand even after market saturation.

This principle would become central to electronics. A household can only buy its first refrigerator once, but manufacturers can sell replacements if features, fashion, efficiency, or failure make new models attractive. Smartphones compressed the cycle dramatically. Software subscriptions would go further by converting ownership into continuous payment.

GM’s annual model anticipated the subscription logic psychologically: the product stays current only temporarily.

## Headquarters Became a Capital Market

The most important room at General Motors was increasingly not the room where a car was designed.

It was the room where executives compared divisions.

Decentralization only works if the center can decide which decentralized unit deserves more capital. That requires a common language. Revenue alone is not enough. A larger division will usually produce more revenue than a smaller one. Profit alone is not enough if one unit requires vastly more investment to produce the profit.

Financial controls gave headquarters a way to compare unlike businesses through ratios and returns.

The conceptual leap was profound.

A corporation could contain multiple operating worlds and still govern them through a common financial framework. Cadillac managers did not need to run Chevrolet. Chevrolet managers did not need to run Buick. Headquarters did not need to design every engine. The center needed enough information to decide whether each unit was using corporate capital well.

The corporation became an internal capital market.

That model allowed firms to become larger without forcing every senior executive to understand every local operation in detail. It also created a new source of power for finance inside the enterprise. What can be expressed in comparable numbers becomes easier for headquarters to govern. What cannot be expressed risks losing the argument.

The benefit is discipline.

The danger is that measurement can make unlike things look more comparable than they really are.

A division investing in a long technical transition may temporarily look weak. A mature division harvesting an old product may look efficient until the future arrives. Managers who know the scorecard can optimize the scorecard.

GM did not solve these problems permanently.

It made them manageable enough for the multidivisional corporation to spread.

## Dealers Were the Human Edge of the System

GM’s corporate architecture extended beyond factories and headquarters into independently owned dealerships.

The dealer was simultaneously a local entrepreneur and the face of a national manufacturer. Dealers supplied capital, local market knowledge, service capacity, used-car expertise, and customer relationships. GM supplied products, brands, advertising, financing structures, and a production system no local merchant could reproduce.

This relationship resembled franchising before the fast-food model became famous.

The corporation could reach towns across the country without owning every retail property or employing every salesperson directly. Local businesspeople translated national brands into local trust.

The arrangement also generated conflict because manufacturer and dealer incentives could diverge.

Factories wanted volume.

Dealers cared about local demand, inventory carrying costs, margins, and service relationships. New models could excite customers and create old inventory. Corporate pricing decisions could alter dealership economics. The national company depended on thousands of local balance sheets it did not completely control.

Again, scale required governance across legal boundaries.

The modern corporation increasingly operated through networks of partly independent businesses held together by contracts, standards, finance, and brand.

McDonald's would later make this architecture famous in restaurants. GM was already living a version of it in automobiles.

## Consumer Credit Became a Demand Engine

GMAC did more than make cars affordable.

It made future wages legible to the seller.

An automobile buyer did not need to possess the full price today if the finance system believed the buyer could make payments over time. This enlarged the market immediately and turned repayment behavior into part of the corporation's economic model.

Credit therefore linked factory output to household expectations about the future.

A family confident in employment could buy earlier. A dealer could move more inventory. The manufacturer could sustain higher production. Finance converted anticipated income into current demand.

The same mechanism that smooths consumption also increases fragility when income collapses.

The Great Depression would make that obvious.

A system built around future payments assumes the future arrives close enough to plan. When unemployment surges and asset prices fall, installment obligations do not disappear merely because confidence did.

GM's consumer-finance model was therefore part of the 1920s achievement and part of the decade's broader vulnerability.

The modern economy would embrace this bargain repeatedly because the benefits are immediate and visible.

Cars, appliances, homes, education, credit cards, and later subscription services all invite households to pull future purchasing power toward the present.

GM helped industrialize the practice around one of the century's defining consumer goods.

## Why GM, Not RCA?

RCA is the strongest counterfactual because radio may have changed American culture more rapidly than any automobile company did during the decade.

Radio created simultaneous national attention. It brought news, music, advertising, sports, and political speech into homes. It helped create celebrity at electronic speed and provided the commercial foundation for broadcasting.

If this book were organized around media and culture, RCA would be hard to dislodge.

GM wins because its innovation was portable across industries in a different way.

The company demonstrated how a giant corporation could manage distinct products, distinct brands, decentralized operating units, consumer finance, local distribution, and central capital allocation inside one system.

That architecture became a template for modern management.

RCA changed what Americans heard.

GM changed how large companies could be organized.

The latter is less visible and more directly aligned with this book's argument about companies as operating systems.

The decade’s other candidates remain formidable. General Electric linked electricity to appliances and research. DuPont was becoming a modern chemical company. Ford remained a manufacturing giant even as its Model T formula aged.

GM wins because it combined management, finance, branding, industrial scale, and consumption into one organizational system that other corporations could study and imitate.

Sloan’s achievement was not a car.

It was a way to govern complexity.

That distinction sets up the next decade. The Great Depression would make demand brutally uncertain. Companies could no longer rely on prosperity to carry every brand. Radio, meanwhile, would become the dominant mass medium in American homes. Procter & Gamble would learn to manage brands as if each were its own business and would sponsor serialized daytime dramas that entered households through the new medium.

GM had turned product variety into corporate architecture.

P&G would turn brands into managers’ jobs—and advertising into entertainment.
