How a Handful of Machines, Mines, and Ships Run the Global Economy.

The working thesis is that global scale has stopped being the main source of economic power. A national economy can be enormous, diversified, and technologically advanced and still depend, at some specific step in some specific supply chain, on a single company, a single strait, a single lake, a single mine, or a few hundred people who know how to run a particular machine. The book calls that step a chokepoint and argues that in an interconnected world, controlling one well-chosen chokepoint is often worth more than owning a much larger but substitutable share of the whole economy.

Each chapter starts with a real, dated event — a container ship stuck sideways in a canal, a lake too shallow to fill its own locks, a cable cut on the floor of the Baltic Sea, a mine that went bankrupt and then became a Pentagon shareholding — and uses it to show how concentration formed, who benefited from it, who was hurt by it, and what happens when it breaks. The book is skeptical of both extremes: the free-trade assumption that markets will always route around a blockage, and the alarmist assumption that concentrated infrastructure is inherently fragile. Chokepoints are usually concentrated for a reason, and the reason is often a good one, right up until it isn't.

## Editorial principles

1. **Start with the event, not the theory.** Every chapter opens with a specific date, place, and set of named actors before naming the underlying dynamic.
2. **Name a mechanism, not just a metaphor.** "Bottleneck" and "chokepoint" describe an outcome; each chapter also explains the physical, technical, contractual, or financial reason concentration formed there.
3. **Give both sides of concentration.** Efficiency, specialization, and quality control usually built the chokepoint before geopolitics started exploiting it. Say so.
4. **Use current, checkable figures and flag when they move.** Market shares, export-control lists, and stockpile levels in this book's subject matter change year to year; the manuscript states the reporting date next to volatile numbers rather than presenting them as permanent facts.
5. **Do not manufacture false symmetry.** Some chokepoints are natural (a strait, a lake, a mineral deposit). Some are engineered (a patent thicket, a messaging standard, a licensing regime). The book keeps that distinction visible rather than treating all scarcity as equivalent.
6. **Let institutions and workers share the story with executives.** A tugboat captain, a cobalt digger, and a field-service engineer appear as often as a CEO.
7. **Resist a tidy formula.** Chokepoints differ enough in kind that the book avoids reducing them to one universal law; the final chapter offers a small number of tested heuristics, not a model.

## Continuity motifs

- **Concentration is usually a solved problem wearing a target.** Most chokepoints exist because someone solved a hard coordination, physics, or trust problem so well that everyone converged on their solution.
- **The failure reveals the dependency.** Nobody drew a supply-chain map of the Suez Canal, ASML, or a cobalt mine until each one stopped working.
- **Redundancy is expensive on purpose.** A second supplier, a second route, or a second reserve looks wasteful until the day it is the only thing that works.
- **A chokepoint can be a place, a machine, a molecule, a contract, or a person.** The book deliberately mixes these categories rather than treating physical infrastructure as the only kind of scarcity that matters.
- **Whoever is squeezed writes the account of the squeeze.** Prices, headlines, and policy respond to the buyer's pain; the people doing the underlying work — dockworkers, artisanal miners, field engineers — are usually the last to be quoted and the first to bear the cost.
