# Chapter 9 — The Cartel of Trust

On February 26, 2022, two days after Russian forces crossed into Ukraine, the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom, and Canada issued a joint statement announcing that they would remove selected Russian banks from SWIFT, the Belgium-based messaging network that most of the world's financial institutions use to instruct each other to move money across borders. The announcement was covered, reasonably, as a sweeping and unprecedented financial sanction. It was also, if you looked past the geopolitical drama, a fairly precise demonstration of what a chokepoint made entirely of trust and standardized messaging, rather than physical infrastructure, actually looks like in practice.

SWIFT — the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication — does not, contrary to a common misconception, move money at all. It moves messages: standardized, authenticated instructions that tell a bank in one country to credit or debit an account on behalf of a bank in another. The actual transfer of value happens through a web of correspondent banking relationships — banks holding accounts with each other, or with shared intermediary banks, that let money conceptually cross a border without physically crossing it. SWIFT is, in essence, the common language that lets more than 11,000 financial institutions across over 200 countries and territories tell each other, reliably and in a mutually trusted format, what they want that underlying correspondent network to do. Founded in 1973 by a consortium of European and North American banks specifically to replace unreliable and inconsistent telex messaging, SWIFT's entire value proposition was standardization: it succeeded not because it invented a new way to move money, but because it got a critical mass of the world's banks to agree on one shared format for instructing an old way to move money, and that agreement, once achieved, became almost impossible for any individual institution to walk away from without cutting itself off from the rest of the network.

That is the chokepoint. SWIFT is not a bank, does not hold deposits, and does not have anything close to a monopoly on the technical ability to send a secure financial message between two institutions — alternative messaging systems have existed for decades. What SWIFT has is something harder to build and much harder to dislodge: near-universal adoption. A payment network is only as useful as the number of other parties already using it, and once nearly every bank a company might need to pay or be paid by is reachable through SWIFT, the cost of being the one major institution that insists on using something else becomes, for almost any bank, prohibitively high. This is the same dynamic that makes a dominant social network hard to displace even when a technically superior alternative exists, applied to the far higher-stakes domain of cross-border settlement.

The February 2022 action targeted seven Russian banks in its first wave — including VTB Bank, Bank Otkritie, Novikombank, Promsvyazbank, Bank Rossiya, Sovcombank, and VEB — with two of Russia's largest financial institutions, Sberbank and Gazprombank, initially exempted specifically because European economies remained heavily dependent on Russian oil and gas payments that flowed through those two banks. That carve-out is itself instructive: even a coalition of governments willing to use one of the world's most consequential financial chokepoints as a weapon of war chose, deliberately, not to sever every connection at once, because doing so would have disrupted their own energy imports as severely as it disrupted Russia's financial system. A chokepoint, once you control it, does not eliminate the underlying interdependence between the party wielding it and the party being squeezed; it just gives the wielder a lever to apply selectively. By May 2022, as the EU worked out alternative energy-payment arrangements, Sberbank and several additional institutions were added to the exclusion list as well.

The practical effect on Russian banks cut off from SWIFT was significant but not total, because the messaging network is a convenience layer sitting on top of underlying correspondent relationships, not the only conceivable way to instruct them. Russian banks and their counterparties shifted toward alternatives including China's Cross-Border Interbank Payment System, or CIPS, launched by the People's Bank of China in 2015 specifically to reduce Chinese banks' reliance on Western-controlled financial infrastructure, as well as bilateral arrangements, non-SWIFT messaging formats, and, for some transactions, more manual and labor-intensive methods that had mostly disappeared from large-scale international finance decades earlier. None of these alternatives matched SWIFT's speed, reliability, or reach, and Russian trade finance and cross-border payments became measurably slower and more expensive as a direct result — but the country's financial system did not collapse the way some early commentary predicted, in part because energy exports continued flowing through channels the sanctions coalition had deliberately chosen not to sever, and in part because China, India, and a number of other trading partners were both willing and increasingly equipped to route around the chokepoint rather than enforce it.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it complicates a simplistic reading of financial chokepoints as tools any dominant power can wield indefinitely. Every time a chokepoint like SWIFT access is used as a weapon, it also functions as a demonstration to everyone watching — including countries not currently under sanction — of exactly how much leverage that chokepoint provides, and therefore how much it might be worth investing in an alternative. China's CIPS system, which processed a comparatively modest volume of transactions for years after its 2015 launch, has grown its participant base and transaction volume substantially since 2022, a growth trajectory multiple financial analysts have linked directly to heightened concern, in Beijing and among other governments, about being similarly exposed to a Western-controlled payment chokepoint in some future dispute. The BRICS bloc of major emerging economies — Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa, later expanded to include several additional members — has floated, with varying degrees of seriousness and progress, proposals for a shared cross-border payment mechanism explicitly framed as reducing collective dependence on SWIFT and on dollar-denominated settlement more broadly. None of these alternatives has come close to matching SWIFT's global reach as of the mid-2020s, and building one that does is a coordination problem at least as difficult as the one SWIFT itself solved in the 1970s. But the direction of travel is clear enough that Western policymakers who study financial sanctions, including officials who have spoken publicly about the risk, generally treat SWIFT's dominance as a depreciating asset rather than a permanent one — a chokepoint whose value to whoever controls it erodes a little further each time it gets used.

This is the clearest illustration in the book of a chokepoint built entirely out of standardization and trust rather than physical scarcity, chemistry, or geography, and it is worth contrasting directly with the ASML and TSMC chapters earlier in the book. Nobody can build a second Suez Canal quickly, and nobody can replicate ASML's four decades of accumulated EUV engineering on a shorter timeline no matter how much money is thrown at the problem. A payment-messaging standard, by contrast, is a coordination problem, not a physics problem — hard to displace precisely because everyone is already using it, but not protected by any law of nature or any impossible-to-replicate manufacturing process once a sufficiently motivated and sufficiently large coalition decides the cost of coordinating around an alternative is worth paying. SWIFT's dominance persists not because no one could technically build a competitor, but because, until 2022, no one outside a fairly narrow set of sanctioned states had a strong enough reason to try. Whether that remains true for the next decade is one of the more consequential open questions in this book, because a financial system's chokepoints, unlike a canal's or a mine's, can in principle be redrawn on the timescale of a political decision rather than a geological one — the map just has not caught up yet.
