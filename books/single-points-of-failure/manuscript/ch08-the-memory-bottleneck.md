# Chapter 8 — The Memory Bottleneck

On August 26, 2022, Nvidia disclosed in a securities filing that the U.S. government had informed the company it would need a special license to continue exporting two of its most advanced AI processors, the A100 and the newly announced H100, to China and Russia. The restriction, formalized more broadly by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security two months later in October 2022, marked the opening move in what has become one of the most consequential technology trade conflicts of the decade — and it targeted, specifically, the chips capable of training the large AI models that were, at that exact moment, about to become the most economically significant category of computing hardware in the world.

The chips themselves are built largely by TSMC, using tools from ASML, in the fabrication story told two chapters earlier in this book. But the export-control fight over AI hardware has increasingly centered on a second, less publicly understood component sitting right next to the processor on the same package: high-bandwidth memory, or HBM. A modern AI accelerator like Nvidia's H100 or its successors is not limited primarily by how fast its logic transistors can switch; it is limited by how quickly it can move enormous volumes of data between memory and processor, a constraint engineers call the "memory wall." HBM solves this by stacking multiple layers of memory chips vertically, connected by thousands of microscopic vertical interconnects, and mounting that stack immediately next to the processor die rather than on a separate part of the circuit board — a manufacturing feat considerably harder than it sounds, requiring extreme precision in stacking, bonding, and cooling that only a handful of companies in the world have managed to industrialize.

Those companies are, almost exclusively, three: SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron, all East Asian and American memory manufacturers with decades of accumulated expertise in DRAM production, the ordinary memory technology HBM is built from. Nvidia does not manufacture its own memory and, according to reporting from outlets including Introl and multiple semiconductor-industry analysts, has depended on SK Hynix for something on the order of 80 to 90 percent of the HBM it uses in its highest-end AI accelerators through the mid-2020s, a dependency that makes SK Hynix, a company most consumers outside the semiconductor industry have never heard of, one of the most quietly important suppliers to the entire generative-AI boom. Market-share data compiled by research firms including Counterpoint and TrendForce through 2025 and into 2026 has shown SK Hynix holding a majority share of the overall HBM market — figures in various quarters ranging from roughly 50 to over 60 percent — with Samsung and Micron splitting most of the remainder, and reporting from TrendForce in early 2026 indicated SK Hynix was set to supply roughly two-thirds of Nvidia's orders for HBM4, the next generation of the technology, for its upcoming Vera Rubin AI platform.

This concentration means the export-control fight over AI chips is really, underneath the headlines about Nvidia GPUs, a fight over a supply chain with at least three simultaneous chokepoints stacked on top of each other: the EUV lithography tools needed to fabricate the logic die, concentrated in one Dutch company; the fabrication of the die itself, concentrated overwhelmingly in one company on one island; and the high-bandwidth memory stacked alongside it, concentrated in effectively one South Korean company with two smaller competitors. A government trying to restrict a rival's access to frontier AI compute does not need to control all three chokepoints. Controlling enough of any one of them, at the right moment, can throttle the entire finished product, because a cutting-edge AI accelerator that is missing its memory stack, or that cannot be fabricated at the necessary node, or that cannot be patterned by an EUV-class tool, is not a slightly worse product. It is not a product at all.

The export-control regime born from the original 2022 restrictions has moved in an unusually fast and unusually public sequence of tightenings and loosenings that illustrates how contested this chokepoint has become as a matter of open industrial policy rather than quiet technical negotiation. After the initial A100 and H100 restrictions, Nvidia engineered downgraded variants — the A800 and H800 — specifically calibrated to fall just below the government's technical thresholds, reducing chip-to-chip interconnect bandwidth from 600 gigabytes per second to 400, so it could keep selling into the Chinese market at all. In October 2023, the Commerce Department closed that loophole, tightening the performance thresholds and licensing requirements to cover the A800, H800, and a wider list of chips including lower-end models like the L40S and even certain gaming-oriented processors capable of AI workloads, an expansion that congressional researchers at the Congressional Research Service later documented as a direct response to Nvidia's earlier workaround. Enforcement gaps persisted regardless: in one case reported by CNBC in late 2025, federal authorities charged individuals in an alleged smuggling ring accused of routing more than $160 million worth of restricted H100 and H200 chips to China between October 2024 and May 2025, using shell companies and transshipment through third countries — a reminder that an export-control chokepoint, unlike a physical chokepoint like a canal or a cable, has to be actively policed against a black market that has every financial incentive to route around it.

Then, in a move that surprised much of the industry, President Trump announced in December 2025 that the United States would permit Nvidia to resume exporting its H200 chips — a more capable processor than the originally restricted H100 — to China, reversing, at least partially, three years of steadily tightening restrictions under a different administration. The reversal was reported as tied to a broader set of trade and diplomatic considerations rather than a judgment that the underlying strategic competition over AI compute had eased, and it drew immediate criticism from members of Congress and China hawks within the administration's own coalition who argued it undercut years of accumulated leverage. Whatever one makes of the policy merits on either side, the episode is a useful illustration of a dynamic that recurs throughout this book: a chokepoint built on export licensing, unlike one built on geology or physics, can be loosened or tightened by a single administrative decision, on a timescale of months rather than the years or decades it takes to build or dismantle a mine, a fab, or a cable network. That makes policy-based chokepoints simultaneously more responsive to changing incentives and considerably harder for any company or government on the receiving end to plan around with confidence.

The memory side of this story has its own reversal worth noting, because it shows how quickly the balance of power inside a chokepoint can shift even without any government intervention at all. For years, Samsung had been the larger and more profitable of Korea's two memory giants. The AI-driven HBM boom inverted that relationship: in results reported in January 2026, SK Hynix posted annual profit that exceeded Samsung's for the first time in the companies' history, a milestone widely attributed by industry analysts, including those cited by CNBC, directly to SK Hynix's earlier and deeper bet on HBM production for Nvidia. A memory chokepoint that most consumers have never heard of had, within roughly three years of the ChatGPT-driven AI boom beginning in earnest, reordered the balance of power between two of the largest technology companies in South Korea.

What should a reader take from a chapter this tangled in acronyms and shifting policy? Mainly this: the compute chokepoint underneath the current AI boom is not one thing, and it is not stable. It is a stack of at least three separate, geographically distinct scarcities — Dutch lithography, Taiwanese fabrication, Korean memory — each independently concentrated enough that a shock to any single layer can constrain the finished product, layered underneath a fourth, purely political scarcity in the form of export licensing that a single government decision can tighten or loosen far faster than any of the physical layers beneath it can adjust. Anyone trying to reason about the future of the AI industry by looking only at model architectures or data-center construction announcements is looking one or two layers too high. The actual constraint, more often than not, sits somewhere in this stack of chokepoints, most of which most people trying to predict the industry's trajectory have never had reason to learn the names of.
