# Chapter 2 — The Machine That Prints Money

The machine is roughly the size of a bus, weighs around 180 tons, ships in more than 40 freight containers, takes months to install, and costs, in its newest configuration, somewhere north of $400 million. Only one company on Earth makes it. That company is not based in the United States, China, Japan, or South Korea — the countries most people would guess if asked who controls the technology behind the modern computer chip. It is based in Veldhoven, a town in the southern Netherlands most of its own customers had never heard of before they needed what it built.

The company is ASML. The machine is an extreme ultraviolet, or EUV, lithography system, and it does one job: it projects a pattern onto a silicon wafer using light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometers, thin enough to etch the transistor features — many now smaller than 5 nanometers, or roughly 40 silicon atoms wide — that define the most advanced logic and memory chips in the world. Every leading-edge processor made since the late 2010s, whether it ends up in an iPhone, a data-center GPU, or a fighter jet's targeting computer, was patterned at some stage of its life by a machine that only ASML makes.

This is not market dominance in the ordinary sense of one competitor beating two or three others. In extreme ultraviolet lithography, there are no other competitors. Nikon and Canon, ASML's longtime rivals in older lithography technology, never fielded a working EUV system at all; both companies concentrated their efforts on less advanced tools using longer wavelengths of light, and neither has announced a credible path back into the EUV market as of the mid-2020s. In the broader lithography-equipment market, including older and less demanding machine types, ASML holds something like 90-plus percent share, with Canon and Nikon splitting most of the remainder. In EUV specifically, ASML's share, several industry analysts and trade publications like TrendForce have noted, functions as an effective 100 percent, because there is nothing else to compare it to.

Understanding how one small Dutch company came to own an entire technology stratum requires going back to a decision most large companies of the era refused to make: an all-or-nothing bet on light with almost no forgiveness for error.

Photolithography, the underlying idea, is older than ASML itself and easy enough to describe. Light passes through a mask carrying the pattern of a circuit, is focused through a lens, and exposes a light-sensitive chemical coating on a silicon wafer, in roughly the way a photographic enlarger projects a negative onto paper. Etch away what the exposed chemical protects or fails to protect, and you have carved the first layer of a chip. The whole history of semiconductor progress since the 1960s can be told as a history of squeezing that light into a smaller and smaller wavelength, because a shorter wavelength lets you draw a finer line, and a finer line lets you pack more transistors into the same square millimeter of silicon.

By the 2000s, the industry had pushed visible and ultraviolet light about as far as clever engineering tricks could take it, using a workaround called immersion lithography that put water between the lens and the wafer to bend light further than air alone would allow. Everyone in the industry — Nikon, Canon, ASML, and the chipmakers who bought their machines — knew that the next real jump required abandoning ordinary optics altogether and working instead in the extreme ultraviolet range, at 13.5 nanometers, a wavelength so short that it is absorbed by almost everything, including ordinary lenses and even air. An EUV system cannot use glass lenses at all; it has to use a chain of specially coated mirrors, operating in a near-vacuum, each one absorbing a fraction of the light, in a process reflective enough times over that only about 2 percent of the original light typically survives to reach the wafer. Generating that light in the first place means firing a high-powered carbon dioxide laser at microscopic droplets of molten tin — roughly 50,000 times a second — vaporizing each droplet into a plasma hot enough to emit EUV radiation, then collecting that light with mirrors polished to a tolerance measured in atoms.

ASML did not invent all of these pieces on its own. It assembled them, at enormous and sustained cost, from some of the most specialized optics and precision-engineering firms in the world — most critically Carl Zeiss SMT of Germany, which supplies ASML's mirror optics and is itself a chokepoint sitting one layer beneath ASML's own, and Cymer, a San Diego–based light-source maker ASML acquired outright in 2013 for roughly $2.7 billion specifically to secure control of the plasma-generation technology at the heart of the machine. Development consumed, by ASML's own account and outside industry estimates, more than three decades and over €40 billion when construction, R&D, and the associated supply chain are counted together, much of it spent before the company could be confident the physics would even cooperate. For years, EUV was a running joke in parts of the industry — perpetually five years away, the semiconductor equivalent of nuclear fusion.

It stopped being a joke once the alternative ran out of road. By the early 2010s, chipmakers using older deep-ultraviolet tools were resorting to "multi-patterning" — exposing a single layer of a chip several times, with several separate masks, to fake the resolution a single EUV exposure could achieve directly. Multi-patterning worked, but it was slower, more expensive, and more prone to errors that compounded with each additional pass. TSMC, Samsung, and Intel, the three companies capable of manufacturing at the most advanced nodes, all eventually concluded that EUV was not an optional upgrade but a requirement for staying in the leading-edge business at all. And there was only one place to buy it.

That single fact reorganizes an enormous amount of downstream power. When Samsung and Intel wanted to buy their way into the future of chipmaking, they discovered that customers could not simply outbid one another for a scarce commodity the way they might for oil or wheat; ASML's EUV systems are made in limited numbers per year — Bloomberg and TrendForce reporting on ASML shipments put full-year High-NA and standard EUV unit counts in the dozens rather than the hundreds — and allocated years in advance through customer relationships, not spot purchases. Both companies took the unusual step of taking equity stakes directly in ASML during its development years, hoping a financial relationship would translate into priority in the delivery queue when the machines finally worked. Intel invested roughly €1.5 billion in ASML in 2012 as part of a broader multi-billion-euro co-investment program; Samsung and TSMC made smaller but similarly strategic commitments. This is not how mature capital equipment markets normally function. Airlines do not take equity stakes in Boeing to make sure they get their 737s on time. The fact that three of the most valuable technology companies on Earth felt they needed to is a fairly precise measure of how tightly EUV supply was, and still is, constrained.

The chokepoint has a geopolitical layer as thick as its technical one. Because ASML is a Dutch company selling equipment the U.S. government considers strategically decisive, the machines fall under an unusual overlapping jurisdiction: the Netherlands controls the export license because the company is Dutch, but American diplomatic pressure has repeatedly shaped what that license permits. Beginning in 2019 and tightening through the following years, the Dutch government, under sustained pressure from Washington, restricted ASML's ability to ship its most advanced EUV systems to China at all, and later extended similar restrictions to older but still capable deep-ultraviolet machines. China, which buys enormous quantities of ASML's less advanced equipment for older-node production, has been effectively frozen out of the newest tier entirely — not because ASML wanted to lose a major customer, but because a chokepoint that sits inside one company's factory floor turns out to be reachable by two different governments at once, each with its own reasons for wanting to control what flows through it.

None of this makes ASML villainous, or even unusual in its behavior; the company has spent decades trying to expand output, train more field-service engineers, and reduce single points of failure inside its own supply chain, aware better than almost anyone how precarious its position looks from the outside. What it demonstrates is how a chokepoint forms without any one actor deciding to create one. Every company in this story — ASML, Zeiss, Cymer, TSMC, Samsung, Intel — was solving a real engineering problem as efficiently as it knew how. Nobody set out to build a machine so hard to replicate that entire national governments would eventually treat licensing decisions over it as instruments of foreign policy. They set out to make transistors smaller. The chokepoint was the byproduct of getting extremely good at one very narrow, very difficult thing, at a moment when almost the entire world's digital economy had quietly come to depend on someone doing exactly that.

The next chapter follows what happens after the machine leaves Veldhoven: where those transistors actually get built, on an island that has turned its own indispensability into a form of national security.
