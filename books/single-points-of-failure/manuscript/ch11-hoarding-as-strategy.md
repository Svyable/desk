# Chapter 11 — Hoarding as Strategy

In October 1973, in the days after Egypt and Syria launched a joint attack against Israel to open the Yom Kippur War, the Arab-majority members of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries decided to use their control over a genuinely global chokepoint — oil, at a moment when OPEC's member states controlled the majority of the world's proven reserves and spare production capacity — as a direct instrument of political pressure. They cut exports to the United States and other countries that had supported Israel militarily, and within months the price of oil had roughly quadrupled, from about $2.90 a barrel before the embargo to $11.65 a barrel by January 1974, according to figures compiled by the U.S. State Department's historical archive. Gas lines stretched around city blocks in the United States. The embargo lasted only until March 1974, but the shock it delivered to countries that had built their entire postwar industrial economies on the assumption of cheap, freely available oil left a policy scar that outlasted the embargo itself by decades.

The most durable institutional response to that scar was the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve, created under the Energy Policy and Conservation Act that President Gerald Ford signed in December 1975. The idea was almost embarrassingly simple compared to the geopolitical sophistication of the crisis that inspired it: dig enormous underground salt caverns along the Gulf Coast, fill them with crude oil bought and held by the federal government, and keep enough of it on hand — the reserve's authorized capacity eventually reached roughly 714 million barrels, later expanded on paper to as much as one billion — that a future embargo, war, or hurricane-driven supply disruption could be absorbed by releasing government-held oil onto the market rather than letting prices spike uncontrolled while the country waited for private supply to adjust. It is, in the vocabulary of this book, a chokepoint countermeasure: since the United States could not quickly build a second Persian Gulf or eliminate its reliance on globally traded oil, it built a buffer instead, deliberately holding idle inventory whose entire economic value lay in not being used most of the time.

Buffers like this are strange assets to manage politically, because their success is invisible by design. A Strategic Petroleum Reserve that is never drawn upon looks, to a budget-conscious legislator scanning a balance sheet, like billions of dollars of oil sitting uselessly in the ground, generating no return, costing money to maintain. Its entire value is optionality against a future that has not happened yet, and postponed disasters are notoriously difficult to get political credit for preventing. The reserve has, in fact, been drawn down for genuine emergencies only a handful of times in five decades — most prominently after Hurricane Katrina disrupted Gulf Coast refining capacity in 2005, and again in 2022 when the Biden administration authorized the largest release in the reserve's history, roughly 180 million barrels, to blunt a global oil-price spike following Russia's invasion of Ukraine — and used more routinely, and more controversially, for smaller releases tied to shorter-term price or budgetary considerations. Critics on both the political left and right have periodically argued the reserve is undersized, oversized, mismanaged, or used too casually for reasons that have little to do with genuine strategic emergencies. What almost no one across that political range has seriously argued, across five decades, is that the underlying concept — hold a buffer against a chokepoint you cannot control — was a mistake.

That same logic has, somewhat belatedly, begun extending well beyond oil, driven directly by the chokepoints described in the earlier chapters of this book. The United States' National Defense Stockpile, a considerably older and historically much less prominent program than the petroleum reserve, dating to a 1939 law passed in the shadow of an approaching world war, exists to hold government reserves of materials considered critical to defense production — a list that by the mid-2020s explicitly included copper, nickel, lithium, antimony, and all sixteen elements commonly classified as rare earths. For most of the stockpile's post–Cold War history, it had been allowed to shrink and, in the view of a 2024 U.S. Government Accountability Office review, had not kept pace with an increasingly obvious set of supply-chain vulnerabilities in exactly the categories of material discussed in this book's rare-earths and cobalt chapters. That changed abruptly in the mid-2020s: the fiscal year 2024 National Defense Authorization Act newly authorized multi-year procurement contracts for domestically processed critical minerals, and subsequent legislation — including a 2025 reconciliation bill that appropriated $2 billion specifically to expand the stockpile through fiscal year 2025, alongside a separate $5 billion Industrial Base Fund and $1 billion in additional Defense Production Act financing authority extending through 2027 — represented, by the account of trade publications tracking defense procurement policy, the most significant expansion of American strategic stockpiling in critical minerals in decades.

The most striking illustration of how far this shift has gone is the deal, described in the rare-earths chapter, in which the Department of Defense became the largest shareholder of MP Materials in July 2025. A government taking a direct equity position in a mining and processing company, rather than simply buying and storing the finished material the way the petroleum reserve buys and stores oil, represents a meaningfully different and more aggressive form of chokepoint countermeasure: instead of insuring against a supply disruption after the fact by holding a buffer, the government is now directly subsidizing and partially owning the creation of an alternative supply chain before a disruption forces the issue. Whether this approach proves more durable than simple stockpiling — whether, for instance, MP Materials can actually reach cost parity with Chinese-processed rare earths without permanent government support, or whether the equity stake is a temporary bridge toward a genuinely competitive private-sector alternative — is not yet resolved, and honest accounts of the policy should say so rather than declaring victory before the company has demonstrated it can compete unsubsidized.

Stockpiling has an underappreciated dark side that earlier chapters have already brushed against without naming directly: hoarding by one actor can itself trigger the very instability the hoarding was meant to guard against. When China restricted rare-earth exports amid a 2010 dispute with Japan, the resulting price spike incentivized both stockpiling and substitution efforts around the world that arguably did more long-term damage to China's market position than the original dispute achieved in leverage. When governments and private firms alike, spooked by the memory chapter's account of AI-chip export controls, begin stockpiling chips, memory, or rare-earth magnets defensively, that stockpiling itself absorbs supply that would otherwise go to ordinary commercial buyers, tightening the market and validating everyone else's fear enough to accelerate their own stockpiling in turn — a feedback loop economists studying commodity markets have long recognized as a self-fulfilling scarcity spiral, distinct from and sometimes worse than the original supply shock that triggered it.

This is the tension at the heart of any strategy built around buffers rather than fixing the underlying chokepoint: a stockpile is a hedge against a chokepoint failing, not a repair of the chokepoint itself. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve did not make the United States less dependent on globally traded oil; it made a specific kind of short-term price shock more survivable while that dependence persisted for another half-century. The 2020s critical-minerals stockpiling wave, similarly, buys time and reduces the odds of an acute crisis, but it does not, by itself, rebuild the separation chemistry, the fabrication yield, or the decades of accumulated engineering knowledge that made China's rare-earth processing or Taiwan's chip fabrication so hard to replace in the first place. Whether a government spends that bought time actually rebuilding the underlying capability, the way MP Materials and the Arizona TSMC fabs represent early, still-unproven attempts to do, or simply spends it feeling less anxious until the next crisis arrives, is a political choice the stockpile itself cannot make. It can only buy the room in which that choice gets made.
