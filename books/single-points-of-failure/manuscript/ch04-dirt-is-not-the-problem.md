# Chapter 4 — Dirt Is Not the Problem

Mountain Pass, California, sits in the Mojave Desert about an hour southwest of Las Vegas, and for most of the second half of the twentieth century it was the largest source of rare-earth elements on the planet. From the 1960s through the 1980s, the mine supplied the bulk of the world's rare earths — the group of seventeen chemically similar elements used in everything from color television phosphors to the powerful magnets that make modern electric motors, wind turbines, and precision-guided weapons possible. If the story of rare earths were only a story about where they are found in the ground, it would be, at least in part, an American story.

It is not, and the reason why is the actual subject of this chapter. Rare earths are not, despite the name, especially rare in the earth's crust; several of them are more abundant than copper or lead. What is scarce is the ability to separate them cheaply, safely, and at scale, because rare-earth ore does not come out of the ground as seventeen convenient, distinct elements. It comes out bound together in complex minerals, chemically similar enough to each other that separating one rare earth from its neighbors requires hundreds of repeated chemical extraction steps, historically using solvents and acids that produce large volumes of toxic and mildly radioactive waste, because rare-earth ores are almost always found alongside thorium and uranium.

Mountain Pass discovered this the hard way. Through the 1990s, a series of wastewater pipeline leaks released radioactive and heavy-metal-contaminated fluid into the surrounding desert, drawing sustained regulatory scrutiny from California and federal environmental agencies. Unocal, which then owned the mine, suspended mining operations in 2002 rather than absorb the cost of the environmental remediation and modernized processing infrastructure regulators were demanding. China, over the same two decades, had been doing the opposite: building out rare-earth separation capacity aggressively, with looser environmental enforcement, state subsidies, and — as importantly as either of those — a willingness to treat the toxic byproducts of separation chemistry as an acceptable cost of controlling a strategically valuable industry. By the time Mountain Pass went quiet, China supplied the overwhelming majority of the world's rare earths, and Western industry, having lost the capability at home, simply adjusted to sourcing from China as though that had always been the natural order of things.

It had not been, and someone eventually tried to reverse it. In 2008, a company called Molycorp acquired the dormant Mountain Pass site and launched what it called Project Phoenix, a roughly $1.7 billion effort to modernize the mine and rebuild American rare-earth processing capacity from the ground up. The timing looked shrewd at first: rare-earth prices spiked dramatically around 2010 and 2011, after China tightened export quotas amid a diplomatic dispute with Japan, and Molycorp's stock briefly made it one of the more closely watched mining companies in the country. The spike did not last. Global rare-earth prices collapsed as China's export policy eased and as the high prices themselves incentivized new supply and substitution efforts worldwide; Project Phoenix ran over budget and underperformed its projected extraction rates, and Molycorp filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2015, one of the more visible cautionary tales in American industrial policy of the past two decades. A mine that had once supplied most of the free world's rare earths went dark for a second time, this time from financial rather than environmental failure.

The lesson usually drawn from Molycorp's collapse — that America simply lacks the rare-earth deposits China has — gets the geology backward. The lesson its collapse actually supports is that mining the ore was never the hard, capital-intensive, or strategically decisive part of the chain. Separation chemistry was, and China had spent thirty years building an integrated ecosystem of separation plants, magnet manufacturers, and downstream customers that no amount of raw ore, by itself, could compete with. As of the mid-2020s, industry estimates commonly cited by trade and policy analysts put China's share of global rare-earth ore mining at roughly 60 to 70 percent, but its share of global rare-earth refining and separation capacity considerably higher — figures circulating in trade press and government analyses put Chinese control of refining capacity in the low-to-mid 90-percent range, and its share of rare-earth magnet manufacturing even higher still, often cited above 90 percent. The gap between those two numbers is the entire chapter. China does not dominate rare earths because it has most of the dirt. It dominates rare earths because it has almost all of the chemistry, and chemistry, unlike ore bodies, cannot be discovered by drilling somewhere else.

A company called MP Materials, formed by a group of investors led by James Litinsky, bought the abandoned Mountain Pass site out of Molycorp's bankruptcy in 2017 and has spent the years since trying to build the missing half of the supply chain domestically — not just mining rare-earth ore again, but constructing the separation and magnet-manufacturing capacity that Molycorp never got working reliably. The effort has drawn direct government backing on a scale that has few recent precedents in American industrial policy: in July 2025, the U.S. Department of Defense announced what it called a "transformational" public-private partnership with MP Materials, under which the Pentagon took a direct equity stake in the company — reportedly becoming its largest shareholder — alongside financing commitments intended to guarantee a domestic supply of rare-earth magnets for defense applications. It is worth pausing on how unusual that is. The U.S. government does not, as a rule, become the largest shareholder of a mining company. That it did so here is a fairly direct signal of how seriously Washington now treats a supply chain that most Americans had never thought about until car and defense manufacturers began warning, publicly, that they were running low on magnets.

That warning has already been tested. China has demonstrated, more than once in the 2020s, a willingness to use its control of rare-earth exports as diplomatic and trade leverage — restricting exports of specific rare-earth elements and, at times, of the magnets made from them, in response to tariff disputes and technology export controls imposed by the United States and its allies. Because there is no meaningful alternative supplier of separated, magnet-grade rare earths at scale outside China, these restrictions do not function like an ordinary trade dispute in which the affected buyers can simply source from a competitor. They function more like a valve at the single narrowest point in a pipe supplying nearly every advanced manufacturing sector in the world at once — electric vehicles, wind turbines, precision-guided munitions, MRI machines, and the very lithography and chip-manufacturing tools described in the previous two chapters, several of which depend on rare-earth magnets and optical components.

There is a broader pattern worth naming here, because it recurs in the chapters ahead on cobalt and on strategic stockpiles: raw material abundance and processing concentration are frequently different stories entirely, and policy conversations that focus on "who has the reserves" often miss where the real chokepoint sits. The United States, Australia, and several other countries have identified substantial rare-earth deposits of their own; reserves are not, in the aggregate, the scarce input. What is scarce, and what took China three decades of sustained investment, tolerance for environmental cost, and integration across mining, refining, and manufacturing to build, is the capability to turn that ore into a usable, separated, magnet-grade material at a price the rest of the world's manufacturers can actually afford to pay. Rebuilding that capability outside China, as MP Materials and a small number of similar efforts in Australia, Estonia, and elsewhere are now attempting, is less a mining project than an industrial-chemistry project, and industrial-chemistry projects of this kind have historically taken a decade or more to reach real scale, assuming the economics hold together long enough for investors to stay the course — the exact assumption that broke Molycorp the first time prices fell.

Dirt, in other words, was never the problem. It was always the easiest part of the chain to find, and the hardest part to notice was missing.
