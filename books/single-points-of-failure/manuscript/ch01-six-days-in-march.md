# Chapter 1 — Six Days in March

At 7:40 in the morning on March 23, 2021, a container ship called the *Ever Given* was moving north through the Suez Canal in a sandstorm, drawing high winds against a hull the length of four football fields, when its bow swung to the right and drove into the eastern bank. The stern swung the other way and lodged against the west bank. For a few minutes it might have looked like an ordinary grounding, the kind that gets a tug and an apologetic captain's log entry. It was not. The ship was wedged diagonally across the entire width of the canal, and it would not move again for six days.

The *Ever Given* was 400 meters long and 59 meters wide, one of the largest container ships afloat, chartered by the Taiwanese carrier Evergreen and registered in Panama. The Suez Canal at that point is roughly 200 meters wide. A ship built to be efficient — to move as many containers as possible per crew member, per transit, per dollar of fuel — had become, by an accident of wind and draft, a 224,000-ton cork in a bottle that carries about 12 percent of world trade.

What happened next is the part worth sitting with, because it is the whole argument of this book compressed into one week.

Roughly 400 other ships backed up at both ends of the canal within days, carrying everything from live sheep to crude oil to sneakers destined for European shelves. Lloyd's List, the shipping industry's trade publication, estimated that the canal's westbound and eastbound traffic together represented something on the order of $9.6 billion a day passing through that one channel. Estimates of the direct cost varied by who was counting and what they were counting — the Suez Canal Authority itself was losing an estimated $14 to $15 million a day in transit fees, insurers and shippers were absorbing costs some analysts put between $18 million and $69 million a day, and the German insurer Allianz put the total drag on the global economy at up to $10 billion a week. The exact number matters less than the fact that serious people who study global trade for a living could not agree on it within an order of magnitude. Nobody had a clean model for what happens when the canal simply stops, because it essentially never had.

The canal is not blocked by an accident like this very often, which is itself worth noticing. Roughly 19,000 ships pass through it in an ordinary year, more than 50 a day, and the vast majority of them do so without incident. The infrastructure works. The pilots who guide large vessels through the channel work. The whole system had absorbed decades of larger ships, more traffic, and tighter schedules without a comparable failure. That is not a coincidence and it is not luck; it is what a functioning chokepoint looks like from the inside; a piece of infrastructure so reliable that shippers stop pricing in the possibility of its failure at all, and then are surprised when the possibility they had priced at nearly zero turns out not to be zero.

The word "chokepoint" originally described a narrow passage of land or water where a smaller force could hold off a larger one — a mountain pass, a river crossing, a strait a fleet has to sail through single file. Military planners have used the term for the Strait of Hormuz, the Strait of Malacca, and the Bosphorus for as long as there have been navies to worry about them. This book borrows the geography but widens the category. A chokepoint, as it is used here, is any point in an economic system through which an unusually large share of value has to pass, and for which there is no fast substitute. It can be a physical passage like the Suez Canal. It can also be a company that makes a single kind of machine nobody else can build, a country that refines a mineral nobody else refines at scale, a lake that has to hold enough water to fill a set of locks, a cable on the seafloor, a handful of people who know how to service one piece of equipment, or a messaging network that most of the world's banks agree to use. What all of these share is not their physical form. It is the shape of the risk: enormous efficiency most of the time, and enormous, hard-to-substitute exposure on the day something goes wrong.

The economist's word for market concentration is monopoly, or in gentler cases, oligopoly, and there is a large and useful literature about what firms do with pricing power once they have it. That literature is relevant to several of the chapters ahead. But "monopoly" describes an actor's position in a market. "Chokepoint" describes something slightly different: a position in a network. A monopolist can raise prices. A chokepoint can also simply fail — through an accident, a drought, a strike, a war, a policy decision, or old equipment reaching the end of its service life — and take an enormous amount of otherwise-unrelated economic activity down with it, whether or not anyone intended to exercise power at all. The *Ever Given* did not choke the world economy on purpose. Its owners had every incentive to keep it moving. The chokepoint failed the same way a light switch fails: not because someone wanted the room dark, but because a single point in the circuit stopped conducting.

The ship was eventually freed on March 29 by a combination of dredgers removing an estimated 30,000 cubic meters of sand and mud from around the hull, tugboats, and a spring tide that raised the water level enough to help float it off. The backlog took roughly another ten days to clear. Egypt's government, whose canal authority collects several billion dollars a year in transit fees and for whom the canal is a strategic national asset in the most literal sense, later impounded the ship in an Egyptian port for more than three months over a compensation dispute before Evergreen's insurers and the canal authority settled for an undisclosed sum reported to be in the hundreds of millions of dollars.

The rest of this book is not about shipping. It is about a pattern the *Ever Given* made unusually visible, because a blocked canal is easy to photograph and impossible to ignore, that recurs in places most people never see: a single company in the Netherlands that makes the only machines capable of printing the smallest transistors in the world; an island ninety miles off the coast of China that fabricates the overwhelming majority of the most advanced chips on Earth; a set of chemical separation plants, concentrated almost entirely in one country, without which rare-earth ore is just expensive dirt; a lake in Panama that has to be full enough, in a given year, to keep an entirely different and much older canal open; a few dozen specialized ships that are the only vessels on the planet capable of repairing the cables that carry practically all of the world's intercontinental data; a province in central Africa that produces most of the cobalt in every lithium-ion battery; a handful of memory-chip factories without which the largest AI models in the world cannot be trained at all; a messaging network that most of the world's banks rely on to move money across borders; and a small, aging, and increasingly contested population of engineers who know, in granular practical detail, how to keep some of this machinery running.

None of these chokepoints exists because someone drew a map and decided to concentrate risk there. Each one exists because, at some point, concentrating that activity was simply the best available answer to a hard problem — a problem of physics, of cost, of trust, of specialization, or of geography that nobody had a cheaper way to solve. The book's argument is not that concentration is a mistake to be corrected. It is that concentration is usually a rational, often admirable, solution to a real problem, and that the same concentration which makes a system efficient nine years out of ten is exactly what makes the tenth year so expensive. Efficiency and fragility, in a chokepoint, are not opposing forces to be traded off against each other. They are frequently the same fact, described from two different distances.

There is a second, quieter pattern worth naming before the *Ever Given* recedes into the rearview mirror: how differently a single week of disruption looks depending on where you are standing in the chain. Retailers in Europe and North America who were waiting on furniture, electronics, and clothing experienced March 2021 as a headline and, at worst, a delayed delivery date. The approximately 25 crew members aboard the *Ever Given*, most of them Indian nationals, spent months confined to the ship under investigation, unable to go home, while lawyers in London and Cairo argued over liability. The canal pilots, the tugboat operators, and the dredging crews who did the actual work of freeing the ship did so under enormous public pressure and very little public attention once the ship was moving again. Chokepoints generate very asymmetric stories. The disruption is reported from the point of view of whoever was inconvenienced by it, which is usually the buyer, the shareholder, or the country that imports the most. The people physically closest to the chokepoint — running it, maintaining it, digging it, or crewing it — are almost never the ones whose account of the event becomes the official one. That asymmetry recurs throughout this book and is worth watching for.

By the time the canal reopened, insurers had begun modeling scenarios they had not seriously modeled before, shipping lines had started quietly asking whether a second, wider east–west route was worth the investment it would take to build, and a piece of infrastructure that had operated in relative obscurity for a century and a half had become, briefly, the most closely watched 120 miles of water on Earth. Six days is not very long. It was long enough to demonstrate, in public and in real time, what happens to a $9.6-billion-a-day artery when the ship in it stops being a ship and becomes, for a week, the only variable that matters.
