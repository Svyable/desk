# Chapter 5 — The Lake That Runs Dry

The Panama Canal does not run on seawater. This surprises most people who have not studied it closely, including, for most of their lives, the people who ship goods through it every week without thinking much about how the locks actually work. The canal is a freshwater system. Every time a ship passes through one of its locks, an enormous volume of fresh water — drawn from an artificial reservoir called Gatun Lake, formed in the early twentieth century by damming the Chagres River — is used to raise or lower the vessel, and that water then drains out to sea rather than being recycled. A single transit by a large ship can use roughly 50 million gallons of fresh water, and in an ordinary year the canal handles somewhere in the range of 13,000 to 14,000 transits. The canal's constraint, in other words, was never really steel, engineering, or geopolitics in the way the Suez chokepoint is. It was rain.

That constraint stayed mostly invisible for over a century because Panama's climate reliably delivered enough of it. Panama sits in one of the wettest regions on Earth, and Gatun Lake's watershed had, historically, refilled itself dependably enough that the canal's operators rarely had to think about water scarcity as an operational risk. Then, beginning in 2023, an unusually intense El Niño weather pattern combined with what climate researchers including analysts at Woodwell Climate Research Center have described as a broader long-term drying trend to produce the driest year on record for the canal's watershed since rainfall measurements there began, with Gatun Lake's water levels falling to their lowest point since at least 1965.

The Panama Canal Authority responded the way any operator of a chokepoint has to respond when its core physical input runs short: by rationing. Starting in mid-2023 and deepening through early 2024, the authority progressively cut the number of vessels permitted to transit each day and restricted the maximum draft — the depth ships are allowed to sit in the water, which determines how much cargo they can carry — for the ships that were still allowed through. Daily transits fell from a normal range of 36 to 38 vessels in July 2023 to as few as 18 per day by February 2024, according to data reported by the U.S. Energy Information Administration and multiple shipping-industry trackers. Container ships, which require the deepest and most water-intensive lock cycles to move efficiently, were hit especially hard: monthly container-vessel transits fell from a pre-drought range of roughly 300 to 330 down to fewer than 250 at points during the restriction. Across the Panama Canal Authority's full 2024 fiscal year, which ran from October 2023 through September 2024, total transits fell 29 percent compared with the prior year, from 12,638 down to 9,936, according to the authority's own reporting.

The effects rippled outward in ways that had little to do with Panama itself. Shippers facing months-long waiting lists or the prospect of turning away cargo altogether began rerouting vessels around the southern tip of South America, through the Strait of Magellan or around Cape Horn — a detour that can add ten days or more to a voyage between the U.S. Gulf Coast and Asia. Liquefied natural gas and liquefied petroleum gas carriers, many of them too specialized to easily reroute and too large to fit the canal's reduced draft restrictions, saw shipping costs and delivery delays rise sharply enough that the EIA's *Today in Energy* newsletter dedicated repeated coverage to the canal's effect on U.S. energy exports during the drought's peak. Auction-style booking systems the canal authority introduced to allocate the scarce transit slots among competing shippers pushed premium transit fees for guaranteed passage into the hundreds of thousands of dollars per ship at the worst of the crunch, on top of ordinary tolls.

What makes the Panama Canal's 2023–2024 crisis different from the *Ever Given*'s six days in the previous canal chapter is not the severity — the disruption was, in aggregate tonnage terms, considerably larger and lasted the better part of a year — but the cause. Nobody grounded a ship. Nobody made a policy decision to restrict a rival's access to strategic infrastructure. A chokepoint failed because the climate that had reliably supplied its single physical input for more than a century stopped being reliable, and there was no quick engineering fix for a shortage of rain. The canal authority had, to its credit, been investing for years in water-saving basins that recycle a portion of each lock's water rather than discharging all of it to sea, and it accelerated broader watershed-management and even desalination studies once the drought made the risk undeniable. But those investments operate on a timescale of years to decades, while a drought can arrive and deepen within a single dry season.

Water levels began recovering in early 2024 as rainfall returned, and by August 2024 the canal authority had lifted most restrictions and restored close to normal transit levels; by 2025 traffic had substantially recovered, though canal officials and outside analysts, including Woodwell's researchers, have been explicit that the underlying long-term drying trend in the region makes a comparably severe future drought a matter of "when," not "if." Panama has since begun a considerably more ambitious set of long-term projects, including proposals for a new reservoir on the Indio River intended to supplement Gatun Lake's capacity, to reduce the odds of the 2023–2024 scenario repeating at the same severity.

The deeper point of this chapter is not really about Panama's rainfall, though the rainfall is the immediate mechanism. It is about a category of chokepoint the previous two chapters did not really contain: a chokepoint whose scarce input is not a manufactured technology or a mineral deposit but an ecological cycle that most of the system's users had implicitly assumed was a constant rather than a variable. The Suez chokepoint depends on a ship not running aground in a channel that has existed, in more or less its current form, since 1869. The Panama chokepoint depends on a specific volume of rain falling on a specific watershed in a specific window of time, year after year, which is a very different kind of dependency to build a trade artery on top of that, in a normal year, carries something on the order of 6 percent of global maritime trade by the canal authority's own account. Climate volatility does not respect the assumptions that infrastructure planners made when they built a system in a climate regime that, at the time, looked stable enough to plan around for a century.

This matters for the rest of the book because several of the chokepoints ahead — the cobalt supply chain in the Democratic Republic of Congo, the undersea-cable network, even the talent pipeline behind machines like ASML's EUV systems — sit at the intersection of a hard engineering or economic constraint and a softer, slower-moving environmental or social one that planners have historically treated as background noise rather than a variable that can move fast enough to matter within a single fiscal year. The Panama Canal's drought is the clearest example in this book of what happens when that assumption turns out to be wrong at exactly the moment nobody had built in the redundancy to absorb it.
