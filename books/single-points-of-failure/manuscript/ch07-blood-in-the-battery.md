# Chapter 7 — Blood in the Battery

Siddharth Kara spent parts of 2018 through 2021 traveling through the Democratic Republic of Congo's Lualaba and Haut-Katanga provinces, documenting what he later called, in the title of his 2023 book, *Cobalt Red* — a phrase meant to invoke both the metal's industrial color and the human cost of extracting it. Kara, a researcher affiliated with Harvard's T.H. Chan School of Public Health and its Kennedy School, described conditions in the artisanal cobalt-mining sector that read, in his account and in the reporting of journalists and human-rights organizations who have covered the same region for years, less like a modern industrial supply chain and more like a nineteenth-century extraction economy operating underneath the branding of a green-technology revolution: hand-dug tunnels prone to collapse, workers including children digging with rudimentary tools for wages of a few dollars a day, and toxic dust exposure from ore that frequently carries uranium alongside cobalt.

The reason any of this touches a reader who has never been near central Africa is straightforward. Cobalt is a key component of the cathodes used in most lithium-ion batteries — the batteries in smartphones, laptops, and, at far larger scale, electric vehicles and grid storage systems — because it stabilizes the battery's crystal structure and allows it to be charged and discharged repeatedly without the capacity loss or fire risk that some cobalt-free chemistries have historically struggled to avoid entirely. The Democratic Republic of Congo, according to figures from the International Energy Agency, the U.S. Geological Survey, and the industry-funded Cobalt Institute, accounted for somewhere between two-thirds and roughly three-quarters of global cobalt production through the mid-2020s — most estimates for 2024 clustering around 70 to 78 percent. No other country is remotely close. Indonesia, Russia, and Australia produce meaningful but much smaller shares, and no credible near-term substitute source exists at the volume the battery industry now requires.

Within that overwhelming national share sits a further, more uncomfortable concentration. The large majority of DRC cobalt is mined industrially, at highly mechanized sites operated by major mining companies including the Chinese firm CMOC and Swiss-based Glencore, under conditions that meet most international labor and safety standards. But a meaningful minority — estimates from organizations including the World Economic Forum and academic researchers publishing in journals like *PNAS* have generally placed the artisanal and small-scale mining share at somewhere between 15 and 30 percent of the DRC's total cobalt output, which itself translates to something like 10 to 15 percent of the entire world's cobalt supply — is dug by hand by artisanal miners working outside formal safety regulation, frequently on land adjacent to or even encroaching on industrial concessions. It is this artisanal segment that has drawn the sustained attention of investigative reporting, including Kara's, because it is where child labor, fatal tunnel collapses, and the most severe occupational hazards concentrate most heavily. Formalization efforts by the Congolese government and international buyers — including a state-backed entity called Entreprise Générale du Cobalt, created in 2020 to try to bring artisanal mining under a more traceable and regulated supply chain — have made only partial progress, and researchers who study the sector, including those cited by the Wilson Center and the International Institute for Environment and Development, have generally described formalization as a slow, underfunded, and still largely unfinished project.

The industrial buyers at the top of this supply chain — battery manufacturers, automakers, and electronics companies — have spent the years since reporting like Kara's gained wide attention building elaborate traceability and responsible-sourcing programs, third-party audits, and blockchain-based chain-of-custody pilots intended to certify that cobalt reaching their factories did not pass through the worst artisanal sites. Independent researchers and journalists have repeatedly found reasons to be skeptical of how completely these programs actually work in practice, because cobalt from artisanal and industrial sites is frequently mixed at intermediate trading depots before export, and because the financial incentive for a trader to launder artisanal material into an industrial-labeled shipment is considerable when industrial-sourced cobalt commands a real price premium. This is a distinctly different flavor of chokepoint from the ones in earlier chapters: the scarcity is not really in the metal, which the DRC has in genuinely enormous quantity, but in the world's ability to source it in a way that satisfies the ethical standards its own largest buyers publicly claim to hold themselves to. Demand for an ethically verified supply chain, in other words, is itself a bottleneck layered on top of a geological one.

Even setting the labor question aside, the cobalt chokepoint has demonstrated, in just the past two years, how much leverage a single country holding roughly three-quarters of a critical mineral can exercise when it chooses to. In February 2025, the Congolese government suspended all cobalt exports outright, citing a need to stabilize a market that had been oversupplied and depressed in price for several years, extending the suspension by another three months that June before finally lifting it in October 2025 in favor of a quota system intended to govern production and exports through at least 2027 — capping exports for the remainder of 2025 at roughly 18,125 metric tons and setting a 2026 annual quota near 96,600 tons, according to reporting from trade publications including Fastmarkets and Benchmark Mineral Intelligence. The market reaction was immediate and sharp: cobalt prices, which had been trading around $9.50 to $10.50 per pound in the weeks before the ban, climbed above $16 per pound within a few weeks and, according to figures cited by Congolese officials, the underlying ton-denominated price roughly doubled, moving from around $22,000 a ton before the suspension toward $54,000 to $55,000 a ton after it. A single government decision, executed to manage its own domestic market rather than to exert geopolitical leverage over any particular buyer, moved a globally traded industrial commodity more sharply in a matter of weeks than most central-bank interventions move a currency.

That the DRC used this power for domestic market management rather than as an instrument of foreign policy is itself worth sitting with, because it is a different motive than the export-control stories in the rare-earths and chip chapters, where states have used chokepoint control explicitly and knowingly as diplomatic leverage. The Congolese cobalt ban demonstrates that a chokepoint does not need to be wielded strategically to have strategic effects. A government trying to solve an entirely domestic problem — depressed prices squeezing its own mining revenue — can, simply by virtue of controlling three-quarters of a global input, send shockwaves through battery and electric-vehicle supply chains on three other continents as a side effect.

Battery chemists have been racing to reduce this exposure from the demand side for years, and it is worth noting plainly that they have made real progress: lithium iron phosphate, or LFP, battery chemistry, which uses no cobalt at all, grew from a niche technology to a substantial share of the global electric-vehicle battery market through the 2020s, driven heavily by Chinese manufacturers including CATL and BYD. Nickel-rich cathode chemistries that reduce, without eliminating, cobalt content have also gained ground. Neither substitution has been fast enough, however, to meaningfully loosen the world's dependence on Congolese cobalt within the timeframe this book is being written, and cobalt remains, as of the mid-2020s, the preferred choice for the highest-performance and longest-lasting battery applications, including much of the premium electric-vehicle segment and virtually all consumer electronics.

The uncomfortable throughline connecting this chapter to the rare-earths chapter before it is that both chokepoints sit, at least in part, on a foundation of externalized environmental and human cost that the countries and consumers benefiting most from cheap batteries and cheap magnets have historically been slow to see, price, or pay for. China's dominance in rare-earth separation was built partly on a tolerance for toxic waste that American regulators, reasonably, would no longer permit at Mountain Pass. The DRC's dominance in cobalt supply persists partly because the labor conditions Kara and others have documented remain cheap enough, and geographically distant enough from the phones and cars the cobalt ends up inside, that market pressure to fix them has been real but has not yet been decisive. Neither of these facts makes the chokepoints less real or less strategically important. They do mean that any account of why a particular country or company ended up controlling a critical input is incomplete if it stops at geology, chemistry, or corporate strategy and never asks who bore the cost that made the concentration cheap enough to happen in the first place.
