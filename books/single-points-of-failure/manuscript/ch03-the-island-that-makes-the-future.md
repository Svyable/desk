# Chapter 3 — The Island That Makes the Future

In 1985, Taiwan's government offered a 54-year-old semiconductor executive named Morris Chang what he later described as a blank check to build an industry that did not yet exist. Chang had spent 25 years at Texas Instruments, rising to head its global semiconductor division, then left for a brief and unhappy stint running a struggling instruments company before Taiwan's Minister of Economic Affairs, K.T. Li, recruited him to run the Industrial Technology Research Institute and, soon after, to found a new chip company from scratch.

Chang's idea, when he founded Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company in 1987, was structurally strange for its time and is now the dominant model for the entire industry. Every major chip company then in existence — Intel, Texas Instruments, Motorola, NEC — designed its own chips and manufactured them in its own factories, an "integrated device manufacturer" model in which design and fabrication were inseparable. Chang proposed a company that would manufacture chips designed by other people and never compete with its own customers by designing chips of its own. He called it the "pure-play foundry" model, and when he pitched it, most of the American semiconductor establishment thought it wouldn't work; a foundry with no products of its own, they argued, would always be a second-tier commodity manufacturer taking whatever margin its customers left behind.

That skepticism nearly killed the company before it started. Chang could not initially raise the capital Taiwan's government wanted him to raise from private investors, and TSMC's early survival depended on Philips, the Dutch electronics giant, putting up roughly $58 million and transferring production technology and patent licenses in exchange for a 27.5 percent equity stake — the same Philips whose Netherlands-based lithography division would later be spun out, decades on, as ASML, tying the two companies at the center of this book together at their origins in a way neither fully anticipated.

The foundry model turned out to solve a problem the rest of the industry didn't yet know it had. As chip design grew more specialized and capital-intensive, a growing number of companies — first small, fabless design shops, then eventually giants like Apple, Nvidia, and Qualcomm — realized they would rather focus entirely on design and pay someone else to carry the multi-billion-dollar cost and risk of building and running a fabrication plant, or "fab." TSMC absorbed that risk, spread it across hundreds of customers, and reinvested the resulting scale into staying at the leading edge of manufacturing technology, node after node, for four decades. The specialization worked so well that today the fabless model Chang bet on — Apple designs the chip in an iPhone, but does not manufacture a single one of them — is simply how the industry works.

The scale that resulted from getting that bet right is difficult to overstate. As of the mid-2020s, TSMC's share of the global semiconductor foundry market — the market for manufacturing chips designed by other companies — has been reported by the market-research firm TrendForce at roughly 64 percent in 2024, climbing toward 70 percent and above through 2025 as AI-driven demand concentrated even further around the most capable manufacturer. At the true leading edge — the 3-nanometer and 5-nanometer process nodes that produce the chips inside the newest smartphones, the fastest data-center processors, and virtually every advanced AI accelerator on the market — TSMC's share has been estimated at roughly 90 percent, because Samsung and Intel, its only real competitors at that tier, have struggled with yield and schedule at the most advanced nodes for much of the past decade. For the newest chips in the world, there is effectively one company that can manufacture them at meaningful volume, and it operates the overwhelming majority of that capacity on one island.

The island is Taiwan, a self-governing democracy of about 23 million people that the government of the People's Republic of China claims as its own territory and has not renounced the use of force to eventually bring under its control. This is the fact that turns TSMC from a remarkable business story into one of the most closely watched variables in global security policy. Taiwan sits roughly 100 miles off the coast of mainland China, across a strait that both the U.S. Navy and China's People's Liberation Army treat as one of the most consequential stretches of water on Earth, and inside that strait's shadow sits the single company the entire world's advanced electronics industry depends on for its most critical component.

This is the origin of a phrase that has become common shorthand in security and technology commentary: the "silicon shield." The argument runs that Taiwan's outsized importance to global chip supply gives the rest of the world — and above all the United States, China, and every economy that depends on advanced electronics — an overwhelming interest in the island's stability, and that this interest functions as a deterrent against military conflict that a purely economic or demographic reading of Taiwan's power would not predict on its own. Whether the silicon shield actually deters an invasion, or would instead make Taiwan a more attractive target precisely because seizing or destroying its fabs would be so consequential, is a genuinely contested question among strategists, not a settled one; this book takes no position on which reading is correct, because nobody has run the experiment. What is not contested is that the theory exists, that Taiwanese officials have at times invoked it explicitly, and that its plausibility depends entirely on how concentrated TSMC's leading-edge capacity actually is.

TSMC's own leadership has been unusually candid about how difficult that concentration would be to replicate elsewhere on short notice. A modern TSMC fab is not simply a large building; it is a synchronized ecosystem of equipment suppliers, chemical suppliers, ultra-pure water and gas infrastructure, a workforce with institutional knowledge built up over decades, and hundreds of the precision tools described in the previous chapter, arranged and tuned in a specific sequence that TSMC engineers have spent years optimizing for yield — the percentage of chips on a wafer that actually work. Yield is not public information companies share precisely, but industry analysts have repeatedly noted that TSMC's yields at new nodes have consistently outpaced Samsung's and Intel's at comparable stages of maturity, and that this gap, more than any single patent or piece of equipment, is what customers are actually paying for when they choose TSMC over its rivals. That kind of institutional yield advantage cannot be exported by shipping equipment to a new country. It has to be rebuilt, plant by plant, mistake by mistake, over years.

The United States has been trying to rebuild a version of it anyway, at considerable public expense. Under the 2022 CHIPS and Science Act, the U.S. government committed roughly $52 billion in subsidies and incentives aimed at reviving domestic semiconductor manufacturing, and TSMC itself became one of the largest beneficiaries, breaking ground on fabs in Phoenix, Arizona, with a combined planned investment the company has revised upward several times, from an initial $12 billion pledge in 2020 to a cumulative commitment exceeding $65 billion by the mid-2020s. The Arizona fabs are real, and they are producing chips; TSMC has said its first Arizona fab reached production yields comparable to its Taiwan facilities faster than the company expected. But even TSMC's own public statements have been careful to note that Arizona will manufacture a meaningful share of American demand, not replace Taiwan's role as the company's primary and most advanced production base, and independent estimates suggest the most cutting-edge nodes will continue to be manufactured predominantly in Taiwan for years to come, because that is where TSMC's newest and largest fabs, its deepest supplier ecosystem, and the vast majority of its most experienced workforce remain.

There is a version of this chapter that would end with a warning about a Chinese invasion knocking out the global chip supply overnight. That scenario is real enough that governments plan around it, but it is worth resisting as the chapter's central image, because it obscures a quieter and more probable set of risks that a war narrative tends to crowd out: an earthquake, in a country that sits on one of the most seismically active fault systems on Earth and has experienced fab-disrupting quakes before; a drought severe enough to threaten the enormous volumes of ultra-pure water fab lithography and cleaning processes require; a labor shortage as TSMC competes with Samsung, Intel, and its own new overseas fabs for a limited pool of process engineers; or simply the slow attrition of the institutional knowledge that makes Taiwan's yields the best in the world, as senior engineers retire and are replaced by a workforce spread thinner across a growing number of countries. A chokepoint rarely fails the way the most dramatic scenario imagines it failing. It usually fails in a smaller, less cinematic way first, and the dramatic scenario is simply the tail risk everyone has agreed to worry about out loud because it is easier to plan a response to a war than to a decade of accumulated small strains.

What TSMC and ASML share, and what makes it useful to read these two chapters together, is that neither chokepoint was built to be a chokepoint. Chang was trying to solve a business-model problem; ASML's engineers were trying to solve an optics problem. Both companies discovered, well after the fact, that solving their respective problems better than anyone else had turned an ordinary competitive advantage into something closer to critical infrastructure — the kind of asset whose loss a government has to plan for the way it plans for the loss of a power grid or a water supply, rather than the way it plans for the loss of an ordinary vendor.

The next chapter follows the supply chain one step further back, to a raw material problem that looks, on paper, nothing like Taiwan's or ASML's advantage in precision engineering, and turns out to run on almost exactly the same logic.
