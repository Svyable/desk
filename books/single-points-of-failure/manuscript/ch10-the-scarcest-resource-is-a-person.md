# Chapter 10 — The Scarcest Resource Is a Person

Every chokepoint in this book so far has had a machine, a mine, a canal, or a cable at its center, and it would be easy to close the book with the impression that scarcity in the modern economy is mostly a story about objects. It is not. Behind almost every chokepoint described in the preceding chapters sits a much smaller and much less discussed chokepoint: the limited number of human beings who actually know how to build, calibrate, repair, or operate the thing.

Start with ASML, the company whose extreme ultraviolet lithography systems occupied an entire chapter earlier in this book. Building the machine is only half the business. Once a $400 million EUV system is installed at a customer's fab, it does not run itself; it requires continuous, highly specialized field service, because a tool operating at the tolerances EUV lithography demands drifts out of calibration constantly and has to be brought back into spec by engineers who understand both the machine's optics and the specific fab environment it sits in. ASML's own reported headcount roughly doubled between 2020 and 2025, growing from about 28,000 to around 46,000 employees, and engineering functions make up close to half of that workforce — but growing the number of engineers on a payroll is not the same as growing the number of engineers who have spent years learning the specific, often undocumented judgment calls that separate a merely competent EUV field-service engineer from an excellent one. That kind of expertise is what economists and organizational researchers sometimes call tacit knowledge: know-how that resists being fully captured in a manual, because much of it consists of pattern recognition built from having personally seen a machine fail in a dozen idiosyncratic ways before.

The competition for that expertise has become sharp enough to show up in corporate strategy. In 2026, as ASML worked to protect its innovation pipeline while cutting roughly 1,700 management positions elsewhere in the company amid a broader restructuring, it moved to lock in key engineering and R&D staff — reported by industry press to include retention packages worth as much as €20,000 per employee — specifically because rivals including Samsung, TSMC, and SK Hynix were said to be aggressively recruiting away the same narrow pool of specialists the entire advanced semiconductor industry depends on. Notice what this means in practice: companies that are fierce competitors for chip-manufacturing market share are, at the same time, competing directly against each other for the same small population of people, because there simply are not many humans on Earth who have spent a career learning how EUV optics, or advanced packaging, or high-bandwidth memory stacking actually behave under real operating conditions rather than in a textbook description of them.

The pattern generalizes well beyond semiconductors. TSMC's advantage over Samsung and Intel at the leading edge, discussed in an earlier chapter, is frequently described by industry analysts not primarily as a difference in equipment — all three companies can, in principle, buy the same ASML tools — but as a difference in yield, the percentage of chips on a wafer that come out working. Yield is substantially a function of accumulated process knowledge: thousands of small adjustments to chemistry, timing, and technique that a fab's engineers have learned, often through years of trial and error on a specific production line, and that do not transfer cleanly even when a company builds an identical fab in a different country with identical machines. When TSMC opened its new fab in Arizona, the company was explicit that closing the yield gap with its Taiwan facilities would take time, not primarily because the equipment was different, but because a large share of the specialized workforce and the tacit process knowledge that Taiwan's fabs had spent decades accumulating had to be rebuilt, in a new location, essentially from a smaller starting base.

Some of the most consequential examples of this human chokepoint are far more literal, and far more narrow, than an engineering workforce measured in the thousands. Consider the pilots who guide oversized ships through the Suez and Panama Canals: certification to guide the largest vessels through either canal requires years of specific apprenticeship on that exact waterway, and the number of pilots licensed for the largest ship classes at any one canal numbers in the dozens, not the thousands — meaning the canal's actual daily throughput capacity depends not only on lock or channel dimensions but on how many qualified pilots are on shift on a given day. Consider the specialized welders and technicians certified to work on nuclear submarine hulls, a workforce the U.S. Navy has repeatedly and publicly described as a bottleneck constraining submarine production schedules more tightly than shipyard capacity itself. Consider air-traffic controllers, a workforce so specialized and so slow to train — a multi-year certification process that cannot be meaningfully compressed by adding money — that a shortage in this single occupational category has been directly blamed by U.S. aviation regulators and airline executives for flight delays and capacity constraints across the entire national airspace system in recent years.

What links these examples is that each involves a skill requiring years of accumulated, largely non-transferable experience, operating inside a system where the number of qualified people is small enough that losing even a modest fraction of them — to retirement, poaching, burnout, or simple population aging — creates a bottleneck no amount of capital can immediately fix. Money can buy a second EUV machine faster than money can produce a second engineer with fifteen years of hands-on experience calibrating one. A shipyard can be expanded with enough investment; a certified nuclear welder cannot be trained overnight regardless of how much a shipyard is willing to pay, because the training itself takes years and the certification process exists precisely because the skill cannot be safely rushed.

This creates a strategic asymmetry that companies and governments have been slow to fully internalize, because industrial policy has historically been better at funding machines than at funding people. The 2022 CHIPS and Science Act, discussed in the Taiwan chapter, allocated tens of billions of dollars toward fabs, and a comparatively much smaller — though not negligible — portion toward workforce development, even though multiple industry surveys conducted around the same period, including ones cited by the Semiconductor Industry Association, projected that the United States would face a shortfall of tens of thousands of skilled technicians and engineers needed to actually staff the new fabs the Act was funding. Building a fab that no one is trained to run at full yield does not solve a chokepoint. It just relocates the chokepoint from concrete and steel to community colleges and apprenticeship pipelines, where it is considerably less visible to policymakers used to thinking about industrial capacity in terms of square footage and machine counts.

There is a genuinely difficult tension buried in this chapter that the book should not pretend to resolve neatly. On one hand, concentrating rare expertise in a small number of highly experienced people is often exactly how a system achieves excellence — the same logic that makes TSMC's yields the best in the industry applies to why a small number of veteran surgeons perform certain rare procedures far more safely than a larger pool of less-practiced ones would. Deliberately spreading expertise thinner, in the name of redundancy, can genuinely make a system worse at its core job most of the time, even as it makes catastrophic single-point failure less likely. On the other hand, a system that has optimized itself down to a genuinely small number of irreplaceable people is one bad flu season, one aggressive competitor's recruiting offer, or one generation's worth of retirements away from a crisis that no amount of capital investment can fix on a useful timescale. The honest position is that there is no universal answer to how much redundancy a given kind of expertise deserves; it depends on how long training actually takes, how catastrophic a shortfall would be, and how replaceable the underlying knowledge really is once written down — questions that require asking, case by case, rather than a slogan that applies equally to canal pilots, chip engineers, and air-traffic controllers alike.
