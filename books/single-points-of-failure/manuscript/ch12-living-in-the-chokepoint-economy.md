# Chapter 12 — Living in the Chokepoint Economy

Return, for a moment, to the *Ever Given*, wedged sideways in the Suez Canal in March 2021. The ship's owners had not been careless. The canal authority had not been negligent. Every institution involved had spent decades optimizing its part of the system — larger ships to lower the cost per container, tighter schedules to reduce inventory costs across the entire retail industry, a pilotage system refined over a century and a half of continuous operation. The blockage was not a story about anyone failing to do their job. It was a story about what happens when a system optimized this thoroughly for the ordinary case meets the one week in a decade that is not ordinary.

That sentence could describe nearly every chokepoint in this book. ASML did not build a monopoly on EUV lithography by trying to corner a market; it built one by being the only company willing to sustain thirty years of expensive, uncertain research into a technology everyone else eventually needed. China did not come to dominate rare-earth processing through some singular act of strategic foresight so much as through a willingness, sustained over decades, to do an unglamorous and environmentally costly job that the rest of the world had walked away from. SK Hynix did not set out to become one of the most important companies in the AI boom; it made an early, uncertain bet on high-bandwidth memory that happened to pay off spectacularly once the boom arrived. Concentration, again and again in these chapters, turns out to be less a conspiracy than a residue — what is left over after a genuinely hard problem gets solved unusually well by one actor, in a world that then quietly builds its dependence on that solution without ever quite deciding to.

This matters because it should discourage a lazy conclusion the book has tried to avoid at every turn: that chokepoints are simply villains to be broken up, or that concentration is always a policy failure waiting to be corrected. Sometimes it is. A regulator who discovers that one company has captured a market through anticompetitive conduct rather than superior execution has every reason to intervene, and nothing in this book argues otherwise. But several of the chokepoints described here — ASML's engineering, TSMC's yield advantage, the accumulated tacit knowledge inside a veteran field-service engineer — are concentrated because concentration was, for a long stretch of time, the most efficient available answer to a genuinely hard problem, and dismantling that concentration in the name of resilience would mean deliberately accepting worse products, higher costs, or slower progress in exchange for a hedge against a risk that might not materialize for years. There is no formula that tells a company or a country exactly how much of that trade to make. There is only the discipline of asking the question honestly, chokepoint by chokepoint, rather than defaulting to either extreme.

What the book can offer, in place of a formula, is a short set of questions worth asking about any dependency that matters enough to worry about — a household relying on one grocery supplier, a company relying on one manufacturer, a country relying on one trading partner. None of these is a guarantee. Each is simply a habit of noticing, drawn from watching the same failure pattern recur across a canal, a lithography machine, a lake, a cable network, a mine, a memory chip, a payment system, a workforce, and a warehouse of stockpiled oil.

The first question is how fast the dependency could actually be replaced if it disappeared tomorrow, and whether that timescale is measured in days, months, years, or decades. A blocked canal is a matter of days because ships can eventually reroute around a continent, expensively but genuinely. A collapsed rare-earth separation industry is a matter of a decade or more, because the missing ingredient is not a stock of material but an accumulated body of chemistry and engineering practice that has to be relearned largely from scratch. Two dependencies that look equally severe on the day they fail can have wildly different real costs depending on which of these timescales they sit on, and the honest answer to "how fast could we replace this" is very often "we don't actually know," which is itself useful information, because it tells you the dependency has never been stress-tested and probably should be.

The second is whether the concentration exists because of physics and geography, or because of a standard, contract, or convention that people simply agreed to. A strait a fleet has to sail through single file is concentrated by the shape of the earth. A payment-messaging network that a bank could, in principle, replace with a different technical standard is concentrated by the fact that everyone else already uses it. The first kind of chokepoint can be routed around only by building genuinely new physical infrastructure, on a timescale that map and geology dictate. The second kind can, in principle, be redrawn on the timescale of a large enough coalition deciding the switching cost is worth paying — slower than it sounds, because coordination problems are genuinely hard, but faster than moving a mountain range, and worth knowing which kind of problem you are actually facing before assuming either that change is impossible or that it is easy.

The third is who is actually exposed when the chokepoint fails, and whether that is the same party who benefits when it holds. The cobalt chapter made this uncomfortably explicit: the people bearing the sharpest cost of a fragile, poorly regulated artisanal mining sector are rarely the same people enjoying the low prices and reliable supply that sector, most of the time, delivers. A household, a company, or a government that only asks "is this dependency risky for me" without also asking "who is absorbing the cost that makes it cheap for me" is measuring half the picture, and the half it is skipping tends to be exactly the half that eventually shows up as a labor shortage, a regulatory crackdown, an export ban, or a reputational crisis nobody upstream saw coming because nobody upstream had been looking.

The fourth is whether redundancy has actually been built, or merely assumed. Diversifying suppliers on paper is not the same as diversifying suppliers who could actually absorb full volume on short notice; many companies that believed they had a "second source" for a critical component discovered, when the first source failed, that the second source had never actually been tested at the volume now suddenly required of it. The Panama Canal's water-recycling basins and the Strategic Petroleum Reserve's salt caverns are real redundancy because they have been built, funded, and in the reserve's case, actually drawn upon under genuine emergency conditions. A backup supplier that exists only as a line item in a risk-management slide deck is not redundancy. It is a description of redundancy, which is a different thing entirely, and the gap between the two tends to reveal itself at the worst possible time.

None of these four questions resolves into a single number, a formula, or a checklist that can be applied mechanically and trusted to produce the right answer, and a book that pretended otherwise would be selling something false. What they offer instead is a way of looking at an ordinary, unremarkable dependency — a supplier, a route, a certification, a machine, a person with fifteen years of undocumented experience — and asking whether it has quietly become something else: a single point through which an unreasonable share of value now flows, held up by a solution to a hard problem that was excellent right up until the day it wasn't.

The chapters in this book covered a canal, a Dutch machine, an island, a desert mine, a Panamanian lake, an ocean floor, a Congolese hillside, a stack of memory chips, a Belgian messaging cooperative, a small population of specialized engineers, and a set of underground salt caverns built to hold oil nobody wants to use. They have almost nothing in common on the surface. What they share is a shape: enormous, unremarked efficiency for a very long time, followed by a sudden and often expensive education, delivered to whoever least expected it, about exactly how much they had been depending on something they had never had reason to look at closely before. The *Ever Given* did not create that shape. It just happened to run aground in a place narrow enough, and a moment public enough, for the rest of the world to finally see it.
