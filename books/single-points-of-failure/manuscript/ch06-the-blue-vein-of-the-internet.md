# Chapter 6 — The Blue Vein of the Internet

Almost nothing about the internet's popular imagery matches how it actually moves. Ask most people how a video call, a bank transfer, or an email crosses an ocean, and the honest answer for many would involve a satellite, because satellites are the part of the system anyone can point to in the sky. The real answer is duller and much more fragile: it travels through a fiber-optic cable roughly the diameter of a garden hose, lying on the seafloor, that was almost certainly laid by one of a small number of specialized ships and that can, and periodically does, get snagged by a fishing trawler's anchor or a dragging container-ship anchor and severed.

More than 99 percent of intercontinental data traffic — the international calls, financial transactions, and internet backbone connections that cross oceans rather than staying within a single country — travels through a global network of roughly 570 to 600 commercial submarine cables stretching, depending on the year counted, somewhere over 1.4 million kilometers, according to figures compiled by the industry research group TeleGeography and cited by the International Telecommunication Union. Satellites carry a genuinely useful but comparatively small share of specialized, latency-tolerant, or remote traffic. For the overwhelming bulk of the data that makes a globalized economy function — the trading systems that clear billions of dollars in transactions between London and New York in milliseconds, the cloud services a company in Singapore uses to serve customers in Germany — the physical medium is glass fiber on the ocean floor, and it has been since the first transatlantic telegraph cable in 1858 and the first transatlantic telephone cable, TAT-1, in 1956.

Cables break more often than most of the traveling public would guess, and the internet mostly does not notice, because well-connected regions have enough redundant cables that traffic reroutes automatically when one link goes down — the same logic, inverted, that makes a break in a poorly connected region so consequential. TeleGeography and industry insurers have estimated that somewhere on the order of 150 to 200 subsea cable faults occur globally each year, the overwhelming majority of them accidental: fishing gear, ship anchors dragged in shallow coastal water, and, less commonly, seismic activity or underwater landslides. West Africa, parts of the Pacific, and other regions served by only one or two cables have experienced sudden, near-total loss of international connectivity from a single break, because there was no redundant path for traffic to take instead — a vivid demonstration that the internet's resilience is a property of specific well-connected corridors, not a universal law of the network's design.

Fixing a broken cable is a far more specialized undertaking than most infrastructure repair, and this is where the chapter's real chokepoint sits. Repairing a subsea cable requires a purpose-built cable ship, capable of locating the break using onboard sonar and ROVs, hauling the cable up from potentially several kilometers of depth, splicing the fiber back together in a floating shipboard cleanroom, and re-laying it — a process that, even when everything goes smoothly, typically takes one to several weeks. As of the mid-2020s, the entire global fleet of cable-laying and cable-repair vessels numbered around 60, according to industry counts reported by outlets including the *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists* and DataCenterDynamics, split between vessels dedicated to laying new cable and a smaller subset on standby, under maintenance-zone contracts, specifically for emergency repairs. That is the entire worldwide capacity to fix the physical medium carrying nearly all of the planet's intercontinental data — a fleet smaller than the passenger jet fleet of many individual regional airlines.

The fleet is also aging. Industry analyses cited by DataCenterDynamics have noted that a large share of the world's cable-repair ships were built decades ago and that close to half of the current fleet is expected to reach the end of its operational life by 2040, at a moment when the volume of subsea cable being laid — driven by hyperscale cloud providers like Google, Meta, and Amazon building their own private transoceanic cable systems to carry data between their data centers — is growing considerably faster than the repair fleet itself. A capacity crunch of the kind described in industry trade press is not a hypothetical: cable operators in some regions have reported repair backlogs stretching for weeks or months simply because the nearest available repair ship was already committed to another job.

This underlying scarcity has taken on a sharper edge as cable damage has, in a handful of recent and closely scrutinized incidents, appeared to be deliberate rather than accidental. In late 2024 and through 2025, several undersea cables and pipelines in the Baltic Sea — a body of water crossed by dense cable traffic connecting Scandinavia, the Baltic states, Germany, and Poland — were damaged in incidents that European officials and NATO investigators publicly suspected, though in most cases could not conclusively prove, involved vessels linked to Russia's so-called "shadow fleet" of tankers dragging anchors across the seabed, whether deliberately or through reckless navigation. NATO responded in early 2025 by launching an enhanced naval patrol mission in the Baltic specifically to monitor and, where possible, deter suspected cable-damaging activity, an unusually direct acknowledgment from a military alliance that seabed cable infrastructure now sits inside the category of assets worth actively defending rather than simply repairing after the fact.

Africa's cable-repair story illustrates the chokepoint's unevenness most starkly. For years, the continent's Atlantic and Indian Ocean coastlines — served by cables like WACS and SAT-3 that connect dozens of countries to the global internet — depended almost entirely on cable-repair ships based in Europe or elsewhere, meaning that a break off the coast of West Africa could take weeks longer to fix than a comparable break in a region with a repair vessel already stationed nearby, simply because of the transit time required to get a ship to the site. A 2025 investigation by the outlet *Rest of World* profiled what it described as one of the only cable-repair vessels based on the African continent, illustrating both how thin this layer of global infrastructure is and how unevenly its scarcity is distributed: wealthy, well-connected regions experience a cable cut as a barely noticeable rerouting event, while regions served by fewer cables and fewer nearby repair ships can experience the same category of event as a weeks-long national outage.

What makes the undersea cable network a particularly instructive chokepoint for this book is how little it resembles the ones built around a single dominant company or a single national deposit. No one owns the whole system; it is a patchwork of consortium-financed cables, national telecom operators, and, increasingly, the hyperscale cloud companies laying their own private infrastructure. The chokepoint here is not corporate concentration but physical and industrial scarcity one layer removed from the cables themselves: a small, aging, highly specialized fleet of repair ships, built by a handful of shipyards, crewed by a comparatively small number of trained cable engineers, that the entire world's intercontinental data traffic depends on staying operational, expanding, and — increasingly — staying safe from deliberate interference in contested waters. It is a reminder that a chokepoint does not need a monopolist sitting at its center to be dangerously thin. Sometimes thinness is simply what happens when a critical capability grows more slowly than the demand placed on it, for decades, without anyone in particular being responsible for correcting the gap.
