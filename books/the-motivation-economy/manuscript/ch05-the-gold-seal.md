# The Gold Seal

Edward Deci was trained originally as a mathematician before he later became a psychologist, and that earlier mathematical training shows clearly and consistently in the precise, carefully controlled design of the experiment that would go on to make his career. Where Harlow had let his monkeys loose with a puzzle and simply watched what happened, Deci built a controlled, multi-session study precise enough to isolate a single variable and measure its effect, using a three-dimensional plastic puzzle called a Soma cube — seven interlocking pieces that can be assembled into a cube itself or into dozens of other geometric shapes, chosen, Deci later wrote, because pilot testing suggested most college students found it genuinely absorbing on its own terms, without needing to be told it mattered.

The 1971 study recruited twenty-four undergraduates and ran each of them through three separate sessions, one per day, framed to participants as an ordinary study of problem-solving. In the first session, every participant simply worked on a set of Soma-cube configurations, establishing a baseline. In the second session, the group was split: half were told they would be paid a dollar for every puzzle configuration they solved correctly, while the other half worked under identical conditions with no payment at all. The critical manipulation came in the third session. Partway through, the experimenter left the room on a pretext, ostensibly to check on something unrelated, leaving participants alone with the puzzle pieces, a stack of unrelated magazines, and no observer present — or so participants believed, since the room was in fact equipped for concealed observation. Whatever a participant chose to do during that unsupervised interval, when there was no puzzle to solve for credit and no one apparently watching, was the actual measurement. Deci called it the free-choice period, and it became the methodological backbone of the entire field that followed him.

The finding was as clean as the design that produced it. Participants who had been paid for solving puzzles in the second session spent measurably less free-choice time voluntarily returning to the puzzle in the third session than participants who had received no payment at all — despite both groups having found the task interesting enough, by their own reports and by their session-one behavior, to spend meaningful time on it before money entered the picture. Getting paid to do something you already enjoyed did not simply fail to add extra motivation once the payment stopped. It appeared to actively subtract from the motivation that had been there before any money was offered.

Deci replicated the core finding in several follow-up experiments through the early 1970s, varying the specific puzzle, the specific reward, and the population studied, and by the middle of the decade he and his Rochester colleague Richard Ryan had begun assembling the finding into a broader theoretical account they called cognitive evaluation theory — an attempt to explain not just that rewards could undermine intrinsic motivation, but why. Their proposed mechanism turned on a distinction that would prove central to everything downstream of it: a reward, they argued, carries two separable pieces of information for the person receiving it. It carries controlling information — the sense that "I am doing this because of the reward, not because I chose to" — and it can also carry informational content about competence — the sense that "receiving this reward tells me I am good at this." When the controlling aspect of a reward dominates a person's experience of it, Deci and Ryan argued, intrinsic motivation tends to erode, because the activity's psychological center of gravity shifts from the person's own sense of choosing it to an external contingency dictating it. When the informational aspect dominates instead — when a reward mainly signals competence without feeling coercive — intrinsic motivation can hold steady or even strengthen. A cash payment tied directly and exclusively to output, the specific design used in the Soma-cube studies, sits about as far toward the controlling end of that spectrum as a reward can get, which is part of why it produced such a clear undermining effect.

That distinction — controlling versus informational — turned out to matter enormously for how far the finding could reasonably be generalized, and it is the piece of the theory most often lost in popular retellings that compress "rewards can undermine motivation" into "rewards are bad." They are not interchangeable claims, and the difference between them is where a great deal of legitimate scientific controversy over the following three decades actually lived.

The controversy is worth describing honestly rather than glossing over, because a book willing to cite Deci's finding without acknowledging how contested it became would be misrepresenting the state of the science. In 1994, the researchers Judy Cameron and W. David Pierce published a meta-analysis pooling dozens of reward-and-motivation experiments and concluded that the undermining effect, while real in some narrow circumstances, was far more limited than Deci's camp had claimed, and largely avoidable in applied settings such as schools and workplaces through careful reward design. The paper landed as a direct challenge to what had, by the early 1990s, become something close to orthodoxy in educational and organizational psychology. Deci, joined by Richard Koestner and Ryan, responded in 1999 with their own meta-analysis, this one pooling 128 experiments and organized specifically to test the predictions of cognitive evaluation theory rather than a looser question about rewards in general. Their conclusion pushed back firmly: tangible, expected rewards tied directly to task performance did significantly and substantially undermine intrinsic motivation across the pooled studies, and several of Cameron and Pierce's methodological choices, in Deci's camp's assessment, had obscured rather than clarified the effect. Cameron, joined by Pierce and the psychologist Robert Eisenberger, published a rebuttal the same year in the same journal, arguing among other things that Deci's group had conflated different theoretical constructs and overstated the practical stakes. The exchange continued, with further replies and counter-replies, well into the following decade, and readers looking for a single, universally accepted verdict on "do rewards undermine intrinsic motivation" will not find one in the primary literature, even today. What the accumulated three decades of back-and-forth did settle, with more confidence than either side's opening position, is something closer to the boundary-condition account this book adopts: the undermining effect is real and replicable under specific conditions — tangible rewards, expected in advance, tied directly to simply performing or completing a task a person already found interesting — and considerably weaker or absent under other conditions, including unexpected rewards, rewards framed as recognition of competence rather than payment for output, and tasks that were not particularly interesting to begin with, where there was little intrinsic motivation available to undermine in the first place.

It is that last category — tasks that were not interesting to begin with — that connects Deci's Soma-cube adults back to a very different set of experimental subjects: preschoolers, magic markers, and a certificate with a gold seal and a red ribbon.

In 1973, the Stanford researchers Mark Lepper, David Greene, and Richard Nisbett set out to test what they called the overjustification hypothesis, drawing on a related but distinct theoretical tradition — self-perception theory, associated with the psychologist Daryl Bem — which proposed that people often infer their own motives the same way an outside observer would: by watching their own behavior and asking what explanation best accounts for it. If a person notices themselves doing something in exchange for an obvious external reward, self-perception theory predicted, they may reasonably conclude that the reward, rather than any inherent interest, explains their behavior — even if, before the reward was introduced, genuine interest had been the real and sufficient explanation all along.

Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett tested the prediction on fifty-one preschool children at a Stanford-affiliated nursery school, using felt-tip markers and drawing paper — an activity that observation through one-way mirrors during an earlier baseline period had already confirmed the children found genuinely, spontaneously enjoyable during ordinary free-play time, well before any experimenter proposed a reward for it. Children were sorted into three conditions. One group was told in advance that drawing with the markers would earn them a "Good Player Award," an elaborate certificate decorated with a gold seal and a red ribbon, and were given the award after completing the activity as promised. A second group was not told about any award in advance but received the same certificate afterward as a surprise. A third group received no award at all. One to two weeks later, researchers returned to the classroom and covertly observed, during ordinary free-play periods, how much time each child spontaneously chose to spend drawing with markers, comparing it against each child's own baseline level of interest recorded before the experiment began.

The children who had expected the award going in showed a marked decline in their free-play interest in drawing compared to their own baseline — roughly half as much time spent drawing, in the study's reporting, as children in the other two conditions. Children who received the same certificate as a complete surprise, with no expectation attached beforehand, showed no comparable decline; their interest in drawing held roughly steady. The distinguishing variable was not the reward itself, which was identical in both conditions, but whether the child had gone into the activity expecting it, framing the drawing, in Lepper and his colleagues' language, as a means to an extrinsic end rather than as something pursued for its own sake.

The overjustification effect, as the finding came to be called, has proven remarkably durable in the literature, replicated across a range of ages, tasks, and reward types over the following decades, even as researchers have continued to argue, much as they did over Deci's adult studies, about exactly how far it generalizes and how large the practical stakes really are outside a research nursery school. But the specific mechanism Lepper's team proposed — that an expected, task-contingent reward can retroactively recast an activity a person already found interesting as merely instrumental, a means to the reward rather than an end in itself — sits comfortably alongside Deci and Ryan's controlling-versus-informational distinction, and together the two lines of research triangulate on roughly the same practical conclusion from two independent directions, one built on paid college students solving geometric puzzles and the other on unpaid four-year-olds drawing with markers.

It is worth drawing the connection to the present labor market directly here, because this is one of the few points in the book where a fifty-year-old psychology finding maps almost too neatly onto a live, contemporary design problem. Gig-economy platforms — ride-hailing apps, delivery services, freelance marketplaces — have spent the past decade building extraordinarily precise systems of tangible, expected, task-contingent reward: a fixed payment per delivery, a bonus for completing a quota of trips within a window, a streak reward for consecutive days worked, badges and tiers that function, structurally, as a more sophisticated and more continuously updated version of the Good Player Award's gold seal and red ribbon. These systems are often extremely effective at producing short-term compliance, which is part of why they have proliferated so widely; a worker chasing a completion bonus will, in the moment, take one more delivery than she otherwise would have. What the overjustification research and the cognitive-evaluation-theory literature together suggest, more cautiously, is that this same design — tangible, expected, output-contingent reward, applied relentlessly — is close to the exact configuration most reliably associated with eroding whatever intrinsic interest a worker might otherwise have found in the underlying activity, whether that activity is navigating an unfamiliar city, solving a customer's problem, or simply getting good at a craft. A gig platform optimizing purely for near-term task completion may be optimizing, without necessarily intending to, against the durable version of engagement this book is ultimately concerned with — the kind that survives a bad week, an ambiguous assignment, or the absence of a bonus altogether.

None of this licenses the opposite overcorrection, which shows up often enough in popular business writing to deserve a direct rebuttal: the claim that paying people well, or attaching any reward at all to good performance, is inherently corrosive to motivation and should be avoided. That is not what the research says, and Deci himself was careful, across four decades of subsequent writing, to distinguish his findings from that cruder conclusion. Salary itself functions, in the cognitive-evaluation-theory framework, primarily as a baseline condition rather than as a task-contingent reward in the sense the Soma-cube and marker studies tested; being paid a fair, dependable wage to do a job is different, structurally and psychologically, from being paid an extra dollar for each individual puzzle solved or each individual delivery completed. Recognition, praise, and even bonuses can function as informational rather than controlling when they are unexpected, tied to genuine competence rather than mere output, and delivered in a way that affirms a person's own sense of choosing the work rather than being steered by it. The practical lesson embedded in fifty years of contested, carefully qualified research is narrower and more useful than either "rewards work" or "rewards backfire": the specific combination of expected, tangible, output-contingent payment, layered onto a task a person might otherwise have found interesting on its own terms, is the configuration most likely to convert genuine engagement into mere compliance — and that configuration describes an uncomfortable share of how both traditional entry-level jobs and newer gig-platform work have actually been designed.

The findings also traveled, over the following decades, well beyond psychology departments and into fields where the stakes of getting the answer wrong were considerably higher than a laboratory's free-choice period. Educational researchers used the overjustification literature to argue against grading and reward structures that made external evaluation the explicit, advertised point of an assignment rather than a byproduct of it, contributing to a long-running and still unresolved debate within American education about the proper role of grades, gold stars, and prize-based reading programs — a debate this book will not attempt to settle, but one in which Lepper's fifty-one preschoolers are still cited, more than half a century later, on both sides. Public health researchers testing cash incentives for smoking cessation, medication adherence, and vaccination — behaviors nobody would describe as intrinsically enjoyable to begin with, which already places them closer to the boundary condition where the undermining effect should matter least — found a more mixed picture than the overjustification literature alone would predict: financial incentives for these behaviors often worked reasonably well while the payment lasted, with the harder open question being what happened to the behavior once the incentive program ended and the free-choice period, in effect, arrived for good — a question closer in spirit to Deci's original three-session design than to any single dramatic finding, and one public health researchers are still actively studying rather than resolving. Corporate incentive design absorbed a heavily diluted version of the same research, producing, by the 2000s and 2010s, a substantial management-consulting literature on the difference between "extrinsic" and "intrinsic" rewards that often flattened fifty years of careful, contested experimental nuance into a slide-deck dichotomy — precisely the kind of oversimplification this chapter has tried to resist, even while drawing on the same underlying science that dichotomy claims to represent. Sales-force compensation design, perhaps the most reward-saturated corner of the modern economy, largely ignored the debate altogether, continuing to build commission structures around exactly the tangible, expected, output-contingent model the research flags as riskiest, on the reasonable but rarely examined assumption that sales work was never going to be intrinsically interesting to most of the people doing it in the first place, and so had little engagement left to undermine.

It is worth returning, briefly, to the boundary conditions this chapter established, because the temptation to overclaim runs in both directions and this book has already resisted the crude version of one direction. The undermining effect is not evidence that human beings are fragile creatures whose motivation shatters at the first sight of a paycheck; billions of people work for money every day, plenty of them well, without their underlying interest in craft or competence visibly eroding by the week. What the fifty years of research following Deci and Lepper actually support is a narrower, more diagnostic claim: certain specific reward structures — expected, tangible, tightly tied to simple output, applied to a task a person might otherwise have found absorbing — carry a real, replicated, if still debated, risk of converting engagement into mere compliance, and a labor market or a gig platform that leans heavily and specifically on that particular structure is choosing, whether or not it means to, the version of incentive design most likely to produce workers who do exactly what they are paid to do and nothing more, the moment the payment structure allows it.

What neither Deci's puzzle-solving undergraduates nor Lepper's marker-wielding preschoolers can explain, on their own, is what makes an activity absorbing in the first place — what has to be true about a task's structure, independent of any reward attached to it, for a person to want to keep doing it once the external contingency is removed. That question occupied the researcher who picked up where Deci and Lepper left off, studying not why rewards undermine engagement but what engagement itself actually consists of, through years of interviews with rock climbers, surgeons, chess players, and factory workers who had, by their own accounts, found a way to love work that looked, from the outside, indistinguishable from drudgery.

His answer would give the field a word that has since escaped academic psychology entirely and entered ordinary conversation, used casually by people who have never read a page of the underlying research to describe the specific, absorbed state they enter when a difficult task and their own skill happen to line up exactly right. Getting from Deci's free-choice period to that word requires leaving the tightly controlled laboratory experiment behind almost entirely, in favor of a very different and considerably messier research method: interrupting people, repeatedly and at random intervals throughout their ordinary working days, and simply asking what they were doing right at that exact moment, and how absorbed, capable, skilled, and genuinely in control they felt while they were doing it. The device that eventually made this possible was almost comically low-tech for the era — a pager, worn on the belt, buzzing at random points throughout the day to prompt a written response in a small paper booklet — but the data it produced, gathered from thousands of ordinary people across ordinary working lives rather than from twenty-four undergraduates in a single laboratory room, would give motivation research something Harlow, Deci, and Lepper had never quite managed to capture: a map of what the good version of absorption actually feels like from the inside, moment to moment, once you stop asking whether a reward is present and start asking what the task itself is doing to the person performing it.
