# The Deal

In 1967, a British historian named E. P. Thompson published a long essay in the journal *Past and Present* that began, oddly, with clocks.

Thompson was not writing about horology. He was trying to answer a question that sounds almost too simple to need forty pages of dense argument: why do people who work for wages show up at a specific time, and why does that feel like the most natural thing in the world, when for most of human history it was not natural at all? His answer, built from parish records, diaries, factory rulebooks, and the complaints of employers about workers who refused to behave as employers wished, was that the modern relationship between a person and their working hours was manufactured — assembled deliberately, over roughly two centuries, out of a society that had previously organized labor around tasks rather than time.

Task-oriented work, in Thompson's account, is what most agricultural and craft labor looked like before industrialization gave the clock authority over the day. A shepherd's day was organized by what needed doing to the sheep, not by a schedule someone else had drawn up. A harvest required intense, exhausting labor for a short, unpredictable stretch and then long fallow periods with comparatively little to do. A weaver working from home on a putting-out system — collecting raw material from a merchant, spinning or weaving it on his own equipment, on his own hours, and returning finished cloth for payment by the piece — might work through the night on a Thursday and barely touch the loom on a Monday, a pattern common and unremarkable enough that it had its own name: Saint Monday, the informal, widely tolerated custom of treating the day after a weekend or a market day as an extension of leisure. Nobody was punching a clock, because there was no clock whose opinion mattered. There was only the task, and whatever rhythm getting it done actually required.

This was not, it is worth saying clearly, some pastoral idyll worth mourning. Task-oriented labor was often precarious, exhausting, and entirely at the mercy of weather, harvest failure, and the whims of a landlord or guild master with far more power than the person doing the work. Nobody in this arrangement had a pension, a grievance process, or reliable protection from being worked to exhaustion during a harvest and left with nothing during a bad winter. The romantic version of pre-industrial labor, in which independent artisans set their own hours in blissful autonomy, describes a small and fortunate slice of that world rather than its typical experience. The point of revisiting it is not nostalgia. It is to notice, by contrast, exactly what changed when factories arrived and what that change was actually for.

What changed was that production stopped being organized around a single worker's task and started being organized around a machine, a building, and a schedule that had to synchronize dozens or hundreds of people at once. A factory does not work if half the weavers show up on Tuesday afternoon because Monday was Saint Monday. Water wheels and steam engines ran continuously and expensively, and idle capital sitting in an idle mill was a cost employers had every incentive to eliminate. The solution employers reached for, again and again across the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, was not to persuade workers that punctuality was virtuous, though a great deal of moralizing literature tried exactly that. It was to attach pay directly and visibly to time: the wage per hour, the fine for lateness, the locked gate at the appointed hour, the factory bell that did the work a church bell used to do, except now it announced the start of labor rather than the start of worship. Time itself became, in Thompson's phrase, currency — not passed, in his words, but spent.

Benjamin Franklin had put the same idea more bluntly decades earlier, in a piece of advice to a young tradesman that has been quoted so often it has drifted loose from its original context: time is money. Franklin meant it almost literally as an accounting instruction — an hour spent idle was an hour of possible earnings foregone — and the instruction only makes sense in a world where labor has already been converted into a rate. You cannot say time is money to a shepherd. You can say it, and industrial employers said it constantly, to a worker whose entire relationship to their employer had just been redefined as an exchange of hours for coin.

This is the part of the story that matters most for the argument of this book: the wage did not simply pay people for their labor. It replaced a much older, messier, and more paternalistic set of customary obligations — a lord's duty to feed his tenants in a bad year, a guild's duty to look after a master craftsman's family, a village's diffuse and unenforceable expectation that people would look out for their neighbors — with a single, clean, contractual fact. You worked, you were paid. You did not work, you were not paid, and eventually you could not eat. That fact required no cultural scaffolding to function. It required no manager skilled in motivating people, no mission statement, no effort to make the work meaningful. It required only that people needed money to live and that the employer controlled access to money in exchange for hours. Necessity did the rest.

The moral vocabulary that grew up around the new arrangement is worth pausing on, because it did work that the wage contract alone could not do. A purely contractual relationship — you work, you are paid — does not by itself explain why lateness came to be treated as a character flaw rather than a scheduling inconvenience, or why idleness, which a pre-industrial villager might have regarded as an ordinary and blameless feature of a slow week, came to be treated in the new factory towns as something close to a sin. The German sociologist Max Weber, writing half a century before Thompson, traced part of this moral shift to the Protestant reformers who preceded industrialization, arguing that a religious tradition which treated worldly diligence as a sign of grace had left behind a residue of feeling — a "work ethic" in the literal sense — long after the specific theology that produced it had faded from most people's daily thinking. Whatever the precise causal weight of Weber's argument, which later historians have qualified considerably, the practical effect by the early industrial period was unmistakable: employers no longer needed to argue that punctuality served the factory's bottom line, because a good part of the workforce had absorbed the idea that punctuality was a mark of personal virtue, and its absence a mark of moral failure. Economic pressure and moral pressure reinforced each other. A worker who arrived late was not merely costing himself a fine. He was, in the eyes of his neighbors and often his own, being a worse kind of person.

That moral overlay mattered because it made the underlying arrangement harder to see clearly, then and now. It is easier to notice a wage than a worldview. Adam Smith, writing in 1776, only a decade before the first cotton mills began reorganizing British labor at scale, had already sketched the logical endpoint of the arrangement in his famous description of a pin factory, where a single worker performing every stage of production alone might struggle to make a handful of pins in a day, while ten workers each specialized in a single narrow step — drawing the wire, straightening it, cutting it, sharpening the point — could together produce tens of thousands. Smith meant the example as an argument for the productive power of specialization, and it is one of the most influential paragraphs in the history of economics. It is also, read from the vantage point of this book, an early and remarkably clean description of exactly the kind of work later chapters will spend the most time on: task fragmented into its smallest, most repeatable, least meaningful components, justified entirely by its output, with no claim made or needed that the wire-straightener found wire-straightening interesting. Smith did not think that mattered. For a long time, most of the people running factories did not think it mattered either.

It is worth being precise about what "the rest" actually included, because economic pressure did not just get people to show up. It shaped how hard they worked once they arrived, how long they were willing to tolerate unpleasant conditions, how much risk they would accept, and how much say they expected to have over the content of their own day. A workforce motivated primarily by the fear of not being paid is a workforce that will show up sick, that will not object loudly to a dangerous machine, that will not ask whether the work itself is interesting, because none of those questions change whether the rent gets paid this month. Early industrial labor conditions — the child labor, the sixteen-hour shifts, the maiming machinery operated by exhausted twelve-year-olds that Parliamentary inquiries in the 1830s and 1840s documented in grim, extensive detail — were not simply the product of employer cruelty, though there was plenty of that. They were what a labor market looks like when the only real motivational lever anyone is pulling is necessity, at full strength, unmoderated by anything else.

The resistance to this arrangement, where it existed, rarely took the form of an argument that work should be more meaningful. It took the form of a fight over its most brutal edges. Britain's Factory Acts, passed in stages across the 1830s and 1840s after Parliamentary commissions documented children as young as five or six working mill shifts of twelve hours or longer, restricted child labor, capped working hours for women and young people, and eventually mandated basic safety measures around the machinery that had, by the inquiries' own accounting, mangled and killed workers with some regularity. None of this legislation questioned whether the wage relationship itself was the right foundation for organizing labor. It questioned only how far employers should be permitted to push the arrangement before the state intervened. The underlying deal — you work, you are paid, necessity enforces the rest — survived every round of nineteenth-century reform essentially intact, because the reforms were aimed at its worst abuses, not its basic structure.

Over the following century and a half, a great deal of what we now think of as labor reform, management theory, and eventually the entire field of organizational psychology can be read as a long, halting argument about whether necessity alone was actually sufficient — whether a workforce driven purely by economic pressure was even good for the employer's own bottom line, let alone for the workers. Factory owners in the early twentieth century began to notice, sometimes accidentally, that workers who were merely compelled rather than engaged produced worse quality, more waste, and more turnover than the raw wage-labor model predicted. The most famous accidental discovery of this kind came out of a set of productivity experiments conducted at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works plant near Chicago between 1924 and 1932. Researchers, initially studying illumination levels on the assumption that better lighting would straightforwardly raise output, instead found that productivity in a small test group of relay assemblers rose after nearly every change the researchers made — brighter lights, dimmer lights, more breaks, fewer breaks — in a pattern that made no sense if illumination or rest periods were the actual mechanism at work. The researchers who eventually took over the study, the Harvard-affiliated team of Fritz Roethlisberger and William Dickson, argued that what had actually changed was the workers' sense of being observed, consulted, and treated as participants in something rather than as interchangeable inputs to a machine. That interpretation, later popularized well beyond academic circles as the now-famous "Hawthorne effect," a term that has since entered the general vocabulary of experimental design far beyond industrial psychology specifically, has been picked apart considerably by methodological critics over the following decades, who have pointed out small sample sizes, confounded variables including a change in supervisors and the removal of two underperforming workers partway through the study, and a general absence of the kind of controls a modern behavioral economist would insist on. This book will not lean on the Hawthorne studies as settled science; they are not. But the instinct behind why it became so famous — the sense among a generation of industrial managers that something other than the wage was clearly operating on their workers, and that they did not have a good vocabulary for what it was — is the exact gap this book spends its middle chapters trying to fill in with better evidence than Hawthorne ever actually provided.

None of this reform trajectory ever fully displaced economic pressure as the load-bearing motivator. It could not, because the underlying fact Thompson identified never went away: most people, in a wage economy, work because they need the money, and every other motive operates on top of that foundation rather than instead of it. Even the most engaged, purpose-driven employee at the most enlightened twenty-first-century company would, if the paycheck stopped, eventually stop coming in. What changed over time was not whether necessity mattered, but how much additional weight employers and workers alike came to expect other motives to carry, and how visible those other motives became once labor movements, minimum-wage laws, unemployment insurance, and eventually a substantial managerial and consulting industry made it harder for firms to rely on raw desperation the way nineteenth-century mill owners once could.

By the middle of the twentieth century, in the wealthier parts of the industrialized world, something like a settlement had formed. Workers were not, on the whole, one missed paycheck away from starvation the way a Manchester textile worker in 1840 plausibly was; unemployment insurance, pensions, and a general rise in real wages had thickened the cushion beneath economic pressure without eliminating it. Employers, for their part, had absorbed decades of evidence — some of it rigorous, some of it as loose as Hawthorne — suggesting that pure compulsion produced mediocre work, and had begun building entire departments, eventually called "human resources," dedicated to managing the softer motivational levers: training, career pathways, recognition programs, a sense of belonging to something larger than a paycheck. None of this dismantled the wage relationship Thompson described. It built a second story on top of the same foundation, one increasingly furnished with the language of purpose, growth, and culture, while the ground floor — you work, you are paid; you do not work, you are not paid — remained structurally unchanged and, for the most part, unexamined.

That settlement is the one now being tested, unevenly and mostly by accident, by the entry-level displacement described in the previous chapter. It is tested unevenly because economic pressure was never distributed evenly to begin with. A senior professional with fifteen years of savings, an established reputation, and a mortgage half paid off experiences a slow AI-driven softening of demand for junior-level tasks very differently than a twenty-three-year-old two years out of school with a student loan, no savings, and a resume that depends entirely on getting hired somewhere long enough to build the first rung of experience. The thinning of the floor identified in the ADP and ONET data is landing, first, on precisely the group with the thinnest cushion above the original nineteenth-century foundation — the group for whom "you do not work, you are not paid, and eventually you cannot eat" remains closer to a literal description of their situation than it has been for most workers in the wealthy world for decades.

This is why the history matters and is not simply scene-setting. The entire architecture of modern work — the training ladders, the apprenticeship structures, the implicit deal in which a junior employee accepts low pay and unglamorous tasks in exchange for eventually being trusted with better ones — was built on the assumption that economic necessity would reliably deliver a stream of people willing to take that deal, because the alternative to taking it was going hungry. Nobody had to ask whether a twenty-two-year-old paralegal found document review intrinsically fascinating. The wage, and the fear beneath the wage, made the question unnecessary. What happens to that architecture, and to the people moving through it, when the wage becomes less reliably available at exactly the rung where the deal has always been struck, is a question the settlement described in this chapter was never designed to answer, because it never had to.

It is tempting, at this point, to reach for a simple prescription: pay people a guaranteed income, remove the fear, and let the better motives — curiosity, purpose, mastery — take over. Later chapters will examine exactly this proposal, in the form of real basic-income trials run in Finland and Kenya, and the honest answer is more complicated and more interesting than either the optimists or the skeptics of guaranteed income usually admit. But before those trials can be understood, it is necessary to know what the "better motives" actually are, in more precise terms than the vague managerial language of purpose and engagement usually provides. That is what a specific, empirically grounded framework — developed not by historians of labor but by two management consultants studying, decades later, exactly the question Hawthorne's researchers stumbled into without quite being able to name — sets out to do. It divides the reasons people work into six categories, only three of which reliably make people better at their jobs the longer those reasons are relied upon, and it puts economic pressure, precisely, on the wrong side of that line.

None of this would matter much as history if the wage-and-necessity settlement Thompson described were simply an artifact of the nineteenth century, replaced by something wiser and more humane by the time anyone reading this book went looking for their first job. It is not. The settlement is still the operating system underneath the modern labor market, purpose statements and engagement surveys notwithstanding, which is exactly why a thinning of the necessity floor at the entry level is not a minor technical adjustment. It is a change to the deal itself, made without anyone renegotiating it, and understanding why is the subject of the next chapter.
