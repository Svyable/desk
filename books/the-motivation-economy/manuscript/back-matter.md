# Back Matter

## About the Book

*The Motivation Economy* is a book about a floor that most institutions never had to build, because necessity built it for them.

For roughly two centuries, the reason most people worked was not a mystery: they needed the money. That fact organized careers, schools, cities, and retirement ages without anyone having to argue for it. It is not reliably true anymore at the bottom of the ladder, where generative AI is absorbing the well-specified, low-stakes cognitive tasks that used to double as a young worker's training ground.

The book does not argue that artificial intelligence is ending work, or that a crisis of a particular size and shape is guaranteed to arrive on a particular schedule. It asks a narrower and, this book believes, more useful question: a science of what makes human effort durable rather than merely compliant already exists, built by researchers who had never heard of a large language model — what does that science say happens next, and what does the historical and cross-national record show about what actually fills the space when economic necessity loosens its grip, deliberately or by accident.

## Notes and Sources

The manuscript was researched chapter by chapter using a source ledger maintained alongside the book, following the same sourcing discipline used elsewhere on this Desk.

The sourcing hierarchy is deliberate. Original academic papers, first-party organizational documents, government and central-bank data, and named research organizations' own published reports are preferred for claims they can answer directly. Contemporaneous journalism is used chiefly for events whose timing or reception is itself part of the claim, such as how a viral video or a trial's results were received. Historical claims are supported by historical scholarship rather than by retrospective anecdote alone.

Every figure describing AI adoption, hiring, or displacement in 2025 and 2026 is a snapshot of a technology and a labor market still actively changing, not a settled historical fact; the source ledger records the date each figure was gathered so a reader can tell an observed result from a forecast or a still-developing trend. Company and platform usage data are treated as one company's or platform's own data, not as a full census of the economy. Trial results from Finland, Kenya, the UK, and Iceland are treated as evidence about the specific populations and durations studied, not as automatic proof of what a differently designed program would produce elsewhere.

Where the evidence is genuinely contested — the causal debate over deaths of despair, the decades-long argument over whether extrinsic rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, the cross-cultural critique of self-determination theory — the manuscript keeps the counter-evidence and the live disagreement rather than resolving it for narrative convenience. The goal throughout has been an argument strong enough to survive its own best objections, not one that wins by leaving them out.

The research files accompanying this manuscript contain the chapter-level source ledger, the research brief, and the chapter-by-chapter argument briefs. Those materials are the canonical source record for the draft.

## Acknowledgments

This book depends on the work of researchers who published their methods as well as their results, across disciplines that rarely share a bibliography: comparative psychologists studying curious monkeys and paid undergraduates, labor economists studying lottery windfalls and payroll records, government statisticians in Helsinki and Tokyo willing to publish uncomfortable numbers, and a development-economics research team still, as of this book's writing, several years from the final results of an experiment it had the patience to design for twelve.
