# The Machines Take the Floor

Return, for a moment, to the number that opened this book: employment for workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five, in the occupations most exposed to generative AI, shrinking at close to 3.8 percent a year as of mid-2026, according to the Canaries Dashboard built by Erik Brynjolfsson and ADP Research. The first chapter used that figure to introduce the book's central question. This chapter earns the right to use it as evidence, by walking through what it actually measures, how confident the underlying research can reasonably be, and what a fuller accounting of the available 2025 and 2026 data adds to the picture — including the substantial parts of that data that complicate, rather than confirm, a simple AI-took-the-jobs narrative.

The methodology behind the exposure measure deserves a second, more careful pass than the introductory chapter had room for. Brynjolfsson's research team did not invent a new occupational classification system from scratch. They built on the U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET database, which has, for decades, broken down thousands of American occupations into their constituent tasks — not job titles, but the actual granular activities a worker in that occupation performs, from "prepare financial statements" to "operate industrial sewing machines." Researchers then assessed which of those specific tasks fell within the demonstrated capability of large language models, drawing on published benchmark performance rather than vendor claims, and aggregated task-level exposure scores up to the occupation level. This approach has real advantages over cruder methods that simply ask survey respondents whether they think their job is "at risk from AI" — a question vulnerable to media hype, personal anxiety, and inconsistent understanding of what current AI systems can actually do. It also has real limitations: task taxonomies age quickly in a period of rapid technological change, benchmark performance on a standardized test does not always translate cleanly into reliable performance on the messy, context-dependent version of the same task inside a real organization, and the exposure score says nothing directly about whether an employer will actually choose to substitute a model for a worker, as opposed to using the model to make the same worker more productive at unchanged headcount.

That last distinction — substitution versus augmentation — is the one on which the entire debate about AI's labor-market effects ultimately turns, and it is worth stating plainly that the aggregate data as of 2026 does not resolve it cleanly in either direction. The Anthropic Economic Index, drawn from aggregated and anonymized records of how people actually use Claude across millions of conversations, offers a genuinely useful complementary window onto this question, because it measures realized usage rather than theoretical exposure. Its March 2026 report found that by February of that year, 49 percent of jobs in its sample had Claude used for at least a quarter of their tasks, up from 36 percent a year earlier — a meaningful jump, but one that leaves slightly more than half of sampled jobs below that threshold, and says nothing by itself about whether the usage that does occur is replacing a worker's hours or simply compressing the time a task used to take. Anthropic's own labor-market analysis, published separately and explicitly designed to look for direct evidence of displacement, reported finding no clear broad unemployment signal in the occupations most exposed to its models as of early 2026. The one effect it did identify with reasonable confidence was narrower and more specific: hiring of workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five into the most exposed roles running roughly 14 percent below where a reasonable counterfactual trend would have put it. Two independent research efforts — one built on payroll records at ADP, the other built on usage patterns inside a single company's own AI products — converged on a strikingly similar conclusion: not mass displacement of existing workers, but a narrowing door for new entrants.

The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025* adds a third, differently constructed vantage point, and it is worth taking seriously precisely because its overall framing runs somewhat against the more alarming reading of the ADP and Anthropic data. The report is built from a large-scale survey of employers, representing a substantial share of the global workforce across dozens of countries and industries, asked to project how their own workforce needs would change through 2030. The headline figures — 170 million new roles projected to be created, against 92 million projected to be displaced, for a net gain of roughly 78 million jobs — describe an economy that employers themselves expect to keep growing in aggregate employment, even as it churns beneath the surface. Roughly 22 percent of today's jobs are expected to be touched by significant disruption, and about 39 percent of the specific skills those jobs currently require are expected to change within five years. Notably, several of the occupations the report identifies as fastest-growing in absolute terms are not glamorous AI-adjacent specialties at all: delivery drivers, care workers, educators, and farmworkers all appear among the categories expected to add the largest number of jobs, work that is, for reasons ranging from physical dexterity to the specific value of human presence in caregiving, considerably harder to hand to a language model than the retrieval-and-drafting tasks discussed in earlier chapters.

Reading these three sources together, rather than reaching for whichever single one best supports a predetermined conclusion, produces a more disciplined and more interesting picture than either the most alarmed or the most dismissive commentary on AI and jobs typically offers. The aggregate labor market, at least as of the 2026 data available to this book, is not visibly shrinking; the World Economic Forum's employer survey and the absence of a broad unemployment signal in Anthropic's own analysis both point toward continued net job creation rather than net job destruction, at least at the scale currently observable. What is shrinking, with more specific and more replicated evidence behind it, is a particular kind of opportunity: entry into certain occupations, at a certain career stage, for tasks that fall inside a fairly well-defined band of AI capability. The effect is real, dated, and measured by two independent methodologies using two different data sources. It is also narrower than a general-purpose "AI is destroying jobs" headline would suggest, and this book's argument depends on getting that narrowness right rather than overselling the scale of what has actually been documented.

One further data point from the Anthropic Economic Index is worth including here because it cuts against the simplest version of an AI-hollows-out-the-middle-class story, even as it supports the entry-level-specific version this book is making. Between January 2025 and February 2026, the average hourly wage of people using Claude.ai fell modestly, from about $49.30 to $47.90 — a decline, but one that left Claude's user base still earning well above the broader national average hourly wage of roughly $37.30 over the same period. In other words, AI usage as of 2026 remained concentrated among relatively well-paid workers, even as the pool of users widened and average earnings within that pool drifted downward, a pattern consistent with adoption spreading outward from an initial core of highly paid early adopters — likely software engineers, consultants, and other knowledge workers with both the tools and the professional latitude to experiment with a new technology — toward a broader and somewhat lower-paid population, without yet reaching deeply into the lowest-wage segments of the economy, where the physical and interpersonal nature of much of the work remains, for now, outside a language model's reach. The picture that emerges is not of AI displacing labor from the bottom of the wage distribution upward, but of AI adoption spreading from the top of the *skill and task* distribution downward — concentrated first in cognitively intensive, computer-based work, regardless of whether that work happens to pay a junior salary or a senior one, which is precisely why the effect shows up first as a generational divide within white-collar occupations rather than as a simple high-wage-versus-low-wage story.

The sectoral unevenness in this data is worth naming explicitly as well, because a reader working outside law, consulting, software, and finance — the industries that dominate most public discussion of AI's labor-market effects — could reasonably wonder whether any of this applies to them at all. It largely does not, or at least not yet, to the same degree. Healthcare occupations requiring physical examination, hands-on procedures, or a trusted human presence during a frightening diagnosis; skilled trades such as electrical work, plumbing, and equipment repair, which require physical manipulation of unpredictable real-world environments; and a wide range of care work, from childcare to eldercare, that depends on sustained human relationship rather than information processing, all score consistently low on the occupational exposure measures this chapter has described, and none of them feature prominently in the Anthropic usage data. The World Economic Forum's inclusion of care roles, delivery drivers, and farmworkers among its fastest-growing job categories through 2030 is not an inconsistency in the data. It is the other half of the same picture: AI's current capability profile maps unevenly onto the economy, concentrated heavily in exactly the computer-mediated, text-and-data-centric occupations that also happen to include most traditional white-collar entry-level career paths, while leaving large swaths of the labor market, for now, largely untouched by the specific mechanism this book is describing.

It is worth naming, directly, specifically, and without euphemism, the exact task categories the underlying research consistently identifies as most exposed to current AI capability, because the specificity matters more than any aggregate percentage. Across the ADP occupational exposure scoring and the Anthropic task-usage data, the categories that recur most consistently are information retrieval — finding, extracting, and organizing existing information rather than generating original analysis; summarization — condensing a longer document, call, or meeting into a shorter one without necessarily adding new judgment; formatting and routine document production — turning content that already exists into a specific required shape, whether a slide deck, a citation table, or a structured report; scheduling and coordination — the logistical work of arranging meetings, tracking calendars, and managing routine correspondence; and first-draft generation — producing an initial version of a document, email, or piece of code that a more experienced person will substantially revise before it is used. Every one of these categories shares a structural feature worth naming explicitly: each is a task with a comparatively well-specified target output, evaluable against a reasonably clear standard, and comparatively low individual stakes if a single instance goes wrong, because a human reviewer downstream typically catches and corrects errors before they reach a client, a patient, or a courtroom. These are, not coincidentally, almost exactly the properties that made these same tasks suitable, historically, to hand to the newest, least experienced person in an organization — safe to delegate precisely because the cost of an individual mistake was low and correctable.

The skills-gap figures buried inside the World Economic Forum's report deserve more attention than they typically receive in coverage that leads with the headline job-creation number, because they point toward the same underlying tension this chapter has been building toward from a different angle. If close to 39 percent of the skills a typical job requires are expected to change within five years, and 63 percent of employers surveyed already cite the resulting skills gap as the primary barrier they face in adapting their workforce, then the practical question facing both workers and employers is not simply whether enough jobs exist in aggregate, but whether the pipeline that used to convert an inexperienced newcomer into a skilled contributor can keep functioning while the specific skills it was built to teach are themselves in flux. A training pipeline designed around teaching document review, citation-checking, and first-draft writing does not automatically know how to retrain itself to teach reviewing and correcting AI-generated output instead, particularly when the organizations running that pipeline are, by the same report's own numbers, already citing a skills gap as their most pressing constraint. The World Economic Forum's broadly optimistic net-job-creation headline and the same report's own data on employer-reported skills-gap anxiety are not actually in tension with each other. They describe two different questions — how many jobs will exist, and how smoothly the workforce can be reshaped to fill them — and this book's argument lives almost entirely inside the second question rather than the first.

There is a further comparison worth drawing, this time to a technological transition considerably older and considerably better studied over a longer historical horizon than either cloud computing or generative AI: the introduction of spreadsheet software into corporate accounting and financial-analysis departments beginning in the early 1980s. Visicalc and its successors eliminated, within a remarkably short period, enormous volumes of manual ledger work that had previously occupied large teams of junior bookkeepers and clerks, and popular commentary at the time predicted, with a confidence this book's own chapters have tried studiously to avoid, that the accounting profession itself would substantially shrink as a result. The actual outcome, well documented in subsequent labor-economics research on the period, was more complicated: total employment in accounting and related financial-analysis occupations grew rather than shrank over the following decades, even as the specific tasks junior accountants performed shifted dramatically away from manual calculation and toward the kind of analysis, judgment, and client interaction spreadsheets had freed up capacity for. This precedent offers genuine grounds for cautious optimism about the AI transition examined in this chapter, though this book will resist over-relying on it for the same reason it has resisted over-relying on any single historical parallel throughout: the spreadsheet transition unfolded across a much slower diffusion curve than generative AI's, giving training pipelines, professional associations, and individual workers considerably more calendar time to adapt than the compressed 2023-2026 timeline this chapter's data describes.

It is also worth noting, briefly, that the specific data this chapter has relied on is heavily weighted toward the United States, where ADP's payroll data and the New York Federal Reserve's graduate-unemployment series both originate, and toward the kind of large, formal, Anglophone employers well represented in both the Anthropic usage data and the World Economic Forum's global employer survey. The dynamic this book describes is very likely not confined to the United States — AI adoption in white-collar, English-language-dominant knowledge work is a global phenomenon, and several of the case studies examined later in this book, including South Korea's youth labor-market withdrawal and Japan's hikikomori population, describe adjacent patterns of entry-level and early-career difficulty in other wealthy economies with very different institutional structures. But this book will not claim, on the strength of primarily American data, that the specific magnitude and timeline described in this chapter applies uniformly around the world. Labor-market institutions, apprenticeship traditions, social-insurance generosity, and the specific mix of industries in a given economy all shape how a given technological shift actually lands on real workers, and a reader in a country with a stronger vocational-training tradition, a more generous unemployment system, or a different industrial mix should expect the details, if not necessarily the underlying mechanism, to look somewhat different.

This convergence is the analytical heart of the book's argument, and it is worth stating it once more, now with the fuller evidentiary base this chapter has assembled, in its most precise form: the category of task that generative AI systems currently perform best, as measured independently by occupational exposure scoring, realized usage data, and employer projections, overlaps heavily with the category of task that entry-level workers have historically been assigned, not because that overlap was inevitable, but because both AI systems and inexperienced humans share a common limitation relative to an expert — neither can yet be trusted with high-stakes judgment calls under genuine uncertainty, and both are best deployed, for now, on well-specified, low-stakes, correctable work. The difference, and it is the difference this book's remaining chapters spend most of their attention on, is that a junior human doing that work is also, whether anyone designed it this way or not, building toward the judgment an AI system is not currently building toward at all. An AI model that gets better at summarizing documents this year does not thereby develop the accumulated pattern recognition, professional relationships, and hard-won intuition a junior analyst doing the same task would have developed doing it imperfectly, under supervision, for two or three years. The task looks the same from outside. What it is doing for the person, or the system, performing it is not.

There is a further wrinkle worth including here, because it complicates the picture in a direction that is genuinely uncertain rather than simply comforting. Some employers and some industry commentary through 2025 and 2026 have begun describing a deliberate redesign of junior roles, rather than their simple elimination, in response to exactly the dynamic this chapter has described — restructuring entry-level positions to spend less time on the now-automatable retrieval-and-drafting tasks and more time earlier than before on judgment-adjacent work: reviewing and correcting AI-generated output, which some firms argue builds a different but comparably valuable form of expertise, closer to editing than to drafting. Whether review-heavy training actually builds the same depth of tacit, pattern-recognition-based competence that write-heavy training used to build is, as Chapter One noted, a genuinely open empirical question, one that will not be settled by philosophical argument but by longitudinal data on how today's AI-native junior cohort performs five and ten years into their careers — data that, as of 2026, simply does not yet exist, because not enough time has passed. This book will not pretend to know the answer in advance. It will say, based on the self-determination theory and flow research covered in earlier chapters, that reviewing a solution and originating one under genuine uncertainty are not obviously equivalent experiences for competence-building, and that an economy currently running this experiment at scale, on an entire generation of new workers, without much deliberate study of its effects, is taking a risk that deserves more attention than it has so far received.

It is worth adding one further comparative data point that helps calibrate the scale of the 2025-2026 disruption against the technology industry's own prior adjustment periods, because AI is not the first general-purpose technology to reshape entry-level knowledge work within recent memory, and the comparison offers a useful, if imperfect, sense of proportion. The rollout of enterprise cloud computing across the 2010s eliminated a considerable volume of routine systems-administration and infrastructure-maintenance work that had previously occupied junior IT staff, prompting a broadly similar wave of concern about entry-level pathways into technology careers at the time; the profession adapted over roughly five to seven years, largely by redirecting junior training toward cloud-architecture and automation skills rather than the server-maintenance tasks the cloud had absorbed, though the adaptation was neither instant nor universally smooth, and some smaller firms and less well-resourced training programs lagged considerably behind the pace larger, better-capitalized organizations managed. If AI-driven displacement of entry-level cognitive tasks follows a broadly similar multi-year adaptation curve rather than a more abrupt one, the current period of acute disruption documented in this chapter's data may prove to be a difficult but ultimately survivable transition, comparable in shape if not in exact mechanism to the cloud-computing transition a decade earlier. This book offers the comparison as a plausible reference point rather than a confident prediction, since the breadth of tasks generative AI touches is considerably wider than cloud computing's more narrowly technical scope, and the comparison could easily understate rather than overstate the difficulty of the adjustment ahead.

One final methodological caution belongs in this chapter rather than left implicit: every figure discussed here is a snapshot of a moving target, gathered in the middle of a technological transition whose ultimate shape nobody, including the researchers producing these figures, currently knows. The ADP exposure scores will need updating as models gain new capabilities; the Anthropic usage data reflects one company's products among several major competitors whose own usage patterns are not fully public; and the World Economic Forum's 2030 projections are, by their own admission, projections rather than realized outcomes, built from employer surveys that ask people to forecast their own future behavior under conditions of considerable uncertainty. This book treats all three sources the way careful research requires: as the best available evidence as of 2026, not as a settled final verdict, and readers encountering this book some years after its writing should expect the specific numbers to have moved, even if the underlying mechanism this chapter has documented — AI capability concentrated disproportionately in well-specified, low-stakes, computer-mediated tasks, colliding directly with an economy that had historically assigned exactly those tasks to its newest and least experienced workers — continues, on the evidence available today, to hold.

The remaining chapters of this section turn from the aggregate, occupation-level data this chapter has covered toward the specific, concentrated experience of the workers most directly inside this transition: the ones for whom the floor described in Chapter One is not an abstraction in a payroll dataset, but the actual, lived difficulty of trying to start a career at the exact moment the traditional first rung became harder to find.
