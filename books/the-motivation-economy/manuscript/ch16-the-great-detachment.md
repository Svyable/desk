# The Great Detachment

On July 25, 2022, a twenty-four-year-old software engineer and part-time musician living in New York City named Zaid Khan sat down and posted a brief, seventeen-second video to the short-form video platform TikTok, shot without any particular production value or apparent expectation that it would travel far beyond his existing followers, that would, within weeks, reshape how a large share of the working public talked about their jobs. Khan's message, delivered in a calm, almost conversational tone rather than the outraged register so much viral workplace content adopts, was simple and delivered without particular urgency: he was not literally quitting his job or walking off the payroll, he explained carefully, but he was quitting the deeper idea that a person's worth should be defined primarily by their labor, and quitting, specifically, the pervasive hustle-culture expectation that going visibly above and beyond — working late nights, chasing every available promotion, treating one's job as the single organizing center of one's entire personal identity — was simply, automatically, what any good and loyal employee was supposed to do without complaint. "You're still performing your duties," Khan said, in the video's now widely quoted phrasing, "but you're no longer subscribing to the hustle culture mentality that work has to be your life." He called the stance quiet quitting, a phrase that would go on to accumulate well over seventeen million views under its own hashtag within a matter of months, spark an intense and often contentious media cycle, and eventually become one of the defining pieces of workplace vocabulary of the decade.

The underlying concept, once stripped away from its viral social-media packaging and its catchy, slightly provocative name, was not actually a new discovery at all, and Khan himself was not claiming originality for the underlying idea, only for the specific framing and the memorable name. Doing precisely what a job description requires, no more and no less, has always been an available option for any employee, and the phenomenon labor economists and organizational psychologists had previously studied under considerably less catchy names — reduced discretionary effort, withdrawal behavior, psychological disengagement — describes essentially the same underlying pattern this book's Chapter Three identified through Doshi and McGregor's framework as the behavioral signature of inertia, the sixth and least productive of the six motives: continuing to show up not because of play, purpose, potential, or even active economic or emotional pressure, but simply because showing up is the path requiring the least active decision. What made quiet quitting different, and what made it spread as fast and as widely as it did, was not the underlying behavior but its sudden, viral, socially sanctioned articulation — a large number of workers who had already been operating this way, often privately and with some accompanying guilt, discovering simultaneously that millions of others were doing the same thing, and that a twenty-four-year-old on TikTok had just given the behavior a name that made it sound like a deliberate, defensible stance rather than a personal failing.

Gallup, which has tracked American and global workplace engagement continuously since the early 2000s using a consistent, well-validated twelve-item survey instrument administered to hundreds of thousands of employees across dozens of countries every year, was unusually well positioned among the organizations that weighed in on the viral moment to test whether it reflected a genuine, measurable shift in underlying employee behavior or merely a catchy new label attached to an already-existing baseline of disengagement that had simply lacked a memorable name until Khan's video supplied one. The organization's data going into 2022 already showed a worrying trend that predated Khan's video by several years: engagement among workers under thirty-five had fallen by roughly six percentage points between 2019 and 2022, and Gallup's own contemporaneous commentary on the quiet-quitting phenomenon was notably unsentimental about where responsibility lay, stating plainly that the pattern was, in the organization's assessment, primarily a symptom of poor management rather than a symptom of employee laziness or entitlement — a framing that, notably, treats disengagement as a rational response to an unmet need rather than as a character flaw, directly consistent with the self-determination-theory framework this book examined in Chapter Seven, in which unmet autonomy, competence, or relatedness needs predict exactly this kind of withdrawal rather than active sabotage.

Roughly two and a half years after Khan's video, Gallup gave a related but distinct pattern its own name, in a research note that generated a second, somewhat quieter wave of workplace-culture commentary: the Great Detachment. Where quiet quitting described workers doing the minimum while remaining in place, Gallup's 2024 and 2025 data pointed to something with an additional, more structural dimension — workers reporting historically low satisfaction with their organizations, historically weak connection to their employer's stated mission, and simultaneously, a historically diminished ability or willingness to actually leave for something better, given a cooling job market that had made switching jobs considerably riskier than it had been during the tight labor market of 2021 and 2022. Gallup's survey data found connection to organizational mission falling from 38 percent in March 2021 to a record low of 30 percent by February 2024, alongside job satisfaction sitting at just 18 percent, and 51 percent of employees reporting they were struggling to find new job opportunities — the highest share Gallup had recorded since 2015. The resulting picture, drawn from a large survey conducted in late 2024 covering over twenty-two thousand employees, was of a workforce simultaneously more disengaged and more trapped than at almost any point in the survey's multi-decade history: neither meaningfully committed to their current employer nor able to credibly threaten to leave, a combination Gallup identified as producing its own distinct and corrosive dynamic, since a workforce with no realistic exit option loses one of the more effective informal pressures that normally pushes organizations to address legitimate employee grievances.

By 2026, Gallup's flagship annual research, the *State of the Global Workplace* report, found the underlying engagement trend had continued rather than reversed: global employee engagement fell to 20 percent in 2025, down from a 2022 peak of 23 percent, marking the first time in the history of Gallup's tracking that global engagement had declined for two consecutive years running, with every major world region showing flat or declining engagement rather than any region bucking the broader trend. The 2025 data broke the global workforce into three familiar categories — 20 percent actively engaged, 64 percent simply not engaged, and 16 percent actively disengaged, meaning actively working against their employer's interests rather than merely failing to advance them — a distribution in which the large "not engaged" middle category — neither actively working against their employer's interests nor genuinely invested in advancing them, dominated instead by workers whose relationship to their job most closely resembles Doshi and McGregor's inertia motive from Chapter Three — constitutes nearly two-thirds of the entire measured global workforce, the single largest and, on this book's argument, single most consequential category in the whole distribution.

The economic scale Gallup attaches to this pattern is worth stating plainly, because it moves the discussion from an abstract cultural observation to a figure large enough to command attention from people who might otherwise dismiss workplace engagement as a soft, secondary business metric. Gallup's 2026 *State of the Global Workplace* report estimated that low engagement costs the global economy roughly ten trillion dollars in lost productivity annually, a figure built from Gallup's own methodology comparing the output of highly engaged teams and organizations against the much larger population operating at the "not engaged" or "actively disengaged" levels described above. This book will not treat Gallup's specific dollar figure as a precisely verified external fact — it is a proprietary estimate produced by an organization with a direct commercial interest in organizations taking workplace engagement seriously, a caveat this book applies consistently to every commercially produced statistic it cites, including Doshi and McGregor's ToMo research in Chapter Three. What the estimate does establish, regardless of its precise magnitude, is that Gallup itself, drawing on decades of accumulated survey data across dozens of countries and industries, regards the scale of the disengagement problem as economically significant enough to warrant headline billing in its flagship annual report, rather than as a marginal or cosmetic concern.

A related and particularly striking finding in Gallup's more recent data concerns managers specifically, a population whose own engagement had historically outpaced that of the individual contributors reporting to them, on the reasonable theory that people promoted into management roles tend to be more invested in their organizations to begin with. That gap has been closing rapidly and in a troubling direction: Gallup's tracking found manager engagement falling from 31 percent in 2022 to 27 percent in 2024 and then to 22 percent in 2025, a five-point single-year decline that Gallup's researchers flagged as particularly consequential, because managers function, in Gallup's own long-standing research framework, as the single largest lever affecting the engagement of the employees who report to them — a manager's own disengagement does not stay contained to that manager's individual experience, but propagates downward through the team she leads, compounding the broader workforce-level disengagement this chapter has already documented. A disengaged manager is considerably less likely to provide the kind of autonomy-supportive, competence-building, relatedness-fostering supervision that Baard, Deci, and Ryan's 2004 corporate field study, examined in Chapter Seven, found predicted stronger performance and psychological adjustment among the employees beneath her, meaning the Great Detachment's managerial dimension risks becoming self-reinforcing: disengaged managers producing disengaged direct reports, some share of whom will themselves eventually be promoted into management roles carrying the same disengagement forward into the next generation of the organizational hierarchy.

The Great Detachment is worth situating within a broader international context as well, since Gallup's tracking, while global in scope, has shown considerable regional variation worth acknowledging rather than treating the American-centric coverage of quiet quitting and the Great Detachment as representative of a uniform worldwide pattern. Gallup's regional breakdowns have consistently found engagement levels varying substantially across world regions, with the United States and Canada historically reporting higher engagement than European regions, which have long lagged behind on Gallup's measures for reasons the organization's own researchers attribute partly to cultural differences in how respondents interpret and answer engagement survey questions, and partly to genuine differences in management practice and workplace autonomy across regions. The 2025 finding that no major world region increased engagement year over year is significant precisely because it cuts across this otherwise persistent regional variation, suggesting whatever combination of forces is driving the decline operates at least partly independent of the specific cultural and management-practice differences that normally separate one region's engagement scores from another's — a pattern more consistent with a shared, cross-regional driver, such as the post-pandemic labor-market shifts Gallup's own researchers emphasize, than with a purely regional or culturally specific explanation.

There is a related term, somewhat less prominent than quiet quitting or the Great Detachment but circulating in overlapping workplace-culture discourse across the same period, worth mentioning briefly here for its conceptual precision: boreout, coined by consultants as a deliberate counterpart to the long-established concept of burnout, describing chronic under-stimulation and a sense of purposelessness at work rather than chronic overwork and exhaustion. Where burnout research, developed over decades by occupational psychologists, describes the costs of too much pressure applied for too long, boreout describes the mirror-image cost of too little challenge, too little purpose, and too little of the potential motive from Chapter Three's framework, sustained over a comparably long period — a useful reminder that the six-motive framework this book has relied on throughout predicts genuine harm at both extremes, not merely from excessive pressure but from its complete absence when nothing meaningful has been built to replace it, precisely the dynamic this book's chapters on Japan's hikikomori population and South Korea's resting generation examined at a far more severe scale.

It would be a significant overreach for this book to claim that this accumulating disengagement data is caused by AI-driven displacement specifically, and this chapter will not make that claim. Gallup's own analysis of the Great Detachment attributes the pattern primarily to a combination of post-pandemic shifts in employee expectations around work-life balance and flexibility, weak middle-management practices that Gallup has documented as a persistent, long-running problem well predating any AI-specific concern, and the specific tightness-to-slackness swing in the broader labor market between 2021 and 2024 — factors that have essentially nothing directly to do with generative AI or the entry-level task displacement examined in earlier chapters of this book. What this chapter argues instead is narrower and, this book believes, more defensible: the vocabulary, the measurement infrastructure, and the underlying psychological mechanism this recent wave of disengagement research has developed and refined — Doshi and McGregor's six motives, Ryan and Deci's three needs, and now Gallup's large-scale, continuously tracked engagement and detachment metrics — describe exactly the phenomenon this book would expect to worsen, from an already elevated 2026 baseline, as the specific AI-driven mechanism traced in Chapters Eight and Nine compounds whatever pressure on engagement, purpose, and organizational trust is already visible in the data before any AI-specific effect is added to it.

It is worth examining, briefly, why the Great Resignation of 2021 and the Great Detachment of 2024 through 2026 look, on the surface, like opposite phenomena — one defined by unusually high voluntary departures, the other by unusually low ones — while this book treats them as two expressions of the same underlying dynamic playing out under different labor-market conditions. The Bureau of Labor Statistics recorded a quits rate reaching 3.0 percent in November and December 2021, its highest level since the metric was first tracked in December 2000, with roughly 4.5 million workers voluntarily leaving their jobs in November 2021 alone and total voluntary quits reaching 47.8 million across 2021 and 50.5 million across 2022, concentrated disproportionately in leisure, hospitality, and other sectors where wages and working conditions had long lagged behind other parts of the economy. Most Great Resignation departures, labor economists studying the period have found, were not workers leaving employment altogether in pursuit of self-actualization, but workers moving toward better-paying, better-structured jobs made suddenly available by an unusually tight, employer-desperate labor market — in the language of this book's Chapter Three framework, workers exercising exactly the kind of exit option that a strong potential motive requires: a credible belief that leaving would lead somewhere better. The Great Detachment describes what happens to the same underlying dissatisfaction once that exit option closes: the 2024 labor market's cooling, reflected in Gallup's own finding that 51 percent of employees reported struggling to find new opportunities, removed the credible exit path without resolving any of the underlying dissatisfaction that had fueled the 2021 wave of departures, leaving workers stuck in place with their potential motive frustrated rather than fulfilled — precisely the combination this book's earlier chapters, particularly the discussion of South Korea's resting generation in Chapter Thirteen, would predict produces withdrawal and inertia rather than either departure or renewed engagement.

There is a generational dimension to this pattern worth drawing out explicitly, because it connects the Great Detachment directly back to the entry-level displacement this book opened with. Gallup's engagement data has consistently shown workers under thirty-five reporting some of the sharpest declines in engagement and organizational connection across the multi-decade tracking period, a pattern that predates the AI-specific concerns of 2025 and 2026 but that this book's earlier chapters would predict compounding rather than resolving as entry-level task volume thins and competence-building opportunities narrow in the specific ways Chapter Nine described. A young worker entering the labor market already amid historically low engagement, historically weak connection to organizational mission, and a historically difficult job-switching environment, and now also facing a narrower and more uncertain path to the kind of competence-building and career progression earlier generations could more reliably expect, is confronting a compounding rather than an isolated set of headwinds — none of which, individually, may be severe enough to produce the kind of dramatic, visible crisis examined in earlier chapters on South Korea and Japan, but which together plausibly describe an early, milder domestic version of the same underlying mechanism, developing gradually rather than announcing itself with a single dramatic statistic.

This chapter should close by being explicit about the specific evidentiary status this book is assigning to the connection it has just drawn, because the discipline of distinguishing established fact from plausible extrapolation has been maintained throughout this book and should not lapse at its most rhetorically tempting moment. Quiet quitting and the Great Detachment are well-documented, large-sample, methodologically serious findings about the current state of the global workforce. Their causal connection to the AI-driven entry-level displacement examined earlier in this book is, at this point, a hypothesis consistent with the mechanisms this book has traced — not an established causal finding, since the relevant longitudinal data, tracking the same cohorts across both the pre-AI and post-AI periods with enough precision to isolate the AI-specific contribution from the many other forces already straining workplace engagement, simply does not yet exist as of this book's writing. What can be said with confidence is that the psychological and organizational-design tools this book has assembled — the six motives, the three needs, the challenge-skill balance — are directly applicable to understanding and potentially reversing the disengagement Gallup has already documented at scale, regardless of how much or how little of that documented disengagement AI turns out to have caused specifically.

It is worth noting, finally, how much of this entire chapter's evidence depends on a research infrastructure — continuous, methodologically consistent, large-sample workplace surveying — that took Gallup decades to build and that few other organizations in the world can currently match at comparable scale or historical depth. This matters for how confidently this book can extrapolate the patterns described here into the AI-specific future its earlier chapters are concerned with, because the same infrastructure that allows Gallup to say with confidence that global engagement fell for two consecutive years running has no comparably established historical baseline yet for measuring the specific psychological effects of AI-driven task displacement on entry-level workers, a phenomenon simply too recent to have generated its own decades-long tracking instrument. Future editions of Gallup's own research, and the work of other organizations building comparable survey infrastructure, will likely be what eventually settles the question this chapter has explicitly declined to settle: how much of the Great Detachment's continued trajectory, in the years following this book's writing, should be attributed to the AI-specific mechanism traced in earlier chapters, as opposed to the broader managerial, generational, and labor-market forces Gallup's own researchers currently emphasize.

That applicability is where this book's argument now turns, deliberately and for the remainder of its length, from diagnosis toward response. The next two chapters examine, in close and specific detail, two different kinds of deliberate institutional intervention — a reduction in working hours without a corresponding reduction in pay, tested at national scale in two different countries, and a small number of individual companies that tried, with documented and imperfect success, to redesign their internal culture explicitly around the direct motives this book has spent its earlier chapters establishing as durable, rather than continuing to rely on the indirect motives whose gradual erosion this chapter has just traced across a decade of accumulating Gallup data.
