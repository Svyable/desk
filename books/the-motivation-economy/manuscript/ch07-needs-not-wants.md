# Needs, Not Wants

By the turn of the millennium, in the year 2000, Edward Deci and Richard Ryan had already spent nearly three full decades extending the original Soma-cube finding into something considerably more ambitious than a single laboratory effect. What began as a demonstration that rewards could undermine intrinsic motivation had grown, through a long sequence of studies conducted across schools, hospitals, sports teams, workplaces, and clinical settings, into a full theory of human motivation, which the two researchers, joined by a growing community of collaborators, called self-determination theory. Its centerpiece claim, published in a 2000 paper in *American Psychologist* that would go on to become one of the most cited articles in the history of the discipline, was deceptively simple: human beings have three basic psychological needs — autonomy, competence, and relatedness — and the degree to which a person's environment satisfies or frustrates those three needs predicts, with remarkable consistency across very different domains of life, whether that person's motivation will be genuinely internalized and durable, or merely controlled and fragile.

The word "need" was chosen carefully, and it is worth pausing at some length on what Ryan and Deci actually meant by it, since the term is doing real theoretical work in their argument that a softer, more casual word like "preference" or "want" simply would not do. A need, in their formulation, is not simply something a person happens to enjoy or find pleasant. It is something whose satisfaction is required for a person's psychological functioning and wellbeing to operate properly, in roughly the same structural sense that a biological need for water or protein is required for physical functioning, and whose chronic frustration produces measurable costs — in mental health, motivation, and performance — regardless of whether a given culture or individual consciously values that need or has been taught to want it. This is a strong claim, and the theory's proponents have spent the following quarter century trying to substantiate it across an unusually wide range of settings: not just laboratory puzzle-solving, but medication adherence among chronically ill patients, dropout rates in competitive youth sports, academic persistence among struggling students, and employee turnover in corporate call centers, among dozens of other applied domains.

**Autonomy**, the first of the three needs, is the one most often misunderstood, because in ordinary English the word suggests independence or freedom from other people — doing whatever you want, unconstrained by obligation. That is not what self-determination theory means by it. Autonomy, in Ryan and Deci's specific technical sense, refers to the experience of one's actions as self-endorsed and volitional — feeling that you are the author of what you are doing, even if what you are doing was requested, expected, or even required by someone else. A surgeon following a rigid, externally mandated safety checklist before every operation can experience high autonomy in Ryan and Deci's sense if she genuinely endorses the checklist's purpose and would choose to follow it even absent the mandate; a teenager doing precisely what she wants with her unstructured free time can experience low autonomy if her choices are driven by anxiety about what her peers will think rather than anything she would authentically choose on reflection. This distinction — autonomy as authentic endorsement rather than as an absence of external structure — is what allows the theory to explain a finding that would otherwise look paradoxical: employees in highly structured, rule-bound jobs sometimes report higher autonomy satisfaction than employees in nominally "flexible" jobs, because the structured job's rules are experienced as sensible and self-endorsed while the flexible job's unstructured demands are experienced as an anxious, ever-present pressure to prove oneself.

**Competence**, the second need, is the direct descendant of Robert White's 1959 effectance concept discussed in Chapter Four, refined and operationalized across decades of subsequent research: the need to feel effective, capable, and able to produce the outcomes one intends, and to experience one's own skill growing over time rather than stagnating or eroding. Self-determination theory's competence need converges directly with Csikszentmihalyi's challenge-skill balance from the previous chapter — an activity pitched too far below a person's current skill starves the competence need through boredom, while one pitched too far above it starves the same need through repeated, demoralizing failure — but the theory extends the concept beyond momentary flow states into a durable, ongoing requirement for wellbeing, one that operates whether or not a person is currently mid-task and absorbed.

**Relatedness**, the third and in some ways least expected of the three needs, given the theory's origins in individual laboratory puzzle-solving, refers to the need to feel meaningfully connected to others — cared about, and caring about others in turn, as part of a functioning social fabric rather than as an isolated economic actor optimizing alone. Its inclusion reflects a genuine theoretical evolution within the research program: Deci's earliest work, rooted in Harlow's solitary monkeys and his own solitary undergraduates working alone in a laboratory room, had comparatively little to say about the social dimension of motivation. As the theory matured through the 1980s and 1990s, incorporating research on parenting, education, and organizational behavior, it became clear that autonomy and competence alone left a substantial share of the variance in human motivation and wellbeing unexplained, and that a sense of belonging — to a family, a team, a profession, a community — did independent, measurable work in predicting whether a person's motivation for a given activity would hold up over time. Employees who report strong relatedness with coworkers and supervisors consistently show better motivational and wellbeing outcomes than equally autonomous, equally competent employees who feel isolated, a finding replicated widely enough across organizational settings that later Doshi and McGregor's own play-purpose-potential framework, discussed in Chapter Three, can be read as one of several later frameworks quietly reorganizing Ryan and Deci's three needs into a more commercially packaged vocabulary rather than discovering something entirely new.

What gives self-determination theory its unusual authority within psychology, relative to some of the other frameworks discussed in this book, is less any single dramatic finding and more the sheer accumulated breadth of its supporting evidence, gathered by an increasingly large international community of researchers over more than four decades and largely independent of the original Rochester laboratory that produced it. Meta-analyses pooling hundreds of individual studies have found the three-needs framework predicting outcomes across education, healthcare, sport, parenting, and organizational settings with a consistency that few competing motivational theories can match, and the theory has been tested, with broadly similar results, across a wide range of national and cultural contexts — a genuine strength relative to a concept like flow, whose cross-cultural claims, as the previous chapter noted, rest on a thinner and more contested evidentiary base.

That said, the theory's claim to full cross-cultural universality has been challenged by researchers working outside the individualist Western tradition in which self-determination theory originated, and the challenge deserves to be taken seriously rather than treated as a footnote. Critics working within more collectivist cultural traditions, particularly in parts of East Asia, have argued that the autonomy need in particular carries an implicit individualist bias — an assumption that self-endorsed, internally driven choice is the healthy default, when in some cultural contexts a person's sense of wellbeing may be more directly and durably tied to fulfilling role-based obligations to family or community, with less of the internal endorsement Ryan and Deci's framework treats as essential. Proponents of self-determination theory have responded to this critique with cross-cultural data of their own, arguing that even in more collectivist societies, people report better outcomes when they experience their role-based obligations as genuinely, internally endorsed rather than externally coerced — which is to say, the theory's defenders argue the autonomy need survives the challenge once autonomy is properly distinguished from individualism, a distinction that echoes the controlling-versus-informational split from Chapter Five. This book treats the debate as live and unresolved rather than settled in either direction, while noting that the sheer volume of supporting evidence, across enough different cultural contexts to make the pattern worth taking seriously, is more extensive than almost any other framework discussed in these chapters.

The theory's application to actual corporate settings, rather than laboratories or schools, is worth examining in more concrete detail than the general survey above has allowed, because it is where the stakes for this book's argument become most direct. In 2004, Deci, joined by Ryan and a third researcher, Paul Baard, published a field study of 124 executives at a branch of a Fortune 500 company, testing whether the three-needs model could predict something a laboratory study never could: real, independently rated job performance evaluations and measures of psychological adjustment, gathered from actual employees doing actual jobs rather than undergraduates solving puzzles for a few dollars. The study found that employees who perceived their managers as supportive of their autonomy — managers who explained the reasoning behind requests, acknowledged employees' perspectives even when overriding them, and offered meaningful choice where choice was genuinely available — reported higher satisfaction of all three basic needs, and that this need satisfaction, not managerial autonomy support directly, was what predicted both stronger performance evaluations and better psychological adjustment. The finding mattered methodologically because it supported the specific causal chain self-determination theory proposes — environment shapes need satisfaction, need satisfaction shapes outcomes — rather than a vaguer, harder-to-test claim that "good management" simply correlates with "good performance" for reasons the theory does not specify.

A year later, the organizational psychologist Marylène Gagné, working with Deci, published a lengthy synthesis specifically applying self-determination theory to work motivation, an article the *Journal of Organizational Behavior* later recognized, on its thirtieth anniversary, as one of the eight most influential pieces it had ever published. Gagné and Deci's contribution was to refine an idea implicit but underdeveloped in the theory's earlier laboratory work: that extrinsic motivation itself is not a single undifferentiated category, as the crude intrinsic-versus-extrinsic dichotomy of the 1970s and 1980s research had often implied, but exists along a spectrum of internalization. A worker who follows a compliance rule purely to avoid punishment sits at one end of that spectrum; a worker who has come to genuinely value the same rule's underlying purpose, even though the rule was originally externally imposed, sits much closer to autonomous motivation despite never having generated the rule herself. This refinement mattered for organizations precisely because it offered a realistic alternative to the false choice between "make every task intrinsically fun" and "rely on economic pressure and compliance" — a great deal of necessary work, the refined theory argued, could be internalized into something closer to autonomous motivation through the right managerial conditions, even when the task itself was never going to feel like play in Doshi and McGregor's narrower sense.

It is worth translating the three needs directly into the specific labor-market situation this book opened with, because the translation clarifies exactly what is structurally at risk as entry-level cognitive tasks are absorbed by AI systems, in a way none of the previous chapters could show quite so precisely. Consider what traditional entry-level knowledge work actually provided, at its structural best, against each of the three needs. Autonomy was often thin at the entry level — junior employees are, almost by professional convention, told what to do rather than asked what they think should be done — but competence-building was the entire structural point of the arrangement: a junior employee's tasks were calibrated, at least in principle, to be difficult enough to genuinely stretch a novice's still-forming skill, in exactly the challenge-skill band Csikszentmihalyi and Ryan and Deci both independently identify as necessary for engagement and growth. Relatedness, meanwhile, was frequently supplied not by the tasks themselves but by the surrounding social structure of early-career work: the cohort of fellow junior employees going through the same trial by fire together, the informal mentorship of a slightly more senior colleague, the shared, often bonding experience of surviving a demanding first year as a group.

If AI absorbs a substantial share of the specific tasks that used to supply the competence-building function — because those were, as earlier chapters established, precisely the well-specified, practice-friendly tasks a model can now perform — then the psychological need most directly threatened by the displacement described in Chapter One is not, primarily, autonomy, which entry-level work rarely provided in abundance to begin with. It is competence: the structured, incremental experience of becoming visibly, measurably better at something difficult, through repeated attempts a novice was allowed to make imperfectly while still being paid to make them. A junior employee whose job increasingly consists of reviewing and approving a model's already-competent output, rather than producing halting, imperfect first attempts of her own, is placed in a strange and understudied psychological position: closer, structurally, to a supervisor than to an apprentice, without having yet earned or built the underlying competence that would normally justify a supervisory role. Self-determination theory does not yet have a large body of direct empirical research on exactly this scenario, because it is too recent to have been studied at scale — a genuine gap in the evidence this book will flag rather than paper over — but the theory's core logic, tested across four decades and dozens of very different domains, gives a reasonably confident prediction about what ought to happen when competence-building opportunities thin out for an entire cohort of early-career workers at once: not necessarily unhappiness in any dramatic, immediately visible sense, but a slower, harder-to-detect erosion in the kind of durable, internalized motivation that self-determination theory has spent forty years showing depends on exactly the need now most directly at risk.

There is a related, more recent theoretical development worth including here, because it sharpens the framework's applicability to exactly the kind of structural, involuntary change this book is concerned with. Later self-determination theorists, working through the 2000s and 2010s, drew an increasingly careful distinction between need satisfaction and its opposite, need frustration, arguing that these are not simply two ends of a single continuum but partially separable phenomena with different consequences. Low autonomy satisfaction, on this refined view, is uncomfortable but survivable; active autonomy frustration — the specific experience of being controlled, pressured, or overridden, rather than merely under-supported — predicts more severe outcomes, including symptoms overlapping with anxiety and depression, in a growing body of clinical and organizational research. The distinction matters for the entry-level displacement question because it offers two structurally different predictions depending on how AI-driven task changes are actually implemented inside a given organization. A junior employee whose task list simply shrinks, with fewer opportunities to build competence but no active new source of control or pressure, is in the less severe, need-under-satisfaction category the theory describes. A junior employee who is actively monitored, second-guessed, or made to feel her judgment has been rendered redundant by a system she is now merely supervising, is closer to the more severe, need-frustration category — and which of these two experiences becomes the norm across a given industry will likely depend far more on specific managerial and organizational design choices than on any technical property of the AI systems themselves.

It is worth adding a brief note on how self-determination theory has itself evolved in response to the cross-cultural critique discussed earlier in this chapter, because the theory's own capacity for revision under evidentiary pressure is part of what distinguishes it from a less scientifically serious framework. Later work within the self-determination theory research program has increasingly distinguished between the specific psychological need for autonomy and the related but distinct construct of independence, arguing that a person can be highly autonomous, in Ryan and Deci's technical sense of self-endorsed volition, while remaining highly interdependent with family, community, or organizational obligations, provided those obligations are genuinely internalized rather than merely externally imposed. This refinement, developed substantially through cross-cultural replication work conducted specifically in response to the collectivist-culture critique, represents exactly the kind of theoretical adjustment in light of new evidence that this book has tried to hold up as a model throughout its own argument, and it strengthens rather than weakens the theory's claim to broad applicability, precisely because it emerged from taking a serious external critique seriously rather than dismissing it.

It is worth being equally clear about what the theory does not license, because self-determination theory has occasionally been invoked, in looser popular writing, to argue for the elimination of structure, rules, and hierarchy altogether, on the theory that autonomy requires the absence of external constraint. That is a misreading the theory's own architects have pushed back against directly and repeatedly. Ryan and Deci's own applied research, including studies of parenting, education, and medical adherence, consistently finds that clear structure, consistent limits, and well-communicated expectations are compatible with — and in many studies actively supportive of — strong autonomy satisfaction, provided the structure is explained, experienced as reasonable, and not imposed through control-language or contingent threats. A junior employee handed a clear, well-explained rubric for what senior review will focus on, and given genuine latitude in how she meets it, can experience meaningfully higher autonomy than a junior employee given nominal freedom but subjected to unpredictable, after-the-fact criticism for choices nobody told her mattered. The practical lesson, again, is narrower and more actionable than either extreme: structure is not the enemy of autonomy. Unexplained, arbitrary, or punitively applied structure is.

This is also the point in the book where the two halves of the argument fully meet for the first time. The first seven chapters have built, piece by piece, the scientific case for what actually sustains durable human motivation and performance — play, purpose, potential, and beneath all three, the three basic needs Ryan and Deci spent their careers documenting. The opening chapters, meanwhile, established a specific, dated, evidence-based claim about which tasks generative AI is displacing first, and why those tasks happened to sit at the exact bottom rung of career ladders that most depended on economic necessity to populate. The chapters that follow turn to a different, harder kind of evidence: not laboratory experiments testing a theory under controlled conditions, but real natural experiments — lottery windfalls, retirement transitions, national basic-income trials, and entire generations of young people — in which economic necessity was removed from real human lives, by chance, by policy, or by structural collapse, and the resulting record shows, messily and unevenly, what actually happens when the foundation this book has spent its first section describing gets pulled away.

None of the seven chapters so far have needed to invoke AI, prediction, or the future at all in order to make their case, and that is deliberate. The science of what makes human effort durable and what merely produces compliance was built almost entirely without any anticipation of a labor market in which economic necessity itself might loosen its grip on a specific rung of the career ladder, at a specific moment, for reasons none of these researchers could have foreseen. That is precisely what makes the science useful now rather than merely fashionable: it was not built to fit this argument, and it does not need this argument to be true in order to remain good science. What it offers, instead, is a set of well-tested predictions about what should happen if economic necessity does loosen — predictions the remaining chapters can now test against real, if messier and less controlled, evidence from the world outside the laboratory.
