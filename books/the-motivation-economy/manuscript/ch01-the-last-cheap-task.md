# The Last Cheap Task

Somewhere inside a vast payroll database covering roughly one in six American workers, quietly and without any single dramatic announcement, a twenty-three-year-old with a marketing degree stopped showing up in the hiring numbers sometime around 2024, and by 2026 the gap had a name and a chart.

The chart came from ADP, the payroll processor that handles compensation for tens of millions of U.S. employees, matched against a task-level measure of how exposed different jobs are to generative AI. The economist behind it, Erik Brynjolfsson of Stanford, had been arguing since 2023 that AI's labor-market effects would show up first and most visibly among the youngest workers in the most exposed occupations, not as a broad wave of layoffs but as a narrowing of the door at the entrance. In August 2025 he and his coauthors published the first version of the finding: employment for early-career workers in the most AI-exposed jobs had fallen roughly 13 percent relative to what the pre-AI trend would have predicted. By June 2026, when Brynjolfsson and ADP Research turned the analysis into a live tracker they called the Canaries Dashboard, the decline had not leveled off. It had gotten steeper. Workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five in the most exposed occupations were shrinking at close to 3.8 percent a year. The least-exposed jobs in that same age band were growing at 2 percent. Workers in their late thirties, doing similar work, were growing too.

The tasks disappearing first were not, on the whole, dramatic ones. Retrieving a figure from a report. Summarizing a call. Formatting a slide deck. Drafting the first version of an email that a senior person would edit down to something usable. None of these were ever, by themselves, the reason anyone wanted a career. They were the reason someone could get one — the low-stakes, well-specified, error-tolerant work that has always been handed to the newest person in the room, partly because it needed doing and partly because it was safe to hand to someone who did not yet know what they were doing.

That is the detail worth sitting with before the rest of this book goes anywhere. The jobs disappearing fastest were not, primarily, the highest-value jobs. They were the cheapest ones — cheap to a firm in the specific sense that a twenty-three-year-old costs less than a forty-year-old, and cheap to the economy as a whole in the sense that the tasks involved required the least judgment and carried the least consequence if done imperfectly. A large language model can, as of 2026, do a great deal of that kind of work adequately, and an employer choosing between paying a junior salary to have a human do it slowly and paying an API bill to have a system do it quickly has a straightforward decision to make, at least for the narrow slice of the job that fits the model's competence.

The people paying closest attention to this pattern have been careful, to their credit, not to oversell it. Anthropic's own Economic Index — an internally run analysis of aggregated, anonymized use of its Claude models — reported in its March 2026 update that by February of that year, Claude was being used for at least a quarter of the tasks in 49 percent of the jobs represented in its sample, up from 36 percent a year before. That sounds like a sweeping number until you notice what it is not saying: out of nearly eighteen thousand distinct tasks catalogued by the U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET system, only about 7.5 percent showed any measurable Claude usage at all. Adoption was not uniform. It was lumpy, concentrated overwhelmingly in tasks that involve retrieving, summarizing, formatting, and drafting text and code — precisely the category of work this chapter has already named. Anthropic's own labor-market analysis, published separately, found no clear signal of broad unemployment in the occupations most exposed to this usage. What it did find, stated plainly, was that hiring of workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five into the most exposed roles had slowed by around 14 percent relative to what would otherwise have been expected.

Slowed hiring at the entry level is a duller, less cinematic finding than mass unemployment. It is also, this book will argue, the more important one, because it changes who experiences the labor market's oldest and most reliable motivational mechanism, and when.

That mechanism has a name, though it rarely gets used out loud: economic pressure. For most of the last two centuries, the reason the overwhelming majority of people got out of bed and went to a job they did not love was that they needed money, and there was no other reliable way to get it. Whatever else was true about a given worker on a given morning — whether they found the work interesting, whether they liked their manager, whether the mission statement moved them — underneath all of it sat the fact that not going would eventually mean not eating, or not paying rent, or letting someone who depended on them go without. Employers never had to engineer that motivation. It came pre-installed. A century of management theory has been written about how to make work more engaging, more meaningful, more aligned with human psychology, and almost none of it has had to grapple with the question of what happens to a workforce for whom the baseline necessity has gone soft, because that question has rarely needed asking.

It is starting to need asking now, and not because artificial intelligence is about to make human labor obsolete across the board — the evidence in this book does not support that claim, and this book will not make it. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, a large, survey-based projection compiled from employers representing a substantial share of the global workforce, forecasts a net increase of roughly 78 million jobs by 2030, the result of about 170 million new roles being created against 92 million displaced. Read carefully, that is not a portrait of a labor market disappearing. It is a portrait of a labor market being reshuffled unevenly, with disruption touching an estimated 22 percent of today's jobs and around 39 percent of the skills those jobs currently require changing within five years. Some of the fastest-growing categories in the same report are not exotic AI specialties at all, but delivery drivers, care workers, educators, and farmworkers — work that is, for different reasons, hard to hand to a language model.

The claim this book makes is narrower and, I think, more useful than either "AI is ending work" or "AI is nothing to worry about." It is this: the earliest and clearest effect of generative AI on the labor market is a disproportionate removal of the cheap, well-specified, low-stakes cognitive tasks that have historically been assigned to the newest, least experienced workers — the tasks a person does not because they are the point of the job, but because doing them competently is how a person becomes trusted with the tasks that are the point. And because those tasks sat, for two centuries, on top of an economic-pressure foundation that nobody had to design because it simply existed, their disappearance is the first real test of a question that has mostly stayed theoretical: what happens to human effort, ambition, and identity once the foundation gets thinner than it used to be, starting at the bottom, for the people who have the least other structure in their lives to fall back on.

It helps to be specific about what the Canaries Dashboard actually measures, because "AI-exposed occupation" can sound like a euphemism doing a lot of unexamined work. Brynjolfsson and his coauthors did not simply ask whether a job title sounded technological. They built an exposure score from the detailed task descriptions the Department of Labor already collects for every occupation in the country, then checked which of those tasks language models could plausibly perform, based on published capability evaluations rather than press releases. A paralegal's job, on this measure, is highly exposed not because paralegals are about to vanish but because a large share of what a first-year paralegal actually does in a given week — reviewing documents for a specific clause, summarizing a deposition, building a citation table — overlaps heavily with what a model can now do adequately in seconds. A construction electrician's job scores as far less exposed, not because electricians are unimportant, but because the physical judgment involved in fishing wire through an unfamiliar wall resists the kind of pattern-matching a language model performs well. The dashboard's contribution was to take that occupation-level exposure score and cross it against real payroll data — who was actually hired, at what age, into which job codes — rather than relying on surveys of what workers or employers believed was happening. That is part of why the finding carried weight with other labor economists who had been skeptical of earlier, more speculative AI-displacement claims: it did not ask anyone's opinion. It counted paychecks.

It is also worth naming a specific occupation rather than leaving the pattern abstract. Entry-level paralegal and legal-research roles are among the more exposed categories in the ADP data, and law is a useful test case precisely because the profession has spent a century building an apprenticeship model almost entirely around the kind of task now being absorbed. A first-year associate at a large law firm has traditionally earned a six-figure salary while producing relatively little independent judgment, because the point was never that the firm needed a twenty-six-year-old's judgment. The point was that document review, first-pass research memos, and citation-checking had to be done by someone, and doing thousands of hours of it badly, slowly, and under supervision was how that twenty-six-year-old eventually became a partner capable of judgment worth paying for. When a firm can route a large share of that first-pass work through a model in minutes, the economic logic that justified the six-figure training wage weakens, whether or not any single firm consciously decides to hire fewer associates for that reason alone. Legal-sector hiring is noisy and firm-specific enough that this book will not attach a precise industry-wide number to it, but the mechanism is the same one the ADP data captures more rigorously across occupations generally: demand for the judgment senior professionals provide has not shown a comparable decline, while demand for the volume of first-pass, entry-level task work that used to justify a training wage has.

There is a reasonable objection here, and it deserves to be stated before this book tries to answer it. Youth labor markets weaken for reasons that have nothing to do with AI. In June 2026, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York — the same institution whose data on recent-graduate unemployment shows the widest gap on record between young degree-holders (5.6 percent unemployed as of early 2026) and the workforce as a whole (4.2 percent) — published its own analysis arguing that a majority of the increase, by its estimate around 64 percent, was better explained by employers' post-pandemic caution about hiring inexperienced people into remote roles than by AI displacement specifically. Remote work makes it harder to informally supervise, correct, and absorb a junior employee's mistakes; a firm that has moved most of its work off-site has one more reason to think twice before hiring someone who needs to learn on the job, independent of whether AI exists at all.

This book takes that counter-explanation seriously rather than waving it away, because a habit of collapsing every inconvenient labor-market trend into "AI did it" would make the argument weaker, not stronger. The honest version of the claim is that several forces — remote-work-driven hiring caution, a general cooling in white-collar hiring after the rapid expansion of 2021 and 2022, and the specific substitution of AI for entry-level cognitive tasks — are probably operating at once, and untangling their exact respective shares with the data available in 2026 is not something anyone can do with confidence. What can be said with more confidence is the shape of the tasks being absorbed, which the Anthropic and ADP data both describe with unusual specificity, and the fact that whatever the ultimate cause, the practical experience for a young worker trying to enter a knowledge-economy job in 2026 already looks different from the experience of doing the same thing in 2015.

It is worth pausing on what that "cheap task" actually did for the people who used to do it, because it did more than one thing at once, and losing it costs more than one thing at once.

Economically, it was the entry fee — the low-value-added work a junior person could be trusted with while still being paid something, which let firms afford to hire people who were not yet worth their eventual salary in exchange for a few years of below-market labor while they learned. Pedagogically, it was the training ground — the place where a new lawyer learned what a contract actually looked like by drafting a hundred boilerplate ones, where a new consultant learned what a client actually wanted by sitting through the meetings nobody senior wanted to attend, where a new engineer learned the codebase by fixing the bugs nobody senior wanted to touch. Psychologically, and this is the part the rest of this book spends its time on, that early, humble, often tedious work was also where economic necessity and something else — curiosity, competence, the private satisfaction of getting slightly better at a thing — first got tangled together, in a way that made it hard to tell, even for the person doing the work, which motive was actually driving them on any given Tuesday.

Software engineering offers a second, slightly different version of the same pattern, and it is worth walking through because the direction of the effect is less obvious at first glance. Junior developers have traditionally earned their keep, and their education, by writing the unglamorous parts of a codebase: the boilerplate functions, the routine test coverage, the small bug fixes nobody senior wants to spend an afternoon on. Generative coding tools are unusually good at exactly that category of work, arguably better and faster at it than at almost anything else language models currently do, which is part of why software has shown up prominently in both the Anthropic usage data and the broader industry conversation about AI-driven productivity gains. The complicating detail is that this does not necessarily mean fewer junior developers are needed in an absolute sense — a senior engineer freed from routine debugging can, in principle, take on more ambitious projects that require more people to execute, some of them junior. What it does mean, and what several engineering leaders have said openly in industry discussion of the shift, is that the junior developer's traditional path to competence is being rerouted. The tasks that used to teach a new hire how a large system actually behaves under pressure — chasing a stack trace through unfamiliar code, understanding why a particular edge case breaks a particular function — are increasingly done first by a model, with a human reviewing the output rather than producing it. Reviewing a solution and originating one under uncertainty are different cognitive experiences, and it is not yet clear, at the scale of an entire industry, whether review-heavy junior work builds the same depth of understanding that write-heavy junior work used to.

A third domain worth including alongside law and software engineering is management consulting, an industry whose entire junior-associate business model has historically rested on exactly the kind of task this chapter has identified as most exposed: synthesizing large volumes of research into structured slide decks, building financial models from client-provided data, and producing the polished first-draft deliverables that senior partners then present to clients as their own firm's insight. Consulting firms have been notably public, relative to law and software, about actively integrating generative AI tools directly into junior associate workflows, with several major firms publicly announcing internal AI deployments explicitly framed around accelerating exactly the research-synthesis and deck-production work that has traditionally consumed the majority of a first- and second-year consultant's billable hours. Industry commentary on this shift has split along a familiar fault line: some firm leaders argue the change will let junior consultants spend more time on client-facing strategic thinking earlier in their careers than the traditional apprenticeship model allowed, while others, including some current and former consultants writing publicly about the shift, have raised concerns closely mirroring this book's own argument — that the research-synthesis grunt work being automated away was never merely a cost center to be optimized out, but was, for generations of consultants, the specific mechanism through which they learned to recognize a well-structured argument, a defensible data source, and a client's actual underlying problem beneath its stated one.

That uncertainty is worth sitting with rather than resolving prematurely, because it is the shape of the uncertainty running through this entire book. Nobody — not Brynjolfsson, not Anthropic's own researchers, not the World Economic Forum's survey respondents — is claiming to know with precision how large these effects will ultimately be, how quickly they will spread beyond the occupations where they are currently most visible, or how much of the labor market's response will come from firms redesigning entry-level work thoughtfully rather than simply eliminating it. What the data supports, cautiously, is a direction and a starting point: the removal of task volume is beginning at the bottom of experience ladders, in tasks that were never valuable in themselves but were valuable as training, and it is beginning at a moment when the workers most affected have the least savings, the least seniority, and the least institutional standing to absorb a slower, harder path into a career.

That tangle is the subject of the whole book, and it is worth being honest about why it matters now in a way it did not, quite as urgently, a decade ago. As long as economic necessity reliably sat underneath everything else, nobody had to figure out whether the other motives — curiosity, mastery, purpose, the simple pleasure of being good at something — were strong enough to carry the weight on their own. They never had to. The floor was always there. What the 2025 and 2026 data on entry-level hiring describes, cautiously and with real uncertainty about magnitude, is the floor thinning first exactly where people have the least accumulated skill, savings, seniority, or social standing to compensate. It is not a story about robots taking over. It is a story about who gets asked, first and with the least warning, what they were actually working for.

The rest of this book is an attempt to answer that question honestly, using evidence that mostly has nothing to do with artificial intelligence at all. Some of the best data available on what happens when economic necessity is removed comes from psychology experiments run on preschoolers in the 1970s, from lottery winners interviewed in the 1980s, from a basic-income trial run in Finland and a much longer one run in Kenya, from a generation of South Korean twenty-somethings who have stopped looking for work altogether, and from an American mortality trend that a pair of economists named, with deliberate bluntness, deaths of despair. None of those sources knew AI was coming. All of them describe, in different ways and at different scales, what fills the space where necessity used to sit — sometimes well, more often badly, and only rarely by accident.

The Total Motivation research this book leans on most heavily — developed by two former McKinsey consultants, Neel Doshi and Lindsay McGregor, and published in 2015 — starts from a deceptively simple premise: there are six distinct reasons people work, only some of which make people better at their jobs the longer they rely on them, and economic pressure is one of the ones that does not. It is not that economic pressure fails to motivate. Clearly it does; it is, historically, the most reliable motivator there is. It is that it motivates in a narrow, brittle way — enough to make someone show up, rarely enough to make someone excellent — and organizations that lean on it too heavily tend to get exactly the mediocre, compliance-shaped performance that heavy reliance on it predicts.

For two centuries, most workers never had the luxury of finding out whether that was true for them personally, because economic pressure was doing so much of the work by default that the other five motives barely needed to prove themselves. The chapters ahead trace what the science says those other motives actually are, what happens when the pressure that used to mask their absence gets removed — badly, through chance or policy failure, and well, through deliberate redesign — and what any of that suggests for a labor market where, for the first time in a long time, the cheapest tasks are no longer reliably available to teach a new generation what work is even for.
