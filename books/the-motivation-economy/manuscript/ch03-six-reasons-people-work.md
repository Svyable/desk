# Six Reasons People Work

Neel Doshi spent his early career doing the kind of work McKinsey consultants do: flying into companies that were underperforming, running the numbers, and recommending the standard remedies — reorganize the reporting lines, cut the underperforming division, tighten the incentive structure. Somewhere in the accumulation of these engagements, he and a colleague named Lindsay McGregor started noticing a pattern that the standard remedies did not explain. Two companies with nearly identical strategies, cost structures, and incentive plans could produce wildly different results, and the difference tracked something that showed up nowhere in a balance sheet: how the people inside each company actually felt about the work itself.

That observation is not, by itself, original. Every manager who has ever worked somewhere with good morale and somewhere with bad morale already suspects it is true. What Doshi and McGregor did that was less common was try to measure it with the same rigor a management consultancy would apply to a supply chain, and to publish both the method and the results — first in a 2015 *Harvard Business Review* article titled "How Company Culture Shapes Employee Motivation," and then at greater length in their book *Primed to Perform*. Their central tool was a survey instrument they called the Total Motivation factor, or ToMo, built around a claim borrowed and adapted from decades of academic motivation research but sharpened into something a manager could actually score: there are six distinct reasons a person does their job, and they are not interchangeable.

The first three, in the Doshi-McGregor framework, are called direct motives, because the reward for doing the work is contained inside the work itself.

**Play** is the most literal of the three: doing something because the activity itself is enjoyable, the way a puzzle is enjoyable to someone who likes puzzles, independent of any prize for finishing it. A programmer who finds a gnarly bug satisfying to track down, regardless of whether anyone will ever thank her for finding it, is motivated by play. So is a salesperson who genuinely enjoys the improvisational challenge of a difficult negotiation. Play does not require the underlying task to be trivial or fun in a conventional sense; plenty of people experience something like play in activities that would look, from outside, like drudgery.

**Purpose** is different: it is doing something because the outcome matters to you, even if the moment-to-moment experience of doing it is not particularly enjoyable. A nurse changing a difficult dressing at three in the morning is unlikely to describe the act itself as fun, but she may be strongly motivated by its consequence — a patient who heals rather than suffers an infection. Purpose connects the task to a value the person actually holds, rather than to the sensation of doing the task.

**Potential** is the third direct motive, and the least intuitive: doing something because it moves you toward a future you want, even when neither the activity nor its immediate outcome is especially rewarding on its own terms. A junior analyst working through a tedious spreadsheet because she believes it will make her a better analyst, and that being a better analyst matters to the life she is building, is motivated by potential. It is future-oriented in a way play and purpose are not, and it is also, Doshi and McGregor argue, more fragile than the other two, because it depends on the person's continued belief that the connection between today's tedious task and tomorrow's better outcome is real.

The other three motives are called indirect, because the reward for doing the work is separated from the work itself, mediated by something external — money, approval, or simple habit.

**Emotional pressure** is doing something to avoid a negative feeling: guilt, shame, fear of disappointing someone, anxiety about how you will be judged. An employee who stays late not because the work interests her but because she is afraid of what her manager will think if she leaves on time is operating on emotional pressure. It is a real and often powerful motivator, and it is also, the framework argues, corrosive over time, because the emotional cost accumulates independent of whether the work itself gets easier or more meaningful.

**Economic pressure** is the motive this book has already spent a full chapter tracing to its historical roots: doing something because of an external reward or punishment attached to money — the paycheck you need, the bonus you want, the fine you are trying to avoid. It is, as Chapter Two argued, the oldest and most reliable lever available to any employer, and the Doshi-McGregor research treats it exactly as reliably useless for producing anything beyond baseline compliance: strong enough to get someone to show up, weak at getting someone to do their best work once they are there.

**Inertia** is the last and, in some ways, the bleakest of the six: doing something simply because you did it yesterday, with no active reason pulling you toward it and no active reason pushing you away. An employee who has stopped asking whether the job suits her, who shows up because showing up is the path of least resistance and quitting would require an effort she cannot currently summon, is operating almost entirely on inertia. It is the motive most associated, in the framework's later applications, with the specific flavor of disengagement that later chapters of this book will describe as quiet quitting and the Great Detachment — not active unhappiness, but the absence of any motive strong enough to produce either genuine effort or genuine departure.

It helps to see the six motives operating at once inside a single, ordinary job, because the framework is easiest to misread as a set of six separate employee types rather than six forces that coexist, in different proportions, inside the same person on the same afternoon. Take two junior accountants doing the identical task: reconciling a stack of expense reports against receipts, a job that involves no creativity, no client contact, and no visible reward beyond a paycheck. The first accountant might be doing it primarily out of economic pressure — she needs the job, the job requires this, she does it — with a thin layer of inertia underneath, since she has done this specific task every Thursday for a year and no longer actively asks herself whether she wants to. The second accountant might be doing the same task with a meaningfully different internal experience: some genuine play in the puzzle-like satisfaction of getting a stubborn number to reconcile, some purpose in the belief that clean books matter to a small company's survival, and some potential in the sense that mastering the unglamorous mechanics of reconciliation is a real step toward becoming a controller one day. Both accountants produce, on a given Thursday, an identical spreadsheet. The Doshi-McGregor claim, and the deeper research tradition behind it, is that these are not equivalent states even when the output looks the same, because the second accountant's performance will hold up better under pressure, novelty, and time, while the first accountant's performance is only as reliable as the paycheck and the habit that currently sustain it — remove either one, and there is comparatively little left underneath.

This is also where the framework's relationship to a much older management tradition becomes useful to name directly. Frederick Winslow Taylor, whose *The Principles of Scientific Management* appeared in 1911 and became one of the most influential management texts of the twentieth century, built an entire methodology around the opposite premise: that the fastest, most reliable way to improve output was to break work into its smallest measurable components, time each component with a stopwatch, design the single most efficient method for performing it, and then pay workers a bonus for hitting the target — a system Taylor called, without irony, scientific management, and one which assumed, more or less explicitly, that economic pressure calibrated with enough precision was sufficient to produce excellent performance, because the worker's own judgment, interest, or sense of purpose was treated as an unreliable variable to be engineered around rather than cultivated. Taylorism worked, by its own metrics, remarkably well in the narrow domains it was designed for — repetitive manual tasks with a single correct method and an easily measured output, like shoveling pig iron, the task Taylor himself used as his most famous illustration. It worked far less well, later management researchers would argue, as factories and then offices came to depend more heavily on judgment, adaptability, and tasks whose "one best way" could not be timed with a stopwatch because it did not exist. The Doshi-McGregor framework can be read, historically, as one of the more recent entries in a century-long argument between Taylor's tradition and its critics, restating in the vocabulary of a 2015 management bestseller a claim that industrial psychologists had been building evidence for since well before Hawthorne: that economic pressure, applied with ever-increasing precision, eventually runs into diminishing and even negative returns on the kind of work that requires more than mechanical compliance.

The claim that makes this more than a tidy taxonomy is what Doshi and McGregor say happens when you combine these six motives into a single score, weighting the direct motives positively and the indirect ones negatively, and correlate that score against independently measured performance. Across the organizations they studied — a set that in their published work included large public companies, small businesses, and public-sector organizations such as school systems — higher ToMo scores tracked with higher customer satisfaction, higher quality, lower turnover, and stronger financial results than organizations whose workforce relied more heavily on the indirect motives to get through the day, even when the indirect-motive organizations paid competitively and had reasonable-looking incentive structures on paper. The specific companies Doshi and McGregor point to as exemplifying strong direct-motive cultures — Southwest Airlines and Apple are two of the names that recur most often in their book and its subsequent press coverage — were not selected because they pay unusually well or offer unusually generous economic incentives, but because their internal practices, hiring philosophies, and reward structures appeared to lean deliberately toward play, purpose, and potential rather than toward pressure and compliance.

It is worth being honest, at this point, about the limits of this evidence, because the framework is compelling enough that it is tempting to treat it as more settled than it is. The ToMo survey is a proprietary instrument, developed and licensed by Doshi and McGregor's consultancy, Vega Factor, rather than an open academic measure that other researchers can freely replicate and critique the way they can with, say, the self-determination theory instruments discussed later in this book. The company case studies in *Primed to Perform* are illustrative rather than randomized; Doshi and McGregor did not, and could not, randomly assign some companies to have Southwest Airlines' culture and others to have a rigid, compliance-heavy one and then compare results, because that is not how companies work. What they can show, and did show with real survey data across a reasonably large sample of organizations, is correlation between measured direct-motive strength and measured performance outcomes — a genuinely useful finding, but one that leaves open exactly how much of the causal arrow runs from culture to performance rather than from performance to the kind of profitable slack that lets a company afford a more humane culture in the first place. A company doing well for other reasons — a strong product, a favorable market position — can afford better training, more autonomy, and a more forgiving culture more easily than a company barely surviving on thin margins, which raises the possibility that some of what Doshi and McGregor measure as "high ToMo causing high performance" is partly "high performance enabling high ToMo."

This book treats the framework the way it deserves to be treated: as a genuinely useful, empirically grounded, but commercially developed model — one tool among several for describing something real, not a law of organizational physics. Its real contribution, for the purposes of the argument this book is making, is not the specific survey instrument or the specific company anecdotes. It is the underlying claim, which does have deeper and more rigorously tested roots in academic psychology that the chapters immediately following this one will trace in detail: that the motives which sustain high performance over time are structurally different in kind from the motives that merely get someone to comply, and that an organization — or an economy — which has relied for two centuries primarily on the compliance-producing motive is likely to discover, the moment that motive weakens, that it never built much capacity for the other kind.

That is precisely the position this book argues the labor market is now in. For as long as economic pressure reliably delivered a workforce that showed up, employers had comparatively little incentive to invest seriously in cultivating play, purpose, and potential at the entry level, where turnover was expected and treated as a cost of doing business rather than a design failure. Junior roles were, in the language of this framework, disproportionately organized around economic pressure and inertia — do this task because you are paid to, and because it is simply what the job requires — with play, purpose, and potential treated as a bonus if the work happened to be interesting, rather than as something deliberately engineered in. If AI is now absorbing a meaningful share of exactly those tasks, the workers who remain in entry-level roles, and the ones trying to enter a labor market that has fewer of those roles to offer, are encountering a moment when the indirect motive that used to carry the weight of getting people through unglamorous early-career work is weakening, without any corresponding strengthening of the direct motives that might have replaced it.

It is worth situating Doshi and McGregor's specific contribution against the broader landscape of earlier management theory it drew from, because the six-motive framework did not emerge in a vacuum, and understanding its intellectual lineage helps clarify both its genuine originality and its genuine debts. Frederick Herzberg's two-factor theory, developed in the late 1950s, had already distinguished between "hygiene factors" — pay, working conditions, job security — whose absence produces dissatisfaction but whose presence does not reliably produce genuine motivation, and "motivator factors" — achievement, recognition, the work itself — capable of producing real engagement, a distinction that anticipates the direct-versus-indirect split at the center of Doshi and McGregor's later framework by more than half a century. Herzberg's own research methodology, based on asking workers to recall specific incidents of exceptional satisfaction and dissatisfaction, has drawn its own share of methodological criticism over the decades, particularly regarding the risk that people attribute satisfying experiences to their own effort and dissatisfying ones to external circumstances regardless of the actual underlying cause. Doshi and McGregor's contribution, read against this longer history, was less a wholly new discovery than a synthesis: combining Herzberg's hygiene-versus-motivator distinction with the by-then more rigorously established self-determination theory research examined later in this book, and packaging the result into a measurable survey instrument marketed directly to corporate decision-makers in a way neither Herzberg nor the academic self-determination theory literature had managed to do at comparable scale.

A second and related limit is worth naming, because it bears directly on the entry-level argument of the previous two chapters. Doshi and McGregor's own published work draws most of its detailed organizational examples from companies that had the resources and stability to invest in direct-motive culture-building in the first place — established, profitable, often large firms with dedicated people-operations functions. Less is said, in their published research, about what a strong ToMo culture looks like inside a small, thinly capitalized business trying to survive its first three years, or inside the specific entry-level roles this book is most concerned with, where a junior worker's task list is often assembled for the convenience of the person delegating it rather than the development of the person receiving it. It is one thing to say that Southwest Airlines has cultivated play and purpose among experienced flight crews with decades of institutional culture behind them. It is another to say that a generic customer-support queue, staffed by twenty-two-year-olds on their first job, six months into a role they expect to leave as soon as something better appears, can realistically be redesigned around play, purpose, and potential without substantial investment that many employers, particularly smaller ones, have neither the margin nor the patience to make. The framework describes an achievable state. It does not, on its own, describe how common that state actually is across the full range of entry-level work in a real economy, and a fair reading of the evidence suggests it is considerably less common than the framework's own flagship examples might imply.

There is a useful, if uncomfortable, symmetry worth naming here. Doshi and McGregor's argument was originally aimed at managers who had convinced themselves that raises, bonuses, and fear of being fired were the primary levers available to improve performance, and who were, in the authors' view, systematically underinvesting in the direct motives that would have produced better results at lower cost and with less turnover. Their prescription was not to eliminate pay — nobody was arguing employees should work for free — but to stop treating pay as the primary motivational tool and start treating it as a threshold condition: pay people fairly enough that money stops being a source of anxiety, and then invest the real organizational effort in play, purpose, and potential. The labor market now facing a thinner economic-pressure floor at the entry level is being handed, involuntarily and with none of the deliberate design Doshi and McGregor recommend, a version of the same experiment their consulting practice was built to help companies run on purpose. The question this book keeps returning to is whether the direct motives can actually bear that weight when they are not engineered in deliberately — when the change arrives as an involuntary contraction in available entry-level work rather than as a thoughtful redesign of it.

It is worth adding one further empirical check on the framework before moving on, because a claim this central to the rest of the book deserves more than a single source's own account of its validity. Independent academic researchers outside Doshi and McGregor's own consultancy have since examined related total-motivation-style survey instruments in peer-reviewed organizational psychology journals, generally finding the underlying direct-versus-indirect distinction replicates reasonably well as a predictor of self-reported engagement and turnover intention, while finding weaker and more inconsistent support for the specific, proprietary scoring methodology Vega Factor markets to corporate clients. This pattern — a genuine, replicable underlying psychological distinction, wrapped in a commercially packaged measurement tool whose specific numeric outputs carry less independent validation than the underlying concept itself — is common enough across the management-consulting industry that this book will flag it as a general pattern worth watching for in later chapters as well, not as a criticism unique to Doshi and McGregor's particular framework.

None of this is a reason to discard the framework. It is a reason to use it the way the rest of this book will: as a vocabulary for naming what is at stake, checked at every turn against evidence with fewer commercial incentives attached to a tidy conclusion. The six motives give this book a language for a distinction that would otherwise stay vague — the difference between a worker who shows up and a worker who thrives, between a job that merely gets done and a job that gets done well and sustains the person doing it. That distinction existed long before Doshi and McGregor gave it a name, and long before anyone thought to ask what would happen to an economy that had spent two centuries relying on the wrong half of it.

Answering that question requires going further back than a 2015 *Harvard Business Review* article, because Doshi and McGregor did not invent the distinction between direct and indirect motivation. They inherited it, filtered it through their own consulting practice, and gave it a memorable name and a marketable survey instrument, but the underlying science had already been building for more than sixty years by the time *Primed to Perform* was published, starting with an experiment that had nothing to do with offices, paychecks, or management consultants at all, and everything to do with eight rhesus monkeys and a set of mechanical puzzles nobody had asked them to solve.
