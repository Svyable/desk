# Shorter Weeks, Same Pay

Between June and December 2022, sixty-one companies spread across the United Kingdom, spanning a genuinely wide range of sectors and employing roughly 2,900 workers between them in total, tried something that ran directly against an entire century of accumulated industrial assumption about the relationship between hours worked and total output produced: they moved their employees, all at once and without a gradual phase-in period, to a four-day working week, kept pay at its full, entirely unreduced five-day level, and asked managers and staff together, collaboratively, to figure out how to maintain roughly the same total output anyway. The trial, coordinated by the nonprofit advocacy group 4 Day Week Global in partnership with the UK-based Autonomy Institute and academic researchers at Cambridge University and Boston College, including the sociologist Juliet Schor as one of its lead investigators, became, at the time, the largest structured trial of reduced working hours ever conducted, spanning industries from marketing and financial services to manufacturing and hospitality, and drawing close, skeptical attention from business media inclined to expect the experiment would confirm the conventional wisdom it was designed to test.

It did not confirm that conventional wisdom. Across the trial period, participating companies reported revenue broadly stable relative to the comparable period the previous year, and in aggregate actually up roughly 35 percent year over year across the trial group — a figure this book will treat cautiously, since revenue growth across a group of companies over a six-month window reflects many factors beyond working-hours policy, and the trial's design did not include a randomized control group of otherwise similar companies operating on a conventional schedule over the same period. What the trial's other metrics captured more directly and more credibly, because they were measured through employee surveys and administrative records within each participating firm rather than compared against an uncontrolled external benchmark, were considerably more striking: staff turnover fell by 57 percent across the trial group relative to the same firms' pre-trial baseline, 71 percent of employees reported reduced burnout by the end of the trial, and 39 percent reported feeling less stressed specifically, alongside improvements in both physical and mental health measures collected through standardized survey instruments administered before and after the trial period.

The retention signal deserves particular attention within the framework this book has built across its earlier chapters, because reduced turnover is one of the clearest available behavioral proxies for the kind of durable, direct-motive-driven engagement Doshi and McGregor's Total Motivation research, examined in Chapter Three, identifies as distinct from mere compliance. An employee retained primarily through economic or emotional pressure — fear of losing income, fear of disappointing a manager — tends to leave the moment a comparable or better economic opportunity appears elsewhere, since the pressure holding her in place was never attached to the work itself. A 57 percent reduction in departures, sustained across a genuinely diverse range of participating industries rather than concentrated in one favorable sector, is difficult to explain through the economic-pressure or emotional-pressure channels alone, and considerably easier to explain if the reduced-hours structure genuinely increased play, purpose, or potential — more time and energy available outside work to recover, pursue other interests, and return to the job with renewed rather than depleted engagement, exactly the mechanism the trial's own qualitative interviews with participating employees repeatedly described in their own words.

The trial's one-year follow-up, published by the Autonomy Institute under the title *Making It Stick*, addressed directly the most obvious skeptical objection to any short-term pilot: that enthusiasm generated by a novel, closely observed experiment tends to fade once the observation ends and ordinary organizational inertia reasserts itself. The follow-up found that 56 of the original 61 companies — 92 percent — had retained the four-day week a full year after the formal trial period concluded, with 18 of those companies making the policy permanent and irreversible rather than treating it as an ongoing experiment subject to review. Companies surveyed a year out reported continued positive effects on staff wellbeing in 82 percent of cases, continued reductions in staff turnover in roughly half of cases, and improved recruitment — the ability to attract new candidates specifically because of the four-day policy — in close to a third of cases, suggesting the reduced-hours structure had become not merely a retained internal policy but an active competitive advantage in hiring, a genuinely durable outcome rather than a temporary novelty effect that a shorter observation window might have overstated.

Iceland's experience, running considerably earlier and over a considerably longer period, offers a second and in some ways more rigorous test of the same underlying question, because its trials were conducted within the public sector rather than among self-selected private companies, removing one obvious source of selection bias present in the UK's opt-in corporate pilot. Between 2015 and 2019, Icelandic municipal and national government agencies — coordinated through Reykjavík City Council and the national government, and eventually covering 66 separate workplaces and roughly 2,500 workers — reduced standard working hours from forty to thirty-five or thirty-six hours per week without any reduction in pay, in a public-sector setting where participation was determined by government workforce planning rather than by which employers happened to be enthusiastic early adopters of a management fad. The resulting research, published jointly by the Icelandic research organization Alda and Britain's Autonomy Institute under the title *Going Public: Iceland's Journey to a Shorter Working Week*, described the trials as, in the researchers' own assessment, an overwhelming success by essentially every measure examined: productivity held steady or, in some measured workplaces, improved, while employees reported reduced stress and a markedly improved balance between work and the rest of their lives.

The scale of Iceland's eventual policy adoption, following directly from the trial's documented results, is worth stating precisely because it demonstrates something the UK trial's one-year follow-up could only gesture toward: durability measured not in a single follow-up year but across a subsequent half-decade of actual national labor-market practice. By the time later analyses, including reporting by CNN Business in 2024, assessed the trials' broader legacy, Icelandic trade unions and employer associations had negotiated country-wide collective bargaining agreements making reduced working hours a standard, formalized feature of Icelandic employment rather than a contained experimental pilot, with the resulting reporting indicating that roughly 86 percent of Iceland's entire working population had, by the mid-2020s, either moved to shorter hours already or gained the contractual right to shorten their hours going forward — a policy shift touching the overwhelming majority of an entire nation's workforce, traceable directly back to a public-sector trial that began less than a decade earlier.

It is worth being appropriately cautious about the macroeconomic context surrounding Iceland's subsequent performance, because the temptation to attribute every favorable economic indicator that followed the trials directly to the working-hours policy itself would overstate what this evidence can actually support. Iceland's GDP growth reached 4.1 percent in 2023, comfortably above both the European Union and broader OECD averages for the same year, and the country's unemployment rate stood at a low 3.6 percent, figures frequently cited alongside the four-day-week story in favorable retrospective coverage. This book will not claim the shorter working week caused Iceland's subsequent macroeconomic strength; Iceland's economy is small, geographically distinctive, heavily influenced by tourism and fisheries, and subject to numerous forces entirely unrelated to its working-hours policy, and a single small country's aggregate growth figures cannot cleanly isolate the causal contribution of any one domestic labor-market reform. What can be said with more confidence, drawing on the trial's own internal, controlled comparisons between participating and non-participating workplaces rather than on the national growth figures, is that the specific claim motivating the trial — that reducing hours without reducing pay would necessarily and immediately destroy productivity — was not borne out by the data actually collected.

Both trials share a structural feature worth examining directly, because it connects this chapter back to the challenge-skill balance and autonomy research examined in Chapters Six and Seven: neither the UK nor the Iceland trial simply asked employees to compress the same total volume of tasks into fewer hours through sheer additional effort, an approach that would predict rising stress and eventual burnout rather than the improvements both trials actually documented. Instead, participating organizations in both trials were explicitly encouraged, and in many cases required, to examine their existing workflows and eliminate genuinely low-value activity — unnecessary recurring meetings, redundant approval chains, and low-priority tasks that had persisted mainly out of habit rather than demonstrated value — a process that several participating managers in both trials described, in post-trial interviews, as more organizationally beneficial on its own terms than the hours reduction itself, since it forced a deliberate audit of work that many organizations had never previously undertaken. This detail matters because it suggests part of the trials' success came not merely from employees working fewer hours under identical conditions, but from organizations being compelled, by the trial's own structure, to actively identify and remove exactly the kind of low-value, inertia-driven work this book's Chapter Three identified as the behavioral signature of Doshi and McGregor's least productive motive.

There is a further international dimension worth adding briefly, because the UK and Iceland trials examined in detail so far are not the only jurisdictions to have tested comparable reforms, and the broader pattern of results across countries strengthens the case that this chapter's findings are not simply an artifact of two unusually favorable national contexts. Belgium introduced legislation in 2022 giving workers a legal right to request a four-day week without loss of pay, compressing the same total hours into fewer days rather than reducing total hours outright, a meaningfully different design than the UK and Iceland trials' genuine hour reduction, worth distinguishing carefully since compressed scheduling and genuine hour reduction are not the same intervention and should not be expected to produce identical results. Japan, grappling with its own well-documented culture of extreme overwork, has encouraged, though not mandated, four-day-week adoption among willing employers since 2021 as part of a broader work-style reform effort, with adoption remaining modest as of the most recent reporting but growing steadily rather than reversing. This international spread, while still early and unevenly documented compared to the UK and Iceland trials this chapter has focused on, suggests the underlying question this chapter has examined is being taken seriously as a live policy matter well beyond the two countries whose evidence this chapter has drawn on most heavily.

It is essential to name the trials' shared methodological limitations directly, because this book has applied the same scrutiny to every other body of evidence it has drawn on, and the four-day-week research deserves no exemption. Both trials involved participants who volunteered or were selected into the program with foreknowledge of what was being tested, introducing a real risk that participating organizations and employees, aware they were part of a closely watched experiment expected to demonstrate a favorable result, responded partly to the attention and novelty of participation itself — a dynamic with an uncomfortable resemblance to the contested Hawthorne effect discussed in Chapter Two, and one neither trial's published methodology fully rules out. The UK trial in particular skewed toward smaller, knowledge-intensive firms in sectors like marketing, technology, and professional services, where flexible restructuring of workflows is considerably easier to accomplish than in sectors requiring continuous physical presence, such as manufacturing lines, healthcare shift work, or retail staffing, meaning the trial's most favorable results may not generalize cleanly to the harder, less flexible cases where a four-day week would be more operationally difficult to implement without either reducing service availability or requiring additional hiring to cover the lost hours.

It is worth examining, in slightly more detail than the aggregate turnover figures alone convey, exactly what participating employees in the UK trial reported doing with their newly available day off, because the specific pattern bears directly on this book's broader argument about what fills the space when pressure recedes. Post-trial surveys found the reclaimed time was not spent uniformly on passive leisure; participants reported using the additional day for a genuinely wide range of activities spanning several of the direct motives this book's Chapter Three identified — additional caregiving for children or aging parents, consistent with the relatedness need from Chapter Seven; pursuit of hobbies, exercise, and creative projects, consistent with the play motive; and in a meaningful minority of cases, further education, side businesses, or freelance work, consistent with the potential motive. This diversity of use is itself evidence against a simple "people just wanted more rest" interpretation of the trial's success, and toward the more specific, multi-motive account this book has built across its earlier chapters: what employees did with reclaimed time varied by individual, but the reclaiming itself, across nearly every participant, appears to have enabled a broader range of intrinsically chosen activity than a compressed five-day structure had allowed.

There is a further, more specific limitation worth naming for readers evaluating how directly this evidence transfers to the entry-level AI-displacement question examined earlier in this book: neither trial specifically isolated or reported results for junior, early-career employees as a distinct subgroup, meaning this chapter cannot report with confidence whether the reduced-hours structure's documented benefits held equally, more strongly, or more weakly for the newest members of a participating organization's workforce, who may have experienced the change differently than the more senior colleagues whose experiences dominate both trials' published qualitative material. This is a genuine gap in the available evidence rather than a limitation this book can paper over with confident extrapolation, and it points toward a specific, valuable direction for future research that neither trial, as currently published, is positioned to answer.

It is worth situating both trials within the considerably longer history of working-hours reduction, because the four-day week is not, contrary to some breathless contemporary coverage, a genuinely novel idea, but rather the latest entry in a pattern of hours reduction that has recurred, with remarkable regularity, across more than a century of industrial history, each time accompanied by warnings from employers that output would necessarily collapse. The eight-hour workday, now so thoroughly normalized that it barely registers as a policy choice at all, was itself the product of decades of labor organizing and legislative fights across the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, resisted at the time by employers using arguments structurally identical to those raised against the four-day week today — that reducing hours would proportionally reduce output, damage competitiveness, and ultimately harm the very workers the reduction was meant to help. Henry Ford's decision in 1926 to move his automobile plants to a five-day, forty-hour week, well before it became standard practice or legal requirement across American industry, was justified by Ford himself not on humanitarian grounds but on the explicit business argument that well-rested workers with leisure time to spend money were more productive employees and better consumers simultaneously — an early, if self-interested, articulation of exactly the argument this chapter's evidence has since substantiated with considerably more rigorous data than Ford had available to him at the time.

This longer historical pattern matters for how this chapter's evidence should be weighted, because it suggests the UK and Iceland trials are not testing an untested, radical hypothesis so much as extending an already well-established historical regularity one further increment. Each previous reduction in standard working hours — from the twelve- and fourteen-hour days common in nineteenth-century factories down to ten hours, then eight, then the standardization of the two-day weekend across the early-to-mid twentieth century — was met with comparable predictions of economic catastrophe from employers at the time, predictions that, in retrospect, were not borne out by the subsequent historical record of sustained economic growth across the same decades. This does not prove that every further reduction in working hours will necessarily produce similarly benign results indefinitely; there is presumably some point at which further reductions would genuinely harm output for reasons specific to particular industries or roles. But it does suggest that the burden of proof reasonably falls on the specific claim that forty hours, rather than thirty-five or thirty-six, represents some kind of natural or economically optimal floor, rather than simply the most recent resting point in a longer historical trend that the UK and Iceland trials suggest may not yet be finished.

It is also worth addressing, briefly, the question of who actually benefits from this kind of reform, because reduced-hours policies of the kind examined in this chapter remain, as of 2026, considerably more common and considerably more feasible in exactly the knowledge-work, white-collar sectors already discussed throughout this book than in the physically demanding, shift-based, or customer-facing roles that make up a very large share of total global employment. A hospital nurse, a retail cashier, or a warehouse worker cannot simply compress her existing workload into fewer hours through a more efficient meeting schedule, because her job's output is tied directly to continuous physical presence and coverage rather than to discretionary task completion — meaning the four-day week, as currently piloted and currently adopted, risks becoming another benefit disproportionately available to the same relatively privileged knowledge-work population already better positioned to benefit from remote work, flexible scheduling, and the other workplace reforms of the past decade, while workers in less flexible occupations watch a widely publicized reform pass them by entirely. This book raises the concern not to dismiss the trials' genuine and well-documented value, but to note, consistent with the equity concerns raised throughout this book's earlier chapters, that deliberate redesign of work along the lines this chapter describes is not, on current evidence, a solution equally available to every worker regardless of occupation.

What both trials do establish, with a level of real-world, large-sample, multi-year evidence that few other interventions examined in this book can match, is that the relationship between hours worked and value produced is considerably less mechanical than a century of factory-floor management assumption has generally treated it as being — a finding entirely consistent with the challenge-skill balance research from Chapter Six, in which output depends heavily on engagement and structural design rather than on raw hours logged, and entirely consistent with the busy-ethic and retirement research from Chapter Eleven, in which the structure and meaning of time, not merely its total quantity, determines whether time spent produces genuine wellbeing and genuine contribution or merely the appearance of both. Reduced hours, deliberately engineered around a genuine audit of what work actually needs doing, appear capable of shifting an organization's underlying motivational mix away from the economic-pressure-driven compliance this book's Chapter Two traced back to the earliest days of industrial wage labor, and toward something closer to the direct, durable motives — play, purpose, potential — this book has argued were always the more productive foundation, even before any employer had reason to test the proposition directly and at this scale.

The next chapter turns from a structural change in the quantity of working hours to a considerably more ambitious and more contested kind of deliberate redesign: organizations that attempted to rebuild not merely how much time employees spent at work, but the entire internal architecture of authority, evaluation, and daily decision-making surrounding that time, with results that prove, on close examination, to be considerably more mixed than either company's own promotional materials have historically suggested.
