# Two Trials, Two Baskets

In January 2017, the Finnish government, working through its social insurance institution, began mailing checks to two thousand randomly selected unemployed people scattered across the country in villages, towns, and cities of every size, a monthly payment of 560 euros, tax-exempt, unconditional, and guaranteed to continue for two full years regardless of whether the recipient found work, refused work, or did nothing at all. It was, at the time, one of the most closely watched social-policy experiments in the world, administered by the Finnish social insurance institution Kela and covered extensively by international media outlets from across Europe, North America, and beyond as a genuine, rigorously designed test of an idea — universal basic income — that had, until then, mostly existed as a subject of academic debate, think-tank white papers, and utopian speculation rather than as lived, randomized, government-administered policy affecting real people's real monthly budgets.

The design was deliberately rigorous by the standards of social-policy experimentation, considerably more so than most of the small-scale pilot programs and think-tank thought experiments that had previously dominated public discussion of basic income: recipients were selected through random assignment from the pool of people already receiving unemployment benefits, allowing researchers to compare their subsequent employment and wellbeing outcomes, tracked through both administrative records and repeated survey waves, against a matched control group that continued receiving Finland's ordinary, conditional unemployment benefits throughout the same two-year period instead. The core question the trial was actually built to answer, as opposed to the much broader and vaguer question international headlines tended to attach to it, was narrower than the popular framing of "does basic income work" might suggest: specifically, whether removing the bureaucratic conditions and benefit-clawback penalties attached to ordinary unemployment insurance — provisions that could reduce or eliminate a person's benefit if they took even short-term or part-time work, creating what economists call a benefit trap that discourages exactly the kind of tentative, exploratory job-seeking that might eventually lead to stable employment — would measurably increase employment among a population that conventional welfare-state design was structurally discouraging from working.

The results, released by Finland's Ministry of Social Affairs and Health across a preliminary report after the first year and a fuller report after the full two years concluded, were more modest and more textured than either basic-income advocates or basic-income skeptics generally wanted to hear. Employment effects were small: recipients averaged roughly six additional days of employment over the trial period compared to the control group, a real but far from transformative difference, and one whose interpretation was further complicated by a concurrent, unrelated Finnish policy change — a new activation model introduced partway through 2018 that tightened conditions on the ordinary unemployment-insurance control group specifically, making the comparison between the two groups somewhat murkier than the original clean randomized design had intended. Wellbeing effects, by contrast, were considerably clearer and more consistently positive: basic-income recipients reported significantly better perceived economic security, greater life satisfaction, and less mental strain than the control group, alongside a more positive overall perception of their financial situation, differences robust enough to appear across multiple survey waves and multiple measurement instruments rather than resting on a single ambiguous indicator.

Read against the frameworks built across this book's earlier chapters, the Finnish result is close to exactly what a self-determination-theory-informed prediction would anticipate, rather than a disappointing or confusing outcome. Two years is a short window, particularly for a population — long-term unemployed adults, many of whom had already spent considerable time navigating Finland's existing benefit system before the trial began — whose relationship to work, competence, and purpose had likely been shaped by years of prior circumstance well before the experiment started. Removing bureaucratic benefit-trap penalties measurably reduced the anxiety, autonomy-frustration, and administrative burden this population had been living under, which shows up clearly in the wellbeing data as improved life satisfaction and reduced mental strain. What two years of unconditional payment could not plausibly be expected to do, on the evidence assembled in earlier chapters, is rapidly rebuild whatever combination of competence, purpose, and professional network a person may have lost, or never fully built, across a longer period of unemployment or economic marginalization — the deeper, slower-moving structures this book's chapters on entry-level competence-building and retirement adjustment have argued take years, not months, to establish or restore.

Kenya's GiveDirectly-funded universal basic income study, launched in 2016 and still running as of this book's writing a decade later, offers a genuinely different kind of natural experiment, not simply a larger version of the Finnish trial but a differently structured one, built specifically to test the question the Finnish trial's short duration left unanswered: does the time horizon of a guaranteed income itself change the outcome, independent of the total amount transferred. The Kenya study, run by the research organization Innovations for Poverty Action in partnership with academic researchers, distributed cash across roughly two hundred rural villages, randomly assigning villages to one of several distinct treatment arms — a single lump-sum payment, a shorter guaranteed monthly payment lasting two years, and a considerably longer guaranteed monthly payment lasting a full twelve years — with each arm calibrated to deliver a broadly similar cumulative transfer within the first two years, allowing researchers to isolate the effect of time horizon and payment structure from the effect of total dollar amount, a distinction the Finnish trial, with its single two-year design, had no way to test.

The results, reported by GiveDirectly and independently analyzed by academic researchers including teams associated with MIT, and covered in detail by NPR and other outlets in late 2023 as some of the earliest substantial findings from the ongoing study began to emerge, produced a genuinely striking finding: on several important economic measures, the two-year-only guaranteed-income arm performed noticeably worse than either the one-time lump-sum arm or the full twelve-year guaranteed-income arm, despite all three groups having received a similar cumulative transfer over the comparable early period. Recipients in the twelve-year arm showed the strongest results across the board — more new enterprises started, higher business revenues, and a meaningful structural shift in the local economy, with labor supply moving away from wage employment and toward self-employment and small-business formation, concentrated specifically in non-agricultural activity. The lump-sum recipients, who received a large payment all at once rather than spread across years, also outperformed the two-year monthly recipients on several of the same measures, suggesting that the specific two-year monthly design — enough money to matter, but not enough certainty about the future to justify a genuinely long-horizon investment — occupied an unusually unproductive middle ground: too drawn out to function like the lump sum's immediate capital injection, and too short to give a recipient the confidence that a twelve-year guarantee provides to make riskier, longer-payoff decisions like starting a new business rather than simply smoothing consumption month to month.

That finding deserves to be sat with for a moment, because it directly answers the question the Finnish trial's short duration could only gesture toward. A recipient deciding whether to start a small business, invest in additional training, or take a meaningful risk on a new source of income is making a decision under uncertainty about the future, and the Kenya data suggests that uncertainty about how long the support will last matters nearly as much as the amount of support itself. A guarantee that ends in two years gives a rational recipient relatively little reason to make a long-payoff investment she cannot be confident she will still be able to fund and sustain once the payments stop; a guarantee that extends twelve years, or a lump sum that removes the timing question altogether by delivering everything up front, gives considerably more room for exactly the kind of purposeful, competence-building, potential-oriented investment this book's earlier chapters, particularly the discussion of Doshi and McGregor's framework in Chapter Three, would predict flourishes once genuine security, rather than merely temporary relief, is actually in place.

One further design detail of the Kenya study strengthens confidence in the enterprise-formation finding beyond what a single cross-sectional comparison could provide: because villages, rather than individuals, were randomly assigned to treatment arms, researchers were able to examine spillover effects on non-recipients living in treated villages alongside the direct effects on recipients themselves. If the observed increase in local enterprise activity in the long-horizon villages were driven purely by wealthier recipients outcompeting and displacing non-recipient neighbors' existing businesses — a plausible concern with any local cash-transfer intervention, since a village-level income boost could simply reshuffle a fixed amount of local economic activity rather than creating any genuinely new activity — the researchers would expect to see corresponding declines in non-recipient business activity within the same villages. The published research instead found broader village-level economic growth extending beyond direct recipients, evidence against the reshuffling explanation and in favor of the study's own genuine-growth interpretation, though this book notes, consistent with its treatment of every other body of evidence examined across these chapters, that spillover effects in development economics are notoriously difficult to measure with full precision, and this finding should be read as suggestive corroboration rather than definitive proof.

There is a body of psychological research, developed independently of either trial, that helps explain why the specific mechanism identified in the Kenya data — short-horizon support producing weaker outcomes than long-horizon support, even at similar cumulative cost — is plausible rather than merely a statistical curiosity. The economist Sendhil Mullainathan and the psychologist Eldar Shafir, in a body of research culminating in their widely read 2013 book *Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much*, argued that poverty and financial precarity impose a measurable cognitive tax, consuming mental bandwidth that would otherwise be available for planning, forecasting, and exactly the kind of longer-horizon decision-making that starting a business or investing in training requires. Their research, built on both laboratory experiments and field studies, found that people under acute scarcity tend to focus disproportionately on the most immediate, pressing demands on their limited resources — a pattern the researchers termed tunneling — often at the expense of decisions that would pay off only later, not because people under scarcity are less capable or less far-sighted by disposition, but because the scarcity itself narrows the effective cognitive space available for anything beyond the current crisis. A two-year guaranteed income, on this account, may relieve enough acute scarcity to improve day-to-day wellbeing — exactly the pattern the Finnish trial found — without relieving enough uncertainty about the future to fully lift the tunneling effect, since a recipient still has to reckon, from the very first payment, with what happens when the guarantee runs out in twenty-four months. A twelve-year guarantee, or an irreversible lump sum, removes that specific uncertainty in a way a shorter program cannot, which may be a meaningful part of why the Kenya data shows such a stark divergence between its short- and long-horizon treatment arms despite comparable early cumulative transfers.

It is also worth taking seriously a methodological critique that applies to both trials and to the broader genre of randomized basic-income experiments generally, one raised by economists skeptical of extrapolating small-scale, geographically bounded trials into national policy: a randomized trial covering two thousand Finnish citizens or two hundred Kenyan villages necessarily leaves prices, local labor markets, and social norms in the surrounding economy essentially unchanged, because the treatment group is too small relative to the whole economy to move any of those larger variables. A genuinely universal, nationwide basic income, rolled out to an entire country's population simultaneously rather than to a small randomized subset, could plausibly produce different aggregate effects — on local wages, on rents, on the broader labor market's willingness to offer better working conditions to compete for workers no longer desperate for any job at any wage — that no small-scale randomized trial, however well designed, is capable of detecting, because those general-equilibrium effects only emerge at a scale no ethically defensible experiment could ethically or practically randomize. This critique does not invalidate either the Finnish or the Kenyan findings as evidence about individual and household-level behavior, which is what both trials were actually designed to measure and what this chapter has drawn on them for. It does mean that neither trial should be read as a complete preview of what a full-scale national policy response to AI-driven labor disruption would produce, a caveat this book applies consistently to every piece of trial-based evidence it uses, and one worth restating plainly here rather than leaving implicit.

It is worth being explicit about why this book will not treat either trial as a simple verdict on whether universal basic income "works," because the two countries differ from each other, and from the wealthy post-industrial economies where most discussion of AI-driven labor displacement is centered, in ways significant enough to make a direct policy transplant from either trial genuinely risky. Finland is a small, wealthy, high-trust Nordic welfare state with an already extensive social safety net, meaning its basic-income trial was testing a comparatively modest marginal change — removing specific bureaucratic frictions from an unemployment-insurance system that was already, by global standards, unusually generous — rather than testing what unconditional cash does for people with no safety net at all. Kenya, by contrast, is testing the effect of cash transfers on rural households with very limited pre-existing access to credit, insurance, or formal employment, a setting where even a modest transfer can unlock investment opportunities — a small shop, a boda-boda motorcycle taxi, additional livestock — that were previously foreclosed not by a lack of ambition or purpose but by a simple, binding lack of capital, a dynamic considerably less central to the entry-level knowledge-work disruption this book is primarily concerned with in wealthy, capital-abundant economies. Neither trial's specific numbers should be read as a direct prediction for what a basic income would do for a displaced twenty-four-year-old paralegal in Chicago or London, and this book will resist the temptation, common in basic-income advocacy, to treat any single trial's results as portable evidence for an unrelated policy context.

What does transfer cleanly across both settings, and across the very different economic contexts of Helsinki and rural western Kenya, is the underlying lesson about design and duration this chapter has been building toward: the psychological and behavioral response to removing economic pressure depends heavily on how confidently a person can plan around that removal actually lasting, not simply on the dollar amount involved in a given month. A short, uncertain reprieve from economic pressure produces short, cautious behavioral change — consistent with what Finland's two-year trial found, and consistent with the two-year Kenyan arm's comparatively weak enterprise-formation results despite receiving a similar cumulative transfer to the other arms. A longer, more confidently guaranteed removal of economic pressure, or an immediate, irreversible lump-sum removal of it, appears to unlock a different and more consequential kind of behavior — the kind involving real risk, real investment, and real structural change to how a person spends her time and builds her livelihood, exactly the kind of behavior this book's earlier chapters, from the challenge-skill research in Chapter Six to the potential-motive discussion in Chapter Three, would predict becomes available only once a person can trust the ground beneath her long enough to build something on it.

One further piece of context to the Kenya findings deserves adding here, since the initial reporting sometimes compressed out of the headline result: the shift toward non-agricultural self-employment documented in the long-horizon arm did not simply mean recipients stopped doing productive work and started puttering at low-stakes hobby businesses instead. Independent evaluation associated with the study found recipients in the twelve-year arm reallocating labor away from casual agricultural wage work — often low-paying, physically demanding, and seasonally unreliable — toward small enterprises they owned and controlled themselves, a shift that maps unusually cleanly onto the autonomy need from Ryan and Deci's self-determination theory in Chapter Seven: recipients were not becoming less economically active, but were becoming economically active on terms they had chosen rather than terms dictated by whichever wage labor happened to be available that season. This detail matters for this book's argument because it undercuts a lazy but common misreading of guaranteed-income research, one that assumes any reduction in traditional wage employment automatically represents a loss of productive contribution. The Kenya data suggests something closer to the opposite: freed from the acute scarcity Mullainathan and Shafir describe, and given a long enough horizon to trust the freedom would last, a meaningful share of recipients used the opportunity to build something with more autonomy and, per the enterprise-revenue figures, comparable or greater economic output than the wage work it replaced.

This lesson bears directly, and somewhat uncomfortably, on any policy response to the AI-driven entry-level disruption examined earlier in this book. A short-term, temporary support program for displaced or hard-to-employ young workers — a few months of extended unemployment benefits, a one-time retraining stipend — may, on the evidence assembled in this chapter, produce results closer to Finland's modest, wellbeing-focused outcome or the weaker of Kenya's two guaranteed-income arms than to the more transformative outcomes associated with a longer, more confidently guaranteed form of support. Whether any government currently discussing AI-related labor-market policy is politically prepared to offer support on a twelve-year horizon, rather than a considerably more palatable but, on this chapter's evidence, behaviorally less effective shorter one, is a question this book cannot answer and will not pretend to. What the evidence gathered here does suggest, with reasonable confidence, is that horizon and credibility of duration is exactly the right variable to be asking policymakers about, rather than the narrower, shorter-horizon question — how much, and for how many months — that most current policy discussion actually poses instead.

It is worth noting, finally, that both trials examined in this chapter have continued generating data well past the points summarized here, and that a book written at any single moment during an ongoing study is necessarily working with an incomplete picture. The Kenya study in particular, designed from the outset to run a full twelve years, will not reach its true endpoint until the early 2030s, meaning the enterprise-formation and labor-reallocation findings this chapter has drawn on are themselves early results from an experiment still in progress, subject to revision as researchers gather longer follow-up data on whether the businesses recipients started in the study's early years actually survive, grow, and continue supporting their owners once the guaranteed payments eventually end. This book treats the available results as the best current evidence rather than a final verdict, consistent with the same discipline applied throughout to every other still-developing body of evidence this book has cited, from the AI-displacement data in Chapter Eight to the still-contested retirement-mortality literature in the previous chapter.

Both trials examined in this chapter describe deliberate, carefully designed policy interventions, built by governments and researchers who set out specifically to test what happens when economic necessity loosens. The next two chapters turn to something considerably less deliberate and far less carefully designed: two countries where a large share of an entire generation's relationship to work and economic necessity has shifted, quietly and largely without any single architect, not through any single designed policy experiment, but through a slower, more structural erosion of opportunity that nobody planned and nobody, until relatively recently, fully named.
