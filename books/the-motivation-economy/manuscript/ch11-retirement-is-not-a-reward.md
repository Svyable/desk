# Retirement Is Not a Reward

For most of the twentieth century, and well into the twenty-first, retirement was marketed, almost without irony, in advertisements for pension funds, financial advisers, and retirement communities, as the reward at the end of a working life — the gold watch, the pension, the decades of leisure a person had earned through decades of labor. The premise embedded in that marketing is worth stating plainly, because the rest of this chapter is largely an argument against it: that removing the economic necessity to work, once a person has accumulated enough savings or pension entitlement to survive without a paycheck, is an unambiguous good, requiring no further design or thought beyond making sure the money holds out. If that premise were straightforwardly true, it would be difficult to square with a peculiar and persistent finding in retirement research, one dating back decades and still debated today: some studies find that people who retire earlier die sooner than people who keep working past a typical retirement age, a finding dramatic enough that it periodically resurfaces in popular media as a cautionary tale about the dangers of stopping work too soon.

The finding is real in the sense that certain studies have found it. Whether it means what the cautionary headlines imply is a considerably more complicated question, and untangling it requires taking seriously one of the more subtle traps in observational research: the healthy worker effect. People who retire early are not a random sample of the working population. They are disproportionately people who could afford to retire early, which correlates strongly with higher lifetime earnings, more education, and better access to healthcare — all factors independently associated with longer life, entirely apart from anything to do with retirement itself. They are also, in a meaningful share of cases, people who retired early specifically because they were already in poor health, a pattern that reverses the naive causal story entirely: it is not that early retirement caused poor health and earlier death, but that poor health caused early retirement, with death following from the same underlying condition rather than from the retirement decision itself. A raw comparison of early retirees against people who kept working, without carefully adjusting for these confounding factors, risks concluding that retirement kills people, when the more defensible reading of the same data is closer to the opposite: healthier, wealthier, better-connected people are more able to retire early, and they tend to live longer regardless of when they retire, for reasons that have little to do with the retirement decision itself.

A large, comprehensive review published in the *Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health*, pooling results from twenty-five longitudinal studies across the United States, Canada, Japan, and several European countries, addressed this confound directly by focusing specifically on early retirement relative to a country's statutory retirement age, adjusting for health status prior to retirement wherever the underlying data allowed. Its conclusion pushed back firmly against the simple cautionary-tale version of the finding: early retirement, properly measured and adjusted for pre-existing health differences, did not appear to increase the risk of dying earlier compared to working until the standard retirement age. Other studies using different methodologies and different national datasets have found the opposite — one frequently cited analysis found that each additional year of delayed retirement was associated with an eleven percent lower risk of all-cause mortality — and the honest summary of this literature, as of the evidence available in 2026, is that it remains genuinely contested, with the direction and magnitude of any effect depending heavily on exactly how researchers handle the health-selection problem this chapter has just described. This book will not pretend the debate is settled. What can be said with more confidence is that the crude, one-line version of the claim — retiring early shortens your life — is not something the underlying research actually supports as a clean, unqualified statement, and readers encountering that claim in casual conversation should treat it with considerably more skepticism than it usually receives.

It is worth dwelling a moment longer on the healthy worker effect itself, because it is one of the more important methodological traps in this entire book's evidence base and deserves to be understood well enough to recognize elsewhere, not just in retirement research. The term originated in occupational epidemiology, describing a related but distinct phenomenon: workers as a group tend to be healthier than the general population, because seriously ill or disabled people are less likely to be employed in the first place, which can make workplace exposures look artificially safe when researchers compare employed populations against the general public rather than against an equally healthy comparison group. The retirement version of the same trap works in reverse but produces an analogous distortion: comparing people who retired at sixty against people who kept working past sixty-five conflates two populations that differ systematically before the retirement decision is ever made, since the sixty-five-plus working group has already been filtered, by the simple fact of continuing to hold a job, for reasonably good health, while the early-retiree group contains both voluntarily comfortable retirees and involuntary early retirees pushed out by declining health, lumped together in a single category that obscures rather than reveals the actual mechanism at work. A 2020 cohort study specifically examining the health status of workers around age sixty before their retirement, published in the same broad literature this chapter has already cited, found exactly this pattern: workers in poorer health before reaching normal retirement age faced a meaningfully higher risk of early death after compulsory retirement than healthier workers did, a finding that locates the causal weight squarely on pre-existing health rather than on the retirement transition itself, and offers a clean, well-controlled illustration of exactly why the raw, unadjusted version of the popular "retiring early kills you" claim collapses the moment the underlying selection problem is taken seriously and properly accounted for in the study design, rather than left to distort a simple headline comparison between two groups that were never actually comparable to begin with.

There is a related and increasingly well-documented phenomenon worth mentioning briefly here, because it offers indirect but suggestive evidence for this chapter's broader argument: unretirement, the growing pattern of people who formally retire and then, months or years later, return to paid work, whether full-time, part-time, or in a consulting capacity. Surveys of unretirees, conducted across a range of countries with very different pension generosity and very different cultural attitudes toward late-life employment, consistently find that financial necessity is only one driver among several, and frequently not the primary one; a substantial share cite boredom, loss of social contact, and a diminished sense of purpose as their actual motivation for returning, which is to say they are reporting, in their own words and independent of any academic survey instrument, precisely the competence, relatedness, and purpose gaps this chapter's discussion of the busy ethic and retirement-adjustment research would predict for a retiree who had not built an adequate replacement structure before leaving work. It is worth adding a brief note on the diversity hidden inside the broad category of "retirement" itself, since early research in this tradition sometimes treated retirement as a single, uniform event rather than the varied set of pathways later researchers have documented, ranging from full, immediate cessation of paid work to a gradual, multi-year "bridge employment" phase in which a person reduces hours or shifts into part-time or consulting work before fully exiting the labor force. Later gerontological research has found bridge-employment retirees reporting measurably better adjustment on average than full-cessation retirees, a finding consistent with this book's broader argument: a gradual transition gives a person more calendar time to build the replacement structures Ekerdt's busy-ethic research identifies as necessary, rather than requiring them constructed all at once under time pressure. Unretirement itself is not evidence that retirement is a mistake, and most retirees, it should be said clearly, do not unretire; large majorities report being satisfied with the transition and have no interest in returning. It is evidence that for the specific minority who do return, the reason is rarely a shortfall of money and disproportionately a shortfall of exactly the psychological ingredients this book has spent its earlier chapters identifying.

If the mortality question is genuinely unresolved, a related and, for the purposes of this book, more directly useful question has a clearer answer: what actually happens, psychologically rather than mortally, to the structure of a person's days once economic necessity to work disappears on a known, anticipated schedule, rather than by the sudden chance of a lottery ticket examined in the previous chapter. Here the sociological research is more consistent, and one concept in particular, developed by the University of Kansas sociologist David Ekerdt in a 1986 paper for the journal *The Gerontologist*, has proven durable enough to still organize how researchers think about the question four decades later.

Ekerdt observed that American retirees, rather than simply embracing leisure as an end in itself once freed from the obligation to work, tended instead to reorganize their newly unstructured time around a recognizable moral logic he called the busy ethic: an implicit set of norms holding that a good retirement is a busy retirement, filled with volunteering, hobbies pursued with something close to professional seriousness, travel undertaken with itineraries as demanding as any work trip, and grandchildren cared for on a schedule. The busy ethic, in Ekerdt's account, was not simply a personality trait some retirees happened to have and others lacked. It functioned as a socially available script, doing real psychological and social work for the people who adopted it: it justified a form of leisure that a culture still deeply organized around the work ethic might otherwise regard with suspicion, it defended retirees against the specific accusation of senescence — of having become useless, idle, a drain rather than a contributor — and it gave definition and daily structure to a life stage that, stripped of the busy ethic's script, would otherwise consist of a great deal of unstructured, potentially disorienting free time.

What Ekerdt's framework makes visible, in other words, is that the ordinary experience of "successful" retirement, as lived by the majority of retirees who report reasonably high satisfaction with the transition, is not actually the wholesale abandonment of structure, obligation, and purposeful activity that the pop-cultural image of retirement — the beach chair, the endless vacation — implies. It is closer to a substitution: the specific structure and obligation of paid employment is replaced by a different, self-generated structure and obligation, one a person chooses and can therefore, in principle, tailor to whatever combination of play, purpose, potential, competence, and relatedness her particular temperament and circumstances call for, but one that still needs to be actively built, in most cases, rather than arriving automatically the moment a paycheck stops. Retirees who fail to build a replacement structure — who report, in the language later researchers have used to study this population, a poor "retirement adjustment" — describe experiences that will sound familiar from earlier chapters of this book: a loss of the daily rhythm work had provided, an erosion of the social contact that had come bundled with a job whether or not it was ever consciously valued, and a diffuse loss of the sense of being useful to anyone, all of which correlate, across a substantial body of subsequent gerontological research, with worse mental health outcomes in the years immediately following retirement, independent of the physical health questions the mortality literature continues to debate.

The variation in outcomes here is the single most important finding for this book's purposes, because it undercuts any simple, one-size-fits-all story about what removing economic necessity does to a person, whether that removal happens through retirement, a lottery win, or the kind of AI-driven task displacement discussed in earlier chapters. Retirement outcomes vary enormously, and the variation tracks factors this book's earlier chapters would predict: retirees who retire voluntarily, on their own terms and their own timeline, tend to fare considerably better than retirees forced out by layoffs, health crises, or mandatory retirement policies, a distinction that maps directly onto the autonomy need from Ryan and Deci's self-determination theory in Chapter Seven — the same transition, imposed versus chosen, producing measurably different psychological outcomes. Retirees with strong pre-existing social networks outside of work, robust marriages, and active community ties tend to fare better than retirees whose primary social contact had been concentrated at their job, a pattern that maps directly onto the relatedness need from the same framework. And retirees who enter the transition with an existing hobby, craft, volunteer commitment, or other structured activity already partially built — rather than needing to construct an entire new source of purpose and competence from scratch, under time pressure, after the fact — tend to fare better than retirees who had let work absorb essentially all of their available time and identity for decades, leaving nothing pre-built to expand into the newly available space.

It is worth situating this entire discussion against the historical backdrop of how recently retirement, as a mass social institution rather than a privilege of the wealthy few, actually came into existence, because the busy-ethic research this chapter has relied on describes people improvising a response to a life stage that, in evolutionary and even in ordinary historical terms, barely existed until recently. For most of human history, people worked, in whatever form their society organized labor, until physical decline or death made it impossible to continue; retirement as a distinct, extended, broadly anticipated life stage, lasting potentially decades and organized around an expectation of leisure rather than labor, is largely a twentieth-century invention, built on the back of public and private pension systems that only became widespread in the decades following the Second World War, alongside genuine, dramatic gains in life expectancy that gave the institution somewhere meaningful to happen. Germany's introduction of a state pension system in the 1880s under Otto von Bismarck is often cited as the earliest large-scale precedent, though it was originally set at an age few workers of the era were expected to survive to reach; the more familiar mass institution of a multi-decade retirement, common enough to require its own cultural scripts and its own sociological research program, is closer to seventy or eighty years old in most wealthy countries, which means the busy ethic Ekerdt documented in 1986 was describing a still-forming set of cultural norms, worked out in real time by the first few generations of people who actually had to figure out, without much inherited wisdom to draw on, what an extended, non-working adult life was supposed to consist of.

That historical youth matters directly for this book's argument, because it suggests the busy ethic and its associated retirement-adjustment research describe something considerably more fragile and culturally contingent than a fixed feature of human psychology. If the scripts around a "good retirement" were still being actively worked out as recently as the 1980s, in a society that had by then already had several decades of mass retirement to practice on, there is little reason to expect that an entirely different, faster, less anticipated loosening of economic necessity — arriving not at the culturally sanctioned end of a career but unpredictably in its middle or even at its very beginning, as the AI-driven entry-level disruption described in earlier chapters threatens to do — will find any comparable script already waiting for it. Retirees in the 1980s and after had, whatever their individual struggles, an entire cultural apparatus available to help them interpret and structure their newly unstructured time: a recognized social category, a body of popular advice literature, community organizations built specifically to serve them, and peers going through the identical transition on a predictable, age-linked schedule they could compare notes with. A twenty-four-year-old whose career ladder's bottom rung disappears earlier than she expected has none of that apparatus available to her, no socially recognized category to occupy, no peer cohort going through an identical, well-understood transition, and no decades of accumulated cultural wisdom about how to build a replacement structure for something that was never supposed to disappear this early in the first place.

This last pattern deserves particular emphasis, because it is where the retirement research offers its clearest, most transferable lesson for the AI-driven labor transition this book is centrally concerned with. A person who spends her thirties and forties building only professional competence, with no parallel investment in the kind of self-directed, intrinsically motivated activity Chapters Four through Seven of this book examined in detail, arrives at whatever moment her economic necessity to work eventually loosens — whether through a planned retirement decades from now, or through an unplanned contraction in available entry-level and mid-career work considerably sooner — with comparatively little already built to fall back on, and correspondingly worse odds, according to the retirement-adjustment research, of navigating that transition well. This is emphatically not an argument that people currently worried about AI-driven job displacement should simply take up a hobby, cultivate a richer inner life, and consider the underlying economic problem thereby solved — a conclusion this book's own later emphasis, in Chapter Nineteen, on why the meaning gap is not a luxury problem, explicitly rules out. The stakes involved in an involuntary, unplanned, and in many cases economically painful career disruption are categorically different from a voluntary retirement decision made after decades of stable saving and deliberate preparation, and this book will not minimize, or paper over, that difference. It is an argument that the retirement research, imperfect and still-debated as parts of it remain, offers real, transferable evidence for a claim the rest of this book keeps returning to from different angles: the structures that make a loosening of economic necessity survivable, rather than corrosive, are mostly not built in the moment necessity loosens. They are built, or left unbuilt, in the years and decades before it happens, which is exactly why the entry-level competence-and-purpose-building years examined in Chapters Eight and Nine matter so disproportionately for a generation now encountering AI-driven disruption earlier in that building process than any generation has had to before.

The retirement literature examined here describes individuals navigating a transition mostly on their own, with whatever personal resources, relationships, and habits they happened to bring into it. The next two chapters turn to something considerably larger and more deliberate: two national governments that tried, through carefully designed policy experiments rather than personal improvisation, to answer a version of the same question at scale — what happens when a whole population's economic necessity is loosened not by chance, not by a career-long accumulation of savings, and not by the slow arrival of a culturally scripted life stage, but by direct, deliberate, government-administered cash transfer — and whether the duration and design of that transfer changes the resulting answer as much as this book's evidence, accumulated so far across lottery winners and retirees alike, would predict it should.
