# Chapter 9 causality audit: AI exposure, remote work, and young-worker hiring

Accessed 2026-09-11.

Chapter 9 is strongest when it refuses to turn the current weakness in early-career hiring into a single-cause story. Two fresh primary/authoritative sources now make that tension much sharper than the existing secondary citation trail.

## What the New York Fed actually finds

Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington, and Amanda Pallais argue in a June 1, 2026 Liberty Street Economics analysis that remote work can explain about 64 percent of the increase in unemployment among young college graduates between the 2017–19 and 2022–24 periods. Their mechanism is not technological substitution. It is training and supervision: younger workers are harder to onboard and mentor in distributed settings, which can make employers more reluctant to hire inexperienced workers into highly remotable jobs.

The timing matters. Their rise in young-graduate unemployment begins before generative AI diffused broadly. They also report that the younger-versus-older unemployment gap persists within both more-remotable and less-remotable occupation groups even when AI exposure is held constant. That is strong counterevidence against using recent-graduate unemployment by itself as an AI-effect estimate.

Primary/authoritative source:

- Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Liberty Street Economics, “Remote Work Leaves Younger Workers Sidelined,” June 1, 2026: https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/06/remote-work-leaves-younger-workers-sidelined/
- DOI/index record: https://doi.org/10.59576/lse.20260601

## What the updated Stanford/ADP evidence adds

Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen’s revised August 12, 2026 *Canaries in the Coal Mine?* analysis uses high-frequency ADP payroll data through June 2026. The authors report that employment among workers aged 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations stands about 19 percent below where it would have been had it kept pace with less-exposed occupations for workers of the same age. In levels, employment for 22–25-year-olds in the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11 percent from November 2022 to June 2026 while employment for the same age group in the three least-exposed quintiles rose about 10 percent.

The mechanism in their data is mainly reduced hiring, not increased separations. The divergence is also stronger in occupations where measured AI use appears more substitutive than augmentative.

Most important for Chapter 9, the Stanford team explicitly tests remote work as an alternative explanation. The young-worker exposure gap persists when they control for remote-work exposure, as well as when they exclude technology firms and computer occupations and control for interest-rate sensitivity. That does not prove AI caused the gap. The authors themselves call the results early descriptive indicators rather than causal estimates, note that the patterns weaken when education is controlled for, acknowledge some pre-generative-AI divergence, and report that the effect is more pronounced in their ADP sample than in national survey benchmarks.

Primary source:

- Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, and Ruyu Chen, “Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence,” Stanford Digital Economy Lab, revised August 12, 2026: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/

## The claim boundary the chapter should preserve

These findings are not mutually exclusive because they estimate different things from different populations and time windows.

The New York Fed asks why unemployment rose for young college graduates and finds that the timing and remotability pattern can explain a large share of that increase before the generative-AI period. Stanford/ADP asks whether employment trajectories after November 2022 diverge by age and AI exposure inside a very large payroll sample, and finds a widening exposure gradient among 22–25-year-olds that survives a remote-work control.

The cleanest defensible conclusion is therefore not “AI caused the graduate jobs crisis” or “remote work explains it, not AI.” It is narrower and more useful to the book: the apprenticeship environment for young workers appears to be weakening from at least two directions at once. Remote work can reduce incidental supervision and make firms wary of inexperienced hires; AI can reduce demand for some highly exposed junior task bundles. The observational designs do not yet identify how much each channel contributes to any one worker’s outcome.

A future manuscript pass should use the disagreement narratively rather than smoothing it away: one dataset says the trouble starts too early to blame ChatGPT; another says that after ChatGPT, an AI-exposure gradient among the youngest workers keeps widening even after controlling for remote work. That is a better story than a single culprit because it makes the institutional training problem—the book’s actual subject—harder to evade.
