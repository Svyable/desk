# Selection ledger — The Visitors

This ledger records the working winner, strongest alternatives, and the reason the choice could change. It is editorial evidence, not a claim of objective ranking.

| Decade | Selection | Strongest alternatives | Why this selection currently wins | What could overturn it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1770s | Conrad Alexandre Gérard (France) | Marquis de Lafayette; Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben | French recognition, the 1778 alliance, and Gérard's reception as the first French minister concentrate the transition from rebellion to internationally recognized statehood. | A weighting that prioritizes military or personal influence over diplomatic recognition would strengthen Lafayette or Steuben. |
| 1780s | Diego de Gardoqui (Spain) | Pieter Johan van Berckel; British diplomatic representatives after independence | The Jay-Gardoqui negotiation over the Mississippi exposes Confederation weakness, sectional conflict, western allegiance, and the difference between territorial claim and usable sovereignty. | Evidence that another envoy's diplomacy affected federal redesign or national survival more directly. |
| 1790s | Edmond-Charles “Citizen” Genêt (France) | George Hammond; French agents associated with the XYZ crisis | Genêt's conduct forced the Washington administration to operationalize neutrality and defend constitutional foreign-policy authority against allied popular pressure. | A weighting toward formal treaty consequence could elevate Hammond/Jay Treaty diplomacy; a weighting toward war risk could elevate the XYZ Affair actors. |
| 1800s | Sidi Soliman Mellimelli (Tunis) | Anthony Merry; Spanish and French envoys around Louisiana | His mission links Barbary diplomacy, naval state capacity, a documented Ramadan accommodation at Jefferson's dinner, and America's first official encounter with a Muslim envoy. | A narrower definition of material consequence could favor diplomats involved in Louisiana or Anglo-American crisis. |
| 1810s | Luis de Onís (Spain) | British negotiators at Ghent; Latin American revolutionary representatives | The Adams-Onís settlement transfers Florida, settles continental boundaries, and temporarily places Texas outside U.S. claims, revealing negotiated expansion rather than inevitable map growth. | A weighting toward war termination could place a Ghent negotiator first. |
| 1820s | Marquis de Lafayette (France) | Representatives of new Latin American republics; British diplomats around hemispheric policy | The 1824–25 tour through all twenty-four states turns living Revolutionary memory into national civic inheritance at the moment the founding generation is disappearing. | A weighting toward formal foreign-policy doctrine could elevate Latin American or British diplomatic actors. |
| 1830s | Alexis de Tocqueville (France) | Mexican representatives during the Texas crisis; British envoys | His officially commissioned prison-study journey produces the most durable foreign interpretation of American democracy and gives Americans a vocabulary they repeatedly adopt for themselves. | Restricting “dignitary” to accredited diplomats or treaty negotiators would disqualify him. |
| 1840s | Lord Ashburton (United Kingdom) | Mexican diplomats before/during the Mexican-American War; Oregon negotiators | The Webster-Ashburton settlement demonstrates that Britain and the U.S. can remove serious ignition points through negotiation before expansionist America fights elsewhere. | Weighting direct territorial consequence from the Mexican War more heavily could produce a Mexican selection. |
| 1850s | Lajos Kossuth (Hungary) | British and Mexican diplomats; foreign figures connected to filibustering crises | His national tour tests whether American revolutionary sympathy creates an obligation to intervene abroad and exposes the gap between moral identification and strategic commitment. | A material-policy weighting could favor a treaty or crisis diplomat over a revolutionary guest. |
| 1860s | Eduard de Stoeckl (Russia) | British diplomats during the Civil War; French representatives over Mexico | The Alaska purchase marks the surviving Union turning from existential war back toward continental/Pacific expansion, with Stoeckl central to the negotiated transfer. | A weighting toward Civil War survival could elevate a British diplomat whose actions affected Confederate recognition or neutrality. |
| 1870s | Emperor Pedro II (Brazil) | Grand Duke Alexei Alexandrovich; Centennial foreign delegations | His Centennial visit and documented Bell telephone demonstration capture America becoming a country foreign rulers study for technology and industrial modernity. | A weighting toward formal diplomacy rather than technological/status signaling could replace him. |
| 1880s | Min Yong-ik (Korea) | Japanese and Chinese senior envoys; Pan-American diplomats | The 1883 Bobingsa mission is reconnaissance by a vulnerable state studying American institutions, technology, and power shortly after establishing formal relations. | Stronger evidence for another Asian mission's direct effect on U.S. policy could overturn it. |
| 1890s | Li Hongzhang (Qing China) | Queen Liliʻuokalani; Japanese diplomats; Spanish representatives | His 1896 reception exposes the contradiction between American desire for Chinese markets and a domestic regime of Chinese exclusion as the U.S. becomes a Pacific power. | A weighting toward annexation or war could favor Hawaiian, Japanese, or Spanish candidates. |
| 1900s | Prince Henry of Prussia (Germany) | Baron Kaneko Kentarō; Portsmouth peace delegates; British/Latin American diplomats | His 1902 charm tour captures two rising industrial states trying to improve public feeling while strategic rivalry grows underneath cultural familiarity. | A weighting toward negotiated peace could favor a Russo-Japanese War or Portsmouth figure. |
| 1910s | Arthur Balfour (United Kingdom) | Marshal Joseph Joffre; René Viviani; Japanese diplomats | The 1917 British mission converts U.S. entry into practical coalition work across finance, shipping, naval affairs, trade, and war planning, revealing the shift toward American indispensability. | A stronger emphasis on public mobilization for war could elevate Joffre/Viviani. |
| 1920s | Queen Marie of Romania | Prince of Wales; Washington Naval Conference delegates | Her 1926 tour is the strongest lens on diplomacy becoming celebrity and mass-media performance, especially for a smaller state seeking American attention rather than alliance. | This is among the most vulnerable selections; prioritizing hard policy would likely elevate a naval-conference participant. |
| 1930s | King George VI (United Kingdom) | William Lyon Mackenzie King; Latin American leaders under the Good Neighbor policy | The 1939 royal visit makes Britain emotionally familiar to Americans on the eve of war while Roosevelt and the king discuss real strategic danger beneath the public social program. | A weighting toward hemispheric policy could elevate a Latin American leader; direct executive influence favors Mackenzie King. |
| 1940s | Winston Churchill (United Kingdom) | Charles de Gaulle; Vyacheslav Molotov; King Ibn Saud | Arcadia institutionalizes wartime Anglo-American coordination; Churchill's 1946 U.S. speech also helps give public language to the emerging postwar division. | A narrower decade-specific weighting toward the Middle East could elevate Ibn Saud; toward coalition legitimacy, de Gaulle or Molotov. |
| 1950s | Nikita Khrushchev (Soviet Union) | Queen Elizabeth II; Jawaharlal Nehru; Konrad Adenauer | The 1959 tour and Camp David talks put the nuclear adversary inside American public life and normalize direct summit contact without pretending the short thaw was durable. | A weighting toward alliance construction or nonalignment could elevate Adenauer or Nehru. |
| 1960s | Levi Eshkol (Israel) | Charles de Gaulle; Ayub Khan; Harold Wilson; foreign leaders connected to Vietnam | His 1964 official visit marks a consequential stage in the gradual reclassification and deepening of the U.S.-Israel relationship before its later form had become inevitable. | If direct wartime impact is weighted more heavily, a Vietnam-era allied or adversarial leader could replace him. |
| 1970s | Deng Xiaoping (People's Republic of China) | Anwar Sadat; Menachem Begin; Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi | The 1979 visit, immediately after normalization, makes the new U.S.-China relationship publicly imaginable while Deng studies American industry, technology, and management. | A weighting toward direct peace agreements could elevate Sadat or Begin because of Camp David. |
| 1980s | Mikhail Gorbachev (Soviet Union) | Margaret Thatcher; Helmut Kohl; Yasuhiro Nakasone | The 1987 Washington summit produces the INF Treaty, eliminating an entire missile class with verification while making a changing Soviet leader legible to American publics. | Very strong selection; would require evidence another visitor altered the decade's security order more directly. |
| 1990s | Nelson Mandela (South Africa) | Boris Yeltsin; Yitzhak Rabin and Yasser Arafat; Václav Havel | The 1990 tour helps consolidate U.S. treatment of the ANC as a legitimate future governing force, preserves pressure on apartheid, and reflects America's own civil-rights history back at the host country. | A weighting toward the collapse of the Soviet order could favor Yeltsin; toward a single peace process, Rabin/Arafat. |
| 2000s | Tony Blair (United Kingdom) | Jacques Chirac; Manmohan Singh; Pervez Musharraf; Hamid Karzai | Blair's repeated partnership with George W. Bush supplies forces, diplomatic companionship, and additional legitimacy to post-9/11 war—especially Iraq—despite the asymmetry of power. | A weighting toward strategic durability may favor Singh and the U.S.-India nuclear initiative; a weighting toward constraining American power may favor Chirac's no. |
| 2010s | Xi Jinping (People's Republic of China) | Pope Francis; Angela Merkel; Narendra Modi; Benjamin Netanyahu | Xi's 2012, 2013, and 2015 encounters capture the breakdown of engagement as a sufficient theory as U.S.-China interdependence becomes inseparable from cyber, technology, trade, climate, and security competition. | Weighting moral/cultural influence more heavily could elevate Francis; alliance leadership could favor Merkel. |
| 2020s | King Charles III (United Kingdom) — provisional | Volodymyr Zelenskyy; Narendra Modi; Israeli and NATO leaders; future candidates | His April 2026 state visit and address to Congress during America's 250th anniversary create the book's most exact historical loop: the sovereign of the former imperial power commemorating the republic's survival after rebellion against the crown. | Zelenskyy likely has the stronger direct material-policy claim as of 2026. Any later visitor can replace Charles if the decade's eventual evidence warrants it. |

## Cross-decade checks

The list contains repeated British figures because the U.S.-U.K. relationship is unusually useful for tracing America's changing status: enemy empire, negotiated neighbor, wartime partner, alliance, and ceremonial continuity. That repetition is historically defensible but should be challenged during publication review rather than accepted automatically.

The list also moves deliberately from formal diplomats toward leaders whose public presence itself becomes politically important as media, mass politics, aviation, and television change the nature of a visit. That shift is part of the book's argument, not a loosening of the selection standard without explanation.

The 1920s and 2020s selections are currently the most vulnerable. The 1980s selection is the least vulnerable. The 1830s selection depends most heavily on the broad definition of dignitary. The 2000s selection is most likely to be mistaken for moral approval and therefore requires the clearest separation of importance from endorsement.
