# The Visit That Changed Israel's Place

When Israeli prime minister Levi Eshkol came to Washington in June 1964, the United States had recognized Israel for sixteen years.

Recognition was not the same thing as alliance.

That distinction is easy to lose from the far side of history.

The early American relationship with Israel was important but cautious, shaped by competing concerns about Arab states, oil, Soviet influence, refugees, regional war, domestic politics, nuclear proliferation, and the danger of being pulled too deeply into Middle Eastern conflict. France, not the United States, had been Israel's most important external arms supplier in much of the 1950s and early 1960s. President Dwight Eisenhower had pressured Israel, Britain, and France to withdraw after the 1956 Suez Crisis.

The relationship existed.

Its final shape had not been decided.

Eshkol's visit belongs to the decade because it helped make a closer relationship visible at the highest level.

President Lyndon Johnson received him at the White House. The meetings dealt with security, water, arms, Arab-Israeli tensions, refugees, and Israel's nuclear program. The two leaders did not settle every disagreement. The significance was the political geometry of the encounter.

An Israeli prime minister was being treated as a partner whose security concerns required sustained presidential attention.

That was a change in status.

The United States had supported Israel diplomatically and economically before Johnson. Harry Truman's rapid recognition in 1948 is one of the most famous decisions of his presidency. Congress and American public opinion contained strong support for Israel. American Jewish organizations built political and philanthropic ties. Successive administrations tried to manage the Arab-Israeli conflict while limiting Soviet influence in the region.

But presidents before Johnson had often tried to keep a visible distance from an automatic security commitment.

The Middle East was a balance problem.

The United States wanted relationships with Arab governments as well as Israel. It wanted oil supplies and regional access. It wanted to prevent Moscow from gaining clients. It wanted to reduce the risk that a local war would become a superpower confrontation.

Every move toward one state affected the others.

Eshkol arrived while that balance was changing.

The broader Cold War had entered the Middle East deeply. The Soviet Union supplied major Arab states with weapons. Egypt under Gamal Abdel Nasser had become a central force in Arab nationalism and a leader in the nonaligned world even as it developed close military ties with Moscow. Syria's politics were unstable. Jordan occupied a difficult strategic position. The Palestinian refugee crisis created by the 1948 war remained unresolved.

Israel saw itself surrounded by hostile states and argued that military deterrence was essential to survival.

Washington saw a region in which every arms transfer could intensify an arms race.

This is why weapons were diplomacy.

The Kennedy administration had already moved toward a significant American arms relationship by agreeing to sell Hawk anti-aircraft missiles to Israel. Under Johnson, the relationship widened. The United States later approved sales of tanks and combat aircraft, steps that helped move America toward the role of Israel's principal great-power supplier as France's relationship with Israel changed.

Eshkol's visit did not by itself create that transition.

It helped normalize it.

Normalization is one of the hidden forms of diplomatic change.

A policy that would have required extraordinary justification becomes one item on an agenda.

A leader who once needed an informal meeting receives an official welcome.

Security cooperation moves from exception toward expectation.

The relationship acquires routines.

Routines are how alliances form before treaties declare them.

The nuclear issue revealed the limits of intimacy.

American officials were concerned about Israel's nuclear activities at Dimona and sought inspections and assurances about the purpose of the program. Israel guarded its strategic ambiguity and resisted arrangements that could compromise what it considered essential security interests.

The disagreement mattered because it showed that closer relations did not eliminate mistrust.

Friends can still conceal.

Partners can still pressure.

A relationship can deepen while central questions remain unresolved.

That is more historically useful than describing the visit as the moment America simply "chose" Israel.

The United States was moving closer while still trying to preserve a regional strategy larger than one bilateral relationship.

The transformation accelerated after the 1967 Six-Day War.

Israel's rapid military victory over Egypt, Jordan, and Syria changed the strategic map. Israel captured the Sinai Peninsula, Gaza Strip, West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Golan Heights. The territorial consequences created new wars, occupations, settlement disputes, diplomatic negotiations, Palestinian displacement and resistance, and legal controversies that continue to shape the region.

The war also changed how American strategists evaluated Israel.

A state that had appeared vulnerable demonstrated substantial military capability. In a Cold War frame, that capability had strategic value.

The Johnson administration's relationship with Israel became part of a larger pattern in which the United States increasingly saw the country as a durable regional partner.

Again, 1964 was not the endpoint.

It was the hinge before the hinge became obvious.

That is why Eshkol is a useful visitor.

A famous leader arriving after the alliance had fully matured would show the finished architecture. Eshkol arrived while the architecture was still being built.

His visit therefore makes uncertainty visible.

That uncertainty included American domestic politics.

Support for Israel drew on many sources: sympathy after the Holocaust, religious and cultural connections, Cold War calculations, democratic affinity, organized political advocacy, strategic assessments, and personal relationships among leaders. Opposition or caution also drew on multiple sources: fear of alienating Arab states, concern about regional instability, oil interests, refugee questions, and doubts about territorial conflict.

Foreign policy was not produced by one lobby, one president, or one strategic theory.

It emerged from a coalition of motives that strengthened over time.

Johnson's personal style mattered inside that coalition. He valued political relationships and had long-standing ties to American Jewish leaders and supporters of Israel. Yet presidential preference operated inside institutional constraints. The State Department, Pentagon, intelligence agencies, Congress, and allies all had views. Arms decisions required balancing regional consequences. Nuclear questions required technical verification. War could reorder priorities overnight.

Personal warmth is not policy by itself.

It can make some policies easier to imagine.

That was one of the functions of the visit.

Eshkol himself was not a theatrical figure in the mold of Ben-Gurion. His political importance came from managing transition. He had succeeded Israel's founding prime minister and inherited difficult security and domestic questions. In Washington, that less dramatic style may have helped present Israel not as a revolutionary project seeking recognition but as an established state seeking a durable relationship.

States become ordinary through protocol.

An official visit is one of the rituals that makes ordinariness visible.

Motorcades, meetings, photographs, dinners, communiqués, and joint appearances can look ceremonial compared with wars or arms deals.

Ceremony is political infrastructure.

It tells bureaucracies and publics how a relationship should be categorized.

Guest.

Friend.

Partner.

Ally.

Those words are not interchangeable.

The 1964 visit moved the category.

The decade's runner-ups are formidable. Charles de Gaulle's relationship with Washington exposed tensions inside the Western alliance. West German leaders participated in the consolidation of the Atlantic order. Pakistani and Indian leaders came as the United States navigated South Asian rivalry. The shah of Iran's visits reflected another increasingly consequential American partnership. Leaders from newly independent African states challenged Washington to reconcile Cold War strategy with civil rights and decolonization.

Eshkol wins because the bilateral relationship visible in his visit would become one of the most durable and politically consequential in later American foreign policy.

That consequence includes benefits and costs.

Supporters of the relationship emphasize intelligence cooperation, military interoperability, technological ties, democratic affinity, and Israel's ability to deter hostile powers. Critics emphasize the ways American backing has entangled the United States in occupation, settlement policy, Palestinian dispossession, regional war, and recurring diplomatic costs.

A historically important relationship is not an uncomplicated one.

The visitor chapter should make that complexity harder to avoid, not easier.

The United States did not encounter Israel for the first time in 1964.

It encountered a new version of its own commitment.

Before Eshkol's visit, Israel was already a state America had recognized, assisted, debated, and pressured.

After the decade, it was increasingly a state America armed, coordinated with, and treated as a central strategic partner.

The difference was built through many decisions.

The White House visit was one of the moments that taught both governments to behave as though the closer relationship might last.

Diplomacy often works that way.

The important event is not always the signed alliance.

Sometimes it is the day the guest begins to look permanent.
