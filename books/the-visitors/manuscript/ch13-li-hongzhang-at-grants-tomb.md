# Li Hongzhang at Grant’s Tomb

When Li Hongzhang came to the United States in 1896, Americans treated him as one of the most important statesmen in Asia and as a representative of a civilization their own laws had decided many ordinary Chinese people were unfit to join.

The contradiction traveled with him.

Li was a senior Qing official whose career had spanned rebellion, military reform, diplomacy, industrial projects, and China’s difficult encounters with expanding Western and Japanese power. He had helped suppress the Taiping Rebellion, promoted parts of the Self-Strengthening Movement, negotiated with foreign governments, and signed the Treaty of Shimonoseki after China’s defeat by Japan in 1895.

He arrived in America the following year as a famous and controversial figure.

He was received by President Grover Cleveland. He visited New York, Philadelphia, industrial and civic sites, and the tomb of Ulysses S. Grant, whom he had met during Grant’s post-presidential world tour. Newspapers scrutinized his clothes, entourage, diet, habits, and opinions with the relentless exoticism reserved for a foreign dignitary who was both respected and racialized.

Popular myth later attached one especially durable falsehood to the visit: that chop suey was invented for Li in New York. The story is almost certainly a fabrication, one of those legends that survives because it gives cultural change a single dinner and a famous guest.

The real visit does not need the myth.

It contained a much larger story.

Li Hongzhang is the most important foreign dignitary of the 1890s because his tour forced the United States to confront two incompatible desires at the moment it was becoming a Pacific power: Americans wanted access to China while restricting access by Chinese people.

The Chinese Exclusion Act had been enacted in 1882 and extended in the 1890s. It represented the first major federal law to bar immigration on the basis of nationality and class in a way directed against a specific ethnic labor population. Chinese workers already in the United States faced violence, discrimination, exclusion from citizenship, and local campaigns designed to drive them out.

Yet the Qing empire was too important to insult casually.

China represented an enormous potential market. American missionaries, merchants, manufacturers, diplomats, and strategists cared deeply about access. The United States did not possess colonies on the Chinese mainland, but it wanted the commercial privileges European powers and Japan were extracting. Within a few years the Open Door notes would articulate a policy favoring equal commercial opportunity and preservation of Chinese territorial integrity, at least in formal principle.

Li arrived in the gap between the country America wanted to trade with and the people America wanted to exclude.

He understood the contradiction.

Chinese officials had protested exclusion and mistreatment for years. The Burlingame Treaty of 1868 had initially recognized broad migration rights, but American anti-Chinese politics pushed the relationship in the opposite direction. The Page Act of 1875 restricted the entry of Chinese women under discriminatory assumptions, and the 1882 exclusion law dramatically narrowed Chinese immigration. The Geary Act of 1892 extended exclusion and imposed additional documentation requirements.

By 1896, American law had made racial restriction a feature of foreign relations.

Li’s presence gave the policy a diplomatic face.

This is one reason elite visits matter in the history of immigration. Governments can ignore the grievances of marginalized migrants more easily when those grievances remain local. A foreign dignitary can convert discrimination into interstate friction by saying: these people you mistreat are also subjects of the government I represent.

The argument does not require the foreign government to be egalitarian.

Qing China had its own hierarchies and severe internal inequalities. Li himself was an imperial official, not a liberal democrat. His objection to American exclusion did not arise from a universal modern doctrine of open borders.

He objected because discrimination against Chinese subjects damaged China’s dignity and interests.

That was enough to make American racism a foreign-policy problem.

The United States has repeatedly discovered that domestic categories travel.

Race laws affect diplomacy. Immigration quotas affect alliances. Segregation affects propaganda. Religious restrictions affect security partnerships. Treatment of foreign students affects scientific relationships. A government cannot maintain one moral identity at home and assume every foreign audience will accept a different one abroad.

Li’s visit made this visible before the United States became a formal global superpower.

The problem would become even more acute in the twentieth century. During the Second World War, U.S. officials worried that Chinese exclusion aided Japanese propaganda among Asian audiences. Congress repealed the Chinese Exclusion Act in 1943, though immigration remained tightly limited. During the Cold War, segregation and racial violence damaged American claims to lead the “free world” and gave the Soviet Union propaganda material.

The pattern existed earlier.

Li Hongzhang arrived as both honored guest and representative of an excluded people.

The reception therefore had an element of status theater.

American elites distinguished the Chinese statesman from Chinese laborers. Li’s rank opened doors exclusion closed to others. He could meet Cleveland because he embodied sovereign China; ordinary migrants were treated as a racial threat.

This separation between elite mobility and mass exclusion remains familiar.

Countries often welcome wealthy investors, scientists, diplomats, executives, and students while restricting poorer migrants from the same society. The distinction may be defended in economic or security terms, but it also reveals how status can override prejudices that otherwise appear categorical.

Li’s visit dramatized the hierarchy physically.

He moved through American institutions surrounded by official attention while Chinese communities lived under legal suspicion.

His stop at Grant’s Tomb added another layer.

Ulysses S. Grant had met Li during Grant’s world tour after leaving the presidency. Grant’s travels had turned a former American president into an unofficial global dignitary, and his encounters in China created a personal connection Li remembered. When Li visited Grant’s tomb in New York, he was honoring a foreign statesman he had known rather than performing an abstract diplomatic ritual.

Personal relationships matter in international politics, but they are easiest to romanticize.

Friendship between prominent men does not dissolve structural conflict between states.

Li could respect Grant and still object to American exclusion. American officials could honor Li while sustaining the laws he disliked. The warmth of a memorial visit could coexist with racialized policy.

This is a useful antidote to the mythology of leader chemistry.

Summits generate photographs that imply relationships are personal. Sometimes personal trust helps. Sometimes distrust blocks agreement. But states possess interests and institutions that survive individuals. Grant’s friendship with Li could create goodwill without rewriting Congress.

Li’s American observations also came at a moment of profound crisis for Qing China.

The defeat by Japan in 1894–95 had shocked Chinese elites. Japan, a country long situated within the same East Asian cultural world and once viewed by many Chinese officials as less powerful, had modernized rapidly and defeated the Qing empire decisively. The result intensified debates over military, educational, industrial, and political reform.

Li had been one of the leading figures associated with selective modernization.

His enemies blamed him for failures. His supporters saw him as one of the few officials who understood the scale of the external threat. Foreign governments treated him as a man with whom major agreements could be made.

His U.S. tour therefore continued the pattern Min Yong-ik’s mission had established in the previous decade: Asian officials coming to America partly to observe power.

But the scale had changed.

By the 1890s the United States was on the verge of becoming an overseas imperial power. Two years after Li’s visit it would fight Spain, annex Puerto Rico and Guam, take control of the Philippines, and establish a much larger strategic position in the Pacific and Caribbean. Hawaii would be annexed in 1898. The country whose factories Li toured was becoming the country whose navy would matter directly in Asian balances of power.

The Open Door policy would follow.

American policymakers wanted China open to trade without being partitioned into exclusive spheres controlled by rival powers. The policy could be presented as support for Chinese territorial integrity, and at times it did constrain more predatory possibilities. It was also a statement of American commercial self-interest: if China was going to be penetrated by foreign commerce, U.S. firms wanted access.

Li’s visit occurred just before the United States acquired enough Pacific presence to turn that preference into a more persistent strategy.

The irony is sharp.

America wanted an open door in China while maintaining a closed door for Chinese workers.

No slogan captures the asymmetry better.

The point is not to declare every trade-opening policy hypocritical. Commercial access and immigration policy are different domains. A government can coherently support nondiscriminatory trade access abroad while regulating entry at home. The contradiction becomes moral and political when the domestic regulation is explicitly racialized and when officials speak the language of equal treatment for American interests while denying equal dignity to Chinese people.

Li’s criticism gave the contradiction an external witness.

His tour also generated a revealing American fascination with bodily difference.

Newspapers asked about his food, clothing, age, physical appearance, and entourage. Such reporting was not unusual for foreign dignitaries, but racial hierarchy amplified it. The visitor was translated into spectacle before his political ideas were considered.

This is how exoticism works.

It makes the powerful foreigner entertaining enough to consume without requiring the audience to understand the society he represents.

The chop suey myth is a perfect example. It turns one of the era’s major diplomats into a culinary anecdote. The story survived partly because Americans liked imagining Chinese-American food emerging from a famous man’s appetite rather than from the complicated history of Chinese migration, restaurant entrepreneurship, regional adaptation, racial segregation, and American consumer taste.

Myth reduces systems to moments.

History has to restore the system.

Li’s own career refuses simplification. He was not “China” any more than Cleveland was “America.” He was a factional actor in an enormous empire undergoing crisis. His compromises with foreign powers made him a target of nationalist criticism. His modernization projects were partial. He could be pragmatic, authoritarian, adaptive, and self-interested in the same career.

That complexity makes his American reception more revealing.

Americans wanted him to be a legible representative of an ancient civilization entering modernity.

He was actually a politician fighting inside that transition.

The most consequential foreign visitors often arrive carrying domestic political struggles invisible to their hosts. American audiences see the office; the visitor feels the coalition behind it.

Deng Xiaoping would come in 1979 carrying a very different Chinese political order but a similarly urgent modernization agenda. Xi Jinping would arrive in the 2010s representing a China that no longer came primarily to learn how modern power worked. By then China had become a power Americans were trying to understand and constrain.

Li marks an earlier stage in that long reversal.

The asymmetry favored the United States increasingly, but Americans did not yet appreciate how much the relationship could change.

Other candidates for the 1890s were formidable. Queen Liliʻuokalani of Hawaii, though deposed rather than a visiting foreign ruler in the normal sense, became central to the American annexation story and the contested sovereignty of Hawaii. Japanese diplomats operated in a relationship growing more important after Japan’s victory over China. Spanish representatives confronted the United States as tensions over Cuba moved toward war. Latin American diplomats participated in the early Pan-American system.

Li wins because his visit captures the decade’s emerging Pacific paradox more cleanly than any other.

The United States was about to become a power in Asia.

It wanted Asian markets.

It did not want all Asian people.

No amount of ceremonial respect for Li Hongzhang could make those desires fit comfortably.

He stood at Grant’s Tomb as an honored guest of a republic proud of its modernity.

Then he returned to a China whose people American law still treated as excludable.

The visit had exposed a problem the next century would make much harder to hide: a country’s foreign policy travels with its domestic idea of who belongs.
