# Britain Comes to Washington

Arthur James Balfour came to the United States in April 1917 less than three weeks after Congress declared war on Germany.

He did not come to congratulate America on joining history.

He came because Britain needed help now.

Balfour was foreign secretary, former prime minister, philosopher, aristocrat, and one of the most experienced statesmen in the British government. He headed a special mission that included military, naval, financial, and technical officials. Washington received the delegation with extraordinary warmth. Secretary of State Robert Lansing welcomed Balfour as a guest of the nation.

The language of welcome concealed urgency.

Britain and its allies had been at war since 1914. Millions were dead or wounded. German submarines were attacking shipping. Allied finances were under enormous strain. The United States had supplied goods and credit while remaining formally neutral, but neutrality had ended. The question was no longer whether America would enter.

The question was how quickly American resources could matter.

Balfour is the most important foreign dignitary of the 1910s because his mission marks the moment when the United States stopped being merely an external source of money, food, matériel, and moral weight and began being integrated into the machinery of a coalition war.

The change sounds obvious after a declaration of war.

It was not automatic.

A government can declare war in a day. Mobilizing a society takes longer.

The United States had a relatively small army compared with the mass armies fighting in Europe. It needed to raise, train, equip, transport, and sustain forces across the Atlantic while maintaining shipping, credit, industrial production, and domestic political support. Britain and France possessed years of battlefield experience but were exhausted. American strength was immense in potential and incomplete in immediate military form.

The Balfour Mission was partly about translating potential into coordination.

Delegates discussed finance, shipping, naval cooperation, supplies, military planning, and the practical relationships necessary for a country that had kept European war at arm’s length to become an associate power fighting beside the Allies.

The phrase “associate power” mattered.

President Woodrow Wilson resisted describing the United States as simply another Allied member bound to every objective the European powers had developed before American entry. He wanted to preserve independence in war aims and in the eventual peace. The United States would cooperate intensely without entirely surrendering the claim that it had entered for purposes of its own.

This tension between coalition and autonomy echoes the first French alliance of 1778.

Weak America had once needed France while fearing dependence.

Powerful America now joined Britain and France while insisting it had not merely enlisted in their imperial agenda.

Balfour had to work inside that sensitivity.

He was a particularly apt visitor because he embodied the old British establishment at the moment Britain was becoming dependent on the resources of a former colony.

The historical reversal was impossible to miss.

In 1778 a French minister’s credentials had helped the United States survive a war against Britain. In 1917 Britain’s foreign secretary crossed the Atlantic to coordinate a war effort that increasingly depended on the United States.

The former empire needed the republic’s balance sheet.

Finance was central.

Before formal entry, American banks and markets had already become deeply important to Allied purchasing. War required imports on a scale Britain and France could not indefinitely finance through their own resources. Once the United States entered, government-to-government credits became a major mechanism of support.

Money was strategy.

That fact represented one of the most important transformations in American power. The United States had entered the nineteenth century as a debtor nation. By the First World War it was becoming a major creditor. European belligerents needed American capital and production. The war accelerated a financial shift whose consequences would extend far beyond the armistice.

Balfour’s mission made the shift personal.

A British foreign secretary could discuss lofty principles in public while his delegation worked through shipping tonnage, procurement, loans, and naval cooperation.

Great-power status is built out of such boring quantities.

A country matters because it can supply wheat, shells, steel, ships, dollars, soldiers, and fuel at scale.

Wilson’s rhetoric later elevated the war into a struggle over democracy and a new international order. Those ideas mattered. The capacity to make them militarily relevant came from logistics.

Balfour knew this better than ceremonial accounts of the visit usually suggest.

He came from a government managing existential constraints.

The German submarine campaign posed a direct threat to Atlantic supply. The British blockade of Germany was itself a massive maritime system. Shipping losses affected food, munitions, troop transport, and coalition endurance. American naval cooperation could help escort convoys and expand anti-submarine operations.

The Atlantic was again the medium through which European war became American policy.

In the 1790s Genêt had tried to use American ports against Britain. In 1812 maritime disputes helped bring Britain and the United States to war. By 1917 their navies were coordinating against Germany.

No relationship in this book changes more dramatically over the long arc.

The transition had been built through decades of avoided conflict. Ashburton’s 1842 mission settled dangerous disputes. The Oregon boundary was negotiated. The Alabama claims after the Civil War went to arbitration. Trade and investment deepened. Cultural and elite ties expanded. Strategic interests increasingly aligned.

By 1917 accumulated habit could be converted into coalition.

This does not mean the “special relationship” already existed in its later form.

Britain remained an empire with interests the United States did not automatically share. Irish-American opinion complicated Anglophilia. Many Americans remained suspicious of British motives. Wilson and his advisers sometimes resented what they regarded as Allied attempts to shape American policy. The postwar settlement would expose significant disagreements.

But military necessity accelerates intimacy.

Officials who have to move ships, share intelligence, coordinate credit, and plan operations become interdependent faster than societies do.

The Balfour Mission helped create that working intimacy.

It also represented a new style of diplomatic visit: the multidisciplinary mission.

Earlier dignitaries often arrived with small entourages and broad authority. Modern industrial war required specialists. Finance could not be separated from shipping. Shipping could not be separated from naval protection. Munitions could not be separated from production capacity. Military planning could not be separated from transportation schedules.

Foreign policy had become systems integration.

The visitor at the center was still a famous statesman, but the consequential work happened across committees and offices.

This is an important correction to leader-centric history.

Balfour matters because he headed the mission, not because every agreement flowed from his personal genius.

The larger apparatus mattered more than the personality.

That becomes increasingly true through the twentieth century. State visits still feature handshakes, but the real relationship is often carried by thousands of officials, officers, scientists, bankers, lawyers, and administrators working across institutions.

The dignitary is the visible node in an invisible network.

Balfour’s own name would soon become attached to a document far more famous than his American mission.

In November 1917, as foreign secretary, he signed the letter known as the Balfour Declaration expressing British support for the establishment in Palestine of a “national home for the Jewish people,” while stating that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities.

The declaration became one of the foundational documents in the history of Zionism, the British Mandate, Israel, and the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

Its later consequences are so enormous that they can swallow Balfour’s other diplomacy.

For the American story of 1917, however, his spring mission matters independently.

It arrived before the declaration and concerned a different strategic transformation: the integration of American power into a European war whose outcome would reorder the international system.

The mission also complicates the idea that the United States “saved” the Allies.

American entry was enormously consequential. U.S. financial, industrial, naval, and eventually military contributions helped shift the balance. But Britain, France, Russia, Italy, and other belligerents had already endured years of war at staggering cost. Britain’s empire mobilized soldiers and resources from across the world. France bore destruction on its own territory. Russia’s war effort collapsed into revolution. The arrival of American power did not erase what the Allies had done before it.

Balfour’s urgency is evidence of Allied need, not Allied helplessness.

Similarly, American forces did not instantly appear at decisive strength. The American Expeditionary Forces had to be built. General John J. Pershing resisted pressure to feed U.S. troops into British and French armies purely as replacements, another expression of American insistence on autonomy within coalition.

Cooperation was negotiated at every level.

That is the deeper theme of the visit.

Alliance is not fusion.

Partners share enough interests to act together while bargaining constantly over how the action will occur, who will command, who will pay, what political objectives the war serves, and what happens after victory.

The First World War gave the United States a compressed education in coalition politics.

Balfour was one of the teachers.

The public reception of the mission helped prepare Americans emotionally for the new relationship.

Britain had to be narrated differently from the empire of American revolutionary memory. Balfour’s dignity, reserve, and intellectual reputation made him useful to that reframing. He could be presented as a representative of a constitutional democracy fighting alongside France against German militarism rather than as an emissary of the monarchy Americans had overthrown.

Again, the guest helps edit historical memory.

George III receded.

The Somme came forward.

This transformation would become even more complete in 1939 when King George VI visited Franklin Roosevelt and in 1941 when Winston Churchill effectively moved into the White House after Pearl Harbor.

Balfour stands between Ashburton and Churchill in the long construction of Anglo-American intimacy.

The line is not sentimental.

It is strategic.

Britain and the United States increasingly shared concerns about the European balance, maritime security, finance, and later Germany. Cultural affinity made cooperation easier in some circles. Common interests made it durable enough to survive disagreements.

The strongest rival candidates for the 1910s include French Marshal Joseph Joffre and former prime minister René Viviani, who led the French mission to the United States around the same time and received an enthusiastic welcome. Their visit helped personalize France’s war effort and appeal to the memory of Lafayette. Japanese diplomats were also consequential as the Pacific order shifted. Latin American leaders and representatives navigated U.S. interventions in Mexico, the Caribbean, and Central America.

Why Balfour?

Because the British mission reveals the decade’s largest structural change in American power.

The United States entered the war as an associate whose resources had become indispensable to old powers.

That is the beginning of a financial and strategic role it would never entirely give back.

After the war, Americans would reject membership in the League of Nations and spend the 1920s trying to recover a sense of distance from European political commitments. The word “isolationism” would later be applied too broadly to a country deeply involved in finance, arms limitation, trade, diplomacy, and the Western Hemisphere.

The contradiction began in 1917.

America could reject permanent political obligations and still be too important for the world economy to behave as though it were isolated.

Balfour had crossed the Atlantic because that fact had become undeniable.

The British Empire, the greatest global power of the nineteenth century, was asking the United States to convert latent capacity into immediate action.

For generations Americans had measured their independence partly by distance from Britain.

In 1917 they began measuring their power partly by how much Britain needed them.
