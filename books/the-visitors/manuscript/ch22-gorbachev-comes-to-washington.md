# Gorbachev Comes to Washington

When Mikhail Gorbachev arrived in Washington in December 1987, the Cold War had already lasted long enough to feel permanent.

Three days later, he and Ronald Reagan signed a treaty eliminating an entire class of nuclear missiles.

That was not peace.

It was proof that permanence had limits.

The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty required the United States and Soviet Union to eliminate their ground-launched ballistic and cruise missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometers, along with associated launchers. Unlike earlier arms-control agreements that often capped or regulated arsenals, the INF Treaty destroyed deployed weapons on both sides and created unprecedented verification procedures.

Missiles were not merely counted.

They were removed.

That distinction gave the Washington visit its historical weight.

Gorbachev had been general secretary of the Communist Party only since 1985. He inherited an economy struggling with low productivity, technological lag, bureaucratic rigidity, and the costs of military competition. The Soviet war in Afghanistan continued. Relations with the United States had been intensely hostile earlier in the decade. Reagan had expanded defense spending, denounced the Soviet Union in moral terms, and promoted the Strategic Defense Initiative.

Yet Reagan also wanted nuclear reductions.

That combination—ideological confrontation and arms-control ambition—made the relationship harder to summarize than later memory often allows.

Gorbachev brought a different Soviet strategy.

Perestroika aimed to restructure the economy. Glasnost expanded public discussion and loosened some constraints on information. In foreign policy, Gorbachev increasingly argued that Soviet security could not be achieved indefinitely through military competition with the West.

He did not arrive in Washington to surrender the Cold War.

He arrived to change its operating rules.

The two leaders had already met in Geneva in 1985 and Reykjavík in 1986. The Reykjavík summit had ended without a final agreement despite extraordinarily ambitious discussions about nuclear weapons. Reagan's commitment to missile defense remained a major obstacle. The meeting looked like a failure in the moment.

It created momentum anyway.

Diplomacy often advances through failed agreements that clarify the remaining disagreement.

By 1987, negotiators had worked through enough of the INF problem to make a treaty possible.

That meant Gorbachev's Washington visit was not a spontaneous breakthrough produced by chemistry between two leaders.

It was the visible summit of bureaucratic labor.

Arms control depends on technicians, intelligence analysts, military officers, diplomats, lawyers, translators, weapons experts, and verification specialists who convert political aspiration into clauses capable of surviving suspicion.

The treaty had to answer practical questions.

Which missiles counted?

Where were they located?

How would destruction be verified?

What inspections would be permitted?

What data would be exchanged?

What happened if one side suspected cheating?

Trust was not the substitute for verification.

Verification was the mechanism that made limited trust possible.

Reagan's famous use of the Russian proverb usually rendered as "trust, but verify" became associated with the relationship because it captured the political challenge in plain language.

The phrase can be romanticized.

The treaty's real innovation was less poetic.

It built procedures around distrust.

That is what durable agreements often do.

They do not require former adversaries to become friends.

They make some forms of cheating harder and some forms of compliance observable.

Institutions turn good intentions into inspectable behavior.

The Washington summit therefore belongs in a history of American government as much as a history of foreign visitors.

The United States had spent decades building a national-security state organized around Soviet capabilities. Intelligence agencies tracked missiles. defense contractors built counterforces. NATO deployments shaped European strategy. Military bases, budgets, laboratories, and political careers grew around the assumption of long-term rivalry.

Arms reduction threatened interests as well as dangers.

A weapon system is never only a weapon.

It is jobs, contracts, doctrine, alliance commitments, training, basing, and bureaucratic authority.

Eliminating a class of missiles required both countries to override parts of their own security establishments.

That is a form of state capacity rarely celebrated.

Governments are often judged by what they can build.

They should also be judged by what they can dismantle deliberately.

The summit's public symbolism mattered because American perceptions of the Soviet leader were changing rapidly.

Gorbachev was younger and more media-savvy than his recent predecessors. His public style contrasted sharply with the gerontocratic image of late Soviet leadership. He spoke in ways Western audiences found comparatively flexible. His wife, Raisa, was visible beside him. He gave interviews. He walked into rooms as a political personality rather than merely an institutional emblem.

The difference did not make him a liberal democrat.

It made him legible to Western publics as a leader who might negotiate differently.

That mattered because Cold War diplomacy had a domestic audience.

Reagan had to persuade conservatives that an agreement with Moscow was not appeasement.

Gorbachev had to persuade Soviet institutions that concessions were compatible with security.

Each leader needed the other to be threatening enough to justify negotiation and credible enough to make negotiation worthwhile.

That is the paradox of summit politics.

An agreement with a harmless adversary proves little.

An agreement with an untrustworthy adversary may be impossible.

The political art lies in moving the opponent from one category toward the other without pretending the rivalry has disappeared.

The INF Treaty did not end nuclear danger.

Strategic arsenals remained enormous. Intercontinental ballistic missiles, submarine-launched missiles, bombers, and thousands of warheads remained outside the agreement. The United States and Soviet Union continued to disagree over Afghanistan, Central America, human rights, Eastern Europe, missile defense, and the future of Germany.

The treaty narrowed the problem.

That was enough.

Intermediate-range missiles had been especially destabilizing in Europe because of short flight times and the fear that a regional nuclear exchange could escalate before political leaders had time to understand what was happening. NATO's deployment of Pershing II missiles and ground-launched cruise missiles in the early 1980s had generated huge protests in Europe. The Soviet Union deployed SS-20 missiles capable of striking European targets.

The confrontation put alliance politics under pressure.

Removing the missiles therefore changed not only the military balance but the political atmosphere inside NATO and the Warsaw Pact.

A weapons treaty can alter public psychology.

If a system believed to be permanently escalating can move backward, then other forms of change become imaginable.

That was the deeper consequence of the Washington summit.

The Cold War began to lose inevitability.

The phrase should not be confused with inevitability of collapse.

Nobody in December 1987 could know that the Berlin Wall would fall less than two years later or that the Soviet Union would dissolve in 1991. Gorbachev was attempting to reform the Soviet system, not abolish it. Reagan wanted a safer rivalry and ideological victory, but he did not possess a timetable for Soviet disintegration.

History accelerated after the visit.

The treaty became evidence later observers could fit into a larger story of peaceful Cold War ending.

The danger is reading the endpoint backward.

In 1987, the endpoint was still open.

That uncertainty makes the meeting more important, not less.

The leaders acted before the outcome was obvious.

The verification regime also had lasting significance.

The INF Treaty permitted forms of on-site inspection that would have been politically extraordinary earlier in the Cold War. Inspectors from one superpower could enter facilities associated with the other's military systems under agreed rules.

Sovereignty became partially transparent by consent.

That is a remarkable institutional achievement.

The Cold War had been built around secrecy.

Arms control required carefully managed visibility.

Each side had to reveal enough to make compliance credible without exposing everything.

The same logic later shaped strategic-arms agreements and other verification systems.

The summit also complicated Reagan's public image.

His presidency is often divided into two versions: the hard-line anti-communist who rebuilt American military power, and the negotiator who worked with Gorbachev to reduce nuclear arsenals.

The two versions belong together.

Reagan believed pressure could create negotiating leverage. Critics argued that his military buildup increased danger and that Gorbachev's reforms were more decisive in enabling progress. Supporters argued that American strength made concessions possible.

The historical answer resists one-variable triumphalism.

The agreement required both changing Soviet policy and sustained American willingness to negotiate.

The visitor is useful because Gorbachev makes the bilateral structure visible.

America could not end the arms race by itself.

The Soviet Union could not normalize relations by itself.

A rivalry is a relationship even when the relationship is hostile.

Changing it requires coordinated action.

The 1980s have other important visitors. Pope John Paul II's American trips had enormous cultural reach. Margaret Thatcher's relationship with Reagan shaped Atlantic conservatism and Cold War politics. Leaders from Japan reflected the rise of a major economic competitor. Ferdinand Marcos, Corazon Aquino, and other Asian leaders intersected with American debates about dictatorship and democracy. Latin American visitors arrived amid wars and debt crises.

Gorbachev wins because no other visitor made the possibility of structural geopolitical change so visible.

The Soviet leader arrived as the head of the system around which American foreign policy had been organized for four decades.

He left having signed away missiles that both sides had spent enormous sums deploying.

That made bureaucracies revise plans.

It made allies revise expectations.

It made citizens revise imagination.

It made later agreements easier to conceive.

The political symbolism extended beyond the treaty table.

Gorbachev's visit became a media event in Washington. Americans watched the Soviet leader move through the capital of the country his government had spent decades defining as the principal capitalist adversary. The encounter had some of the same public quality as Khrushchev's 1959 tour, but the context had changed.

Khrushchev visited during an expanding rivalry.

Gorbachev visited during a rivalry beginning to loosen.

The first visit asked whether superpowers could coexist.

The second asked whether they could dismantle pieces of the machinery built to destroy each other.

That is a much more advanced form of diplomatic contact.

The treaty did not age perfectly.

In the twenty-first century, the United States and Russia accused each other of violations and disputes over compliance intensified. The United States withdrew from the INF Treaty in 2019 after years of alleging that Russia had deployed a prohibited missile, allegations Russia denied while making accusations of its own.

The treaty's end should not be used to erase its success.

Institutions can work for decades and still fail later.

A bridge is not useless because it eventually closes.

The relevant question is what the agreement prevented, constrained, or made possible while it operated.

For more than thirty years, the INF framework eliminated and prohibited a category of land-based missiles that had been central to European nuclear confrontation.

That is a substantial achievement.

Its eventual collapse carries its own lesson.

Arms control is not a one-time act of moral enlightenment.

It is maintenance.

Political relationships change.

Technologies change.

Threat perceptions change.

Verification disputes accumulate.

An agreement survives only if governments continue to believe the constraints serve their interests better than unconstrained competition.

The Washington summit created that belief in 1987.

It could not guarantee it forever.

That limitation returns us to the chapter's opening.

Gorbachev came to Washington when the Cold War felt permanent.

He and Reagan did not end it in three days.

They demonstrated that one piece of permanence could be negotiated away.

The missiles were physical.

The more important thing removed was certainty that the rivalry could move in only one direction.
