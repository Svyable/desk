# The Ally Who Said Yes

Tony Blair did not come to Washington in the 2000s as a supplicant.

He came as an ally who had already decided that the alliance itself would be part of his political identity.

That made his visits consequential.

The United States has hosted foreign leaders who arrived to restrain American power, bargain over it, fear it, flatter it, or redirect it. Blair’s importance in the decade lies in a different role: he helped legitimate the exercise of American power by attaching Britain to it.

The relationship was visible before Iraq.

After the September 11 attacks, Britain became one of the United States’ closest partners in Afghanistan and the broader campaign the Bush administration called the war on terror. Blair was unusually fluent in American political language and unusually comfortable before American audiences. He could present transatlantic cooperation not as a reluctant transaction between governments but as a shared moral project.

That skill mattered because alliances are partly military and partly narrative.

Troops, intelligence, bases, logistics, and diplomatic votes are tangible.

Legitimacy is less tangible.

When the world’s most powerful country acts with a longstanding democratic ally beside it, the action looks different from unilateral power even when the ally cannot determine the outcome.

Iraq put that distinction under maximum pressure.

By early 2003, the Bush administration was preparing for war against Saddam Hussein. Blair’s government sought a second United Nations resolution and continued to argue that Iraq had failed to comply with disarmament obligations. The diplomatic effort did not produce the broader Security Council support Blair wanted.

Britain went to war anyway.

The invasion began in March 2003 with the United Kingdom as the largest military partner in the U.S.-led coalition.

From the American perspective, Blair’s presence did several things at once.

It supplied forces.

It supplied diplomatic partnership.

It supplied a democratic ally capable of making the case for war in language that overlapped with, but was not identical to, the Bush administration’s.

And it supplied evidence that the United States was not acting entirely alone.

This chapter is not an argument that Blair caused the Iraq War.

George W. Bush was president of the United States. The American administration made its own decisions, commanded its own military, and possessed enough power to invade without Britain.

That is exactly why Blair’s role is historically interesting.

A weaker ally can matter most when the stronger country does not strictly need it.

Blair could not compel Washington.

He could confer partnership.

That partnership came at enormous political cost.

The justifications for the war became inseparable from the failure to find the stockpiles of weapons of mass destruction that had figured centrally in the public case. The occupation proved more violent and difficult than the initial military campaign. The intelligence, planning, legal reasoning, diplomacy, and assumptions surrounding the intervention were scrutinized for years.

Britain eventually subjected its own decision-making to the Iraq Inquiry. The inquiry’s findings rejected any comforting story that Britain had simply been dragged unknowingly behind America. Blair and his government had agency. They made choices about timing, diplomacy, intelligence, military readiness, and the risks they were willing to accept.

That matters for this book because foreign visitors are too often treated as decorative figures in American history.

Blair was not decoration.

He was part of the decision environment.

The Camp David meetings, White House appearances, joint statements, press conferences, and congressional attention were not merely ceremonies around a policy already formed. They were mechanisms through which alliance became public argument.

Blair’s political value to Bush depended partly on his difference from Bush.

A Labour prime minister who had supported humanitarian intervention in Kosovo and Sierra Leone could frame action against Saddam Hussein through a language of liberal internationalism, human rights, and the responsibility of democracies. That did not resolve the legal or factual disputes around Iraq.

It broadened the coalition of arguments available in support of the war.

This is a subtle form of influence.

Foreign leaders rarely dictate American policy.

Sometimes they change what American policy can sound like.

Blair also represented the older idea of the “special relationship” between Britain and the United States. The phrase can become ceremonial shorthand, but Iraq tested what it meant under conditions of unequal power.

Was Britain’s closeness to Washington a source of influence inside American decision-making?

Or did closeness make refusal harder?

Could Blair shape Bush by standing beside him?

Or did standing beside him make Britain responsible for choices it could not control?

Those questions outlived both governments.

They recur whenever an ally faces an American administration determined to act.

The strongest runner-up for the 2000s is French president Jacques Chirac, whose opposition to the Iraq invasion exposed the limits of American coalition-building and produced an extraordinary period of anti-French politics in the United States. Chirac changed the argument by saying no.

Blair changed it by saying yes.

The yes had greater direct consequence.

British forces entered the war. British diplomacy reinforced the coalition. Blair repeatedly appeared beside Bush as a co-owner of the project. Their partnership became one of the defining images of the intervention.

The choice is not praise.

Importance is not vindication.

A visitor can matter because his support makes a consequential decision easier to execute and harder to understand as unilateral.

That is Blair’s place in the decade.

His presence also reveals a deeper feature of American power after the Cold War.

The United States could often supply the overwhelming share of military capability in a coalition, yet still seek allied participation because material dominance does not eliminate the need for political legitimacy.

The more powerful the United States became, the more valuable some forms of allied consent could become.

Blair understood that and tried to use closeness as influence.

Whether he succeeded is debatable.

The war went forward on a timetable Britain had struggled to alter. The postwar order proved far less controllable than public assurances suggested. The costs to Iraqi civilians, coalition soldiers, regional stability, and public trust became part of the long aftermath.

But the historical importance of Blair’s visit is not measured by whether his strategy worked.

It is measured by how much American action incorporated his support.

At the White House and Camp David, the United States did not merely receive a British prime minister.

It displayed an alliance as part of the case for war.

The visitor became evidence.

**Verdict:** Tony Blair wins the 2000s because his repeated partnership with George W. Bush, especially over Iraq, gave American power military support, diplomatic companionship, and an additional language of legitimacy at the moment when the United States most needed to show that its post-9/11 wars were not solely American projects.
