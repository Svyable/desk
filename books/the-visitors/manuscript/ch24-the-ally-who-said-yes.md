# The Ally Who Said Yes

Tony Blair did not come to Washington in the 2000s as a supplicant.

He came as an ally who had already decided that the alliance itself would be part of his political identity.

That made his visits consequential.

The United States has hosted foreign leaders who arrived to restrain American power, bargain over it, fear it, flatter it, or redirect it. Blair’s importance in the decade lies in a different role: he helped legitimate the exercise of American power by attaching Britain to it.

The relationship was visible before Iraq.

Blair had become prime minister in 1997, four years before George W. Bush entered the White House. He had worked closely with Bill Clinton, especially during the Kosovo war, and developed a view of international politics in which sovereignty did not always shield governments from outside action when mass atrocities or threats to international security were involved. The doctrine was never as simple as intervention everywhere. Blair understood that military power had limits. But he was more comfortable than many European leaders with the proposition that liberal states sometimes had to use force rather than merely condemn events.

That outlook preceded September 11.

The attacks changed its scale.

Within hours, Blair publicly aligned Britain with the United States. NATO invoked Article 5 for the first time. Britain became one of the United States’ closest military and intelligence partners in Afghanistan and in the broader campaign the Bush administration called the war on terror. British forces joined the effort against the Taliban. British intelligence cooperation, bases, diplomacy, and military capabilities mattered beyond their size because the alliance already possessed decades of institutional depth.

Blair was unusually fluent in American political language and unusually comfortable before American audiences. He could present transatlantic cooperation not as a reluctant transaction between governments but as a shared moral project.

That skill mattered because alliances are partly military and partly narrative.

Troops, intelligence, bases, logistics, diplomatic votes, airspace, financing, and command arrangements are tangible.

Legitimacy is less tangible.

When the world’s most powerful country acts with a longstanding democratic ally beside it, the action looks different from unilateral power even when the ally cannot determine the outcome.

The distinction became central after Afghanistan because the Bush administration’s attention turned toward Iraq.

Saddam Hussein had survived the 1991 Gulf War and remained in power under sanctions and international weapons-inspection regimes. The Iraqi government had a record of aggression, chemical-weapons use, repression, and concealment. After September 11, American officials reassessed risks that had previously been managed through containment. Some in the administration argued that the possibility of hostile regimes acquiring or transferring catastrophic weapons could no longer be tolerated on the assumptions that had governed the 1990s.

Iraq was not responsible for the September 11 attacks.

That fact did not prevent the attacks from transforming the political context in which Iraq was debated.

Blair’s government broadly accepted the view that Saddam Hussein’s noncompliance with international obligations was a serious problem. The harder questions were whether the threat justified immediate war, whether inspections had been exhausted, whether the United Nations would authorize force more explicitly, and what would happen after the regime fell.

Those questions were not administrative details.

They were the war.

Blair’s repeated meetings with Bush—at Camp David, the White House, Crawford, and international summits—became part of an intense effort to align two governments whose power was unequal but whose political exposure would become shared.

Britain sought to influence the route to war, not merely the final battlefield plan.

Blair pushed for the United Nations route in 2002. That effort helped produce Security Council Resolution 1441, which found Iraq in material breach of disarmament obligations and offered a final opportunity to comply with inspection requirements. The resolution did not settle the subsequent legal and diplomatic argument over whether another resolution was required before force could lawfully be used.

Blair wanted broader international support.

He had domestic reasons as well as strategic ones.

British public opinion was divided. Members of his own Labour Party were uneasy. European allies, especially France and Germany, opposed the rush toward invasion. A second Security Council resolution authorizing force more clearly would have reduced the political and legal burden Blair carried at home.

The diplomatic effort failed.

France, Russia, and other Security Council members were unwilling to support the timetable Washington and London were approaching. Inspectors were still working. The United States and Britain argued that Iraq had not met its obligations. Opponents argued that inspections had not been given enough time and that war was not necessary.

Britain went to war anyway.

The invasion began in March 2003 with the United Kingdom as the largest military partner in the U.S.-led coalition.

From the American perspective, Blair’s support did several things at once.

It supplied forces capable of operating at a high level beside the American military.

It supplied diplomatic partnership from a permanent member of the Security Council.

It supplied a democratic ally capable of making the case for war in language that overlapped with, but was not identical to, the Bush administration’s.

And it supplied evidence that the United States was not acting entirely alone.

This chapter is not an argument that Blair caused the Iraq War.

George W. Bush was president of the United States. The American administration made its own decisions, commanded its own military, and possessed enough power to invade without Britain.

That is exactly why Blair’s role is historically interesting.

A weaker ally can matter most when the stronger country does not strictly need it.

Blair could not compel Washington.

He could confer partnership.

That partnership came at enormous political cost.

The public case for war emphasized Iraq’s weapons programs and the danger that Saddam Hussein could possess or develop weapons of mass destruction in defiance of international obligations. The United States and Britain relied on intelligence assessments that proved gravely wrong in their most consequential public implication. Coalition forces did not find the stockpiles whose presumed existence had figured centrally in the case for invasion.

The intelligence failure damaged trust because uncertainty had been converted into confidence at the point where democratic consent was being requested.

Every intelligence system works with incomplete information. The problem was not that analysts lacked omniscience. It was that governments presented judgments about Iraqi capabilities and intent in a political environment where caveats could disappear as claims moved from classified assessment to public argument.

The British government faced the same problem.

The September 2002 dossier on Iraqi weapons became one of the central documents in the domestic controversy. Its language, sourcing, presentation, and relationship to intelligence were scrutinized intensely. The death of weapons expert David Kelly in 2003 deepened the crisis and led to the Hutton Inquiry. Years later the Iraq Inquiry chaired by Sir John Chilcot examined the entire chain of decision-making.

The Chilcot report did not support the comforting interpretation that Britain had simply been dragged unknowingly behind America.

Blair and his government had agency.

They made choices about timing, diplomacy, intelligence, military readiness, legal advice, preparation for occupation, and the risks they were willing to accept. The inquiry concluded that peaceful options had not been exhausted when Britain joined the invasion and that the planning and preparations for Iraq after Saddam Hussein were wholly inadequate in important respects.

That matters for this book because foreign visitors are too often treated as decorative figures in American history.

Blair was not decoration.

He was part of the decision environment.

The Camp David meetings, White House appearances, private memoranda, joint statements, press conferences, and congressional attention were mechanisms through which alliance became public argument.

Blair’s political value to Bush depended partly on his difference from Bush.

A Labour prime minister who had supported interventions in Kosovo and Sierra Leone could frame action against Saddam Hussein through a language of liberal internationalism, human rights, enforcement of international obligations, and the moral danger of leaving brutal regimes unchallenged. Bush could make some of those arguments too, especially as the administration increasingly emphasized democracy after the failure to find stockpiles.

But Blair carried a different political genealogy.

That did not resolve the legal or factual disputes around Iraq.

It broadened the coalition of arguments available in support of the war.

This is a subtle form of foreign influence.

Foreign leaders rarely dictate American policy.

Sometimes they change what American policy can sound like.

The distinction between material assistance and rhetorical assistance is easy to underestimate because rhetoric leaves no shipping manifest. Yet democracies justify force through stories about threat, obligation, alliance, law, and identity. An ally who repeats or reframes those stories can make a policy feel less isolated.

Blair became especially valuable because Britain’s support was not automatic.

The “special relationship” is often spoken of as though London simply follows Washington. The history is less tidy. Britain opposed or resisted American preferences on important occasions. Harold Wilson kept British combat forces out of Vietnam. Margaret Thatcher and Ronald Reagan had real disputes despite their ideological closeness. John Major and George H. W. Bush worked together during the Gulf War, but alignment reflected the circumstances of that coalition and its UN mandate rather than a permanent British promise to endorse American wars.

Blair’s yes therefore had meaning because no treaty required it.

That raises the question that came to define his later reputation: did closeness to Washington increase British influence, or did the desire to preserve closeness reduce Britain’s willingness to refuse?

Blair believed access mattered.

A British prime minister who could speak privately and candidly with an American president might steer decisions from inside the relationship more effectively than a critic shouting from outside it. This was not an irrational theory. Allies often gain influence through trust accumulated before disagreement. Intelligence sharing, military interoperability, and personal access can make advice harder to dismiss.

The problem is that influence is difficult to prove precisely when the larger partner ultimately chooses its preferred course.

Britain helped move the United States toward the United Nations in 2002.

It did not secure the second resolution Blair wanted.

It did not delay the invasion long enough to satisfy critics who wanted inspections to continue.

It did not impose an adequate postwar plan.

It did not prevent many of the occupation decisions that contributed to instability.

The theory of influence therefore confronted a brutal test: if the ally stands beside the stronger power but cannot alter the choices that matter most, what exactly has closeness purchased?

The answer was not nothing.

British intelligence, military forces, diplomatic networks, and political support made the coalition more capable. British officials influenced operational planning and policy details. The relationship after September 11 was substantive at every level.

But the asymmetry remained.

The United States could wage the war without Britain.

Britain could not wage it without the United States.

That inequality shapes alliance behavior even when leaders genuinely regard each other as partners.

It also shapes responsibility.

Blair’s defenders sometimes argued that once Bush was determined to act, Britain faced a choice between preserving influence by participating and losing influence by refusing. Critics responded that a refusal from America’s closest ally could itself have been influential, perhaps by altering the international and domestic political environment in which Washington acted.

The counterfactual cannot be settled.

It is nevertheless the right question.

What is the strategic value of a no?

An ally who always agrees can be comforting. An ally willing to disagree can provide information a powerful state has difficulty generating internally. Great powers suffer from a particular epistemic risk: because they possess the capability to act, they can confuse capability with necessity. A close ally can function as an external check if the relationship is strong enough to survive dissent.

France played that role in 2003, though not as an ally inside the invasion coalition.

President Jacques Chirac became the strongest rival candidate for this decade precisely because France said no.

The American reaction was extraordinary. French opposition produced anger in Congress and the media, boycotts, mockery, and the famous renaming of French fries as “freedom fries” in some congressional cafeterias. A country that had helped the United States win independence was temporarily portrayed in parts of American politics as cowardly or disloyal because it rejected the case for immediate war.

The episode revealed how alliance expectations can turn disagreement into moral judgment.

Chirac changed the argument by demonstrating that a major democratic ally could accept the seriousness of Saddam Hussein’s regime and still reject the timing and basis of invasion.

Blair changed the argument by demonstrating the opposite.

The yes had greater direct consequence.

British forces entered the war. British diplomacy reinforced the coalition. Blair repeatedly appeared beside Bush as a co-owner of the project. Their partnership became one of the defining images of the intervention.

The choice is not praise.

Importance is not vindication.

A visitor can matter because his support makes a consequential decision easier to execute and harder to understand as unilateral.

That is Blair’s place in the decade.

The aftermath turned the meaning of the partnership from strength into burden.

The initial military campaign overthrew Saddam Hussein quickly. The occupation did not produce the stable political transition advocates had suggested might follow. The disbanding of the Iraqi army, de-Baathification, institutional collapse, insurgency, sectarian violence, abuses at Abu Ghraib, terrorist recruitment, regional interference, and political fragmentation transformed the war.

British forces in southern Iraq faced their own severe difficulties. Basra did not become a simple demonstration of a supposedly more sophisticated British counterinsurgency tradition. The United Kingdom discovered, like the United States, that overthrowing a government and constructing legitimate order are different tasks.

The alliance could win the first faster than it could solve the second.

That distinction damaged both leaders.

Bush’s approval fell as the war dragged on. Blair won reelection in 2005 but Iraq corroded trust within his party and the country. He left office in 2007 with his global stature inseparable from the war.

Foreign policy can consume the political capital that made it possible.

Blair had entered the decade as one of the world’s most persuasive democratic leaders. His partnership with Bush gave him extraordinary access. By the decade’s end, the same partnership had become a shorthand used by critics to question his independence and judgment.

The visitor changed in American eyes too.

Immediately after September 11, Blair’s solidarity was emotionally powerful. During the Iraq debate, his eloquence provided reassurance. As the intelligence case collapsed and the occupation deteriorated, the eloquence itself became suspect to some audiences. Persuasion can become evidence against the persuader when the outcome discredits the argument.

That is one reason the chapter cannot end with the invasion.

The most important effect of Blair’s American partnership may have been the debate it created about what allies are for.

Are they force multipliers?

Sources of legitimacy?

Channels of influence?

Checks on overconfidence?

Partners who share risks even when they disagree about details?

The answer is all of these, but a given crisis reveals which function the stronger state values most.

After September 11, Washington valued solidarity enormously. Before Iraq, it also valued allied endorsement. What it valued less was allied veto power over the decision itself.

Blair accepted that hierarchy because he believed the strategic partnership and the substantive case justified participation.

The judgment remains contested because Iraq's consequences remain contested.

The war removed a brutal dictatorship. It also produced costs that advocates underestimated and enemies exploited. The invasion reshaped regional politics, damaged confidence in Western intelligence and intervention, and contributed to a public reaction against large-scale nation-building that would influence later American presidents.

An allied decision made in 2003 therefore affected the political feasibility of interventions years later.

That is a large consequence for a foreign dignitary’s yes.

The decade had other formidable candidates. Hamid Karzai and later Afghan leaders repeatedly visited Washington while the United States tried to build a post-Taliban Afghan state. Pakistani president Pervez Musharraf became central to American counterterrorism strategy despite governing an unstable military regime. Israeli leaders shaped repeated debates over the Middle East. Indian prime minister Manmohan Singh’s 2005 visit and the civil nuclear initiative helped transform U.S.-India relations in ways that may prove more strategically enduring than many events that received greater attention at the time.

Each case deserves serious consideration.

Blair wins because his role reaches beyond a bilateral relationship.

He was the foreign leader most visibly embedded in America’s central choice of the decade: how the United States would use the extraordinary power and sympathy it possessed after September 11.

He helped turn alliance into authorization without possessing the power to decide the American choice.

That is a dangerous and revealing position.

The United States was powerful enough after the Cold War to act without many partners. It still sought them because material dominance does not eliminate the political need to be accompanied.

Blair understood that companionship gave Britain access.

Bush understood that British access gave America companionship.

Both were right.

The unresolved question is whether an alliance serves its strongest member best when the friend beside it says yes—or when the friendship is strong enough to survive a no.
