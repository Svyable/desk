# The Visitors

*250 Years of Foreign Dignitaries Who Changed America, One Decade at a Time*

Sven Hardy Benson

On August 6, 1778, a French diplomat named Conrad Alexandre Gérard entered the room where the Continental Congress was sitting and presented his credentials.

The ceremony would look modest beside the machinery of a modern state visit. There was no Air Force One to photograph on the tarmac, no motorcade sealed behind temporary barriers, no television audience measuring the length of a handshake. The United States did not yet have a White House. It did not have a president under the Constitution. It did not have peace, secure borders, or any guarantee that it would survive the war that had created it.

What it had was a foreign minister carrying proof that another major power had decided the American experiment was real enough to recognize.

That was not etiquette. It was oxygen.

France had formally recognized the United States earlier that year when Gérard, acting for Louis XVI’s government, signed treaties in Paris with Benjamin Franklin, Silas Deane, and Arthur Lee. When he presented himself to Congress as minister plenipotentiary, diplomacy acquired a physical form. An independent foreign state had sent a representative to an independent United States. The room contained a fact the British government still hoped to reverse.

Two hundred and forty-eight years later, in April 2026, King Charles III of the United Kingdom came to Washington during the celebration of America’s 250th anniversary. He addressed a joint meeting of Congress. A British monarch stood before the legislature of the republic that had been born in rebellion against his predecessor’s crown and helped commemorate the survival of the break.

The distance between those two visits is the subject of this book.

American history is ordinarily told from the inside out. The central characters are presidents, legislators, judges, generals, inventors, industrialists, activists, and citizens. Foreign figures enter when they become necessary to a domestic story. A king recognizes independence. An ambassador causes a crisis. A prime minister joins a war. A revolutionary arrives to applause. A rival leader comes to negotiate. Then the camera turns back toward Americans.

There is a good reason for that. Countries should not be written as though their histories were merely imposed from elsewhere. The United States has usually possessed more agency over its choices than a visitor did. Even when foreign pressure was intense, American institutions and American people decided how to answer it.

But there is a cost to keeping the camera pointed inward.

Again and again, foreign dignitaries have arrived at moments when the United States was being forced to decide what kind of country it was. The visitor did not always cause the decision. Sometimes the visitor made an existing choice visible. Sometimes an arrival compressed a larger geopolitical change into a room, a dinner, a treaty table, a train journey, a speech, or a photograph. Sometimes the foreign figure misread America and was defeated by the misreading. Sometimes Americans misread the visitor and only understood the consequence years later.

A history organized around those encounters reveals a different republic.

In the 1780s, a Spanish envoy forced Americans to confront the problem of the Mississippi River and the western lands before the Constitution had even been written. In the 1790s, a French revolutionary diplomat tested whether the new government could remain neutral when public enthusiasm pointed elsewhere. In the 1800s, a Tunisian envoy brought the Barbary conflict, Islam, and the limits of cultural familiarity into Thomas Jefferson’s Washington. In the 1810s, a Spanish minister negotiated the line that transferred Florida and helped define a continental boundary. In the 1820s, Lafayette returned as a living bridge to the Revolution and discovered a country eager to turn memory into national identity.

The pattern keeps changing because America keeps changing.

By the late nineteenth century, foreign visitors were not simply deciding whether the United States existed. They were measuring what it had become. Brazil’s Emperor Pedro II came during the Centennial and encountered Bell’s telephone. A Korean mission crossed the Pacific to learn from a country that had only recently forced its own way into East Asian diplomacy. Li Hongzhang, one of the Qing empire’s most consequential statesmen, toured a United States that was becoming industrially formidable while practicing exclusion against Chinese migrants. Prince Henry of Prussia received an elaborate American welcome in 1902 while his brother’s Germany and Theodore Roosevelt’s United States were learning to regard each other with a more complicated mixture of curiosity, admiration, and suspicion.

The twentieth century changed the scale again. Arthur Balfour arrived after the United States entered the First World War and found Washington turning from debtor and observer into indispensable partner. King George VI came in 1939 while Franklin Roosevelt was trying to move American opinion toward an understanding of the danger gathering in Europe. Winston Churchill came after Pearl Harbor and effectively moved into the White House while Anglo-American war strategy was being built. Nikita Khrushchev came not as a friend but as the leader of a nuclear rival. Levi Eshkol’s official visit marked a consequential stage in the changing relationship between the United States and Israel. Deng Xiaoping toured factories and rodeos at the moment when normalization with the People’s Republic of China began turning into an economic relationship that would reshape both countries.

Then the visits became the history many readers remember living through. Gorbachev in Washington signing the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty. Mandela at the White House after becoming president of a democratic South Africa. Tony Blair at Camp David and then beside George W. Bush as alliance became entangled with the choices that produced the Iraq War. Xi Jinping at the White House as economic interdependence and strategic rivalry occupied the same relationship.

And finally a king returned.

No visitor in this book is included because a ceremonial photograph looked important. Ceremony can exaggerate importance as easily as reveal it. A state dinner is not a strategy. A parade does not prove influence. Crowds can cheer someone whose long-term effect is small and ignore a negotiator whose signature quietly redraws a continent.

That creates the central difficulty of the project: what does “most important” mean?

It cannot mean most famous. Fame is distributed unevenly by language, media, war, mythology, and the interests of later generations. Lafayette is more familiar to Americans than Diego de Gardoqui. That does not make the Mississippi question trivial. Churchill is more familiar than Eduard de Stoeckl. That does not reduce the importance of the Russian minister who negotiated the Alaska purchase.

It cannot mean most powerful. Some visitors represented empires; others represented small or vulnerable states. A visitor can matter because of the weakness of the country he represents, because the encounter exposes the choices a stronger United States makes when power is asymmetric.

It cannot mean most admirable. This is not a hall of heroes. Several figures in these pages made choices that deserve criticism. Some represented governments built on hierarchy, empire, repression, or exclusion. Some came to advance policies Americans had good reason to resist. Some were celebrated at the time for causes later judged differently, and some were distrusted in their own time for reasons that now look revealing.

Importance here means consequence for the American story.

The selection for each decade asks four questions. Did this person’s encounter with the United States materially intersect with a change in American policy, territory, alliance, institution, public imagination, or self-definition? Does the encounter illuminate a pressure larger than the ceremony itself? Can the consequence be supported by evidence rather than hindsight mythology? And does the candidate survive comparison with plausible alternatives from the same decade?

That last question matters. Every chapter has runners-up. Some decades could support a different book.

The 1950s could reasonably be organized around Queen Elizabeth II, Jawaharlal Nehru, or another major visitor. I choose Nikita Khrushchev because the first sustained American encounter with the Soviet leader brought the Cold War rivalry into farms, factories, television studios, and Camp David in a way that exposed both the danger and the strange intimacy of nuclear competition. The 2010s could belong to Pope Francis, whose 2015 visit carried enormous cultural and political resonance. I choose Xi Jinping because the U.S.-China relationship had become so structurally important that the promises, frictions, and misreadings surrounding his visits say more about the strategic problem America carried into the next decade. The 2020s could belong to Volodymyr Zelenskyy, whose wartime address to Congress in December 2022 made the stakes of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine immediate to Americans. I choose Charles III for a different reason: his 2026 visit closes the full 250-year arc of the book with unusual precision. The choice is deliberately historical rather than predictive, and it is provisional because the decade is not over.

The category “foreign dignitary” is similarly broader than “head of state.” It includes sovereigns, prime ministers, senior ministers, ambassadors, special envoys, officially commissioned visitors, and a small number of former or revolutionary leaders who were received in a manner that made their visit a national political event. Alexis de Tocqueville appears because he came as a French magistrate officially commissioned with Gustave de Beaumont to examine American prisons, then used the journey to examine democracy itself. Lajos Kossuth appears because the exiled Hungarian revolutionary leader was brought to the United States on an American warship and received as a political celebrity whose campaign tested the line between sympathy for republican revolutions and the American policy of nonintervention.

These boundary choices are not hidden. They are part of the argument.

The chapters also resist another temptation: attributing a decade to a single visitor as though history were a series of meetings between great men.

It is not.

Conrad Gérard did not win the Revolutionary War. French soldiers, sailors, taxpayers, diplomats, and the strategic choices of Louis XVI’s government mattered alongside American forces and countless other causes. Lord Ashburton did not single-handedly create peace between Britain and the United States; Daniel Webster, British ministers, local interests, military constraints, trade, and the memory of earlier conflict all mattered. Deng did not create the globalized economy by wearing a cowboy hat in Texas. Tony Blair did not cause the Iraq War by standing beside an American president. Xi did not single-handedly transform cooperation into rivalry. King Charles did not reconcile two and a half centuries of Anglo-American history by entering the Capitol.

The visitor is a lens, not a magical cause.

That distinction makes the stories more interesting, not less. A dignitary arrives carrying a foreign government’s interests, assumptions, and constraints. The United States receives that person carrying its own. The meeting becomes a point of compression. Things that are usually dispersed across cables, legislatures, newspaper columns, trade flows, military plans, private prejudices, and public moods are forced into proximity.

The physical details of diplomacy matter for this reason. Who is received? Where? With what title? Who refuses to attend? What is said publicly and what appears in memoranda later? Which city does a visitor ask to see after Washington? A farm can be as revealing as a summit room. So can a factory, a grave, a railroad car, a hot dog, a rodeo, or a dinner timed to the setting sun.

Those objects and places are not decorations. They are where abstraction becomes legible.

Consider the hot dog Franklin Roosevelt served King George VI at Hyde Park in June 1939. It has survived as a charming anecdote, and therefore risks being treated as trivial. But the informality was political. Roosevelt was helping Americans see the British monarch not simply as the embodiment of an old empire but as a guest who could be made familiar at a moment when the president was increasingly worried about the European crisis. The meal did not turn isolationists into interventionists. It did something smaller and more plausible: it contributed to the humanization of a relationship Roosevelt believed might become essential.

Or consider Deng Xiaoping’s cowboy hat in Texas in 1979. The image is irresistible, which is exactly why it must be handled carefully. A hat did not normalize U.S.-China relations; governments had done that work through years of negotiation, and formal diplomatic relations had begun weeks before Deng’s visit. Yet the image captured the political task of the trip. An authoritarian revolutionary state that Americans had been taught to regard as an enemy was trying to make cooperation imaginable. Deng, for his part, was examining technologies, industries, and systems he believed China needed to learn from. The rodeo worked because the underlying strategic transformation was real.

Diplomacy is often accused of being theater. It is theater. The mistake is assuming theater has no political function.

A state visit arranges symbols because symbols can lower the cost of a new relationship, make an alliance visible, reassure an anxious public, flatter a partner, signal hierarchy, or disguise disagreement long enough for negotiation to continue. The spectacle is not separate from policy. It is one of policy’s instruments. The challenge is determining when the instrument mattered and when the photographs outlived the substance.

Over 250 years, one larger change becomes impossible to miss.

The United States begins this story desperate to be recognized and ends it as the country whose invitation itself conveys status. Early American diplomacy asks whether European powers will treat the republic as sovereign. Nineteenth-century America bargains over rivers, frontiers, trade, and recognition while expanding across a continent and arguing over who belongs inside its democracy. By the world wars, Washington becomes a capital other governments must persuade. During the Cold War, a foreign leader’s American itinerary becomes a global media event because the United States has become one pole of a nuclear system. After the Cold War, visits increasingly expose the paradox of American primacy: enormous power combined with persistent dependence on allies, markets, institutions, and foreign choices it cannot command.

The direction is not simply upward.

Power creates new forms of vulnerability. A weak republic needs France to survive. A superpower needs Britain, Israel, China, the Soviet Union, South Africa, and others for different reasons it cannot solve by dominance alone. The more global the American system becomes, the more consequential foreign relationships become to domestic life. A trade decision in Beijing changes prices in Ohio. A war decision in London changes the political legitimacy of a coalition in Washington. A missile negotiation with Moscow changes the number of nuclear weapons aimed at European cities. A diplomatic agreement over a cyber norm becomes relevant to an American company whose intellectual property can cross an ocean invisibly.

Foreign policy stops being foreign in any simple sense.

That may be the deepest reason to tell American history through visitors. They reveal the fiction of national isolation. The country has always been porous to the world—not only through immigration and trade, but through recognition, imitation, rivalry, alliance, fear, admiration, debt, technology, and ideas. Even doctrines of nonintervention were developed in response to foreign pressure. Even the confidence to say “we will stay out” depends on an understanding of what is happening outside.

There is also a humility in the method. Americans are accustomed to looking at foreign countries and asking what the United States did to them. Sometimes the reverse question is more revealing: what did they force the United States to notice about itself?

Genêt forced Washington’s government to discover whether neutrality was merely a preference or an executive policy the government could enforce against popular foreign enthusiasm. Kossuth forced Americans to ask whether their affection for republican revolution implied a duty to intervene abroad. Li Hongzhang’s reception exposed the contradiction between American admiration for China as a civilization and American exclusion of Chinese laborers. George VI’s visit exposed how quickly an old anti-monarchical identity could soften when strategic danger made Britain look less like the empire of 1776 and more like the front line of a threatened order. Mandela’s arrival exposed the distance between decades of American policy toward apartheid South Africa and the moral prestige of the democratic transition.

The visitor becomes a mirror, but not a flattering one.

That is why the stories behind the visits matter more than the guest lists.

Each chapter begins with a person entering an American scene. The story then widens. What did the visitor want? What did Americans think they wanted? What had happened before the arrival that made the meeting consequential? What changed afterward? What did not change? Which later mythology should be stripped away? Who else might have been chosen for the decade, and why does this figure still hold the place?

The answer will not always be final. History rarely gives us rankings that behave like arithmetic.

The 2020s chapter is necessarily the most provisional. It is being written in September 2026, with more than three years left in the decade. A later edition may have a stronger candidate. That possibility belongs in the book because it exposes what the whole exercise is: an argument made from the evidence available, not a marble monument pretending that judgment has ended.

The earlier chapters are more settled only by degree. New archives open. Biographers revise reputations. Diplomatic records complicate official stories. Consequences that looked decisive in one generation can shrink in another, while neglected encounters become legible because later events reveal what was at stake.

Still, a selection has value even when it can be challenged.

To choose is to make the causal argument visible.

A chronological survey can hide behind completeness. A ranking cannot. If I say that Lord Ashburton is the foreign dignitary who mattered most to the United States in the 1840s, I owe an explanation of why a negotiated settlement of dangerous Anglo-American disputes mattered more than other foreign encounters of the decade. If I give the 1960s to Levi Eshkol, I have to show why his official visit belongs in the transformation of the U.S.-Israel relationship rather than simply treating the relationship’s later closeness as inevitable. If I give the 2000s to Tony Blair, I have to account for the consequences of alliance, including the costs, controversies, and intelligence failures surrounding Iraq, rather than writing the “special relationship” as sentimental folklore.

The constraint improves the history.

It forces the book to ask what endured.

Some visitors leave a treaty. Some leave a border. Some leave a model. Some leave a warning. Some leave a photograph whose meaning changes with time. Some leave a relationship more intimate and therefore more dangerous. Some fail so completely that the failure itself becomes institution-building.

Citizen Genêt is in this book partly because he lost. His attempt to mobilize American popular sympathy against Washington’s neutrality policy did not pull the United States into the European war. It helped push the young government toward clearer neutrality procedures and toward a stronger claim that foreign representatives did not get to appeal over the government’s head to “the people” as an alternative sovereign. His importance lies in the boundary the United States drew against him.

That is a recurring pattern. The most consequential dignitary is not always the one who gets what he came for.

A country reveals itself by the guests it embraces, the guests it resists, and the terms on which it does both.

The first chapter begins before the United States was confident enough to take any foreign visitor for granted. The last ends in a Capitol built by a republic that had long since become accustomed to being visited by kings.

Between them are twenty-six arrivals.

They are not the whole history of America and the world.

They are a way to see the history that was already in the room.
