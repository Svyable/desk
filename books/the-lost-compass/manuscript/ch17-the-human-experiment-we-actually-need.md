# The Human Experiment We Actually Need

A decisive human magnetoreception experiment should be designed by people who expect it to fail.

That is not cynicism. It is how fragile effects become credible.

The core design is multi-site, preregistered and adversarial. At least three laboratories should use independently built three-axis coil systems inside characterized electromagnetic shielding. One site should be in the Northern Hemisphere, one in the Southern Hemisphere, and ideally one near the magnetic equator. The field strength and inclination should reproduce local geomagnetic conditions as well as deliberately unnatural controls.

Participants would undergo repeated sessions while EEG, eye movements, head motion, acoustic sensors, temperature probes and electromagnetic artifact channels are recorded. The magnetic rotations that produced alpha suppression in the 2019 study should be reproduced exactly, but embedded among active shams and physically matched conditions that reverse inclination, polarity, speed or rotation geometry.

The primary analysis must be frozen before data collection.

A replication should not merely ask whether alpha power goes down somewhere after some field change. It should test the original response topology: timing, scalp distribution, direction selectivity and dependence on the vertical field component. If the effect shifts freely across those dimensions, replication has failed even if one p-value survives.

A second stage would probe mechanism.

If polarity sensitivity remains, particle-based transduction gains interest. Carefully controlled static bias fields and magnetic-pulse perturbations could test predictions, subject to safety and artifact review. If light conditions matter, retinal cryptochrome mechanisms become more plausible. Sessions under controlled wavelengths, darkness and retinal adaptation could separate those possibilities.

Genetics can come later. Candidate variants in cryptochromes, iron-handling genes or mechanotransduction pathways should not be mined until a stable physiological phenotype exists. Otherwise a small noisy EEG effect will generate an endless association literature.

The best feature of a multi-hemisphere design is that ecology makes predictions. A response calibrated developmentally to local downward or upward inclination should differ systematically across hemispheres. A purely apparatus-driven artifact should not know where on Earth the participant grew up.

Longitudinal relocation could push further. People moving across the magnetic equator could be tested before and after relocation. Does the response retain birthplace calibration, adapt to the new field, or disappear? The answer would distinguish inherited, developmental and plastic models.

Conscious behavior should be measured but not privileged. After neural testing, participants can perform orientation or spatial-learning tasks under controlled magnetic manipulations. If EEG responses exist without behavioral advantage, that is informative rather than disappointing.

The experiment should publish nulls, raw data, coil schematics, calibration records and artifact channels.

If independent sites fail to reproduce the original effect under verified field conditions, the human magnetoreception claim should be downgraded hard. If the effect reproduces with the same ecological asymmetry and survives different hardware, the field will have earned the right to ask where the receptor is.

That is the experiment I want because both outcomes are useful.

A lost compass should be allowed to stay lost.
