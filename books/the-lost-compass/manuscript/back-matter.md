# Back matter

## Chronology

- 1972 — Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko publish foundational work on magnetic compass orientation in European robins.
- 2004 — Ritz and colleagues report radiofrequency disruption of avian magnetic orientation, strengthening radical-pair predictions.
- 2011 — Foley, Gegear and Reppert report that human CRY2 can support light-dependent magnetosensitivity in Drosophila.
- 2012 — Treiber and colleagues show that iron-rich pigeon-beak cells previously interpreted as magnetoreceptors are macrophages.
- 2014 — Engels and colleagues report that weak anthropogenic electromagnetic noise disrupts magnetic compass orientation in European robins under blinded, reversible conditions.
- 2019 — Wang and colleagues report alpha-band EEG responses to ecologically relevant rotations of Earth-strength magnetic fields in humans.
- 2021 — Xu and colleagues report magnetic sensitivity of purified cryptochrome 4 from European robins, stronger under their assay conditions than homologues from chicken and pigeon.
- 2021 — Packmor and colleagues report that a magnet attached to the forehead disrupts magnetic compass orientation in a migratory songbird.
- 2022 — Broadband 75–85 MHz radiofrequency fields are reported to disrupt magnetic compass orientation in night-migratory songbirds, constraining radical-pair models.
- 2025 — Goforth and colleagues report learned magnetic map cues in loggerhead turtles and a dissociation between radiofrequency-sensitive compass behavior and map behavior.

## Closing note

The book does not claim that humans consciously sense north, that cryptochrome is the only animal magnetoreceptor, that magnetite has been localized as the human receptor, or that weak electromagnetic effects in bird navigation imply human health effects. Those claims require separate evidence.
