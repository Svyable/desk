# Human Cryptochrome in a Fly

A human protein can do something in a fruit fly that no human has been shown to do consciously.

In 2011 Lauren Foley, Robert Gegear and Steven Reppert published a result with a title almost designed to be misunderstood: “Human cryptochrome exhibits light-dependent magnetosensitivity.” The experiment did not put humans in magnetic mazes. It used Drosophila.

The researchers worked with flies in which the native cryptochrome gene was manipulated. Human CRY2 was expressed in the fly system, and under the reported conditions it could support a light-dependent magnetic behavioral response. The finding showed that a human cryptochrome protein could participate in magnetically sensitive biology when embedded in another animal’s signaling machinery.

This is evidence of molecular capability. It is not evidence of human sensory deployment.

The distinction is worth dwelling on because biology is full of latent capacities. Put a protein in a new cellular context and it may interact with partners or redox states it never encounters in its native tissue. A receptor component is not a receptor system. Human CRY2 has established roles in the circadian clock. For it to mediate magnetoreception in people, it would need the right chromophore state, cellular location, molecular partners, geometry and downstream neural transduction.

The fly experiment supplies none of those automatically.

It does, however, keep a door open that would otherwise be easy to close. Human cryptochromes are not obviously disqualified from magnetic chemistry merely because modern humans do not report a compass sense. Evolution can retain molecular properties after behavior changes.

That idea becomes more plausible when we stop expecting sensory systems to be single-purpose. Photoreceptor proteins, ion channels and mechanosensory structures are reused. The same molecular family can serve clock functions in one context and sensory functions in another. A protein’s ancestral history may be larger than its current headline job.

But evolutionary storytelling is dangerously easy after the fact. Human CRY2 could be magnetically sensitive in a fly and irrelevant to geomagnetic transduction in humans. The 2019 EEG result could arise from a particle-based mechanism unrelated to cryptochrome. Both could be true at once.

This is why the human chapters need to resist convergence by enthusiasm. We have one line of evidence showing a human protein can support a light-dependent magnetic response in a heterologous insect system. We have another line showing a reported cortical response to Earth-strength field rotations in people. There is no demonstrated causal bridge between them.

A good research program would try to build one.

Start with tissue. Which human retinal or neural cell types express relevant cryptochromes, in what subcellular compartments and with which cofactors? Are the proteins present in states capable of the required photochemistry? Do genetic variants alter any reproducible magnetic EEG phenotype? Does wavelength matter? If a human response persists in complete darkness after sufficient dark adaptation, a light-dependent retinal mechanism becomes harder to maintain. If the response changes systematically with blue-light conditions, that becomes interesting.

The experiment must also separate circadian effects. Cryptochromes are clock proteins. Light changes arousal, retinal physiology and circadian state. A magnetic effect that depends on illumination can be confounded by ordinary photobiology unless field conditions are randomized within tightly matched light exposure.

This is where the romance of an ancestral compass gives way to experimental bureaucracy. The pathway from molecule to sense is made of controls.

Human magnetoreception also carries an ethical and cultural hazard. A weak sensory result can be inflated into claims of special sensitivity, geomagnetic healing, psychic orientation or electromagnetic illness. None follows. If a human magnetic transduction pathway exists, it may be unconscious, small and functionally unimportant.

That would still matter.

A vestigial sensory pathway would tell us something about what natural selection once measured through our lineage. It could also reveal molecular mechanisms shared with other animals. And it would remind sensory neuroscience that absence from consciousness is not absence from the nervous system.

The fly does not prove we have a compass.

It proves that one of our molecules has a more interesting résumé than we usually give it.
