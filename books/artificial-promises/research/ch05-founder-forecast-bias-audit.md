# Chapter 5 research audit — founder forecasts are not one bias

## Why this matters

Chapter 5, **“The Number in the Deck,”** argues that startup numbers can harden into organizational facts even when the assumptions underneath them have not improved. The chapter is careful about precision and projection lineage, but it currently has no direct empirical evidence about whether entrepreneurs' forecasts are actually optimistic, how that changes with horizon, or whether the effect is specific to startups.

The useful research result is less tidy than “founders overforecast.” Different samples and forecast objects produce different patterns. That complication strengthens the book because it shifts the argument away from founder psychology and toward the conditions under which a number becomes unreliable.

## Direct evidence from venture-backed companies

Armstrong, Dávila, Foster, and Hand study one- to five-year management forecasts from private venture-backed U.S. companies. Their central result is strongly horizon-dependent. One-year-ahead revenue forecasts were slightly pessimistic on average, while longer-horizon forecasts became substantially optimistic; expense forecasts moved in the opposite direction, producing increasingly optimistic long-run profit pictures. Forecast bias was also larger where assets were harder to verify or more intangible-intensive.

Source: Christopher S. Armstrong, Antonio Dávila, George Foster, and John R. M. Hand, **“Biases in Multi-Year Management Financial Forecasts: Evidence from Private Venture-Backed U.S. Companies,”** *Review of Accounting Studies* 12 (2007). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11142-007-9033-4. Stanford GSB record: https://gsbpreserve.stanford.edu/view/61198/biases-in-multi-year-management-financial-forecasts-evidence-from-private-venture-backed-us-companies

This is unusually useful for *Artificial Promises* because the unit of analysis is close to the book's actual object: private venture-backed firms producing multi-year management forecasts. The finding also supplies a mechanism more interesting than generic optimism. The further the forecast travels into the future, and the harder the underlying assets are to verify, the more room there is for assumptions to carry the result.

The manuscript should not turn the paper into a universal startup error rate without recovering the full sample construction, forecast definitions, and error metrics. The safe use is directional: in this venture-backed sample, bias changed materially with horizon and verifiability rather than appearing as a constant founder trait.

## Strong counterevidence: small-firm revenue forecasts were not systematically optimistic

Cassar and Gibson longitudinally studied revenue forecasts in a large sample of small firms. Contrary to the simple overoptimism story, they did **not** find systematic revenue overestimation. Forecasts were instead too extreme and tended to overextrapolate prior growth.

Source: Gavin Cassar and Brian Gibson, **“Forecast Rationality in Small Firms,”** *Journal of Small Business Management* 45(3), 2007, 283–302. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-627X.2007.00213.x

This matters because “entrepreneurs are optimists” is too coarse to carry the chapter. A forecast can be poorly calibrated without having a positive mean bias. A company can make numbers dangerous by extrapolating an early growth regime too far, by choosing an extreme point estimate, or by failing to update when the generating process changes. None of those requires every forecast to be too high.

## Nascent entrepreneurs: optimism appears, but the forecast object matters

Cassar's later study of nascent entrepreneurs found substantial overoptimism about whether a nascent activity would become an operating venture and, among ventures that did begin operating, overestimation of future sales and employment. Importantly, the paper also examines whether planning and financial projections are associated with an “inside view” that can increase ex ante bias rather than cure it.

Source: Gavin Cassar, **“Are Individuals Entering Self-Employment Overly Optimistic? An Empirical Test of Plans and Projections on Nascent Entrepreneur Expectations,”** *Strategic Management Journal* 31(8), 2010, 822–840. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.833

Hyytinen, Lahtonen, and Pajarinen likewise find that nascent entrepreneurs in the United States and startups in Finland were overoptimistic on average about venture survival, and that their survival expectations had little ability to distinguish subsequent survival from exit. But the paper also reports that roughly a quarter of entrepreneurs did not provide an estimate for the survival of a typical venture; among those who did, estimates were less overoptimistic. That is suggestive evidence that attention to an outside/base-rate comparison may matter.

Source: Ari Hyytinen, Jukka Lahtonen, and Mika Pajarinen, **“Forecasting Errors of New Venture Survival,”** *Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal* 8(4), 2014, 283–302. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/sej.1187

Again, survival probability, sales, employment, revenue, and profit are not interchangeable forecast objects. The book should resist converting evidence about one into a general claim about “founder forecasts.”

## Crowdfunding evidence: a useful investor-side complication

Jo and Yang study detailed financial forecasts supplied by entrepreneurs raising equity on a leading U.K. crowdfunding platform. They report systematic overestimation of sales, earnings, profit margin, and assets, alongside underestimation of leverage. Investors nevertheless reacted to some forecast signals, including forecasted sales, even though the forecasts were poor in important respects.

Source: Koren M. Jo and Shuo Yang, **“Is There Crowd Wisdom in Accounting? Evidence From Forecasts in Equity-Based Crowdfunding,”** *Journal of Accounting, Auditing & Finance* 36(4), 2021. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/0148558X20912100

This is relevant to the book's “promise as instrument” thesis because it shows a setting where forecasts are not merely private beliefs: they enter a financing mechanism and can affect investor response. But crowdfunding investors are not interchangeable with venture-capital partnerships, and the result should not be used to claim that sophisticated VCs naively accept founder projections.

## Strongest counterargument to the chapter

The strongest empirical objection is not that forecasting works fine. It is that **optimism is not the only, or even always the dominant, forecast error**. Some small-firm samples do not show systematic upward revenue bias. Near-term venture-backed forecasts can be slightly pessimistic. Forecasts can be wrong because they are too extreme, because they extrapolate a transient growth regime, because they use an inside view, because long horizons compound assumptions, or because intangible assets make verification difficult.

That means the chapter should not be revised into a morality tale about founders adding 30 percent to every spreadsheet. The evidence supports a more specific argument: a startup number becomes epistemically dangerous when the organization loses track of the forecast object, horizon, base rate, assumptions, and updating rule that produced it.

## Editorial implication

A later manuscript pass can improve Chapter 5 by replacing some of its hypothetical forecasting discussion with the venture-backed horizon result and the Cassar/Gibson counterexample. The tension is more useful than a single headline statistic:

- short-horizon forecasts can behave differently from five-year forecasts;
- poor calibration does not always mean systematic optimism;
- an early growth curve can be overextrapolated even when the average forecast is not biased upward;
- an “inside view” spreadsheet can organize assumptions while simultaneously insulating the forecaster from a base rate;
- forecast error should be discussed by object and horizon, not as one psychological property called founder optimism.

That would sharpen the chapter's existing distinction between arithmetic and provenance. The question is not merely whether the formula calculates. It is what kind of forecast entered the formula, how far into the future it reaches, and what evidence would force the company to update it.

## Claim boundaries

Do not claim from these studies that all founders are overconfident, that venture capitalists are generally fooled by projections, that a long-horizon forecast is intentionally deceptive, or that planning itself causes optimism. Samples differ by country, firm stage, financing setting, forecast object, and period. Treat each study as evidence about its own population and design.

The cleanest manuscript-level conclusion is narrower: **forecast error in entrepreneurial settings is heterogeneous, and the heterogeneity itself is evidence for preserving forecast lineage rather than attaching one generic credibility discount to every founder number.**
