# Chapter 5 — Estimates, precision, and uncertainty

Checked September 6, 2026.

## Why this note exists

Chapter 5, “The Number in the Deck,” is strongest when it distinguishes useful quantification from false certainty. The manuscript already makes that distinction, but the argument can be grounded more firmly than the psychology-of-anchoring literature alone allows.

The useful institutional comparison is not that startup decks should obey public-company disclosure rules. They should not be conflated. It is that even formal financial reporting—where numbers are produced under accounting rules, audit, and securities regulation—still contains estimates whose apparent precision can exceed the certainty of the assumptions underneath them.

## Primary authoritative source

U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Release Nos. 33-8040 and 34-45149, *Accounting Policies; Cautionary Advice Regarding Disclosure*, issued December 12, 2001 and rechecked on the SEC website September 6, 2026:

https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/2001/12/accounting-policies-cautionary-advice-regarding-disclosure

The Commission warned that reported financial position and results can imply more precision, continuity, and certainty than the changing environment warrants. It specifically urged public companies to explain critical accounting policies in plain English, including the judgments and uncertainties involved and the possibility that materially different amounts would result under different assumptions or conditions.

This is a useful boundary for Chapter 5 because the SEC guidance does **not** say estimates are illegitimate. Quite the opposite: estimates and subjective judgments are unavoidable parts of financial reporting. The regulatory response is to preserve information about assumptions, uncertainty, sensitivity, and alternative conditions rather than pretending the estimate is observation.

## What the source supports

The chapter can safely use the SEC material to support a narrow proposition: numerical outputs can look more settled than the judgments that produced them, and decision-useful disclosure may require exposing the assumptions and uncertainty underneath an estimate.

It also gives the chapter a stronger real-world analogue for its existing distinction among observations, estimates, targets, scenarios, and aspirations. A number does not become dishonest merely because it is estimated. The epistemic problem begins when the category of number, its assumptions, or its sensitivity to plausible alternatives disappears in transmission.

## What it does not support

Do not imply that Release 33-8040 governs private startup pitch decks or venture fundraising generally. It is public-company disclosure guidance concerning critical accounting policies and MD&A.

Do not use it to claim that numerical precision is inherently deceptive. Accounting and management require estimates; operating plans require targets; investors need forecasts. A less precise number can be just as misleading if its assumptions are hidden.

Do not turn the SEC language into proof that startup investors are routinely fooled by precise forecasts. The chapter’s anchoring studies remain bounded laboratory and negotiation evidence, not a field estimate of venture-investor susceptibility.

## Strongest counterargument

The strongest objection to Chapter 5 is practical rather than moral: organizations cannot coordinate on a cloud of probability distributions. Hiring, inventory, runway, capacity, sales quotas, and launch preparation eventually require point estimates and dates. Excessive qualification can make a plan unusable and can become its own form of managerial evasion.

The answer should therefore stay narrower than “show all uncertainty.” A useful number should retain enough provenance that a decision-maker can recover what kind of number it is, which assumptions dominate it, and what changes materially if those assumptions move. The company can still choose a target and act on it.

That makes the chapter’s better distinction **precision versus provenance**, not precision versus honesty.

## Editorial implication for the next prose pass

The current chapter repeats its lesson several times through symmetrical sequences: target → forecast → guidance → promise; repeated “if X, say so” instructions; and a closing “the goal is not / the goal is” construction. A later surgical edit can let the SEC example do some of that explanatory work instead.

The most useful insertion point is near “Significant digits, insignificant knowledge.” One compact paragraph can establish that even regulated financial statements need estimates and that the SEC’s answer is disclosure of assumptions and sensitivity, not abolition of estimates. Then the chapter can cut some of the later procedural restatement.

Preserve the existing line “The decimal is not lying. It is merely obedient.” It earns its place because the evidence now makes the distinction concrete: the problem is not arithmetic but what the arithmetic has been asked to stand for.
