# Chapter 3 — Model 3 reservations as a reflexive promise

## Why this note exists

Chapter 3 argues that some startup promises are not passive forecasts: they can induce commitments that alter the probability of the forecast becoming true. The Model 3 launch is useful because Tesla's contemporaneous filings document both the feedback mechanism and its limits.

## Primary-source record

Tesla unveiled Model 3 on March 31, 2016. In its May 4, 2016 shareholder update, the company said it received more than 325,000 reservations in the first week, despite no advertising or paid endorsements, and characterized those reservations as implying roughly $14 billion in future sales. In the same update Tesla said that, given Model 3 demand, it had decided to move its 500,000-vehicle annual build plan forward from 2020 to 2018. It also warned that increasing production fivefold in two years would be challenging and likely require additional capital.

- Tesla, Q1 2016 shareholder update, filed with the SEC May 4, 2016: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1318605/000119312516577582/d158845dex991.htm

Tesla's May 2016 registered stock offering then reported about 373,000 net Model 3 reservations as of May 15, after roughly 8,000 customer cancellations and about 4,200 reservations Tesla cancelled as suspected speculative duplicates. The filing said Tesla intended to use the offering proceeds to accelerate the Model 3 production ramp and the earlier 500,000-vehicle target. The offering ultimately produced about $1.7 billion in net proceeds after the underwriters exercised their option in full.

The filing classified customer deposits as current liabilities and said they were fully refundable until a vehicle entered production.

## What the episode supports

The episode shows a real feedback chain without requiring us to infer private motives. A future product announcement induced reservations. Tesla publicly treated those reservations as demand evidence, revised its production plan, and raised capital earmarked for accelerating that plan. Customer deposits also increased materially, although the reported deposit balance included deposits for products other than Model 3.

This supports the chapter's narrower claim that a promise can change the resources, commitments, and plans available to the company making it. It does **not** establish that the reservations caused the equity offering to succeed, that investors accepted Tesla's interpretation of demand, or that every reservation was incremental demand created by the promise itself.

## Strongest counterargument

Reservations are unusually weak evidence compared with completed purchases. Tesla's own filings say Model 3 reservations were refundable and warn that there was no assurance any reservation would become a sale or that the reservation count would accurately represent future demand. The company also faced execution risks independent of demand: manufacturing scale, timing, price, features, performance, supplier readiness, capital needs, and margin.

That limitation is central rather than incidental. A reflexive signal can be causally useful while remaining epistemically noisy. Hundreds of thousands of reservations could help Tesla coordinate capital and capacity without proving that the entire commercial thesis was already true.

The same point applies to the deposits. The cash was economically useful, but accounting correctly treated it as a liability because Tesla still owed the customer either a vehicle or a refund. Calling the deposits "revenue" would collapse the exact tense distinction the book is trying to preserve.

## Editorial implication

The manuscript should use the Model 3 sequence to replace a generic hypothetical about infrastructure demand. It gives the reader an observable chain:

1. a future product is shown;
2. customers make revocable commitments;
3. the company interprets those commitments as demand evidence;
4. the company advances its production plan;
5. it raises capital to accelerate the plan;
6. its own filings simultaneously warn that the commitments are not equivalent to completed sales or guaranteed demand.

The strongest formulation is not "Tesla proved demand before the car existed." It is that Tesla obtained a powerful, imperfect signal that changed present decisions before the future product existed at volume. The promise helped alter the system around the promise while leaving the hardest execution claims unresolved.
