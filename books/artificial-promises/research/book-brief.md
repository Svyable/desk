# Artificial Promises — Book Brief

## Working subtitle

How Founders Sell Tomorrow Before It Exists

## Premise

Startups are built around claims about a future that does not yet exist. The product will work. The market will expand. Costs will fall. Customers will come. A small team will become an institution.

Those claims are not merely forecasts. They are coordination devices. They attract capital, talent, customers, partners, suppliers, press, and political attention. By causing people to act in the present, a founder's description of the future can help make that future more likely.

That creates a structural problem. The founder has an incentive to state uncertainty with more confidence than the evidence warrants because confidence itself can improve the odds of success. Over time, fundraising, recruiting, sales, valuation, and internal morale can all become dependent on one version of the future remaining believable.

The book asks how organizations preserve contact with present reality while deliberately selling a future that cannot yet be verified.

## Central thesis

A startup promise is both a prediction and an instrument.

Because the promise can help produce the outcome it predicts, founder optimism cannot be judged as if it were an ordinary forecast. But that same causal power makes overstatement dangerous. Once an organization becomes dependent on a promised future, evidence against the promise becomes costly to transmit.

The core failure mode is therefore not simply founder dishonesty. It is **reality distortion as an organizational dependency**.

The company begins by using confidence to coordinate action. It can end with the organization protecting the confidence from reality.

## Core ideas

- Every startup converts uncertain future states into present commitments.
- Vision is private; companies operate on translated vision: decks, road maps, launch dates, demos, valuations, sales claims, recruiting pitches, and market-size estimates.
- A promise can be self-fulfilling because belief attracts resources that improve the odds of delivery.
- This makes aggressive forecasting rational up to a point.
- The point becomes dangerous when commitments created by the promise make revising the promise financially or socially expensive.
- Demos are unusually important because they can collapse a complex capability claim into a vivid piece of apparent evidence.
- Technical truth and commercial language operate at different levels of precision; misunderstanding can grow in the translation layer between them.
- Boards and investors face an information problem: the founder is both the strongest advocate for the company and often the person with the most control over which evidence becomes salient.
- Employees face asymmetric incentives when bad news threatens financing, morale, launch timing, or the founder's identity.
- Success does not retroactively make reckless claims honest, and failure does not retroactively make good-faith ambition fraudulent.
- The strongest organizations should preserve internal uncertainty even when public communication must remain simple.
- A good founder needs the capacity to sell a future and to kill an obsolete version of that future before the organization does more damage defending it.

## The distinction the book must preserve

The book should never collapse all founder optimism into deception.

A founder can responsibly state an ambitious future when:

- the future claim is clearly separated from current capability;
- material technical uncertainties are known internally and not suppressed;
- demonstrations are described honestly;
- dates are communicated with their actual confidence level when the precision matters;
- customers understand whether a feature exists, is in testing, or is aspirational;
- boards receive enough disconfirming evidence to challenge the dominant narrative;
- the organization can revise the plan without treating revision as betrayal.

The danger rises when:

- present-tense capability claims outrun what the system can reliably do;
- internal evidence contradicting the story is prevented from reaching decision-makers;
- the company depends on new commitments created by claims it cannot independently verify;
- demonstrations are designed to imply general capability from tightly controlled conditions without disclosure;
- estimates become progressively more certain as they travel outward rather than less;
- people who surface contrary evidence are punished as disloyal rather than engaged on the merits;
- the promise becomes impossible to falsify because every failure is reclassified as temporary proof that the ambition is sufficiently large.

## Falsification and counter-evidence

The thesis must survive strong counterexamples.

- Many extraordinary companies required founders to make claims that sounded implausible at the time and later became true.
- Conservative communication can itself destroy value by making it impossible to recruit talent or finance capital-intensive projects.
- Startups face genuine uncertainty; missing a forecast is not evidence that it was dishonest when made.
- Technical teams can also be systematically pessimistic, locally informed but strategically narrow, or resistant to deadlines that force useful integration.
- Public companies, governments, and established corporations make optimistic forecasts too; this is not a pathology unique to founder-led startups.
- Investors are sophisticated counterparties in many financings and may knowingly purchase high-variance narratives rather than naively believing them.
- A founder may have superior integrative knowledge and correctly reject a consensus of specialists who each see only part of the system.

The manuscript should treat these as real constraints on the argument, not objections to dispatch quickly.

## Research lanes

### Founder communication

Compare pitch decks, launch events, investor letters, interviews, regulatory filings, product pages, and internal accounts where available. Record the date and distinguish current-tense claims from forecasts.

### Demos and prototypes

Study how prototypes are used to represent future systems. The central question is what a reasonable audience is invited to infer from the demonstration.

### Venture incentives

Examine portfolio economics, power-law returns, fundraising cycles, valuation signaling, follow-on rounds, and how capital availability changes the reward for ambitious narratives.

### Organizational information flow

Use research on escalation of commitment, groupthink, motivated reasoning, psychological safety, upward communication, forecasting, and project scheduling. Avoid reducing the company to the founder's psychology.

### Boundary cases

Use enforcement records and court documents where possible rather than retrospective storytelling. Theranos and Nikola are obvious legal boundary cases because official records directly address representations about technology and commercial reality.

### Successful audacity

Include cases where an improbable founder promise was substantially fulfilled. These cases are necessary because the book fails if it only studies obvious disasters.

## Chapter discipline

For every major promise discussed, record:

1. What exactly was said?
2. When was it said?
3. Was it about current capability or a future target?
4. What did the speaker plausibly know at the time?
5. What did employees, customers, investors, or regulators reasonably infer?
6. What evidence supported the claim then?
7. What evidence contradicted it then?
8. Did the claim itself help create the conditions for success?
9. What would make the promise reasonable rather than reckless?
10. Are we judging with hindsight that the speaker did not possess?

That ten-question record should prevent the manuscript from becoming a collection of retrospective morality tales.
