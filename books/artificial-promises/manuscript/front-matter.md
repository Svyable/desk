# Artificial Promises

*How Founders Sell Tomorrow Before It Exists*

Every startup begins with a description of something that is not yet true.

The company will build the product. Customers will come. Costs will fall. A market that looks small will become large. A technology that barely works in a laboratory will become reliable enough for ordinary use. A handful of people in a rented office will become the organization capable of making all of this happen.

Without some belief in that future, there is no startup.

The awkward part is that the belief has to spread before the evidence does.

Employees leave secure jobs because they think the company might matter. Investors wire money because they think the company can become more valuable than it is today. Customers tolerate unfinished software because they think it will improve. Partners commit resources because they expect the market to arrive. Journalists write about the possibility. Governments sometimes change rules for it. Suppliers expand capacity for it. A founder's account of the future can become one of the inputs that helps produce that future.

This makes the startup promise different from an ordinary prediction.

A weather forecast does not usually change the weather. A founder forecast can change the company.

Say that a new market will be enormous and you may attract the engineers needed to create it. Say that costs will fall and investors may finance the factories that push them down. Say that the product will reach a capability threshold in two years and a large customer may sign an agreement that gives the company enough revenue to survive until it does.

The promise is partly descriptive and partly causal.

That is why the moral line around founder optimism is harder to draw than it first appears.

Some founders are wrong because the future is difficult to predict. Some are wrong because ambitious technical projects slip. Some simplify because a complicated product is hard to explain. Some choose the best available interpretation of noisy evidence. Some begin believing a claim that later stops being true. Some know exactly where the prototype ends and the story begins and make sure nobody else can see the seam.

The distance between vision and deception is not measured only by whether the future eventually arrives.

A founder can make a reckless promise and get lucky. Another can describe a plausible future in good faith and fail. A third can create something genuinely transformative while repeatedly promising dates, capabilities, or economics that reality refuses to honor.

Results matter, but they do not settle the question.

This book is about the organizational machinery that turns a founder's belief into everybody else's reality.

The standard startup story treats vision as a personal quality. The founder sees something other people cannot yet see. Perhaps that is true. But a company does not run on private vision. It runs on translated vision: decks, demos, road maps, launch events, recruiting pitches, valuation models, product claims, internal targets, interviews, investor updates, keynote slides, market-size estimates, and dates on calendars.

The future has to be packaged.

Once packaged, it begins doing work.

A number in a pitch deck can determine how much capital the company raises. That capital determines how many people it hires. The hiring plan changes the road map. The road map becomes a sales promise. The sales promise becomes a customer dependency. The customer dependency becomes internal pressure to hit the date. The date becomes evidence in the next fundraising round that the company is moving quickly.

The promise travels through the organization and returns to the founder with more weight than it had when it left.

This creates a peculiar danger.

The founder may be the person with the strongest incentives to believe the company's most optimistic interpretation and the least organizational freedom to stop believing it.

A normal employee can say that a project is six months late. A founder saying the same thing may trigger a financing problem, a recruiting problem, a valuation problem, a customer problem, or a press problem. The more dependent the company becomes on the promised future, the more expensive an honest revision of that future can become.

That is how optimism can harden into doctrine without anyone scheduling a meeting to make it happen.

There are dramatic cases where the line was not merely blurred but crossed. The Securities and Exchange Commission charged Theranos and Elizabeth Holmes in 2018 with raising more than $700 million while making false or misleading claims about the company's technology, business, and financial performance. In 2021 the SEC charged Nikola founder Trevor Milton with fraud, alleging repeated false and misleading statements about the company's products, technical achievements, production capabilities, and commercial progress. These are useful boundary cases because the legal allegations were not that the founders had simply dreamed too large. They concerned representations about what existed already.

Most companies never become enforcement cases.

The more interesting territory is everything before that line.

A startup founder says the product is "almost ready." What does almost mean when three unresolved technical problems each have unknown schedules?

A company says it is building an autonomous system. Does a supervised demonstration communicate the current state of the technology or the intended destination?

A pitch describes a trillion-dollar market. Is that a forecast, a thought experiment, or a ritual number required to obtain venture financing?

A product is advertised using language that is literally defensible but systematically encourages customers to imagine a capability beyond what the product reliably delivers.

A founder announces a date knowing that the engineering organization regards it as extremely unlikely, because a softer public date would remove the urgency that might make the aggressive date possible.

These are not all the same act.

They belong to the same organizational problem.

The company needs a future strong enough to coordinate people who cannot yet verify it.

That need creates what I will call an artificial promise: a future stated with more solidity than the underlying evidence can support because the statement itself is expected to help close the gap.

Artificial promises are not necessarily lies.

They are manufactured certainty.

They appear wherever an uncertain future has to be converted into a present decision.

Invest now.

Join now.

Buy now.

Build the factory now.

Trust the road map now.

Approve the partnership now.

The evidence will arrive later.

Sometimes it does.

Silicon Valley is full of ideas that sounded ridiculous until the companies behind them made the ridicule obsolete. A culture that demanded conventional proof before financing unconventional projects would reject many real breakthroughs. The ability to persuade people before certainty exists is not an unfortunate side effect of entrepreneurship. It is one of entrepreneurship's core capabilities.

The problem is that the same skill that coordinates a real breakthrough can coordinate a fiction.

From the outside, the early stages can look remarkably similar.

Both have charismatic advocates. Both have technical explanations outsiders struggle to evaluate. Both recruit impressive people. Both can attract sophisticated investors. Both can produce demonstrations. Both can explain missing evidence as a temporary consequence of moving quickly. Both can argue that skeptics are using yesterday's assumptions to judge tomorrow's technology.

This is why "visionary or fraud" is too crude a frame for the book.

The question is not how to eliminate promises from entrepreneurship. That would eliminate entrepreneurship.

The question is how organizations can remain capable of believing in a future without losing contact with the present.

What information should travel upward even when it damages the story?

How should a board distinguish ambition from reality distortion when ambition is part of the founder's job?

When should a demo be treated as evidence of capability, and when is it theater showing what the company hopes eventually to automate?

What should happen when a sales promise is ahead of engineering reality?

Can an organization preserve a private probability distribution even while its public language requires confidence?

How do you revise a promise without destroying the coordination the promise created?

These are questions of organizational design before they are questions of personality.

That is the central argument of *Artificial Promises*.

Founders sell the future because the future is often the only asset a young company has. The danger begins when the organization becomes financially, socially, and psychologically dependent on one version of that future being true.

At that point, reality is no longer merely something the company is trying to change.

It is something the company may become unable to hear.
