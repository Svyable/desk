# The Number in the Deck

The most persuasive sentence in a startup pitch is often not a sentence. It is a number.

$63.4 billion of market. Revenue growing 200 percent. Eighty percent gross margin at scale. Break-even in eighteen months. The slide appears to have done something rhetoric could not: it has converted belief into arithmetic.

Sometimes it has. A number can contain real work—customers counted, prices observed, contracts signed, costs measured. But the same font can hold a target, a scenario, an estimate, or a hope assembled from assumptions. The visual grammar barely changes. The epistemic status can change completely.

That is why the interesting question is not whether a startup uses numbers before the future is knowable. It has to. Dates coordinate launches. Revenue plans determine hiring. Market estimates help decide whether an opportunity deserves years of work. Cost targets tell engineers what must improve. Management is partly the business of turning uncertainty into commitments before uncertainty has gone away.

The danger begins when a number changes jobs without anyone noticing.

Take the familiar total-addressable-market slide. The question underneath it is legitimate: if this company works, how large could the economic opportunity become? Suppose five million organizations might eventually buy a product at $10,000 a year. The multiplication gives you a $50 billion market. The arithmetic is flawless. Almost everything important happened before the multiplication.

Why five million organizations? Why $10,000? Why annual spend rather than one-time spend? How many of those organizations can actually be reached? What do they buy now? What would make them switch? Which regulations, procurement rules, implementation costs, or competitors shrink the reachable set? Does a cheaper product expand demand, or merely lower the dollars collected from each customer?

Once those verbs are visible, the number gets better. You can argue with it. “The market is $50 billion” suppresses the machinery. “If five million organizations eventually buy at an average of $10,000 a year, the category would represent $50 billion in annual spend” exposes enough of the machinery to inspect.

Bottom-up models often help because they force the founder to confront channels, sales cycles, contract values, implementation capacity, and customer counts. They can also move the fantasy into more cells. Twenty weak assumptions do not become strong because Excel arranged them vertically.

The same problem appears in growth charts. Going from ten customers to thirty really is 200 percent growth. Going from $10,000 of annualized revenue to $100,000 really is 10x. Neither statement tells you whether the mechanism survives scale.

At ten customers, founders may recruit everyone personally. Early adopters may tolerate defects that mainstream buyers will not. One unusually large contract can bend the curve. A launch can create attention that does not repeat. A free product can generate usage without showing willingness to pay. The line rises anyway.

A chart records an outcome. It does not identify the engine pulling it.

That is why experienced investors keep digging under the headline: retention, cohorts, customer concentration, acquisition channels, payback, margins, sales efficiency, support burden. They are trying to determine whether the visible growth came from a repeatable system or from the heroic conditions of being small.

Future unit economics are harder because the optimistic mechanisms are often real. Hardware can get cheaper. Utilization can improve. Support can be automated. Purchasing terms can improve with volume. Fixed costs can spread across more customers. A young company may deserve investment precisely because its current economics are a poor description of what a mature system could become.

The uncertainty is in magnitude, sequence, and timing.

A model can assign a modest improvement to ten different variables and produce a remarkably beautiful company. A little lower churn. A little higher price. A little cheaper infrastructure. A little better utilization. A little lower acquisition cost. Each assumption may look defensible alone. Together they compound into a future no single assumption seems responsible for creating.

The spreadsheet becomes a place where optimism can diversify.

One of the more useful responses to this problem comes from a setting far removed from an early startup deck. In 2024 the Securities and Exchange Commission changed the disclosure rules governing projections in de-SPAC transactions and updated its broader guidance for projections used in Commission filings. The SEC did not respond by banning forecasts. It required more information about what the projections were for, who prepared them, the material bases and assumptions underneath them, and whether management or the board still stood behind them near the time investors would act.

The legal context is obviously different. A private founder speaking to a venture investor is not filing a de-SPAC registration statement. The useful part is the design instinct. When a consequential projection cannot be eliminated, preserve enough of its lineage that another person can tell what kind of claim it is.

That is a harder standard than simply putting “forward-looking” in small type.

A projection prepared to negotiate a transaction may be built differently from one prepared to run the company. A board-approved operating plan may deliberately set a demanding target above the median forecast. A sales quota can be useful precisely because it is aspirational. A launch date can coordinate hundreds of dependencies even when the probability of hitting the exact day is less than one hundred percent.

The same number can be legitimate in each role. Trouble starts when the role disappears.

A target gets copied into a forecast. The forecast gets copied into a financing model. The financing model informs a valuation. The valuation supports hiring. Hiring raises the revenue the company must now achieve. A customer hears the launch date and staffs around it. Six months later, the number comes back into the company wearing the authority of all the decisions it caused.

Nothing new has necessarily been learned. The number has acquired counterparties.

That changes the politics of revision. A spreadsheet assumption is cheap to change while it is still private. Once people have hired, invested, contracted, or reorganized around it, changing the assumption can impose real costs. The social weight of the number rises even if its evidentiary quality does not.

This is how an estimate can harden without becoming more accurate.

Precision adds another layer. In experiments published in 2008, Chris Janiszewski and Dan Uy found that people adjusted less from precise numerical anchors than from rounded ones. A number such as 4,985 can pull judgment differently from 5,000 even when the precision does not represent superior knowledge. Later work by David Loschelder and colleagues complicates the temptation to turn that into a persuasion trick. In their negotiation experiments, increasing precision strengthened anchoring among amateurs, while excessive precision could backfire with experts who inferred that the person offering the number understood the domain less well.

That is almost too perfect for startup life. $84.7 million can look engineered to someone who does not know where the model is fragile. To someone who does, the decimal may look like costume jewelry.

The useful question is what earned the digit.

If a market estimate is honestly a range, the range may contain more information than the point. If September 15 is an internal coordination target rather than a high-confidence delivery forecast, the label matters. If a model depends on three unvalidated assumptions, sensitivity analysis matters more than another decimal place. If a run rate annualizes one exceptional month, the exceptional month belongs in the explanation.

None of this means that management should replace every decision with a probability distribution. People cannot hire 0.63 of a sales team, reserve a conference venue for a cloud of possible dates, or negotiate every supplier agreement as a Monte Carlo simulation. Decisive organizations repeatedly have to choose a point and act as though it matters.

That is the strongest counterargument to the chapter, and it is right.

The mistake is not collapsing uncertainty into a decision. The mistake is allowing the decision to erase the uncertainty that produced it.

A healthy company should be able to reverse the compression. Ask where the $50 billion TAM came from and someone can recover the customer counts, price assumptions, exclusions, and adoption path. Ask why gross margin improves in year three and the model can identify which costs actually fall and which merely move elsewhere. Ask why launch is scheduled for September and engineering can distinguish the date management chose from the probability engineering assigns to hitting it.

This kind of recoverability matters more than ritual humility. A deck covered in ranges, footnotes, and confidence intervals can still be misleading if nobody can defend the inputs. A crisp number can be responsible if the company knows what it means, what would change it, and which decisions depend on it.

That requires keeping categories alive that tend to blur under pressure.

An observation says what happened. A target says what the organization is trying to cause. A forecast says what it currently expects. A scenario says what follows if specified assumptions hold. An aspiration names an outcome valuable enough to organize effort around even when the path remains uncertain.

These are not ranks of seriousness. A target is not a defective forecast. A scenario is not an embarrassed prediction. A useful operating system needs all of them.

The categories matter because each deserves a different response to failure. If an observation is wrong, fix the measurement. If a target is missed, ask what performance or planning failed. If a forecast is missed, inspect the model and the world it was trying to describe. If a scenario no longer fits, discard or rebuild the assumptions. If an aspiration turns out to be impossible or unworthy, stop paying for it.

Confusing the categories makes organizations learn the wrong lesson.

A missed stretch target can be treated as proof that forecasting is poor. A forecast can quietly become a quota, encouraging people to manipulate the inputs required to hit it. A scenario built for fundraising can be treated internally as the base case because everyone has now seen the same chart. The number becomes a coordination device and then, by repetition, a memory of something that never happened.

Repetition impersonates validation.

Chapter 4 described the pitch as reversible compression: a good founder can move from the clean headline back down into the machinery underneath it. Numbers demand the same ability, but they create a special hazard because arithmetic looks like provenance. Once a cell contains a formula, the output feels derived. Often it is. The unresolved question is whether the inputs were observed, negotiated, borrowed, estimated, selected, or wished into existence.

A model can be perfectly calculated and epistemically unserious.

It can also be uncertain and extremely useful.

The difference is whether the organization knows where observation ends and choice begins.

That boundary is worth protecting because artificial promises rarely arrive labeled as lies. More often, a useful internal fiction survives long enough to forget that it was fiction. A demanding goal becomes “what we expect.” A hopeful date becomes “what we told the board.” A financing scenario becomes “the plan.” People downstream receive the number without the conversation that created it, so the assumptions disappear while the precision survives.

This is projection drift.

The cure is not timidity. It is lineage.

For consequential numbers, someone should be able to answer five ordinary questions: Who made this? For what decision? What observations support it? Which assumptions do the work? What would make us stop relying on it?

Those questions are deliberately less glamorous than a new forecasting framework. They are also difficult to fake for long. A company that can answer them can revise a number without pretending the old number was foolish. It can preserve ambition while allowing evidence to move.

That matters because a startup is an argument about a future that does not exist yet. The argument needs numbers. Without them, ambition becomes too slippery to finance, staff, test, or falsify. But the numbers should remain attached to the route by which they entered the company.

Arithmetic can discipline a promise only while the company remembers where the arithmetic came from.
