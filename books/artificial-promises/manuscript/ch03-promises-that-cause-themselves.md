# Promises That Cause Themselves

Some forecasts sit outside the thing they predict. A meteorologist can be wrong about rain without changing the atmosphere very much. An astronomer predicting an eclipse does not pull the moon into position by publishing the forecast. A doctor’s prognosis may affect a patient’s choices, but it does not usually create the disease being described.

Founder promises belong to a different family because they can enter the causal chain. A company says a market will exist. Investors finance capacity for it. Suppliers expand. Engineers join. Customers prepare. Standards emerge. The activity generated by the forecast makes the forecast more likely to become true.

This feedback is one reason entrepreneurship resists the clean moral language of ordinary prediction. If a founder were simply describing an independent future, the standard would be straightforward: state the probability as accurately as possible. But founders are trying to alter the probability distribution with money, recruiting, engineering, persuasion, partnerships, and speed. The promise is part of the intervention.

Most difficult projects require coordinated action before anyone can prove the outcome. A railroad has to be financed before traffic can run on it. A network becomes more valuable after people join. A factory becomes economic after enough suppliers and customers organize around it. A software platform becomes credible after developers decide it is credible enough to build for. A future that requires coordination can fail because too few people believe in it early enough, and the founder’s work is partly to solve that problem.

Tesla made this mechanism unusually visible in the spring of 2016. On March 31 it unveiled the Model 3, a car it had not yet put into volume production. In the first week, more than 325,000 people placed reservations. Tesla described those reservations as evidence of mass-market demand and said they implied roughly $14 billion in future sales. By May 15, its securities filing reported about 373,000 net reservations after cancellations and suspected duplicates.

The reservations did not merely predict demand. They changed what Tesla did next. In its May shareholder update, the company said the response was one reason it was advancing a plan to reach 500,000 vehicles of annual production to 2018, two years earlier than previously planned. Tesla also said the fivefold production increase would be difficult and likely require additional capital. Days later, in a registered stock offering, it told investors that the proceeds would be used to accelerate the Model 3 production ramp. The offering ultimately brought Tesla about $1.7 billion in net proceeds.

That sequence is close to a laboratory specimen of a self-affecting forecast. A prototype and a promise about a cheaper electric car induced hundreds of thousands of people to reserve one. The reservations gave Tesla cash, supplied a visible measure of interest, changed the company’s production plan, and sat in the public record as Tesla raised capital for that plan. Belief about a future product became one of the inputs used to build the system that might deliver it.

But the same filings contain the reason not to romanticize the loop. The reservations were refundable. Tesla warned investors that there was no assurance a reservation would become a sale and that the reservation count might not accurately predict future demand. Customer deposits appeared as liabilities, not earned revenue. By September 2016 Tesla held $690.4 million in customer deposits, up from $283.4 million at the end of 2015, with the increase primarily attributable to Model 3 deposits; that balance still included money the company might have to return.

The reservation book therefore meant something important without meaning everything Tesla wanted the Model 3 eventually to prove. It showed that a remarkable number of people were willing to put down money for the possibility of buying the car. It did not prove that Tesla could manufacture the car at the promised price, ramp on schedule, preserve margins, or convert every reservation into a purchase. The company could use the signal to coordinate more resources while still being wrong about the hardest parts of execution.

That distinction is the respectable mechanism buried inside “fake it till you make it,” a slogan loose enough to obscure the important boundary. There is nothing fake about making commitments that create conditions for execution. The danger is representing a conditional mechanism as a present accomplishment. “We will reach these economics if we finance enough scale and the learning curve continues” is a thesis. “We have these economics” is a fact claim. The first can rationally justify investment before the outcome exists. The second has to be true now.

Self-fulfilling promises make that boundary harder to defend because stronger language can genuinely improve the odds of success. Imagine a capital-intensive startup whose founder believes a major technical milestone has a sixty percent chance of arriving if the company raises enough money to hire a team, secure manufacturing capacity, and survive eighteen months of development. Without the round, the probability drops close to zero. If saying “we have a sixty percent chance” scares investors away while saying “we will reach the milestone” closes the round, more confidence can make the confident statement more likely to become true.

That does not make the stronger statement honest. It explains how serious people begin to treat honesty as an obstacle to truth. The founder may reason that the technology will work if everyone stops hedging and commits; expressing doubt causes the failure the doubt predicts; therefore doubt is sabotage rather than realism. Uncertainty becomes associated with disloyalty precisely because confidence can be productive.

Reflexive systems exist outside startups. Bank runs depend partly on expectations about other depositors. Currencies, political campaigns, consumer brands, and asset markets can all be altered by belief about them. Entrepreneurship has reflexivity in its operating model: valuation affects recruiting, recruiting affects product velocity, product velocity affects customer interest, and customer interest affects the next valuation.

The loop can run backward just as quickly. Miss a milestone and financing becomes harder. Harder financing constrains hiring. Slower hiring delays the next milestone. Delay weakens customer confidence, which makes financing harder again. Founders who fight to maintain narrative momentum are not imagining the causal power of belief. The mistake is concluding that because belief matters, evidence should be subordinated to it.

A company that depends on confidence needs more accurate internal information, not less. Externally, it may need a coherent commitment: this is the market we are building for, this is the product we intend to deliver, this is the strategy we are pursuing. Internally, it needs a live map of uncertainty: which assumptions are weakening, what the schedule actually looks like, where customer behavior differs from the narrative, which technical barriers remain unresolved, and what would justify changing the plan.

Trouble begins when one representation has to serve both functions. A pitch is a terrible internal forecasting system, just as a probabilistic engineering assessment can be a terrible recruiting speech. The organization needs both without pretending they are identical.

Language travels, though. A sentence created for investors appears in an all-hands meeting. A recruiting slogan becomes a customer promise. A board target becomes a public date. The public date then influences engineering planning because nobody wants the company to contradict itself. The causal arrow reverses: the internal forecast no longer determines the external promise; the external promise begins determining the internal forecast.

Early on, the founder says, “Based on what we know, I think we can do this.” Later, after the company has organized around the claim, people start saying, “We have to be able to do this because we said we would.” The first sentence updates from reality. The second asks reality to update from the sentence.

Sometimes reality cooperates. Deadlines can focus attention. Public commitments can force teams to integrate. Customers can create urgency that pure research lacks. A founder can be right that the organization has more capacity than conservative functional estimates suggest. An engineering team estimating components independently may produce a schedule that assumes sequential work, generous buffers, and no strategic reprioritization; a much earlier commitment can trigger parallelization, scope reduction, additional hiring, executive escalation, or a different technical approach. The estimate and the committed date may describe different organizations.

But the causal power of a promise does not tell us whether the promise is wise. The same date that forces one team to discover a better approach can cause another to hide testing problems. A customer promise can inspire useful urgency or produce a rushed release. A high valuation can attract talent or encourage spending disconnected from product-market fit. The founder is choosing both a statement and some of the behavior that statement is intended to induce.

That suggests a practical test: identify the mechanism connecting the promise to the outcome. If a large order is supposed to reduce unit cost, which costs actually fall with volume? If a public launch date is meant to accelerate execution, which decisions become faster because the date exists? If a partnership is supposed to unlock an ecosystem, who is expected to commit in response? If a valuation is expected to help recruiting, is equity compensation actually competitive at that valuation, and what happens if the next round is lower?

A self-fulfilling promise needs a causal theory strong enough to monitor. If scale is supposed to reduce manufacturing cost, track yield, purchasing discounts, labor efficiency, utilization, defect rates, and throughput. If developers are supposed to make a platform more valuable, track active integrations, retention, third-party usage, and whether the ecosystem creates demand that would not otherwise exist. If a bold mission is supposed to attract exceptional talent, compare acceptance rates, quality of hires, compensation tradeoffs, and retention. The promise may help cause itself; it does not get to exempt itself from measurement.

Responsible audacity preserves the evidence that the commitment is trying to outrun. A founder may deliberately set a target above the median forecast because the target changes effort, but the median forecast still has to exist somewhere. A company can say, “Our commitment is June; our current risk-adjusted estimate is August.” The discomfort between those dates is information. It shows how much schedule is being purchased with urgency rather than supported by current evidence and gives management something to use for contingency planning. Forcing the estimate to match the commitment destroys that information.

The same principle applies to fundraising. A founder may need to explain why the company could become enormous without pretending that the enormous outcome is the base case. A disciplined investor can price a distribution. A disciplined company should preserve one internally. It has to know which future it is selling and which future it expects.

Success can otherwise hide the recklessness of the original claim. A founder can make a promise with a poor factual basis, attract resources because of it, and then use those resources to deliver the outcome. Delivery does not answer whether the people who committed resources were entitled to know how uncertain it was at the time. A bridge engineer who says a design has already passed a safety test when the test has not been run does not make the earlier statement true by later improving the design until it passes. Startups are not bridge engineering, but the information principle survives: causality does not retroactively change tense.

That distinction matters because founders learn from experience that belief is productive. Confidence recruits, commitments coordinate, deadlines accelerate, valuations signal, partnerships legitimize, customer logos attract customers, and capital finances progress. The dangerous inference is that more belief is therefore always more productive.

At some point a company needs the ability to disappoint itself. It has to discover that a market is smaller, a date is wrong, a feature is less valuable, a technical path is blocked, or a strategic assumption has expired. Those discoveries can weaken the narrative loops that helped build the organization, which is exactly why they are easy to suppress. They may also save it.

A promise can help cause itself only while reality remains responsive to the actions the promise induces. Capital cannot solve a physical impossibility. Urgency cannot eliminate a safety requirement. Hiring cannot create product-market fit by itself. Public confidence cannot indefinitely substitute for customer value.

The founder’s distinctive skill is not believing hard enough to erase those limits. It is knowing which limits can actually move when people coordinate around a future, then keeping enough doubt alive to notice when the future has stopped listening.
