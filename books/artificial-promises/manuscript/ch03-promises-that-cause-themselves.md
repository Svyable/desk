# Promises That Cause Themselves

Some forecasts sit outside the thing they predict.

A meteorologist can be wrong about rain without changing the atmosphere very much. An astronomer predicting an eclipse does not pull the moon into position by publishing the forecast. A doctor’s prognosis may affect a patient’s choices, but it does not usually create the disease being described.

Founder promises belong to a different family.

They can enter the causal chain.

A company says a market will exist. Investors finance capacity for it. Suppliers expand. Engineers join. Customers prepare. Standards emerge. The activity generated by the forecast makes the forecast more likely to become true.

This feedback is one of the reasons entrepreneurship resists the clean moral language of ordinary prediction.

If a founder were simply describing an independent future, the standard would be straightforward: state the probability as accurately as possible. But founders are not passive observers. They are trying to alter the probability distribution with money, recruiting, engineering, persuasion, partnerships, and speed.

The promise is part of the intervention.

This can be good.

Most difficult projects require coordinated action before anyone can prove the outcome. A railroad has to be financed before traffic can run on it. A network becomes more valuable after people join. A factory becomes economic after enough suppliers and customers organize around it. A software platform becomes credible after developers decide it will be credible enough to build for.

A future that requires coordination can fail because too few people believe in it early enough.

The founder’s work is partly to solve that coordination problem.

This is easiest to see in infrastructure.

Suppose a company believes there will be enormous demand for a new kind of compute. The demand is not yet observable because the software that would consume the compute has not been built. Software companies hesitate because capacity is scarce and expensive. Infrastructure providers hesitate because the applications do not yet exist.

Someone has to move first.

If the infrastructure provider announces a large buildout and customers believe it, software developers may begin designing around the expected capacity. Their work creates future demand. The announced capacity helped create the workload that will eventually justify the buildout.

The original forecast is now hard to classify.

Was it a prediction?

A commitment?

A sales tactic?

A strategy?

It was all four.

Startup promises often work this way at smaller scale.

A founder tells candidates that the company will become the best place in the world to work on a difficult problem. Exceptional candidates join partly because they want that future. Once enough of them join, the statement becomes less aspirational.

The promise recruited the evidence that later made the promise look prescient.

A founder tells customers that the product will become the standard integration layer in an industry. Early customers integrate because they want to influence the standard. Their integrations make later customers more comfortable. The product becomes more standard because people believed it might become standard.

A founder tells investors that scale will drive costs down. Investors fund the scale. Higher volume improves purchasing terms, utilization, manufacturing learning, or data collection. Costs fall.

The founder did not merely predict a cost curve.

Capital purchased movement along it.

This is the respectable version of “fake it till you make it,” and the slogan does it a disservice.

There is nothing fake about making commitments that create conditions for execution.

The danger lies in confusing a conditional mechanism with a present accomplishment.

“We will reach these economics if we finance enough scale and the learning curve continues” is a thesis.

“We have these economics” is a fact claim.

The first can rationally justify investment before the outcome exists. The second has to be true now.

That boundary becomes more important when the promise is self-fulfilling because the company has a motive to make the future sound closer to the present than it is.

The stronger the belief, the stronger the coordination.

This creates a temptation to overstate not because the founder wants to steal, but because stronger language can genuinely improve the odds of success.

Imagine a financing round at a capital-intensive startup.

The founder believes the technology has a sixty percent chance of reaching a major milestone if the company raises enough to hire a team, secure manufacturing capacity, and survive eighteen months of development. Without the round, the probability drops close to zero because the company runs out of money.

The honest uncertainty and the funding probability are not independent.

If saying “we have a sixty percent chance” causes investors to stay away while saying “we will reach the milestone” closes the round, the founder faces a peculiar incentive.

More confidence can make the confident statement more likely to become true.

This does not make the stronger statement honest.

It explains why people can persuade themselves that honesty is an obstacle to truth.

The founder may reason: the technology will work if everyone stops hedging and commits. Expressing doubt causes the very failure the doubt predicts. Therefore doubt is not realism; it is sabotage.

That is the psychological doorway through which artificial promises enter otherwise serious organizations.

Uncertainty becomes associated with disloyalty because confidence is genuinely productive.

There are domains where this effect is obvious outside startups.

Bank runs are partly expectations about other people’s expectations. A bank can fail because depositors believe others will withdraw, causing withdrawals. Currencies, political campaigns, consumer brands, and asset markets all contain reflexive elements. Belief can change the object being believed about.

Entrepreneurship has reflexivity built into its operating model.

A valuation can influence the company’s ability to recruit.

Recruiting influences product velocity.

Product velocity influences customer interest.

Customer interest influences the next valuation.

The loop can be virtuous.

It can also create instability.

A self-fulfilling promise is usually paired with a self-reinforcing disappointment.

If the company misses a milestone, some investors become less willing to finance. The lower financing probability makes it harder to hire. Slower hiring delays the next milestone. The delay weakens customer confidence. Customer hesitation makes the financing harder still.

This is why founders sometimes fight so hard to maintain narrative momentum.

They are not imagining the causal power of belief.

The mistake is concluding that because belief matters, evidence should be subordinated to belief.

A company that depends on confidence needs more accurate internal information, not less.

That sounds paradoxical until the two audiences are separated.

Externally, the company may need a coherent commitment: this is the market we are building for, this is the product we intend to deliver, this is the strategy we are pursuing.

Internally, the company needs a live map of uncertainty: which assumptions are weakening, what the actual probability of the schedule is, where customer behavior differs from the narrative, which technical barriers remain unresolved, and what conditions would justify changing the plan.

The company gets into trouble when one representation has to serve both functions.

A pitch is a terrible internal forecasting system.

A probabilistic engineering assessment is often a terrible recruiting speech.

The organization needs both without forcing them to become identical.

That is difficult because language travels.

A sentence created for investors may appear in an all-hands meeting. A recruiting slogan may become a customer promise. A board target may become a public date. A public date may then influence engineering planning because nobody wants the company to contradict itself.

The causal arrow reverses.

The internal forecast no longer determines the external promise.

The external promise begins determining the internal forecast.

This is one of the most important transitions in a founder-led company.

Early on, the founder says, “Based on what we know, I think we can do this.”

Later, after the company has organized around the claim, people start saying, “We have to be able to do this because we said we would.”

The first sentence updates from reality.

The second asks reality to update from the sentence.

Sometimes reality cooperates.

Deadlines can focus attention. Public commitments can force teams to integrate. Customers can create urgency that pure research lacks. A founder can be right that the organization has more capacity than conservative functional estimates suggest.

There is a reason ambitious deadlines survive despite being missed so often.

They can change behavior.

An engineering team estimating each component independently may produce a schedule that assumes sequential work, generous buffers, and no strategic reprioritization. A founder imposing a much earlier date may trigger parallelization, scope reduction, additional hiring, executive escalation, or a different technical approach. The original estimate and the committed date describe different organizations.

This is real.

It is also dangerous to generalize from successes.

A date that causes one team to discover a better approach can cause another to hide testing problems. A customer promise can inspire useful urgency or produce a rushed release. A high valuation can attract talent or encourage spending disconnected from product-market fit.

The causal power of a promise does not tell us whether the promise is wise.

It tells us why forecast accuracy is not the only variable.

The founder is choosing both a statement and the behavior the statement is intended to induce.

This suggests a better way to evaluate ambitious commitments.

Ask what mechanism connects the promise to the outcome.

If the company says a large order will reduce unit cost, which costs actually fall with volume?

If a public launch date is meant to accelerate execution, what decisions become faster because the date exists?

If announcing a partnership is supposed to unlock an ecosystem, what parties are expected to commit in response?

If a valuation is expected to help recruiting, is equity compensation actually competitive at that valuation and what happens if the next round is lower?

A self-fulfilling promise should contain a causal theory, not merely a hope that confidence creates miracles.

The stronger the theory, the easier it is to monitor.

Suppose the promise is that scale will reduce manufacturing cost. The organization can track yield, purchasing discounts, labor efficiency, utilization, defect rates, and throughput. If those variables do not improve, the story has to change.

Suppose the promise is that developers will make a platform more valuable. The company can track active integrations, retention, third-party usage, and whether the ecosystem creates demand that would not exist otherwise.

Suppose the promise is that a bold mission will attract exceptional talent. The company can compare acceptance rates, quality of hires, compensation tradeoffs, and retention.

The promise is allowed to help cause itself.

It is not allowed to exempt itself from measurement.

This is where responsible audacity differs from reality distortion.

A founder may deliberately set a target above the median forecast because the target changes the organization’s effort. Responsible leadership preserves the median forecast somewhere. It does not rewrite the underlying evidence so the target appears inevitable.

A company can say, “Our commitment is June; our current risk-adjusted estimate is August.”

Those two dates may be uncomfortable together.

The discomfort is useful.

It tells management how much of the schedule is being purchased with urgency rather than supported by current evidence.

The gap can guide contingency planning.

A company that forces the estimates to match the commitment loses that information.

This is the epistemic price of artificial certainty.

The same principle applies to fundraising narratives.

A founder may need to explain why the company could become enormous. That is not the same as pretending the enormous outcome is the base case. A disciplined investor can price the distribution. A disciplined company should preserve it internally.

The organization must know which future it is selling and which future it expects.

If those become permanently identical, the company has confused motivation with measurement.

Self-fulfilling promises have another property that makes them morally difficult: success can hide the recklessness of the original claim.

A founder can make a promise with a poor factual basis, attract resources because of it, and then use those resources to deliver the outcome.

The fact that the outcome arrives does not answer whether the people who committed resources were entitled to know how uncertain it was at the time.

Imagine a bridge engineer who says a design has already passed a safety test when the test has not been run, then uses the resulting funding to improve the design until it later passes. The completed bridge does not make the earlier statement true.

Startups are not bridge engineering, but the information principle survives.

Causality does not retroactively change tense.

A future made true by a promise does not make a false present-tense representation honest when it was made.

This distinction will become central when we reach the legal boundary cases later in the book.

For now, it is enough to see why the boundary becomes blurred so easily.

Founders live in a world where statements can change outcomes.

Confidence recruits.

Commitments coordinate.

Deadlines accelerate.

Valuations signal.

Partnerships legitimize.

Customer logos attract customers.

Capital finances progress.

The founder learns from experience that belief is productive.

The dangerous conclusion is that more belief is always more productive.

At some point a company needs the ability to disappoint itself.

It has to discover that a market is smaller, a date is wrong, a feature is less valuable, a technical path is blocked, or a strategic assumption has expired. Those discoveries feel destructive because they weaken the narrative loops that helped build the organization.

They may save the company.

A promise can cause itself only while reality remains responsive to the actions the promise induces.

Capital cannot solve a physical impossibility.

Urgency cannot eliminate a safety requirement.

Hiring cannot create product-market fit by itself.

Public confidence cannot indefinitely substitute for customer value.

The founder’s distinctive skill is not believing hard enough to erase those limits.

It is identifying which limits can actually move when people coordinate around a future.

That is the constructive core of the artificial promise.

Sell the future strongly enough to bring the necessary present into existence.

Then keep enough private doubt alive to notice when the future has stopped listening.
