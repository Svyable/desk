# The Adjacency Economy

The value of a map depends partly on who controls the roads.

Vector systems are often discussed as if they were neutral infrastructure: an embedding model produces coordinates, an index finds neighbors, an application displays the results. The language is mathematical enough to hide an economic fact.

Discovery is a market.

Attention is scarce. Supply is abundant. Somewhere between the two sits a system that decides which items become candidates for attention.

That system can capture value.

Search engines learned this first at internet scale. Billions of pages existed. Users had a small number of questions. The engine that could place useful pages near those questions became one of the most valuable businesses ever created.

Recommendation platforms extended the model. Users no longer had to ask. The platform could infer what should come next and allocate attention continuously. Music, video, shopping, news, social posts and advertising all became markets in which ranking determined exposure.

Vector systems deepen the same economic structure because they make more forms of supply searchable by relation.

A product need not match a query exactly.

A paper need not share vocabulary.

A creator need not already be famous enough to have many explicit links.

A software tool need not have a memorized command.

A memory need not be addressed by key.

The discovery layer can manufacture candidate sets from learned proximity.

This lowers search costs.

When search costs fall, markets change.

Economists have studied search costs for a long time because buyers and sellers rarely meet frictionlessly. A consumer may not know every store's price. An employer may not know every qualified worker. A worker may not know every opening. A company may not know every supplier. A researcher may not know every relevant paper. Information costs shape which transactions happen.

Digital search reduced many of those costs.

Semantic adjacency reduces a different category: the cost of imperfect description.

You do not need to know the exact name of the item.

You need enough information to locate its neighborhood.

This expands the effective market.

A small supplier can be discovered by capability rather than brand recognition. A niche product can be retrieved by use case. A researcher in one field can encounter work from another. A candidate with an unusual title can be matched through experience rather than exact taxonomy.

That is the optimistic economics of adjacency.

The market becomes thicker because more plausible matches become reachable.

But lower search costs do not automatically decentralize power.

Often they increase the value of the intermediary that controls search.

If millions of suppliers compete to be one of the ten results shown, the ranking layer becomes a tollbooth on attention. A platform does not need to own the products to shape the market. It can own the neighborhood.

This is already obvious in e-commerce and app stores. Merchants care deeply about ranking because visibility affects sales. Creators care about recommendation because discovery affects audience. Publishers care about search because the first page matters more than nominal availability.

Vector-mediated discovery adds new dimensions to that gatekeeping.

The platform can decide not only which known category an item belongs to, but what the item resembles.

That is a subtler form of market power.

Suppose two products are technically different categories but solve the same user problem. A semantic system can make them substitutes by placing them in the same discovery neighborhood.

Suppose two services sound similar but differ economically. A ranking system can place one closer to the user's intention because it converts better, pays higher commission or fits the platform's strategy.

Suppose a marketplace introduces its own private-label product. The vector system can decide how often the private-label product appears near queries that previously led to independent sellers.

The geometry does not have to be falsified for the market effect to be real.

Reranking can do the work.

Business incentives sit on top of semantic relevance.

This has always been true of commercial search. Sponsored results are an explicit example. Vector systems simply broaden the set of discovery surfaces where the same conflict can arise.

The phrase “recommended for you” sounds personal.

It is also an allocation rule.

A platform may optimize user satisfaction, transaction probability, advertising value, margin, retention, supplier diversity, regulatory requirements or some weighted combination. Each objective changes which neighbors receive exposure.

The user sees one row.

The company sees an optimization problem.

The supplier sees a market access decision.

These are three perspectives on the same geometry.

This is why the adjacency economy will produce new forms of search-engine optimization.

When discovery was lexical, producers learned keywords. Websites repeated phrases, structured pages for crawlers and competed for links. E-commerce sellers optimized titles. App developers optimized store descriptions. Creators learned thumbnail and headline conventions.

When discovery becomes more semantic, optimization does not disappear.

It becomes harder to see.

Producers will learn how to become close to valuable intentions.

Product descriptions will be written for embedding models as well as humans. Tool descriptions will be tuned so agents retrieve them. Corporate knowledge pages will be phrased to enter the right semantic neighborhoods. Research summaries may be written for machine discoverability. Creators will experiment with multimodal features that influence recommendation embeddings.

The old keyword game becomes a representation game.

This can improve information quality. A seller who wants to be discovered for a use case may need to describe the product more accurately. A software team may write clearer tool documentation because agents depend on it. A research database may extract richer concepts from papers.

It can also create spam.

Every discovery mechanism invites adversarial adaptation once visibility has value.

If a retrieval system relies on embeddings, attackers can attempt to manipulate the text, metadata or content so that an item lands near desirable queries. The exact tactics differ from keyword stuffing, but the economic incentive is identical.

Be adjacent to attention.

This becomes particularly important for AI agents. Imagine an ecosystem in which agents search for tools, vendors, services and data sources semantically. Companies will want their offerings to be retrieved by agents.

Agent optimization becomes a new marketing discipline.

A human buyer may browse a website, read reviews and develop brand preference. An agent may first retrieve a handful of providers based on machine-readable descriptions, structured credentials, price data and learned similarity.

If you are not in the candidate set, the customer may never know you exist.

The battle for the shelf becomes a battle for the agent's context window.

This could restructure entire industries.

Travel is an obvious example. Today consumers often begin with search engines, airline sites, hotel marketplaces and travel agencies. A capable personal agent could instead represent the user's preferences, retrieve flights and rooms from multiple sources, compare them and act.

The consumer experiences one interface.

Behind it, suppliers compete to become reachable by the agent.

Who controls the embedding and retrieval layer matters enormously.

If the agent provider also sells travel services, conflicts appear. If suppliers can pay for higher semantic visibility, the agent becomes an advertising market. If access to inventory requires proprietary deals, nominally intelligent search can reproduce the same gatekeepers under a new interface.

The front end changes.

The economics of distribution survives.

The same will happen in software procurement, insurance, financial products, professional services and digital content. Agents make comparison cheaper. Lower comparison costs can increase competition among suppliers.

But the agent itself becomes a high-leverage intermediary.

It knows the buyer's preferences.

It controls the query.

It can inspect many suppliers.

It decides which offers enter consideration.

That position is commercially extraordinary.

The company that controls the personal vector—the representation of what you want—may possess a bargaining asset more valuable than a traditional cookie or demographic profile.

A demographic segment says that people like you often buy certain things.

A persistent personal embedding could encode much finer patterns of taste, constraints, behavior and intent.

Used responsibly, this makes discovery dramatically better.

Used commercially, it can make persuasion more precise.

The distinction between helping a user find what she wants and shaping what she comes to want becomes harder to see.

Recommendation has always lived near that boundary.

Persistent AI assistants intensify it because they can mediate across domains. The same system may know your work habits, entertainment preferences, travel patterns, budget, relationships and goals. It can connect regions of your life that today's separate platforms see only partially.

Cross-domain adjacency becomes economically valuable.

The assistant knows that you started running, that your shoes are old, that you have a trip coming and that you dislike shopping. A recommendation can feel uncannily useful.

It can also be an advertisement with privileged access to context.

The governance question is therefore not only data privacy.

It is fiduciary alignment.

Whose objective does the discovery system optimize when interests conflict?

This may become one of the central product distinctions of the agent economy.

Some systems may be advertising-funded and optimize a mixture of user relevance and commercial value. Some may be subscription-funded and promise stronger alignment with the user. Enterprise agents may optimize organizational goals while employees use them for individual work. Marketplace agents may have contractual relationships with suppliers.

The vector space does not resolve these conflicts.

It makes them operational at extraordinary granularity.

This is why transparency around sponsored adjacency may become necessary.

If a result is retrieved because it is semantically relevant, that is one thing.

If it is promoted because a supplier paid, that is another.

If the system blends both without a legible distinction, the user cannot understand why the neighborhood looks the way it does.

Search engines eventually developed conventions around ads because economic influence over ranking mattered.

Agentic discovery will need its own conventions.

There is also a competitive question around embedding infrastructure itself.

General-purpose embedding models can become standardized utilities. Open models and commodity vector databases can reduce dependence on any one vendor. Organizations can embed their own data, operate their own indexes and preserve control over local discovery.

That decentralizes capability.

At the same time, the largest platforms possess data advantages. A service with billions of interactions can learn representations of behavior that a smaller competitor cannot easily reproduce. A marketplace with deep purchase history can optimize matching. A platform with multimodal data can connect text, image, audio and behavior in a richer space.

The map benefits from traffic.

Traffic benefits from the map.

This can create feedback advantages similar to those seen in search and recommendation markets before vectors became fashionable.

More users generate more interaction data.

More interaction data improves representation and ranking.

Better discovery attracts more users and suppliers.

More suppliers increase the value of the discovery layer.

The loop can concentrate power.

But vector technology also creates opportunities for interoperability.

If representations or interfaces become portable enough, users could carry preferences across services. A personal agent could mediate among multiple marketplaces rather than forcing the person into one platform's recommender. Suppliers could expose machine-readable catalogs directly to agents. Open retrieval protocols could reduce the advantage of owning the front door.

This is not guaranteed.

It is a design choice about market architecture.

The early web gained enormous power from open linking. Anyone could publish a page, anyone could link to it and search engines could crawl broadly. Walled platforms narrowed that openness by moving discovery inside proprietary feeds.

The agent era could move in either direction.

A proprietary agent could become the ultimate walled garden: one personalized interface through which a company mediates everything.

Or personal agents could become user-controlled search intermediaries that negotiate across open markets on the person's behalf.

Both futures use vectors.

The political economy differs.

This is a useful reminder that technology does not determine market structure.

Vector search lowers certain costs.

Institutions decide who captures the savings.

Consider recruiting. Semantic matching can reduce the cost of finding candidates whose skills do not match exact job titles. That can widen opportunity and help employers discover unconventional talent.

It can also empower the platform that owns the candidate graph and embeddings. Employers may become more dependent on the intermediary because the platform's learned representation performs better than their internal search. Candidates may need to optimize profiles for machine retrieval. The labor market becomes easier to navigate and more intermediated at the same time.

Consider scientific publishing. Semantic discovery can help researchers find obscure papers and reduce dependence on journal prestige. It can also make the provider of the literature map highly influential. If one system becomes the default research assistant, its retrieval policies can shape which papers enter scientific attention.

Consider commerce. Semantic product search can help small sellers compete on fit rather than brand. It can also allow marketplaces to monetize relevance more precisely.

Adjacency redistributes advantage.

The winners are not automatically the producers of the underlying objects.

They may be the systems that connect objects to intention.

This is why discovery infrastructure belongs in economic analysis alongside production infrastructure.

When supply is abundant, the bottleneck moves toward matching.

Generative AI accelerates this shift because it makes supply even more abundant. There will be more text, more images, more software, more product variants, more synthetic media and more generated ideas than human attention can absorb.

Cheap creation increases the value of good selection.

This is the same inversion we saw in science.

When hypotheses become cheap, experimental attention becomes scarce.

When content becomes cheap, trustworthy discovery becomes scarce.

When software components become cheap, architectural judgment and tool selection become scarce.

When products proliferate, matching becomes scarce.

Abundance raises the price of adjacency.

This may become one of the defining economic laws of the generative era.

The more things can be made, the more valuable it becomes to know which thing belongs near the current need.

A vector space is one mechanism for answering that question cheaply.

The commercial opportunity is enormous because matching sits between abundance and action.

This will create businesses whose core asset is not the content, product or service itself, but the quality of the neighborhood they can construct.

Better research maps.

Better supplier maps.

Better personal memory maps.

Better code maps.

Better molecular maps.

Better capability maps for agents.

Some of these will be public goods. Some will be subscription products. Some will be proprietary infrastructure. Some will become regulated because their rankings affect consequential access.

The category will be broader than “vector database company.”

The database is plumbing.

The economic product is discovery.

This distinction matters for strategy. A company can own excellent vector infrastructure and have no defensible discovery advantage if its representation, data and product experience are generic. Another company can use commodity infrastructure and build an extraordinary map from proprietary interactions, expert curation or a specialized domain.

The moat, where one exists, will often sit in the relation between data, objective and feedback.

Which examples teach the neighborhood?

Which human judgments correct it?

Which outcomes return as learning signals?

Which domain distinctions have been encoded through years of use?

The index can be copied.

A living map may be harder.

That should also make us skeptical of claims that one giant embedding model will commoditize all discovery. General representations will become extremely capable. They reduce the cost of entering many markets.

Local advantage can remain in the last mile of adjacency.

A hospital knows which clinical distinctions matter in its workflow.

A law firm knows which authorities and procedural details govern its practice.

A manufacturer knows how its part numbers, failure modes and suppliers relate.

A scientist knows which similarity is mechanistically meaningful.

An institution can teach a generic map its local roads.

This is why vector systems are likely to become part of organizational capital.

The embeddings themselves may be regenerated. The durable asset is the accumulated set of judgments about what should be near, what should be separated, which filters matter, which exceptions recur and which retrieval failures are unacceptable.

That is institutional knowledge made computational.

It has economic value because it reduces the cost of finding the right thing.

We are accustomed to valuing inventories, patents, customer lists and brands.

In an age of abundance, a high-quality map of relation may deserve similar attention.

The adjacency economy will reward whoever can consistently make the right next possibility easier to reach.

The fight will be over whose definition of “right” governs the map.

The next economic question is whether that definition can be contested.

A discovery system becomes especially powerful when it controls several layers at once: the representation of the buyer, the index of possible suppliers, the ranking rule, the interface and the transaction. At that point the intermediary is not simply helping a market clear. It is defining the market the user is allowed to see.

The difference is easy to miss because the experience can still feel effortless.

Ask an agent to find someone to repair a roof. It may know the house, the budget, the user's tolerance for scheduling delays and the fact that the insurance deductible makes one kind of repair uneconomic. That context can produce a far better shortlist than a generic directory.

But where did the contractors come from?

Did the agent search an open market, a paid partner network or the provider's own marketplace? Were some suppliers excluded because their data was difficult to ingest? Did one provider pay for preferred placement? Was the ranking optimized for the user's total cost, the probability of a completed transaction or the platform's commission?

The intelligence of the recommendation does not answer those questions.

In fact, a better recommendation can make them less visible because the result feels so well fitted to the user's need.

This is where discovery rent can emerge. Lower search costs create savings: less time comparing, fewer bad matches, lower uncertainty, more competition among suppliers. The intermediary that makes those savings possible can capture part of them through subscription fees, commissions, advertising, transaction charges or preferential access.

There is nothing inherently improper about that. Matching is valuable work.

The economic question is whether the intermediary remains contestable once users and suppliers depend on its map.

If a consumer can easily ask a second agent for an independent search, discovery remains more competitive. If suppliers can expose offers through open machine-readable interfaces, the candidate pool can remain broad. If a user's preferences and constraints can move between services, switching costs fall. If commercial influence over ranking is legible, the person can distinguish assistance from promotion.

If none of those conditions hold, a personalized agent can become a remarkably effective gatekeeper precisely because it is so useful.

The personal model itself may become a source of lock-in.

Years of corrections, preferences, rejected recommendations and observed constraints can produce a map that feels uniquely fitted to one person. Leaving the service then means more than moving files. It may mean abandoning accumulated knowledge about how the world should be searched on that person's behalf.

Portability in the agent era may therefore need to include more than raw data.

A user may want to carry durable preferences, explicit constraints, purchase history, exclusions and perhaps a history of corrected recommendations into another discovery system. The exact technical form can vary. The important economic point is that the ability to leave depends on whether the user's side of the map can travel.

Suppliers face a parallel problem.

A small business benefits from semantic discovery only if the system can represent it faithfully enough to place it near the right demand. If access depends on proprietary integrations or opaque ranking signals, the technology can recreate the old problem under a more sophisticated vocabulary: nominally anyone can participate, but only insiders know how to become visible.

This makes auditability commercially important.

A supplier may want to know whether it is absent because its offer is genuinely a poor match, because its metadata is incomplete or because the platform's ranking objective favors something else. A buyer may want to know why one option entered the shortlist and another did not. A regulator or enterprise customer may care whether a supposedly neutral discovery tool systematically routes demand toward affiliated services.

None of this requires publishing an embedding model's weights or exposing every ranking secret.

It requires enough legibility to distinguish relevance from commercial preference and technical omission.

The agent interface could make some of this ordinary.

Show me the best match without sponsored placement.

Compare this shortlist against another marketplace.

Tell me which constraints excluded the other candidates.

Search providers outside your partner network.

Keep my preferences but change the ranking objective from convenience to total cost.

These requests treat the discovery policy itself as something the user can query.

That is a meaningful shift.

Today's platforms mostly expose results. Agentic systems could expose some of the reasoning about how the candidate set was constructed, because conversation makes such questions natural.

The same capability can strengthen or weaken market power.

An agent that refuses to reveal anything about its discovery process can become an opaque broker. An agent that helps the user inspect and alter the search policy can become a tool for buyer power.

This may create a new dimension of product competition.

One service can promise convenience.

Another can promise independence from advertising.

Another can promise open-market comparison.

Another can specialize in a domain where expert curation matters more than breadth.

Another can let the user operate the map locally and choose among multiple models.

Trust in the discovery objective becomes part of the product.

That is particularly important because the system will often act before the user sees the market. In a traditional search interface, a person can scan several results and notice patterns. A personal agent may return one synthesized recommendation or simply complete the transaction. The better the automation, the less visible the discarded candidate set becomes.

Convenience compresses the market into an answer.

Whoever constructs that answer has power over the alternatives that disappear.

This is the economic counterpart to the agent-safety problem from the previous chapter. There, the concern was whether the right tool or precedent entered consideration before action. Here, the concern is whether the right supplier, product, worker, paper or service entered consideration before exchange.

In both cases the candidate set is upstream of everything the user eventually sees.

That is why the economics of adjacency will not be settled by embedding quality alone.

The consequential institutions will form around access to candidate pools, portability of user context, disclosure of commercial influence, the right to compare maps and the ability of new suppliers to become legible without purchasing permission from a dominant intermediary.

Abundance makes the shortlist valuable.

Control the shortlist, and you can shape the market without owning most of what the market sells.

The promise of adjacency is that more good matches become possible.

Its political economy will be decided by whether those possibilities remain open enough for users and suppliers to challenge the map.
