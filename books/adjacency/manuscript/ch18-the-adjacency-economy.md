# The Adjacency Economy

The value of a map depends partly on who controls the roads.

Vector systems are often discussed as if they were neutral infrastructure. A model produces embeddings. An index finds neighbors. An application displays results.

The mathematics can hide the market.

Discovery is an allocation system.

Supply is abundant. Attention is scarce. Somewhere between the two sits a mechanism that decides which possibilities become visible enough to compete.

That mechanism can capture value.

Search engines demonstrated the principle at internet scale. Billions of pages could exist while only a few results appeared above the fold. Recommendation platforms pushed the same allocation logic into music, video, shopping, news, social feeds, and advertising. The user no longer needed to ask a fully formed question. The platform could construct the next candidate set continuously.

Vector systems deepen the same structure because they make more forms of supply searchable by relation.

A product does not have to share the query's vocabulary.

A worker does not have to have the expected job title.

A paper does not have to use the same disciplinary language.

A software tool does not have to be known by name.

A supplier does not have to occupy the category the buyer thought to search.

The discovery layer can manufacture a market around an intention.

That lowers search costs.

Lower search costs sound automatically competitive.

Sometimes they are.

A buyer who can compare more suppliers can bargain better. A small business can be discovered by capability rather than brand recognition. An unconventional candidate can enter a hiring search through experience rather than taxonomy. A niche product can be found by use case. A research paper from another field can become visible because its problem structure is close.

The effective market becomes thicker.

More plausible matches can meet.

But lower search costs do not automatically decentralize power.

They can increase the value of the intermediary that controls the shortlist.

Imagine a homeowner asking a personal agent to find someone to repair a roof after a storm.

The agent knows the address, the age of the house, the likely repair class, the owner's preference for a licensed contractor, the fact that a trip is scheduled next week, and perhaps the rough budget. It can query multiple data sources, retrieve contractors whose services are semantically close to the need, compare availability, inspect reviews, and return three candidates.

The experience is excellent.

The economic question begins one layer earlier.

Where did the contractors come from?

Was the candidate pool an open directory, a paid partner network, a marketplace owned by the agent provider, or a mixture?

Were some contractors missing because their data was hard to ingest?

Did one provider receive a ranking boost because it paid a fee?

Did the agent optimize total expected cost, speed to completion, likelihood of conversion, platform commission, or some weighted mixture?

The intelligence of the recommendation does not answer those questions.

In fact, better personalization can make them harder to notice because the result feels so obviously fitted to the user.

This is the central economic fact of adjacency.

The shortlist is upstream of choice.

Control the shortlist and you can shape a market without owning most of what the market sells.

Search engines learned this with webpages. Marketplaces learned it with products. App stores learned it with software. Vector-mediated agents can extend the same power across services, suppliers, tools, labor, information, and transactions.

A discovery system can become a market maker without looking like one.

The user sees assistance.

The supplier sees distribution.

The platform sees optimization.

These are different views of the same geometry.

Consider a marketplace containing ten thousand sellers.

Nominal availability is not meaningful access. If most users inspect five results, the ranking layer determines practical visibility. A seller can be present in the database and absent from the market that matters.

Vector search makes this more subtle than category placement.

The system is not merely deciding whether a seller belongs in “outdoor furniture.” It can decide whether the seller's product is adjacent to “small balcony dinner setup,” “weatherproof reading corner,” or “something that looks like this photograph but costs less.”

The marketplace can create substitutes the category system never encoded.

That is valuable.

It is also power over substitution.

A platform can make two offerings compete by placing them in the same neighborhood. It can make them functionally invisible to one another by placing them in different candidate pools. It can let a private-label product appear beside a high-intent query that previously led to independent sellers. It can reserve a premium region for suppliers with richer data or tighter integration.

None of this requires corrupting the embedding.

The representation can be honest.

Reranking can do the economics.

Business incentives sit downstream of semantic relevance.

This is why “recommended for you” should be understood as both a convenience and an allocation rule.

A platform may care about user satisfaction, transaction probability, gross margin, advertising value, retention, fulfillment reliability, supplier diversity, legal requirements, inventory position, or strategic partnerships.

The final ranking can reflect all of them.

The user sees one clean row.

The row is the output of institutional priorities.

This does not make ranking illegitimate.

Every useful system has an objective.

The problem is when commercial influence becomes indistinguishable from fit.

Search engines eventually developed conventions for marking sponsored results because the reason for visibility mattered. Agentic discovery will need equivalent distinctions, especially when the interface collapses many candidates into one synthesized recommendation.

If a supplier paid to enter the shortlist, that is different from being retrieved because it was the strongest semantic match.

If the provider's own service received preferential treatment, that is different from neutral comparison.

If a hotel appeared because it had live inventory while another did not expose machine-readable availability, that is yet another reason.

The user may reasonably care which mechanism produced the neighbor.

This becomes more consequential when the agent acts rather than merely recommends.

A traditional search page lets a person notice the market. Several airlines appear. Several insurance products appear. Several vendors appear. The user can scan differences and sometimes recognize what is missing.

An agent can compress the market into an answer.

“I booked the best option.”

“I renewed the plan.”

“I ordered the replacement.”

“I chose the supplier.”

Automation removes friction and also hides the discarded candidate set.

Convenience can make market structure invisible.

That is why the agent era raises a question that old recommendation systems could often postpone: whose objective does the discovery system serve when interests conflict?

A subscription-funded agent may promise strong alignment with the buyer.

An advertising-funded service may mix relevance with commercial value.

An enterprise agent may optimize the employer's policy even when an employee would prefer another outcome.

A marketplace agent may have contractual duties to suppliers.

A bank's assistant may help the customer while also selling the bank's products.

The vector space does not resolve these conflicts.

It gives them a fine-grained surface on which to operate.

The same applies to personal representations.

A persistent assistant may know that a user dislikes long layovers, tends to buy durable products, prefers independent hotels, has a trip coming, and is unusually price-sensitive this month. That context can create excellent matching.

It can also make commercial persuasion much more precise.

The difference between helping a person find what she wants and shaping what she comes to want becomes harder to see when the system knows both the market and the person.

This is why privacy is only part of the governance problem.

Alignment is another part.

A system can use data lawfully and still optimize against the user's economic interest.

It can protect the data and manipulate the shortlist.

The question is not only who can see the personal map.

It is who gets to steer with it.

This will create new forms of competition among agents.

One provider may promise no sponsored ranking.

Another may promise broad partner access and lower prices.

Another may specialize in a domain where expert curation matters more than breadth.

Another may run locally and keep the user's preference model portable.

Another may integrate tightly with one marketplace and offer superior convenience at the cost of narrower comparison.

Trust in the discovery objective becomes part of the product.

That trust can be tested through counterfactual queries.

Show me the best option without sponsored placement.

Search outside your partner network.

Rank by total cost rather than convenience.

Use my constraints but ignore my purchase history.

Compare this shortlist against another marketplace.

Tell me which constraint excluded the next five candidates.

These questions treat ranking policy as something the user can interrogate.

Conversational interfaces make such inspection easier than old search pages if the provider chooses to support it.

That choice matters for market power.

An opaque agent can become a powerful broker.

An inspectable agent can become a tool for buyer power.

The distinction will not be absolute. Ranking systems cannot reveal every proprietary detail without becoming gameable or exposing sensitive information. But users do not need every model weight to understand whether a recommendation was sponsored, whether the candidate pool was restricted, or whether changing the objective would change the winner.

Pipeline legibility is enough to create meaningful contestability.

Suppliers need contestability too.

Semantic discovery sounds favorable to small providers because it can retrieve by fit rather than fame. That promise depends on whether the supplier can become legible to the system.

A small manufacturer may make the ideal component but publish sparse metadata.

A local contractor may be excellent but lack a structured availability feed.

An independent hotel may fit the traveler's preferences but sit outside the agent's booking integrations.

A specialist consultant may solve the exact problem but describe the work in language the dominant embedding does not connect to the buyer's vocabulary.

The market can be theoretically open and practically invisible.

This creates a new form of optimization pressure.

When discovery was lexical, producers learned keywords. Websites optimized titles, tags, backlinks, and copy. E-commerce sellers learned category taxonomies. App makers learned store descriptions.

When discovery becomes semantic, producers will learn how to become adjacent to valuable intentions.

Product descriptions will be written for embedding models as well as people. Tool descriptions will be tuned so agents retrieve them. Company profiles will be phrased to enter desirable capability neighborhoods. Suppliers will expose richer attributes because missing metadata means missing candidate sets.

The old keyword game becomes a representation game.

Some of this is healthy.

Better descriptions can improve matching.

Clearer tool documentation helps humans and machines.

Structured credentials can reduce uncertainty.

Accurate product attributes can make comparison fairer.

Some of it will be adversarial.

Every discovery surface with economic value attracts attempts to manipulate visibility.

The objective remains the same across generations of technology.

Be adjacent to attention.

Agent ecosystems make this pressure especially interesting because the audience is partly machine.

A company may care less whether a human remembers its brand if personal agents reliably retrieve the offering for relevant tasks.

Marketing moves upstream into candidate generation.

The battle for shelf space becomes a battle for context.

This could weaken some traditional brands and strengthen others. A brand that once served as a shortcut for quality may matter less if an agent can inspect detailed evidence directly. But brands may also become valuable machine-readable trust signals when the agent faces uncertainty about suppliers.

Search cost falls.

Verification cost does not necessarily fall at the same rate.

A buyer can discover a hundred obscure vendors and still need to know which ones will deliver.

This moves scarcity downstream again.

Discovery becomes cheaper.

Trust, verification, and recourse become more valuable.

A market with excellent semantic matching and weak verification can produce efficient fraud.

The same technology that finds the obscure legitimate supplier can find the obscure deceptive one.

This is why mature discovery markets will combine adjacency with structured constraints.

Licensing.

Insurance.

Verified inventory.

Delivery history.

Financial condition.

Contract terms.

Reputation.

Return rights.

The relevant constraints differ by market, but the pattern is stable.

Similarity expands the candidate pool.

Institutions determine which candidates are eligible to receive trust.

This makes data quality an economic asset.

A supplier with accurate, machine-readable information can enter more high-intent neighborhoods. A marketplace with reliable structured data can rank with greater confidence. An agent with access to current prices and inventory can outperform one relying on stale webpages even if both use equally capable embeddings.

The model receives attention because it is visible.

The market plumbing often determines who actually wins.

This is one reason the long-term moat in discovery may not sit in the vector database.

Vector infrastructure is increasingly generic. An organization can regenerate embeddings and replace indexes.

The harder asset is the living relationship among domain data, ranking objectives, feedback, trust signals, and correction.

Which outcomes taught the system that one match was good?

Which experts corrected its mistakes?

Which supplier attributes proved predictive of fulfillment?

Which user preferences were explicit and which were inferred?

Which ranking errors created costly failures?

The durable map is partly accumulated judgment.

This creates feedback advantages.

More users create more interactions.

More interactions improve ranking.

Better ranking attracts more users and suppliers.

More suppliers make the marketplace more useful.

The loop can concentrate power.

But the same vector technology also creates opportunities for interoperability.

A personal agent can compare several markets.

Suppliers can expose catalogs directly through open interfaces.

Users can carry explicit constraints across services.

Independent ranking systems can sit above proprietary inventories.

Multiple maps can compete over the same underlying supply.

This is the economic fork in the road.

The agent era could produce the ultimate walled garden: one personalized interface mediating nearly every market, remembering years of preferences, owning the transaction layer, and making alternative discovery expensive.

Or it could produce powerful user-controlled intermediaries that negotiate across markets on the person's behalf.

Both futures use embeddings.

The difference is institutional architecture.

Portability will matter because personalization creates switching costs.

A user can leave a search engine with little more than bookmarks.

Leaving a mature personal agent may mean abandoning years of corrections, constraints, preference history, rejected recommendations, trusted sources, and learned exceptions.

The personal map itself becomes capital.

If it cannot travel, convenience becomes lock-in.

Portability may therefore need to include more than raw data exports.

A person may want to carry durable facts and explicit preferences:

I prefer rail to short flights when the timing is close.

Do not recommend this supplier again.

I value total cost over monthly payment.

I need wheelchair-accessible lodging.

Do not optimize around my purchases from last December; they were gifts.

These statements are not merely history.

They are instructions about how the market should be searched on the person's behalf.

A competing agent that receives only transaction records must relearn them.

The cost of relearning is a switching cost.

Suppliers face a parallel portability problem.

If every major agent ecosystem requires a different proprietary feed, small providers can be locked out by integration cost. If an open machine-readable description can reach many discovery systems, market entry becomes easier.

Standards that look boring at the technical layer can determine competition at the economic layer.

Which fields can a supplier publish?

How are credentials represented?

How are prices and availability queried?

Can an agent verify terms without joining a closed marketplace?

Can the supplier learn why it was excluded from a shortlist?

These design questions shape who can participate.

They also shape the possibility of auditing market power.

A supplier may want to know whether it disappeared because it was a poor match, because its metadata was incomplete, because it violated a hard constraint, or because the platform's commercial objective favored something else.

A buyer may want to know whether the three recommended options came from three thousand possibilities or thirty partners.

A regulator may care whether an apparently neutral agent systematically routes users toward affiliated products.

No one needs a perfect explanation of high-dimensional geometry to answer these questions.

They need an explanation of the pipeline.

Candidate pool.

Eligibility rules.

Ranking objective.

Commercial influence.

Final selection.

The economic product is not the embedding.

It is the neighborhood the embedding helps construct.

That neighborhood can generate what we might call discovery rent: value captured because one intermediary controls access between abundant supply and scarce attention.

The rent is not automatically abusive.

Matching is real work. Better matching can create real surplus. A traveler saves hours. A buyer finds a lower-cost supplier. A job seeker reaches an employer who would otherwise have missed her. A scientist finds a method from another field.

The important question is who captures the savings and whether the intermediary remains contestable once everyone depends on the map.

Can the user compare another map?

Can the supplier reach the user another way?

Can preferences move?

Can sponsored influence be distinguished from relevance?

Can the candidate set be challenged?

Can a new entrant build on common infrastructure rather than reconstructing the market from scratch?

These are competition questions disguised as product design.

They become more important as generative AI increases supply.

There will be more text, more images, more software, more product variants, more generated research ideas, more services, and more machine-produced offers than human attention can absorb.

Cheap creation raises the value of selection.

When hypotheses become cheap, experimental attention becomes scarce.

When content becomes cheap, trusted discovery becomes scarce.

When software components become cheap, architectural judgment and tool selection become scarce.

When products proliferate, matching becomes scarce.

Abundance raises the price of adjacency.

This may be one of the defining economic inversions of the generative era.

The more things can be made, the more valuable it becomes to know which thing belongs near the current need.

That value will attract capital, optimization, gaming, regulation, standards, and conflict.

It will create companies whose primary asset is not the inventory but the map between inventory and intention.

Better research maps.

Better supplier maps.

Better labor maps.

Better personal preference maps.

Better capability maps for agents.

Some will be public goods. Some will be private infrastructure. Some will be subscription services. Some will be advertising markets. Some will become regulated because their rankings govern consequential access.

The category is broader than vector databases.

The database is plumbing.

The economic product is discovery.

And discovery is never only about what exists.

It is about what becomes visible soon enough to matter.

That is why the political economy of adjacency will turn on the shortlist.

Who gets in?

Why?

Who can pay to move?

Who can inspect the route?

Who can choose another map?

Who owns the user model that shapes the journey?

And when one system becomes so useful that nobody wants to leave, what keeps usefulness from becoming gatekeeping by default?

The promise of adjacency is a larger field of possible matches.

Its economic test is whether those possibilities remain open enough for buyers, suppliers, workers, creators, and competing intermediaries to challenge the map.
