# Adjacency

*How the Vector Space Is Revolutionizing Discovery*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

We used to think of discovery as a search problem.

You had a question. Somewhere there was an answer. The difficult part was describing the answer well enough to retrieve it.

That model built libraries, indexes, catalogs, search engines and, eventually, an internet organized around strings of words. It remains one of civilization's great achievements. A person can type a few characters into a box and summon documents written across decades, continents and disciplines.

But the box has always contained a hidden assumption: that the person searching can name what they need.

Often we cannot.

We recognize a song before we know its title. We know the feeling of the jacket we want but not the vocabulary of tailoring. A scientist knows a material behaves strangely before she knows which neighboring compounds deserve attention. A lawyer remembers the shape of a precedent without remembering the phrase that will retrieve it. A reader finishes a novel and wants another book that is not the same book, not the same plot, perhaps not even the same genre, but somehow lives in the same emotional country.

The old search engine asks for the right words.

A vector system asks what is near.

That sounds like a technical distinction. It is becoming a cultural one.

Machine-learning systems can represent a word, paragraph, image, song, protein, molecule, product, user, research paper or other object as a point in a high-dimensional space. The individual coordinates are usually not meaningful in the ordinary human sense. What matters is the pattern of distances and directions. Things the model has learned to regard as similar tend to occupy nearby regions. Things it regards as different tend to separate.

Once that representation exists, a new operation becomes cheap enough to perform constantly: find the neighbors.

This is already changing search, recommendation, scientific literature review, software retrieval, fraud detection, image discovery, molecular screening, machine memory and the way generative AI systems decide which facts to place in context. It is also changing something less visible. It is changing what counts as a plausible next thing to look at.

That is why this book is called *Adjacency*.

The important object is not the vector. The important object is the neighborhood the vector creates.

A vector space is a learned map of relations. It can reveal affinities that no cataloger explicitly wrote down. It can connect a sentence to a photograph, a protein sequence to a structural pattern, a research paper to another paper that shares an intellectual shape without sharing its keywords. It can rescue us from the tyranny of exact phrasing.

It can also make its own mistakes look geometric.

The map is learned from data. The notion of similarity is learned or chosen. The distance metric is chosen. The search algorithm may be approximate. The nearest result may be merely familiar, statistically common, socially biased, commercially convenient or close for reasons that matter to the model and not to you.

Near is not the same as true.

Near is not the same as good.

Near is not the same as causal, fair, safe, original or worth discovering.

The power of vector systems comes from compressing enormous amounts of relational information into a form that can be navigated. Their danger comes from forgetting that compression is an editorial act.

This book is therefore not a celebration of vector databases, nor a warning against them. It is an attempt to understand the new discovery regime they help make possible.

The first half follows the technical and historical shift from words as symbols to words as positions, then outward into semantic search, recommendation, multimodal systems, scientific literature and molecular space. The second half asks harder questions. Who decides what counts as adjacent? What happens when biased history becomes geometry? What is lost when rich objects are compressed into points? What does it mean when a language model retrieves its memories by proximity? How do we preserve serendipity when the machinery of discovery becomes very good at giving us more of what is already near?

There is a larger claim underneath all of this.

Discovery has always depended on adjacency.

A harbor creates adjacent trades. A university creates adjacent disciplines. A city street puts strangers, shops, arguments and accidents within reach. A bookshelf places one author beside another. A laboratory bench puts a failed sample beside the instrument that can explain it. Human knowledge grows partly because ideas that were separated become neighbors in someone's attention.

What has changed is that adjacency can now be manufactured at enormous scale.

We can learn a space, place millions or billions of things inside it, and ask for the next plausible neighbor in milliseconds. We can do this repeatedly, across modalities, and increasingly inside systems that do not merely retrieve but reason, write, recommend and act.

The map is no longer only a map we consult.

It is becoming part of the machinery that chooses where we go next.

The question is not whether this will revolutionize discovery. It already is.

The question is what kind of discoverers we become when the world starts arriving as a neighborhood.
