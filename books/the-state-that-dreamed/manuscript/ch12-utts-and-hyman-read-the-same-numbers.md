# 12. Utts and Hyman Read the Same Numbers

The most valuable thing in the 1995 STAR GATE review is not the verdict. It is the disagreement.

Jessica Utts and Ray Hyman were asked to examine research that had survived years of argument. Utts concluded that the statistical evidence supported the existence of anomalous cognition. Hyman concluded that the case remained unproven. They were not looking at two universes.

This is what extraordinary evidence actually looks like near the boundary: competent people can agree on much of the observed structure and disagree about how much causal weight it can bear.

Utts emphasized effect sizes, consistency and improved controls. Her argument was essentially cumulative. If experiments repeatedly produce above-chance outcomes under conditions designed to exclude ordinary sensory access, at some point chance and obvious artifact cease to be satisfactory explanations.

Hyman emphasized inferential debt. A sequence of studies performed within a specialized program can share unrecognized biases. Replication should be independent. Methodological history matters because each discovered flaw raises the prior probability of undiscovered ones. The absence of a plausible mechanism does not disprove an effect, but it raises the evidentiary price for concluding that the effect represents a new channel of information.

The clean way to state the dispute is not “Utts proved psi; Hyman denied the statistics.” Nor is it “Hyman debunked Utts.” The AIR report itself did not endorse the claim that a paranormal mechanism had been demonstrated, and its operational conclusion was negative.

There is a deep lesson here for frontier science. Statistical evidence does not come with an ontology attached. A low p-value says something about a specified null model. It does not tell you whether the alternative is telepathy, leakage, selection bias, equipment artifact, dependence between trials, unnoticed experimenter effects or a new physical interaction.

The more extraordinary the ontology, the more aggressively alternatives must be discriminated.

That does not license infinite skepticism. If critics can always invent an unspecified unknown flaw, no discovery is possible. The burden on skeptics is to turn doubts into tests. The burden on proponents is to let those tests threaten the effect.

Utts and Hyman therefore point toward the experiment STAR GATE never fully received: an adversarial collaboration where both sides agree beforehand on target generation, viewer selection, judging, effect metric, exclusions, stopping rule, replication threshold and what result would materially update their beliefs.

No historical archive can provide that retroactively. The old data remain old data.

But the disagreement can be inherited productively.

Instead of asking which reviewer was right in 1995, ask what design would make their disagreement expensive today. What outcome would force Utts's intellectual descendants to concede that the effect does not replicate under agreed controls? What outcome would force Hyman's intellectual descendants to concede that ordinary leakage and flexibility are no longer plausible?

That is how science progresses when argument becomes architecture.

The government paid for twenty years of sessions. It never quite paid for the experiment that could make both camps vulnerable at once.
