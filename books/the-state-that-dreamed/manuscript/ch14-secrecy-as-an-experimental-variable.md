# 14. Secrecy as an Experimental Variable

Classification protects targets and destroys denominators.

That is the central methodological problem with intelligence-funded anomalous research. A laboratory trial wants complete target information, trial order, null results, protocols, raw transcripts, scoring instructions and independent replication. Intelligence wants exactly the opposite when operational details are sensitive.

The resulting archive is scientifically deformed.

Some targets remain redacted. Some feedback is missing. Operational success may be described without enough detail for external scoring. Files may be released decades later without the context that once made them legible. Participants remember the extraordinary cases. Mundane taskings blur together.

Secrecy therefore acts as an **experimental variable** even when nobody intends deception. It changes which evidence survives, who can audit it, how quickly criticism arrives and whether failures are publishable.

This produces two symmetrical pathologies. Believers can point to gaps and suggest that the strongest results remain classified. Skeptics can point to gaps and treat all inaccessible context as disqualifying. Neither inference is compelled.

A missing file is a missing file.

There are also active incentives. Program managers need budgets. Intelligence consumers need useful information. security officers need to prevent leaks. Contractors need follow-on work. Skeptical reviewers need to demonstrate rigor. Congressional sponsors may become attached to a project. Journalists prefer the crane to the denominator. Former participants sell books decades later. None of these incentives proves dishonesty. All belong in the causal graph.

The strongest historical analysis therefore distinguishes **provenance** from **content**. Who created the document? For what audience? At what stage in a funding cycle? Was it an internal briefing, external evaluation, scientific paper or retrospective memoir? Did the author have access to ground truth? Was the claim contemporaneous or recalled years later?

A classified briefing saying “85 percent of missions produced accurate target information” is excellent evidence that the program represented itself that way. It is weaker evidence that 85 percent accuracy would survive independent rescoring.

Likewise, an intelligence report saying Soviet researchers claimed telepathic effects is evidence of the American assessment. It is not automatically evidence that the Soviet effect occurred.

Once this discipline is applied, the archive becomes less spectacular and more useful. We can reconstruct institutional belief without confusing it with physical truth.

The same method should govern modern UAP, anomalous health incidents, exotic propulsion claims and alleged special-access programs. Classification is often treated as a magic word that upgrades testimony. Scientifically, it mostly increases missingness.

Missingness can itself be modeled. Which categories of files are absent? Are failures disproportionately unclassified or successes? Are targets redacted independently of outcome? Do release decisions correlate with sensitivity rather than evidentiary strength? An archival science of secrecy would be more informative than endless argument over the most cinematic document.

Remote viewing is the perfect case because secrecy and subjectivity multiply. The phenomenon, if real, is subtle. The records, by design, are incomplete.

A weak signal passing through a classified archive can emerge looking stronger or weaker than it was.

The cure is not suspicion.

It is provenance.
