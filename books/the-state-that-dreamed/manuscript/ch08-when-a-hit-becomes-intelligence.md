# 8. When a Hit Becomes Intelligence

There is a word that appears too easily in this literature: hit.

A hit in experimental psychology might mean a subject selected the correct one of four targets. A hit in free-response remote viewing may mean judges ranked the intended target above decoys. A hit in intelligence means something else entirely: information sufficiently specific, timely and reliable to change what someone does.

Confuse those definitions and the entire program can look better or worse than it was.

The 1995 AIR evaluation is valuable because it examined operational customers rather than only researchers. The conclusion was severe. Remote-viewing reports sometimes appeared accurate about broad background characteristics, but concrete details were inconsistent, subjective interpretation was substantial, and the information had not been used to guide intelligence operations. AIR therefore recommended against continued operational use.

That finding should carry more weight than any retrospective claim that the unit was “85 percent accurate” unless the latter can be reconstructed with a scoring system that means the same thing an intelligence consumer means by accuracy.

Suppose a report on a naval target says “water, metal, enclosed spaces, machinery, movement, long narrow form.” Every submarine target can reward that language. The report may be statistically better than random descriptions and still add almost no intelligence. Base rates devour drama.

Conversely, a single bizarre detail may matter enormously if it is prospective and diagnostic. “A large gantry crane above a building” is stronger than “industrial.” “The submarine has two pressure hulls side by side” would be stronger still, if stated before imagery and unavailable by ordinary means. Intelligence value is concentrated in low-base-rate specifics.

This suggests a metric the program rarely had in clean form: **surprisal-weighted utility**. Score a statement by how unlikely it was under the consumer's prior model, how precisely it maps to the target, whether it was recorded before feedback, and whether it changed a collection or analytic decision. Then charge the method for false specifics.

A capability with ten vague truths and one costly false claim may be worse than silence.

This is also where hindsight contaminates the archive. Once the target is known, language stretches. “Curved silver shape” finds the antenna. “Open area” finds the runway. “Underground feeling” finds the bunker. Unless alternative targets are judged blind, the match is not a measurement; it is a story.

The operational failure does not answer the pure science question. That bears repeating because skeptics sometimes overreach here. A phenomenon could exist at weak effect size and still be useless for espionage. The AIR closure is therefore not a proof that anomalous cognition is impossible. It is strong evidence against the proposition the government actually needed: that the program, as constituted, produced reliable operational intelligence worth its institutional cost.

That distinction makes the history more intellectually expensive. We cannot use the government's funding as proof of psi, and we cannot use the government's closure as proof against every psi effect.

We are left with the laboratory.

And the laboratory has its own problem: somebody has to decide what counts as a resemblance.
