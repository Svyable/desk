# 2. The Cheap Miracle

Intelligence agencies live in a world where absurd ideas can have rational budgets.

Suppose an analyst assigns only a one-in-a-thousand chance that a claimed method can extract information from a denied Soviet installation without satellites, agents, aircraft, signals intercepts or physical access. One in a thousand sounds like dismissal until the payoff is intelligence that would otherwise cost billions, endanger pilots, burn agents, or remain inaccessible. If the experiment costs less than one reconnaissance platform, the expected-value arithmetic stops laughing before the scientists do.

This is not evidence that remote viewing works. It is a theory of why institutions test it.

The Cold War added a second term: adversary uncertainty. American reporting on Soviet and Eastern Bloc parapsychology created the possibility that Moscow was either pursuing a real capability, pursuing a useless one, or deliberately advertising one. Those three possibilities imply different actions, but none implies simple inattention. If the Soviets were wasting money, understanding the waste had intelligence value. If they were developing a capability, ignoring it was worse. If they were exaggerating it, the exaggeration itself was an influence operation worth mapping.

The result is a trap I call the **cheap miracle problem**. A claim can remain fundable long after it fails ordinary scientific standards because the sponsor's utility function is not a journal editor's. Intelligence research asks, among other things: can this help once? can it cue another sensor? can it find the right square mile? can it reveal what the adversary thinks? Science asks: is there a replicable effect under controls that excludes ordinary information pathways? A phenomenon can score differently on those ledgers.

The reverse is also true. A small, real statistical effect may be scientifically revolutionary and operationally useless. Imagine a remote-viewing protocol that improves forced-choice accuracy from 25 percent to 27 percent under laboratory conditions. If robust, physics and neuroscience would have a crisis. An intelligence officer might still prefer a satellite photograph.

This separation appears in the 1995 AIR evaluation. Its research review and operational review were distinct. Utts thought the experimental evidence had crossed a threshold. Hyman did not. The operational users, however, supplied a more practical complaint: the product was vague, inconsistent and required interpretation. Whatever the laboratory anomaly was, it had not become a dependable collection discipline.

The history becomes less mysterious once we stop assuming that twenty years of funding equals twenty years of belief. Programs continue because champions exist, because constituencies form, because sunk costs are real, because Congress intervenes, because an occasional result is dazzling, because the downside of stopping may be framed as strategic surprise, and because a small program can hide inside the rounding error of a defense budget.

This explanation is less flattering to both camps. Believers lose the argument that longevity proves capability. Skeptics lose the argument that longevity proves universal stupidity. Bureaucracies can behave rationally under uncertainty and still spend years studying something that ultimately fails.

The deeper issue is that low-probability, high-impact research is structurally difficult to kill. Every failure can be explained by subject variability, bad target selection, environmental noise or an untrained viewer. Every success can reset the emotional clock. The program only becomes scientifically tractable when those escape routes are specified before the trial.

That sentence will matter again in the twenty-first century, when preregistration finally reaches psi.

But first the Cold War has to invent the threat.
