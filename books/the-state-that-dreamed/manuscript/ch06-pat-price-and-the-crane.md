# 6. Pat Price and the Crane

Every contested field has a jewel. Remote viewing has several, and one of the brightest is usually described as Pat Price's account of a Soviet site near Semipalatinsk.

In retellings, Price produced unexpectedly specific descriptions of an industrial or military installation, including a large crane-like structure. Sponsor personnel reportedly regarded portions as impressive. The case helped persuade people that something more than generic guessing might be occurring.

The public record supports a narrower statement. The episode existed inside the SRI-government program and was treated by participants as a notable operational success. The 1995 AIR report itself discussed famous cases, including Semipalatinsk, while emphasizing how difficult retrospective evaluation was. That is **Class 2 evidence** for an impressive session, not proof of paranormal access.

Why not proof? Because an operational case is not a laboratory trial with a complete information audit. AIR noted a core problem: one cannot know with certainty that the subject had not acquired relevant information through ordinary means, however unlikely that seemed. Nor can a later reviewer easily reconstruct how many statements were generated, how target feedback was handled, what analysts already knew, which details were emphasized, or how competing sessions performed.

This makes the case more interesting, not less. Intelligence work is full of partial provenance. Analysts often use sources they cannot independently reproduce. A human source may have been right five times and wrong twice. A satellite image may be ambiguous. Signals may be deceptive. The question becomes whether a source's output changes posterior belief enough to justify another collection action.

Remote viewing could therefore have been useful as a *cueing source* even if too noisy for direct action. But that claim has to be demonstrated prospectively. Did viewer reports point collection assets toward information they would otherwise have missed? How often? Against what baseline? What was the cost of false cues? AIR's operational review found no convincing record that the information had actually guided operations.

The crane case exposes a recurring asymmetry. A startling hit is cognitively sticky. A vague failure is not. If twenty sessions are mediocre and one draws a crane where a crane exists, the one enters history. Without the denominator, the story cannot tell us its probability.

There is also the specificity problem. A good evaluation should score features according to how diagnostic they are. “Large structure” is cheap. “Tall gantry crane spanning two parallel rails beside a long assembly building” is expensive. But the price must be assigned before the target is revealed, otherwise specificity itself becomes negotiable.

A modern reconstruction of Semipalatinsk would need the original unedited session material, exact tasking, all contemporaneous target knowledge available to subjects and staff, complete analyst feedback, alternative-target scoring, and ideally comparison with unsuccessful operational sessions from the same period. Much of that is difficult or impossible decades later.

So the jewel stays in the case, but not in the crown.

Pat Price and the crane are evidence that the program generated moments serious officials found difficult to dismiss. That fact helps explain persistence. It does not settle the mechanism.

A state can keep paying for a mystery long before the mystery has earned the right to call itself physics.
