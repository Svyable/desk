# 11. SAIC Tightens the Room

The fairest way to evaluate a controversial field is to follow it after the critics teach it how to fail.

By the later phase of government-sponsored anomalous-cognition research, work associated with Science Applications International Corporation incorporated tighter procedures than the early SRI experiments. The point is important because one cannot honestly use a defect from 1974 as a universal solvent for data collected under changed protocols decades later.

Jessica Utts's 1995 review emphasized this later work. She argued that the controlled experiments produced consistent statistical departures from chance and that methodological explanations had become increasingly implausible. In her reading, the existence of some anomalous information-transfer effect was established even if mechanism and operational utility remained uncertain.

That is a strong claim from a professional statistician reviewing government data. It belongs in **Class 2**, not because Utts was unserious, but because the broader scientific community did not converge on her inference and independent replication remained contested.

Ray Hyman's response is often misdescribed as “the skeptic said no.” His argument was more specific. Better experiments can still belong to a research program whose independence is limited. Statistical significance can be real while the causal interpretation remains wrong. A field with a long history of methodological surprises should demand replication by independent investigators who are capable of reproducing the protocol without inheriting hidden assumptions.

This debate anticipates the replication crisis that would later engulf ordinary psychology. For years, respectable findings with p-values below .05 collapsed under larger samples, preregistration and multi-lab replication. Researchers learned that publication bias, optional stopping, flexible exclusions and analytic degrees of freedom can create literatures that look statistically persuasive without any fraud.

That historical turn cuts both ways. It makes Hyman's caution look less like prejudice against the paranormal. It also means psi should not be singled out as uniquely incompetent; mainstream science discovered analogous structural weaknesses in far less exotic domains.

SAIC therefore deserves a strange kind of respect. The cleaner the experiment, the more informative the null or anomaly becomes. If a carefully controlled effect persists, critics must identify a plausible remaining pathway rather than gesture at the word “methodology.” If it disappears, proponents must retire hypotheses rather than moving the goalposts.

What the later government program lacked was the architecture that modern open science now makes possible: preregistration with adversarial sign-off, born-open data, immutable time-stamped protocols, external audit during collection, multi-lab execution and explicit stopping rules.

Those tools do not guarantee truth. They make certain kinds of retrospective rescue harder.

The tragedy of STAR GATE is chronological. It ended just before psychology developed some of its best machinery for settling precisely this kind of dispute.

The room had become tighter.

It had not yet become transparent.
