# 7. Fort Meade Learns to Task a Mind

A laboratory asks whether an effect exists. An intelligence unit asks whether it arrives by Tuesday.

When remote viewing moved into an operational military setting at Fort Meade, that difference became structural. Targets were not selected because they made clean experiments. They were selected because agencies wanted answers: locations, facilities, equipment, people, events. Feedback might be delayed or nonexistent. Ground truth could itself be classified or uncertain. The viewer could not be offered four tidy photographs and asked which one matched.

This is where a scientifically weak capability can sometimes appear operationally attractive and a scientifically real capability can become useless.

Imagine a viewer reliably producing one correct descriptor among ten. In a forced-choice laboratory task that might generate a detectable effect over hundreds of trials. In a hostage search it is a catastrophe. Nine false descriptors consume analyst attention, aircraft time, diplomatic energy or worse. Intelligence utility depends not merely on accuracy but on calibration, specificity, timeliness and decision cost.

Fort Meade's history is therefore best read as a stress test of translation. The government did not merely ask people to guess hidden photographs. It tried to operationalize the claimed phenomenon. Procedures were trained and named. Viewers were tasked by multiple agencies. Reports were summarized and passed to consumers.

The archive contains claims of useful missions and high accuracy rates in internal briefings. Those figures are historically important because they reveal how the unit represented itself. They cannot simply be accepted as performance statistics without reconstruction of scoring rules, mission selection and denominator. “Accurate target information” can mean a report contained some correct material. “Usable intelligence” can mean something less than independently actionable collection. Definitions are weapons in bureaucratic survival.

That does not mean the people involved were lying. A small unit performing unusual work will naturally develop local expertise and local confidence. Analysts who receive a surprising correspondence remember it. Viewers who are repeatedly asked back infer that someone values the product. Sponsors who have invested political capital search for signal. All ordinary human dynamics; no conspiracy required.

The hardest operational control is counterfactual. What would the consumer have believed without the remote-viewing report? If the report said a missing person was “near water and buildings,” did that materially narrow the search? If imagery later showed industrial structures, did the viewing predict a feature analysts did not already suspect? If an analyst says after the fact that a report was “interesting,” what decision changed?

AIR eventually asked versions of these questions by interviewing users. The answer was not flattering. The information could contain broad correspondences but lacked consistent concrete specifics and demanded subjective interpretation.

That operational record is stronger evidence than the program's existence and weaker evidence than a randomized field trial. Intelligence collection almost never permits randomized field trials. This is one reason secrecy and science make awkward roommates.

Fort Meade did, however, teach the program something crucial: if a phenomenon cannot preserve its usefulness when feedback disappears, targets become messy, and judging stops being a laboratory convenience, the road from anomaly to capability is much longer than a significant p-value.

The mind, if it was being tasked, refused to behave like an obedient sensor.
