# 9. The Problem of Judging

The judge is the hidden instrument in free-response anomalous cognition.

Physics students learn to worry about photomultiplier calibration, detector dead time, thermal drift and electronic noise. Remote-viewing experiments often use a more complicated sensor: a human comparing ambiguous language with ambiguous imagery.

If that sounds unfair to the field, consider how much turns on the operation. A subject's transcript may be fixed before target revelation. Good. But the statistical result can still depend on how a judge maps that transcript to targets. If the scoring process contains flexibility, expectation enters after the supposedly paranormal event.

The cleanest design constrains this flexibility. Give judges a fixed set of possible targets. Blind them to which is correct. Ask them to rank all candidates. Standardize transcript presentation. Prevent chronological and procedural cues. Decide the statistic before data collection. Preserve every trial.

Even then, target pools matter. Some images are intrinsically distinctive; others share common features. Water, trees, roads, buildings and vertical structures are common. If a pool accidentally pairs a transcript containing “water” with one watery target and three deserts, the trial becomes easy for reasons unrelated to the subject's information content. Target sets need balancing.

There is an analogy to machine learning. If a classifier performs beautifully on a test set whose labels leak through filenames, nobody claims the network discovered deep structure. The model exploited a shortcut. Human judges can exploit shortcuts unconsciously.

One of the strongest arguments in favor of later remote-viewing work is that researchers knew this and tightened procedures. SAIC-era experiments used improved randomization and controls. Jessica Utts emphasized that the later studies were methodologically stronger and that statistical effects persisted. Ray Hyman agreed that the later work was cleaner but argued that replication outside the core research network and unresolved methodological issues still prevented the extraordinary conclusion.

The dispute often gets caricatured as statistician versus skeptic. It was more useful than that. Utts asked whether repeated controlled results were too consistent to dismiss as chance. Hyman asked whether the research program had met the evidentiary burden for a new human information channel given independence, methodological history and lack of theoretical mechanism.

Both questions are legitimate. The mistake is pretending one automatically answers the other.

A modern version should go further. Replace after-the-fact human similarity judgments where possible with predeclared feature vectors. Build target libraries with quantified visual and semantic distances. Use independent scoring teams. Publish encrypted trial commitments before sessions. Time-stamp all randomization. Let proponents choose the descriptors they think best capture the phenomenon, then freeze them. Let skeptics specify leakage tests, then freeze those too.

The purpose is not to remove humans. The claimed phenomenon is human. The purpose is to remove the judge's ability to rescue a result after seeing what happened.

Every field has a place where discretion hides. In remote viewing, it often sat at the scoring table.
