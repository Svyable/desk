# 15. The Replication Revolution Arrives Late

Psychology eventually discovered that ordinary humans could produce extraordinary literatures without extraordinary phenomena.

Not by fraud, usually. By flexibility.

Researchers ran small studies, tested several outcomes, stopped when results looked persuasive, published successes more readily than failures, changed exclusions after seeing messy data and treated p < .05 as a discovery machine. The replication crisis did not prove psychology false. It exposed how incentives and analytic freedom could manufacture confidence.

Psi research had lived inside that problem for decades before the rest of psychology named it.

This historical irony matters. Critics of parapsychology often demanded standards mainstream psychology itself did not routinely meet. Proponents could fairly ask why their field was singled out. Then the scientific reform movement supplied a better answer: do not lower the standard for mainstream psychology; raise it everywhere.

Preregistration freezes hypotheses and analysis before outcomes are known. Registered reports move publication decisions upstream of results. Multi-lab replication tests portability. Open data permits reanalysis. Adversarial collaboration makes opponents share responsibility for the protocol. Independent audit catches deviations while the evidence is still alive.

These tools are almost perversely well suited to psi.

If an effect is real but small, preregistration protects it from endless accusations of p-hacking. If an effect is artifactual, preregistration removes some escape routes. If proponents claim only gifted participants show the effect, define the selection rule in advance. If skeptics worry about sensory leakage, define leakage audits in advance. If target pools matter, freeze them. If feedback matters, specify it. If optional stopping matters, use a predetermined sequential rule.

The crucial cultural change is that a null becomes valuable.

Old anomalous literatures often behave as if every failed replication is merely another mystery. A mature field must specify defeat conditions. Not metaphysical defeat—no finite series can prove no human anywhere has ever experienced anomalous cognition—but defeat of a particular operational claim under a particular protocol.

This is exactly what the Transparent Psi Project attempted for a famous modern precognition effect associated with Daryl Bem. Proponents and skeptics collaborated on design and analysis. The study was preregistered, data were made openly available, and the target effect did not replicate.

The result is not a remote-viewing experiment. It should not be smuggled across phenomena as if all psi claims were one variable. But it is directly relevant to epistemology. It demonstrates that adversarial design is possible in a field notorious for mutually incompatible standards of proof.

Later work extended the meta-experimental approach on a much larger scale and again did not establish the claimed precognitive effect.

This is what STAR GATE missed by chronology. Its key debates occurred before registered reports, cryptographic commitments, born-open data pipelines and large online sampling became ordinary tools.

The old archive cannot be upgraded.

The claim can be retested.

If remote viewing deserves another serious scientific hearing, it should receive the most dangerous experiment its proponents are willing to sign before the target is chosen.
