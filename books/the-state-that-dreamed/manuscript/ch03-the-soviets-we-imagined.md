# 3. The Soviets We Imagined

There are at least three Soviet psychic programs in the American record.

The first is the Soviet research that actually existed: laboratories, papers, investigators, and state interest in parapsychology, psychophysiology and what Western reports often grouped under psychotronics. The second is the Soviet program reconstructed by U.S. intelligence from open literature, human reporting and threat analysis. The third is the program produced in American imagination when those fragments met worst-case planning.

Only the first can tell us what Soviet researchers truly demonstrated. Much of the surviving U.S. documentation primarily tells us what American officials thought they might be demonstrating.

A 1972 Defense Intelligence Agency report titled *Controlled Offensive Behavior—USSR* surveyed Soviet and Eastern Bloc work in areas including telepathy and psychokinesis. It is a genuine declassified intelligence document. That gives us a hard historical fact: U.S. defense intelligence considered the field strategically relevant enough to assess. It does not convert every reported Soviet claim inside the assessment into a verified result.

The distinction matters because intelligence analysis routinely reasons under incomplete source access. A published Soviet paper could be serious science, prestige theater, ideological fashion, bureaucratic entrepreneurship or a deliberate signal to foreigners. Translating a research vocabulary across systems creates additional distortion. Words such as “bioinformation,” “psychotronics,” “distant influence,” and “biophysical fields” accumulated meanings that were not always stable even within their home institutions.

American analysts faced a familiar strategic dilemma. If a hostile state appears to invest in a technology with potentially transformative military applications, should you wait for scientific consensus before investigating? Nuclear weapons made that posture psychologically impossible. So did radar, rocketry, cryptography and stealth. The history of technological surprise teaches intelligence services to fear the dismissed eccentric almost as much as the obvious engineer.

That fear can become self-exciting. Country A hears that Country B funds psi. A starts a small program to avoid surprise. B detects A's interest and cites it internally as evidence that A takes the field seriously. Each side's precaution becomes part of the other's threat estimate. The loop requires no paranormal effect at all.

This is **speculative synthesis**, not a documented explanation of every Soviet and American budget decision. But reciprocal threat inflation is common enough in intelligence history that it deserves testing against the archive. The relevant evidence would include original Soviet funding records, internal evaluations, termination decisions, translations of technical reports, and U.S. source-quality annotations. Publicly available material is uneven.

The temptation here is to substitute mystery for missing records. If Soviet archives are incomplete, one can imagine a successful program hiding behind them. That is not evidence. Closed archives widen uncertainty; they do not preferentially widen it toward the extraordinary.

Still, the Soviet story is indispensable because it changes the origin myth. STAR GATE did not arise solely from American fascination with gifted subjects. It grew in a strategic environment where another government's alleged interest altered the cost of disbelief.

The useful question is therefore not, “Did the Soviets have psychic weapons?” The public record does not establish that. The better question is, “How much research can an intelligence service rationally buy when the price of being surprised is unknowable?”

Once phrased that way, remote viewing stops being an isolated curiosity. It becomes a case study in how states investigate the edge of plausibility.
