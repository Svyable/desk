# 16. The Transparent Psi Test

The Transparent Psi Project is not famous enough.

It should be taught in research-methods courses even if one regards precognition as nonsense.

A large group of researchers—including people sympathetic to psi claims and prominent skeptics—negotiated a protocol to replicate one of Daryl Bem's controversial precognition experiments. The design and analysis were specified in advance. Data handling was unusually transparent. External oversight was built in. More than two thousand participants completed tens of thousands of critical trials.

The predicted precognition effect was not supported.

The number that matters is almost comically ordinary: performance was essentially at chance. The future declined to leak backward in the agreed experiment.

That sentence should not be weaponized beyond its target. Bem-style precognition is not remote viewing. A failed replication of one paradigm does not prove that all anomalous cognition claims are false. But the methodological lesson travels perfectly.

The project converted a philosophical feud into a shared risk. Proponents could not say afterward that skeptics had designed a deliberately insensitive test because proponents helped design it. Skeptics could not say afterward that any positive result must reflect the usual analytic flexibility because the flexibility had been constrained before collection.

An independent audit later identified protocol deviations, and the project published a correction. That detail makes the work stronger as a model, not weaker. Transparency does not mean no mistakes occur. It means mistakes can become part of the public record without requiring the collapse of the project into tribal warfare.

A 2025 PLOS ONE follow-up applied an advanced meta-experimental protocol across a much larger participant pool—26,483 participants and more than 420,000 critical trials according to the published report—and did not establish the claimed precognitive effect. The scale matters because small effects and researcher flexibility have historically been difficult to disentangle.

Remote viewing now deserves this treatment directly.

Build a target universe large enough to resist guessing strategies. Quantify semantic similarity before sessions. Use hardware randomization whose seed commitments are published cryptographically. Separate viewer, monitor, target server, judge and analyst. Have multiple independent laboratories execute identical code. Let viewers selected by proponents compete against unselected controls. Include a forced-choice endpoint alongside free response. Keep all raw audio, sketches and metadata. Reveal targets only after immutable session hashes exist.

Most importantly, negotiate the outcome thresholds in advance.

What result would proponents call a failure? What result would skeptics call evidence that deserves independent theoretical attention? How many replications? What Bayes factor? What effect size? What leakage audit?

If nobody can answer those questions before the data, the dispute is not ready for resolution.

The Transparent Psi Project shows that adversaries can share an experiment without sharing a worldview.

That may be more important than the null.

It is a technology for disagreeing honestly.
