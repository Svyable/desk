# 4. Two Physicists at SRI

Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff did something more consequential than persuade intelligence officers to watch psychics. They tried to turn an occult claim into an engineering problem.

At Stanford Research Institute in the early 1970s, the language became targets, protocols, sensory shielding, judging and information transfer. The shift mattered. Clairvoyance had cultural baggage. “Remote viewing” sounded almost offensively procedural.

Their 1974 *Nature* paper, “Information transmission under conditions of sensory shielding,” reported experiments involving subjects including Uri Geller and Pat Price. Publication in *Nature* did not certify the phenomenon; journals publish results that later fail. But it placed the work in a scientific arena where methods could be attacked rather than merely believed.

And attacked they were.

The fundamental challenge in free-response psi research is that a session does not naturally output a binary answer. A viewer says water, vertical lines, metallic structure, open land, cold air, motion, a feeling of depth. The target is a bridge. Is that a hit? How many unused descriptors were spoken? How distinctive were they? Could the judge infer the session order from the transcript? Did feedback from an earlier trial contaminate a later one? Was the target pool small enough for elimination strategies? The poetry of the response becomes a statistics problem.

This was where the SRI work was simultaneously most interesting and most vulnerable. Some results looked striking. But later critics, notably David Marks and Richard Kammann, identified cues in transcripts that could help judges match sessions to targets. A remote-viewing experiment can therefore appear sensory-shielded at the moment of viewing while leaking information during scoring.

That is an important general lesson. Experimental blindness is not a room; it is an information graph. Every edge matters: experimenter to subject, target selector to monitor, transcript to judge, previous target to later narrative, feedback to future trial. Block the obvious channel and the information may walk around the building.

The best response to such criticism is not to insist that the subjects were gifted. It is to redesign the experiment so the cue cannot exist. New target sets. Standardized transcripts. Independent randomization. Locked analysis. Judges who cannot infer order. Automation where possible. External replication by teams without personal investment in the original result.

SRI did improve procedures over time, and later SAIC work was designed with several earlier criticisms in mind. This is why the final history cannot fairly be reduced to the earliest cueing problem. But the early episode established a pattern that would recur: each methodological repair made the surviving anomaly more interesting while also shrinking the space in which a dramatic claim could live.

The physics question remained almost embarrassingly large. If information truly crossed distance without a known sensory channel, what exactly was propagating? Targ and Puthoff sometimes used physical language, but no accepted carrier or mechanism emerged. Quantum mechanics would later become a favorite rhetorical refuge for nearly every nonlocal claim, mostly because “nonlocal” sounds like permission. It is not. Quantum nonlocality does not provide a general-purpose signaling channel for classical information.

A real remote-viewing effect would require theory eventually. But mechanism is not logically required before detection. Lightning existed before Maxwell. The first burden is simpler and harsher: make the anomaly survive an experiment where nobody can explain it away by ordinary information flow.

SRI's enduring contribution may be that it made that burden visible.
