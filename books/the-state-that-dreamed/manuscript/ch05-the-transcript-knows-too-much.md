# 5. The Transcript Knows Too Much

The remote-viewing transcript is a dangerous object because it looks like raw data and often is not.

A subject speaks for twenty minutes. The monitor asks questions. Sketches accumulate. Later someone compares the material with a target. A good phrase becomes famous. A bad paragraph disappears into the page count. Decades later the famous phrase survives in a book, stripped of the target pool, judge instructions, session chronology and all the misses surrounding it.

That is how anecdotes acquire muscles.

Marks and Kammann's critique of early SRI work remains important because it attacked the scoring channel rather than the metaphysics. They argued that information embedded in transcripts—references that could reveal session order, for example—might permit judges to match transcripts with targets without paranormal perception. If true, the subject did not need to know the target; the *judge* only needed enough mundane structure to reconstruct which transcript belonged where.

This distinction is devastating to casual accounts because the spectacular correspondence may remain visually real. The problem is how it was selected.

Consider a hypothetical set of five targets: a church, a swimming pool, a bridge, a factory and a forest overlook. A transcript mentions “after yesterday's water place” and describes “vertical hard lines.” Even if the second phrase is vague, the first can identify which target is unavailable. Across a sequence, tiny bookkeeping clues can collapse uncertainty. The eventual match feels psychic because the cue is nowhere near the claimed mechanism.

This is why modern experimental design treats metadata as data. File names, timestamps, ordering, formatting, trial counts and missing entries can all leak target identity. Cybersecurity people understand this instinctively: encrypted content can coexist with revealing traffic patterns. Psi experiments needed the same paranoia.

The strongest version of the proponent's counterargument is also worth preserving. Finding cues in one scoring procedure does not prove that every later positive result came from cues. Later experiments altered protocols. Some studies used methods less vulnerable to transcript-order matching. If an effect persists after a defect is removed, the original defect cannot explain the entire later dataset.

Fine. Then the later dataset must carry the case.

This is the habit the field needed earlier: retire compromised evidence rather than defend it forever. Science does not gain strength by keeping every positive result in the family album. It gains strength by allowing the old evidence to die when the control improves.

There is a second transcript problem, harder than cueing: subjective compression. Free-response material is high-dimensional. Humans are excellent at finding correspondences after seeing the answer. A description of “something circular, metallic, moving, with people nearby” can fit a radar dish, Ferris wheel, storage tank, aircraft, industrial plant or stadium. The judge's mind performs lossy matching without reporting its internal degrees of freedom.

The cure is not to ban description. It is to make the judge's choices explicit before target revelation: rank all candidates; quantify descriptors; use independent judges; freeze scoring rules; preserve complete sessions; report misses with the same fidelity as hits.

A good anomalous experiment should be hostile to its own best story.

The transcript must be prevented from knowing anything the subject was supposed to discover.
