# 10. The National Academy Says No

In 1988 the National Research Council published *Enhancing Human Performance: Issues, Theories, and Techniques*. The Army had asked for a serious look at methods that might improve performance, including claims from the edges of behavioral science. Remote viewing did not emerge vindicated.

The review is sometimes treated by proponents as establishment dismissal and by skeptics as a tombstone. It is more useful as a snapshot of evidentiary standards before the final STAR GATE decade.

The committee found little or no support for the usefulness of remote viewing. That conclusion matters because it came after years of research and because it was not issued by a newspaper columnist making jokes about spoon bending. It came from a formal scientific review connected to the military customer.

But reviews inherit the evidence available to them. Later proponents argued that subsequent SAIC experiments were cleaner and therefore changed the record. If true, a 1988 negative review cannot pre-judge 1990s data. Science is allowed to update.

The better question is how much it updated.

This is where the field repeatedly collided with independence. A mature extraordinary claim should not depend heavily on one institutional lineage, one small network of investigators, or one family of protocols. Replication is not merely repeating the experiment. It is moving the experiment into hands that do not share the original laboratory's tacit knowledge, expectations, scoring habits and incentives.

That standard can be unfair to difficult frontier work. New instruments often require expert teams. Gravitational-wave detectors were not independently replicated by random psychology departments. But the analogy only helps if the phenomenon comes with engineering constraints that explain why expertise matters. If remote viewing depends on specialized viewers, specialized monitors, special target pools and specialized judges, each specialization creates another place where a fragile effect can hide.

The National Academy episode also reveals a political fact: government sponsorship did not insulate the work from government-sponsored criticism. The same broad defense establishment that funded experiments also commissioned skeptical evaluations. There was no single “government view.” There were agencies, program managers, scientists, customers, congressional sponsors and review panels with different priors.

That matters whenever people say “the CIA believed X.” Institutions do not believe in the way individuals do. A CIA office funded SRI. Other officials doubted it. CIA support ended, military programs continued, Congress later redirected the program back for review. The archival subject is plural.

This plurality weakens conspiracy stories and strengthens the case study. We can watch a contested claim move through different bureaucratic filters. Research offices ask whether there is an effect. Operators ask whether it works. Review boards ask whether the methods justify the conclusion. Budget authorities ask whether another year is cheap enough.

By 1988 one major scientific filter said no.

The program did not immediately die.

That gap—between negative scientific review and continued institutional life—is where ordinary explanations such as sponsor incentives, sunk costs, foreign-threat uncertainty and occasional striking results become more powerful than either “proof” or “fraud.”

The state kept dreaming because waking up was also a decision.
