*When AI Moves Faster Than Human Comprehension*

Artificial intelligence is usually described as a capability race: smarter models, longer tasks, more autonomy, more work completed per unit of time. *Breakneck* asks what happens when the limiting resource is no longer machine capability but human comprehension.

The book’s central claim is that a new kind of operational risk appears when AI can generate decisions, code changes, experiments, analyses, transactions, and follow-on actions faster than the people responsible for them can build an independent causal model of what is happening. The result is not merely information overload. It is **comprehension debt**: a growing inventory of consequential machine-produced changes that have been accepted, shipped, or acted upon before any human can explain them well enough to predict failure, challenge assumptions, or reconstruct the chain after something goes wrong.

This is deliberately narrower than *Pace of Change*, which argues for temporal fit across technology, institutions, skills, infrastructure, law, and social systems. *Breakneck* is about the cognitive bottleneck inside human-AI systems. It is also distinct from *Abdicating Intelligence*, which focuses on verification, skill formation, and the erosion of human judgment when machines perform the repetitions through which expertise is learned. Here the pressure is temporal: even capable experts can lose the thread when the system creates more state than they can understand before the next state arrives.

The strongest objection is important. Civilization already depends on systems no single person understands end to end. Pilots do not derive aerodynamics in the cockpit; surgeons do not audit semiconductor fabs before using imaging equipment; programmers rely on compilers, kernels, networks, and libraries they did not write. Total comprehension is neither possible nor necessary. The book therefore does not argue that humans must understand every internal step. It asks a more practical question: **what must remain comprehensible, to whom, and before which actions, for responsibility and recovery to remain real?**
