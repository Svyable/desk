# The Earth Was Not Warning Us

## Earthquake Precursors, Broken Predictions, and the Difference Between a Signal and a Forecast

### Sven Hardy Benson

> The embarrassing thing about an earthquake precursor is not that it might be false. It is that it can be physically real and still tell you almost nothing you needed to know.

## 1. The Signal Before the Signal

There is a particular cruelty in earthquake science. The thing everybody wants is the thing the Earth is least obliged to provide.

A fault can spend centuries loading and then move in seconds. We know a great deal about the tectonic machinery. We can map faults, measure plate motion, watch crustal strain with GPS, catalogue aftershock sequences, estimate long-run probabilities and, once rupture begins, race the seismic waves with electronic warnings. What we cannot presently do is point to Tuesday afternoon and say: there, within that patch of crust, before dinner.

That gap has attracted every kind of signal.

Radon in wells. Water levels. Strange animal behavior. Infrared anomalies. Changes in electrical resistivity. Ultra-low-frequency magnetic noise. Ionospheric total electron content. Lights in the sky. Clouds, sometimes, if one wanders far enough from the literature and toward the internet. The catalogue is easy to mock because the weak claims live beside serious experiments. Mockery is also a bad instrument.

The hard version of the problem begins by granting more than the skeptic usually wants to grant. Rock under stress can do electrical things. Fault rupture can alter magnetic fields. Fluids move. Gas moves. The ionosphere is coupled to the atmosphere below and the magnetosphere above. Luminous phenomena associated with earthquakes have been investigated by geophysicists for decades. A planet made of charged matter does not become electromagnetically inert merely because somebody on YouTube has abused the word frequency.

Then comes the unpleasant second half: none of that is a forecast.

A forecast requires a denominator. If a magnetometer twitches before an earthquake, how often does it twitch when no earthquake follows? How many earthquakes occur without the twitch? How large a region is “near”? How long is “before”? Was the frequency band chosen before the event or after somebody knew where to look? Did the sensor change gain? Was there a geomagnetic storm? A train? A pump? A broken cable? If a satellite sees an ionospheric departure, what was the ionosphere doing at the same local time, longitude and season during the thousand days when the crust did not produce the earthquake we remember?

The word *precursor* smuggles the future into the past. We see the earthquake first, then turn around and discover the omen.

This book is about the turning around.

## 2. Parkfield and the Seduction of the Clock

Parkfield, California, became famous because earthquakes there appeared, for a while, to behave almost politely. Similar moderate earthquakes had occurred on a section of the San Andreas Fault at intervals that encouraged the thought that the next one might be scheduled. The experiment that grew around Parkfield was not foolish. It was one of the cleanest ways to test an intoxicating proposition: if a fault segment had something like a recurrence rhythm, perhaps dense instruments could catch the preparation.

The expected earthquake did not arrive on schedule. When a magnitude 6 event finally occurred in 2004, it was years late relative to the celebrated prediction window.

That failure is more useful than the cheap version of the story, in which scientists thought earthquakes were clocks and learned that clocks are hard. Parkfield helped force a distinction that runs through everything in this book. A system can possess statistical regularity without possessing a short-term countdown. Recurrence can be informative at one timescale and useless at another.

The same error appears in precursor work. Suppose some measurable variable changes more often in the week before large earthquakes. That does not mean a particular change tells us a large earthquake is coming. The base rate matters. The background matters. The alarm volume matters. A test can discover a population-level association and still produce an unusable warning system.

Parkfield also gave earthquake science something precursor hunters often lack: the willingness to instrument the failure in advance. That is the gold standard. You want the sensors in the ground before the famous event. You want the calibration history. You want the quiet years. You want the earthquake that arrives late. You want nature to have the opportunity to humiliate the hypothesis without asking permission.

Prediction science improves when the experiment is designed to lose.

## 3. The Magnetometer at Corralitos

The 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake produced one of the most persistent electromagnetic precursor stories in modern geophysics. A magnetometer at Corralitos, only a few kilometers from the eventual epicenter, recorded unusual ultra-low-frequency magnetic noise before the earthquake. Fraser-Smith and colleagues reported the observations. The case became irresistible: a nearby instrument, an impending destructive earthquake, an anomaly in a frequency range that could be connected to processes in stressed crust.

This is the kind of case from which a field can accidentally build a religion.

Not because the researchers behaved religiously. Because the narrative has the correct bones. The instrument was already there. The signal was not a villager remembering that the chickens behaved strangely. The earthquake was real, large and nearby. The anomaly had a technical record. There were plausible questions about how stressed rock might generate electromagnetic phenomena.

The case therefore deserved exactly what it later received: hostile love.

A good reanalysis does not ask whether the original investigators were serious. It asks whether the instrument was healthy, whether similar baseline changes happened at other times, whether independent stations support the interpretation, and whether the anomaly remains anomalous when the full record is visible.

The difference is profound. “Something changed before Loma Prieta” is an event story. “This change was unusual relative to the instrument's own behavior and to appropriate controls, and it was generated by the earthquake preparation process” is a causal claim.

Those are not the same sentence.

## 4. Twenty-One Months

Thomas, Love and Johnston went back through the Corralitos record. Their 2009 paper examined twenty-one months of available data, the station logbook, magnetic data from elsewhere and geomagnetic activity. Their conclusion was brutal in the precise way science is supposed to be brutal: the reported anomalous magnetic noise was not related to Loma Prieta but was an artifact of sensor-system malfunction.

The important object here is not the verdict. It is the twenty-one months.

A precursor becomes impressive by compression. We put the anomaly and the earthquake on the same page. The horizontal distance between them becomes destiny. Expand the page to include months of baseline shifts, maintenance trouble and unrelated changes and the story can mutate.

This is a recurring law of anomaly research: **the longer record is often the strongest skeptical instrument.**

The lesson is not that magnetometers cannot observe earthquake-related fields. Coseismic electromagnetic effects are not in serious dispute in the same way alleged precursors are. Nor does one failed case prove that no pre-seismic electromagnetic phenomenon exists. It proves something narrower and more dangerous to storytelling: a canonical case can survive for years because the memorable interval is not the relevant denominator.

If we ever find a useful precursor, it will need to survive the boring months.

## 5. The Ionosphere Is Always Doing Something

The ionosphere is a terrible place to go looking for subtlety because it is magnificent at producing change.

Solar radiation creates it. Geomagnetic activity perturbs it. Atmospheric tides reach it. Seasons, latitude, local time and solar cycles matter. Waves propagate upward from below. Satellites sample it along moving tracks. GPS allows total electron content to be estimated across enormous areas, producing a seductive abundance of maps.

Then an earthquake happens.

Put a circle around the epicenter. Scroll backward. Find a patch.

This caricature is unfair to careful seismo-ionospheric research, but it describes the statistical danger. A highly variable field plus a memorable event plus many analytical degrees of freedom is a factory for anomalies. The proper response is not to declare the subject impossible. It is to make the null model earn its salary.

What does normal ionospheric variation look like at the same local time? At the same longitude? During similar geomagnetic conditions? What happens at locations far from the epicenter? What happens during matched windows when no earthquake follows? How many spatial radii, lead times and anomaly thresholds were examined?

The ionosphere may contain earthquake-related information. It also contains almost everything else.

## 6. DEMETER

France launched DEMETER in 2004. Its name expanded to Detection of Electro-Magnetic Emissions Transmitted from Earthquake Regions, which may be the most candid mission title in the history of controversial geophysics. Here was no embarrassed fringe project. A satellite went into low Earth orbit carrying instruments to investigate electromagnetic and ionospheric phenomena, with seismic associations among the explicit scientific interests.

DEMETER matters because it destroys a lazy social story. The subject was not simply “suppressed.” Serious institutions were willing to fly hardware.

The mission also demonstrates why hardware does not settle interpretation. A satellite orbit is a sampling scheme. It visits particular places at particular local times. Earthquakes are not scheduled for its convenience. The ionosphere has structure on scales larger than an epicentral circle. A statistical study must decide which earthquakes qualify, how deep they may be, how large, how close the satellite pass must come, what counts as background and how aftershocks are treated.

Published DEMETER analyses have reported statistical perturbations associated with seismic activity, including nighttime ion-density departures in defined windows. Those findings deserve to be read as findings, not preemptively translated into either “earthquake prediction works” or “all anomalies are nonsense.”

A population-level perturbation is interesting physics. Operational prediction is a different product.

## 7. Hector Mine

The 1999 Hector Mine earthquake generated another influential ionospheric precursor claim. Researchers reported anomalous GPS-derived total electron content before the event. The appeal was obvious. Unlike a lonely magnetometer, GPS networks could provide spatial context. The signal appeared in the sky above a region that would soon rupture.

Thomas and colleagues later reproduced the essential time-series behavior and still rejected the precursor interpretation.

That is an exquisite scientific result. Reproduction of the graph did not reproduce the meaning.

Their longer and broader comparison indicated that the supposed anomaly was part of normal global-scale TEC variation. The signal existed. The proposed cause did not survive.

This distinction should be tattooed onto anomaly science. Skepticism is often imagined as an accusation that somebody fabricated a bump. More often the bump is perfectly real. The fight is over what population the bump belongs to.

Earthquake? Solar forcing? Geomagnetic conditions? Ordinary ionospheric weather?

A real signal can have the wrong parent.

## 8. The Pulse Counter

The QuakeFinder case near Alum Rock is even more instructive because it puts the algorithm itself on trial.

Researchers had reported increases in ultra-low-frequency electromagnetic pulse counts before earthquakes. Wang and colleagues re-examined data from the QuakeFinder site and a Stanford-USGS comparison station. They could corroborate an increase in pulse counts before the 2007 magnitude 5.4 Alum Rock earthquake under analogous counting approaches. That sounds, for one sentence, like vindication.

Then the floor moves.

The number of pulses depended on temporal and amplitude thresholds without a clear physical basis for selecting those thresholds. The analogous increase did not appear before the 2010 magnitude 4.0 Alum Rock earthquake. Many pulses at the stations did not match in time, weakening explanations involving broad common sources. Most damagingly, pulse counts rose during peak human-activity hours, pointing toward local cultural noise.

A precursor algorithm can be a machine for measuring civilization.

That does not make the exercise worthless. It makes it exemplary. The investigation moved from “there are pulses” to “what generates the pulses?” It treated threshold choice as part of the hypothesis. It used a comparison station. It allowed a second earthquake not to cooperate.

The Earth was not warning us. The neighborhood may have been going to work.

## 9. Earthquake Lights

Earthquake lights are where respectable geophysics brushes against folklore and both parties become nervous.

Reports of luminous phenomena around earthquakes are old. The category includes glows, flashes and other transient lights, not all of which share a cause. USGS scientists have published on the subject for decades. Laboratory and theoretical mechanisms have been proposed. In 2025, USGS seismologist Susan Hough even revisited the “ghost light” lore around Summerville, South Carolina, suggesting that at least some observations could plausibly be natural phenomena including earthquake lights associated with small local earthquakes.

This is delightful because it reverses the usual debunking story. Sometimes the ghost is the geophysics.

But lights near seismic activity do not automatically become short-term precursors. Timing matters with merciless precision. A light during shaking can be an earthquake-associated phenomenon without telling anybody beforehand. A light minutes or days earlier makes a stronger claim and demands stronger chain-of-observation evidence.

The right book does not ask whether earthquake lights are “real” as a single binary category. It asks which observations, under what conditions, with what timing, and with what plausible mechanisms.

The category may be real and heterogeneous at the same time.

## 10. Rocks That Carry Charge

One proposed mechanism involves stress-activated charge carriers in rock. Freund and collaborators have argued that mechanical stress in certain rocks can activate electronic charge carriers—often described as positive holes—that migrate and may contribute to electrical, electromagnetic or luminous phenomena. Other mechanisms in the broader literature include electrokinetic effects, piezomagnetism, streaming potentials and processes associated with fracture.

The temptation is to leap from laboratory effect to impending earthquake.

Do not leap.

A mechanism has several lives to survive. Can it occur in relevant crustal materials? At relevant pressures, temperatures and fluid conditions? Over relevant volumes? Can the generated signal propagate through conductive crust? Can it reach a sensor above environmental noise? Does it scale with the part of the fault preparing to rupture? Does it happen reliably enough before large earthquakes? Does it happen without earthquakes? Can we distinguish it from anthropogenic and magnetospheric sources?

Mechanistic plausibility is precious. It narrows the space of magic. It does not issue an evacuation order.

The most productive attitude is almost greedy: keep the mechanism, demand the forecast.

## 11. Radon, Water, and the Chemical Underground

Electromagnetism gets the glamour, but the crust also leaks, breathes and reroutes fluids.

Radon concentration, groundwater chemistry, spring discharge and well levels have all been studied for possible earthquake-related changes. There are sensible reasons to look. Stress and cracking can alter permeability. Fluids respond to pressure. Gas transport can change. Earth tides, rainfall, pumping and barometric pressure also change the same systems.

The groundwater well is another ionosphere: a real physical environment with too many parents.

A useful anomaly must therefore survive environmental accounting. Rain cannot be treated as an afterthought. Pump schedules matter. Instrument drift matters. Seasonal baselines matter. Regional hydrogeology matters. The earthquake catalogue must be defined before the anomaly window is inspected.

The strongest version of this research is not a dramatic single well before a famous earthquake. It is a network that records for years, catches many earthquakes, catches many non-earthquakes, and publishes the failures.

A warning system is built from false alarms as much as hits.

## 12. Animals Leave the Laboratory

Animals appear in earthquake stories because animals possess sensory systems humans do not share at the same thresholds. They detect vibrations, sounds, odors, pressure changes and fields differently. It is not absurd that an animal could respond to some physical change we miss.

It is also almost impossible to interrogate an anecdote after the earthquake.

The dog was restless. The snakes emerged. The birds left. How restless is normal? How many dogs? Compared with which night? Who noticed before the earthquake rather than remembering afterward? Was there weather? Construction? A predator? A person behaving strangely because smaller foreshocks had already been felt?

Modern automated tracking offers a way out. Instrument the animals continuously. Specify the behavior before the earthquake. Keep the quiet months. Compare locations. Publish the nulls.

The animal should become a sensor only after we agree to treat it as one.

## 13. The Problem With “Before”

“Before an earthquake” sounds specific until one tries to code it.

One second before? One hour? Five days? Thirty? Within ten kilometers? Two hundred? Directly above the epicenter? Along the fault? At the magnetic conjugate point? For magnitude 4? Magnitude 6? Shallow only?

Every extra choice is a place for a coincidence to hide.

If a researcher inspects ten lead windows, five radii, four frequency bands and several anomaly thresholds, the intuitive meaning of a striking result changes. This does not mean every exploratory analysis is invalid. Exploration is how one finds hypotheses. The sin is forgetting which stage one is in.

The cure is brutally simple and culturally difficult: freeze the rule, then wait.

Prediction research should feel boring before it feels prophetic.

## 14. The Denominator Nobody Photographs

A successful precursor has two invisible enemies: the earthquake with no precursor and the precursor with no earthquake.

Neither makes good television.

Suppose a network issues twenty alarms and one is followed by a damaging earthquake. The one success will acquire a documentary. The nineteen false alarms will acquire a spreadsheet, if they are lucky. Suppose ten damaging earthquakes occur and the system alarms before one. The hit can still dominate the story.

This is why operational metrics matter more than anecdotal accuracy. Precision. Recall. False-alarm duration. Miss rate. Spatial volume under alarm. Calibration. Skill against a baseline that already knows earthquakes cluster in space and time.

A forecast system can appear clairvoyant by keeping half a country under warning for half the year.

The denominator is not clerical work. It is the experiment.

## 15. Prediction, Forecasting, and Warning

Earthquake prediction, earthquake forecasting and earthquake early warning are often collapsed in public language, which produces needless confusion.

Longer-term forecasting estimates probabilities. It can use fault histories, seismicity and models of earthquake occurrence. Early warning begins after rupture has started; electronic systems detect the event and can sometimes send alerts before the strongest shaking reaches locations farther away. Short-term prediction—the dream in this book—would identify a coming earthquake before rupture with useful specificity in time, place and size.

The fact that early warning works does not mean prediction works. The fact that prediction does not presently work does not mean earthquake science is helpless.

This matters politically. A society waiting for a mystical precursor can neglect the unromantic technologies that save lives without prophecy: building codes, retrofit, hazard maps, automatic shutoffs, drills and seconds of early warning.

The future may contain a precursor. The present contains engineering.

## 16. A Sensor Network Designed to Lose

If I were spending serious money on earthquake precursors, I would build the experiment around humiliation.

The network would measure candidate electromagnetic, geochemical, hydrological and atmospheric variables at multiple stations, but it would also measure the things most likely to impersonate them: geomagnetic activity, lightning, weather, power-grid noise, traffic proxies, pumping, sensor temperature, calibration state and maintenance events. Raw data would be preserved. Algorithms would be versioned. Alarm rules would be frozen for prospective periods.

The earthquake catalogue and magnitude threshold would be declared in advance. The spatial and temporal alarm windows would be declared in advance. Every alarm would remain in the ledger whether or not an earthquake dignified it. Every qualifying earthquake would remain whether or not the system saw anything interesting.

The system would compete against a boring seismic baseline.

Most importantly, nobody would be allowed to rescue a failed prediction by changing the meaning of “near” after the ground moved.

That is what it means to design a sensor network to lose. It is also the only design under which a win would mean much.

## 17. What a Real Precursor Would Look Like

A real precursor would eventually become dull.

It would appear in different instruments built by different teams. Its lead-time distribution would be measurable. Its dependence on magnitude, depth, tectonic setting and distance would be quantified. The physical mechanism would predict something that had not already been used to construct it. False alarms would be known. Misses would be known. Performance would persist out of sample.

There would still be arguments. There are arguments about mature measurements everywhere. But the argument would migrate from *whether the thing exists* toward *how much skill it adds*.

That migration is the tell.

The history in these chapters is mostly stuck earlier. We have effects without forecasts, anomalies without stable attribution, mechanisms without field closure, statistics without operational thresholds, and famous events without sufficient quiet-time denominators.

That is not nothing. It is a map of the distance remaining.

## 18. The Earth Does Not Owe Us a Warning

There is a sentimental idea hiding inside earthquake prediction: catastrophe should announce itself.

We would like nature to become legible before it becomes violent. The dog should bark. The radon should rise. The magnetometer should twitch. The ionosphere should glow in a way that a sufficiently clever civilization can decode.

Maybe some earthquakes do alter their environment before rupture in measurable ways. The physics does not forbid every possibility, and some statistical studies remain interesting enough to justify better experiments. But the history teaches a harsher discipline. A phenomenon can be genuine without being predictive. A signal can be measurable without being attributable. An association can survive statistics without becoming an alarm. A mechanism can work in rock without surviving the crust, the atmosphere, the grid, the Sun and the denominator.

This is why the failed precursors matter.

Corralitos teaches us to inspect the instrument's biography. Hector Mine teaches us that reproducing a curve is not reproducing its cause. QuakeFinder teaches us that an algorithm can discover human schedules. DEMETER teaches us that institutional seriousness does not guarantee operational closure. Earthquake lights teach us that folklore can contain natural phenomena without becoming prophecy.

The deepest lesson is not skeptical. It is methodological.

If the Earth is warning us, we should build an experiment capable of proving that it is not.

Until then, the shaking starts when the shaking starts.
