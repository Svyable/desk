# The Mathematics of Maybe

On May 1, 1973, the Journal of Political Economy published a paper with a title that did not sound like a cultural event: “The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities.” Fischer Black and Myron Scholes were asking how to value a peculiar kind of promise. An option gives its owner a right without imposing the corresponding obligation. You may buy the asset later at an agreed price, but you do not have to. The asymmetry is the point.

The paper became famous because it helped formalize a way to price that asymmetry under a set of assumptions. The formula matters. But for our purposes the more interesting idea is older and broader than the formula: a choice that can be deferred is not the same asset as a choice that must be made now.

That sounds obvious until money is attached to it.

Suppose you are offered two contracts to acquire a warehouse next year. The first requires you to buy it for ten million dollars regardless of what happens. The second gives you the right to buy it for ten million dollars but lets you walk away. Nobody needs advanced mathematics to understand that the second contract is better for the buyer. If demand explodes and warehouses become scarce, you exercise. If the market collapses, you decline. The future has been allowed to reveal something before your obligation becomes final.

The difficult question is how much that flexibility is worth.

Options theory puts machinery around the intuition. The value of an option generally rises with uncertainty in the underlying asset because greater dispersion creates more upside states while the option holder's downside is bounded by the choice not to exercise. This is one of the beautiful inversions in finance. Ordinary assets often make us fear volatility. An option can become more valuable because the world is less predictable.

Outside finance, organizations often behave as if uncertainty should produce the opposite response. The less certain the environment, the stronger the demand for a single forecast. Executives ask for the number. Committees ask for the plan. Governments publish central scenarios. Product teams turn ranges into dates. A point estimate creates relief because it lets coordination begin.

The relief is psychological, not informational.

A forecast does not remove uncertainty. It converts uncertainty into a document.

The mathematical idea of optionality gives us a way to resist that conversion when it becomes dangerous. If the world is volatile, information is arriving, and a decision is hard to reverse, there may be value in waiting. Economists studying irreversible investment developed this insight into what became known as real-options analysis. A factory, a mine, an oil field, a research program, or a market entry can contain option-like features even though no exchange-traded contract is involved. A firm may hold the option to invest, expand, contract, switch inputs, suspend operations, or abandon a project.

The word “real” distinguishes these choices from purely financial securities. It also hides how radical the idea can be. Traditional capital budgeting asks whether the expected cash flows of a project justify the investment. A real-options perspective asks an additional question: what are we giving up by committing now?

This matters most when three conditions meet. The future is uncertain. Waiting produces information. The investment is difficult or expensive to reverse.

Remove any one of the three and delay may lose value. If there is little uncertainty, there is little to learn. If waiting tells you nothing, delay is merely lost time. If the decision is cheaply reversible, you can act now and change later.

That triad is more useful than the vague advice to “keep your options open.” It tells us where optionality deserves attention.

Imagine a pharmaceutical company deciding whether to build a dedicated plant for a drug still in clinical trials. Demand is uncertain because approval is uncertain. Time may reveal trial results and regulatory decisions. The plant is expensive and specialized. Those conditions make staged commitment attractive. The company can buy land, secure permits, reserve supplier capacity, or design a modular facility without yet spending the full capital required for production. Each step purchases some readiness while preserving some ability to stop.

The strategy is not free. Delaying a plant can create capacity shortages if the drug succeeds. Competitors may move first. Construction prices may rise. Option value competes with the cost of waiting.

This is why optionality is not cowardice in mathematical dress. It is a trade.

One side of the trade is flexibility. The other is foregone compounding.

The same structure appears in hiring. A company unsure whether a new business line will work can use contractors before building a permanent team. That preserves flexibility but may sacrifice loyalty, tacit knowledge, and culture. A country can lease infrastructure rather than own it, but dependence on the lessor becomes part of the price. A household can rent rather than buy, but loses some control and potential appreciation. A student can sample courses before choosing a major, but every semester spent sampling is a semester not spent going deeper.

Mathematics helps because it forces us to identify the variables we are otherwise tempted to moralize.

Consider waiting. We often treat waiting as either prudence or procrastination. The option lens asks what information is expected to arrive during the wait and whether that information can change the decision. If nothing decision-relevant will be learned, waiting has little informational value. If tomorrow brings a clinical endpoint, an election result, a court ruling, a weather forecast, or a customer test, waiting may be worth a great deal.

The distinction is especially important in bureaucracies, where “we need more data” can become a respectable way to avoid responsibility. More data is valuable only if some plausible result would cause a different action.

A scientist can make this concrete by specifying a stopping rule before collecting evidence. If the experiment comes out one way, continue. If it comes out another, stop. If every possible result leads to continuation, the experiment was not purchasing an option. It was purchasing a decoration.

The same test can be applied to business pilots. Before running a pilot, ask what outcome would kill the project. If the answer is none, the pilot is theater.

This leads to the first practical principle of optionality: an option has value only if you are willing to exercise or abandon it.

Holding an option forever is not a strategy. It is inventory.

Financial options make this brutally clear because they expire. Time is part of the contract. A call option that would be valuable with a year remaining can be worthless after expiration. Real-world choices have expirations too, though they are rarely printed on paper. A talented employee considering your offer may take another job. A property may be sold. A technology window may close. A political coalition may dissolve. A parent may discover that a conversation postponed for ten years cannot be held with the child who existed ten years earlier.

Optionality decays.

This is one reason “keep your options open” is weak advice. It ignores time. Some options become more valuable as information arrives. Others vanish because the world moves.

The mathematical language also clarifies another source of confusion: uncertainty and risk are not identical. In ordinary speech the words blur together. Frank Knight famously distinguished measurable risk from deeper uncertainty, though the boundary is less clean in practice than textbooks sometimes suggest. An insurance company can model large pools of similar events because frequencies become informative. A founder considering a market that does not yet exist faces a different problem. The probability distribution itself may be unstable.

Options are especially attractive when the distribution is uncertain because they can bound downside while preserving exposure to surprise. This helps explain the appeal of venture capital portfolios. A venture investor expects many companies to fail or return little, a smaller number to do well, and a very small number to produce outsized gains. The portfolio is built around asymmetry. The loss on an individual investment is bounded; the upside of an exceptional company can be many times the original capital.

The pattern is seductive, and it is frequently abused in metaphor. Not every life choice is a venture portfolio. Not every small bet has uncapped upside. People have finite attention, reputation, health, and time. The fact that a strategy has convex financial payoffs does not mean it has convex human consequences.

A founder can run ten experiments. A parent cannot run ten childhoods.

This is where the mathematics of maybe meets ethics. Reversibility is often distributed unevenly. The party with the option may be imposing irreversibility on someone else.

A company can preserve the option to cancel a project by keeping workers on temporary contracts. For the company, labor has become flexible. For the worker, rent remains due. A platform can preserve the option to change its terms of service; sellers who built businesses on the platform absorb the shock. An investor can exit a stock with a click while employees live with the consequences of a restructuring the investor advocated.

The option holder's flexibility can be another person's volatility.

This observation complicates the language of “agility” in management. Organizations often celebrate variable costs because variable costs can be reduced when demand falls. But costs do not become variable by magic. They become someone else's uncertain income, an idle supplier's capacity, a city losing a tax base, or a contractor waiting for the next project.

A serious theory of optionality has to track who owns the option and who underwrites it.

Financial markets make ownership explicit. The buyer pays a premium. The seller receives the premium and accepts an obligation. In ordinary institutions, the premium may be hidden. A company wants the option to scale orders up or down, so a supplier maintains extra capacity. Who pays for that capacity? A consumer wants easy returns, so retailers build reverse logistics and sometimes destroy the economics of low-margin goods. A country wants energy security, so it pays for storage, redundant pipelines, strategic reserves, or domestic capacity that may look uneconomic in normal times.

There is no free flexibility. There is only flexibility whose cost has been recognized or displaced.

Once you see this, many debates change shape. The argument over just-in-time inventory is not really a contest between efficiency and stupidity. Lean inventories can expose defects, reduce carrying costs, and force operational discipline. But a supply chain with little buffer is making a particular bet about the reliability of replenishment. When the environment changes, what looked like waste can become an option to continue operating.

The right inventory level therefore depends on the distribution of disruptions, the cost of stockouts, the speed of substitution, the shelf life of the goods, financing costs, and the network structure of suppliers. “Resilience” is not an amount. It is a relationship between shocks and response paths.

The same is true of cash. Corporate finance textbooks can make excess cash look like inefficient capital. Activist investors may pressure firms to return it. Sometimes they are right. Managers can hoard cash because they enjoy discretion and dislike scrutiny. But cash is also the most general-purpose option an organization can hold. It can buy time, inventory, distressed assets, employee retention, legal defense, or a change in strategy. Its return in tranquil periods understates its value in discontinuities.

Households know this in their bones. An emergency fund often looks financially inferior to a higher-return investment until the emergency arrives during a market decline or a job loss. Liquidity is a claim on time.

The mathematics of options also gives us a way to understand why modular systems can be worth more than their apparent efficiency. A modular product divides a system into components connected through interfaces. If components can be changed independently, the system contains options. You can improve one module without redesigning the whole. You can replace a supplier, upgrade a processor, change a user interface, or experiment with a subsystem.

Modularity usually carries costs: duplicated functionality, interface overhead, constraints imposed by standards, perhaps lower peak performance than a tightly integrated design. But the modular system can adapt through local changes. Its option value increases when technology is moving quickly or when future requirements are uncertain.

This insight is central to software, where interfaces allow extraordinary rates of change. It also appears in biological systems, constitutional systems, and organizations. Separation can be inefficient if nothing changes. Change makes separation valuable.

Again the same inversion: volatility can increase the value of architecture that preserves choice.

But there is a point at which the option should be exercised. Financial options help because expiration forces the issue. Real life often does not. Organizations can keep revisiting decisions, hiring consultants, extending pilots, and creating committees because no contract says the right to wait has expired.

So we need artificial expirations.

A trial period should end. A pilot should have a decision date. A strategic review should produce a commitment or a cancellation. A medical watch-and-wait strategy should contain triggers for intervention. A cease-fire negotiation needs deadlines but also enough flexibility to avoid turning the deadline itself into a cause of war. Good expiration design is delicate because deadlines can improve decisions or distort them.

A deadline creates pressure by reducing optionality on purpose.

This is the paradox at the heart of the chapter. Options are valuable because they let us wait. We often become capable of acting only when the option to wait disappears.

The phenomenon appears in mundane places. A writer can revise indefinitely until the publication date arrives. A student can research indefinitely until the exam begins. A product team can explore indefinitely until customers have been promised a release. Human attention seems to need some irreversibility to convert possibility into work.

Commitment devices deliberately exploit this. People precommit to future actions because they know their preferences will change. Automatic retirement contributions, deadlines, deposits, public promises, and contracts can constrain the future self. In one sense they destroy option value. In another they protect a longer-term preference against short-term renegotiation.

The future self is both beneficiary and counterparty.

Behavioral economics makes the situation richer still. We do not value options neutrally. Loss aversion can make us reluctant to abandon projects because stopping crystallizes a loss. The endowment effect can make ownership inflate perceived value. Status quo bias can make the default path feel safer than alternatives. Ambiguity aversion can make a known mediocre choice more attractive than an uncertain better one. Optionality helps only if the decision process can overcome these distortions.

This is why architecture beats exhortation. Telling people to “be willing to pivot” is weaker than funding a project in stages, with independent review at each stage and explicit criteria for continuation. Telling leaders to “stay open-minded” is weaker than creating a decision process in which dissenting evidence has a formal place before irreversible action.

The mathematics supplies a discipline: identify the right, the obligation, the expiration, the uncertainty, the cost of waiting, the cost of reversal, and the party bearing each exposure.

You do not need to calculate Black-Scholes to benefit from the structure.

Ask of any important decision: what would we pay today to retain the right to change this decision six months from now?

Sometimes the answer will be almost nothing. The decision is easy to reverse, or delay is costly, or the relevant information will not improve. Commit.

Sometimes the answer will be enormous. The system is uncertain, the downside is large, new evidence is close, and the commitment is sticky. Buy time.

Then ask the less comfortable question: who is selling us that option?

If the answer is an employee, a supplier, a citizen, a child, or a future generation that did not negotiate the premium, the mathematics has revealed something the rhetoric of flexibility would prefer to hide.

Maybe is not free.

That is precisely why it can be valuable.
