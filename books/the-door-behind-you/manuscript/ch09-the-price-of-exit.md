# The Price of Exit

There is a genre of consumer experience that begins pleasantly and ends in a telephone queue.

Signing up took forty seconds. Canceling requires locating a hidden page, answering questions, declining offers, waiting for a representative, explaining yourself, and confirming that you really do mean to leave. The asymmetry is not an accident. It is a business model expressed through friction.

For the seller, the arithmetic can be attractive. If a small amount of cancellation difficulty reduces churn across a large subscriber base, the revenue effect compounds. The consumer may dislike the experience but postpone action. Inertia becomes monetizable.

For the consumer, the product has acquired an invisible fee: the price of exit.

Markets are often described as systems of voluntary exchange. The phrase is useful and incomplete. The quality of voluntariness depends partly on how costly it is to stop exchanging.

A one-time purchase has a clear edge. Money changes hands; the transaction ends. A subscription creates a continuing relationship. A mortgage, employment contract, insurance policy, university degree, software platform, mobile operating system, social network, bank account, and marriage all contain much deeper continuity. Entry choices create later states in which exit is possible but costly.

The cost can be money. It can also be time, data, relationships, reputation, inconvenience, identity, uncertainty, or the loss of complementary assets.

Consumer psychology changes when these costs are hidden.

A free trial feels like a reversible decision. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the trial quietly converts to a paid plan and the burden of action moves to the consumer. The default does work. Behavioral economics has documented how strongly defaults can affect outcomes across domains, though effects depend on context and people are not automatons. The practical business insight is obvious enough that firms do not need an academic paper to discover it: people are less likely to take an action than to remain in a state that requires no action.

This can be used benevolently. Automatic enrollment in retirement savings can increase participation while preserving opt-out rights. Automatic renewal can prevent insurance or essential services from lapsing accidentally. Defaults reduce decision costs in environments where repeated active choice would be burdensome.

They can also be used to make exit expensive in attention.

Attention is a real economic resource even when no invoice is issued.

A consumer juggling work, family, bills, health, and passwords may rationally ignore a modest recurring charge because the cancellation process is irritating. The company captures the difference between the consumer's abstract preference and the consumer's limited capacity to act on it.

This is why the design of cancellation matters beyond etiquette.

In October 2024 the U.S. Federal Trade Commission announced a final “click-to-cancel” rule aimed at making cancellation of recurring subscriptions and memberships easier, including a principle that cancellation should be as easy as enrollment. The rule later became entangled in legal and regulatory developments, as major rules often do, but the policy argument itself is revealing. It treats exit friction as a market-design problem.

If firms compete partly by trapping customers after entry, observed retention can overstate satisfaction.

Making exit easier can improve the informational quality of churn.

This is a useful general principle: low exit costs make staying more meaningful.

A company whose customers can leave easily learns more from retention than a company whose customers are captive. A country that permits emigration learns something different from a country that does not. An employer in a competitive labor market receives a different signal from turnover than a company town. A software platform with portable data receives a different kind of loyalty than one where years of work cannot be exported.

Freedom changes the interpretation of stability.

The business case for reducing exit friction can therefore be stronger than consumer kindness. Trust at entry can rise when customers know they can leave. A refundable purchase may attract buyers who would avoid a final commitment. A month-to-month contract can reduce sales friction compared with a long lock-in. A bank that makes transfers simple may appear less threatening. A software service with easy data export can win skeptical enterprise customers.

The company gives up some ability to harvest inertia in exchange for a larger or more trusting funnel.

This is optionality as a sales feature.

The trade is not always favorable. Companies with high upfront acquisition or setup costs may need long contracts to recover investment. A gym that builds facilities cannot necessarily offer every member a costless hour-by-hour decision. A telecom network has fixed infrastructure. A business-software vendor may spend heavily implementing a complex system for one client. Long commitments can align incentives by ensuring both sides have time to earn returns on relationship-specific investment.

Commitment can create value precisely because exit is not free.

The question is whether the cost is explicit and mutual or hidden and asymmetric.

A long-term contract negotiated by sophisticated parties can make the bargain clear. One side promises volume; the other invests in capacity. One side accepts minimum payments; the other offers lower prices. The restriction on exit finances a capability that would not exist under pure spot-market transactions.

A dark pattern does something different. It raises exit costs after the value-creating investment has already been made, often without making the trade legible at entry.

The difference is not merely legal. It is cultural.

Companies teach customers what kind of institution they are when customers try to leave.

A sales process is performance. Cancellation is character.

The same is true of employment. Recruiting brochures describe the firm when both parties want the relationship. Exit policies reveal the firm when interests diverge. Noncompete agreements, vesting schedules, repayment clauses, references, severance, garden leave, intellectual-property provisions, and alumni networks all shape the employee's option set after joining.

Some restrictions can protect legitimate investments or confidential information. Others primarily reduce labor mobility and bargaining power. The policy debate over noncompetes reflects a fundamental question about who should own the option to redeploy human capital.

Skills are unusual assets because the worker carries them.

An employer can purchase labor for a period but cannot literally return the person to a pre-employment state. Training changes the worker. Relationships change the worker. Reputation changes the worker. A strong labor market allows those gains to travel. A closed labor market lets firms capture more of them.

This makes portability politically important.

Portable pensions, transferable licenses, recognized credentials, health insurance that is not tightly coupled to one employer, and enforceable rights to move can change bargaining power without regulating every wage. They reduce the penalty for exit.

The same idea appears in finance. Bank-account portability, open-banking standards, payment interoperability, number portability in telecommunications, and standardized data export can intensify competition by lowering switching costs.

Policy can create markets by creating doors.

But lowering switching costs can also destabilize systems.

Banks illustrate the extreme. Depositors need confidence they can withdraw money. That convertibility makes deposits useful. Yet the ability to withdraw quickly can contribute to runs when confidence collapses. Digital banking can accelerate movement that once required a branch visit. A feature that makes the system convenient in normal times can make collective exit faster in a panic.

The policy response is not to make bank deposits impossible to withdraw. It is to build surrounding institutions—capital requirements, liquidity management, deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort functions, resolution regimes—that reduce the chance that individual optionality becomes systemwide catastrophe.

This is a deep point. Exit rights can create externalities when everyone exercises them together.

The fire door works for one corridor. A financial exit door can become a stampede.

Markets therefore need not only individual options but system architecture capable of absorbing correlated exercise.

Investment funds face a version of this when they hold illiquid assets while offering investors frequent redemption. In calm markets, the structure seems generous. In stress, many investors may want cash at once, forcing asset sales into weak markets. The individual right to exit can impose costs on those who remain.

Liquidity mismatch is optionality promised against insufficiently reversible assets.

The same structure appears in infrastructure and public services. A wealthy household can exit a weak public school by moving or paying for private education. Individually, the choice may be sensible. Collectively, concentrated exit can reduce political pressure and resources available to improve the public institution. People with the greatest capacity to leave may also have the greatest capacity to exercise voice.

This was central to Albert Hirschman's analysis of exit, voice, and loyalty. Exit is a feedback mechanism: customers leaving a firm signal decline. Voice is another: people complain, organize, vote, negotiate, protest, or otherwise try to improve the institution. Loyalty can slow exit and give voice time, but it can also keep people attached to failing systems.

The framework is powerful because it refuses to treat exit as the only form of freedom.

A market fundamentalist caricature says: if you do not like it, leave.

A bureaucratic caricature says: stay and use the process.

Actual human institutions require both.

Easy exit without effective voice can produce churn without learning. Everyone leaves the bad team; nobody fixes the manager. Customers abandon a product; executives misread the cause. Citizens move away from a city; local government loses the constituency most capable of reform.

Voice without exit can become pleading. If the institution knows you cannot leave, it can absorb complaint without changing.

The presence of a credible exit option makes voice more powerful.

This is why workers negotiate differently when other jobs are available. Suppliers negotiate differently when they have multiple customers. Small countries negotiate differently when trade routes diversify. Consumers negotiate differently when data is portable. A relationship changes when “no” can be enacted.

The option need not be exercised to matter.

This is similar to military deterrence and financial options: the existence of the alternative changes bargaining before the alternative is used.

Power often lives in unused exits.

That insight complicates the business obsession with retention. High retention is usually good. But retention generated by product value differs from retention generated by entanglement.

A company should know which one it owns.

One way to find out is to reduce a switching cost and observe what happens. This is terrifying to incumbents because the experiment can reveal that years of “loyalty” were architecture. But the information may be worth having before a competitor, regulator, or technological shift runs the experiment for you.

The ethnography of consumer life is full of tiny entanglements. People keep old email addresses because accounts depend on them. They remain in group chats because relationships live there. They store family photos in one cloud because migration is tedious. They keep credit cards because autopay is attached. They postpone changing doctors because records and familiarity matter. They tolerate a streaming subscription because the children know the interface.

These are not irrationalities in the simple sense. Switching has real costs.

The mistake is to model consumers as choosing products fresh each morning.

Markets are layered histories.

A purchase today changes the menu tomorrow. The smart speaker works better with compatible devices, so the next device is evaluated inside an ecosystem. A game console changes which games friends can play together. A professional tool changes which file formats a worker receives. A social network becomes more useful because the relevant people are already there.

Network effects create value and lock-in at once.

This duality is why antitrust questions around platforms are difficult. A dominant network may be dominant partly because concentration improves the product. Fragmenting it can reduce value. At the same time, high switching costs and control over interfaces can reduce competitive pressure.

The right remedy, when one is warranted, may involve changing portability or interoperability rather than simply breaking the company into pieces. The goal is not maximum fragmentation. It is contestability—the credible possibility that users, developers, suppliers, or advertisers can redirect activity.

Contestability is market optionality.

It makes the incumbent live with a door behind the customer.

The same principle can be applied internally. A company can make its own services contestable by allowing business units to choose external alternatives, or by benchmarking internal functions against the market. This can discipline monopoly behavior inside large organizations. It can also destroy useful shared capabilities if every unit optimizes independently.

Again, exit has system costs.

The most interesting institutions design gradients rather than binaries. They make ordinary exit easy, consequential exit deliberate, and collective exit survivable.

A subscription can be canceled with a click. A pension can be transferred through a regulated process. A bank can be resolved rather than simply evaporating. An employee can resign but may owe reasonable notice in some roles. A partner can leave a partnership under terms that protect the enterprise. A country can withdraw from a treaty through defined procedures rather than pretending commitments are eternal or meaningless.

Procedural exit preserves both freedom and continuity.

This is the civilizational achievement hidden inside boring contract clauses.

Notice periods, termination provisions, buy-sell agreements, bankruptcy procedures, succession rules, portability standards, and appeal rights all answer the same question: what happens when someone wants out?

Institutions that never answer it usually answer it badly under stress.

Founders avoid discussing what happens if one cofounder leaves because the conversation feels disloyal. Families avoid wills because death is unpleasant. Business partners avoid buyout terms because enthusiasm is high. Alliances avoid withdrawal mechanics because unity is the message. Then the relationship changes, which relationships do, and the absence of an exit architecture turns disagreement into crisis.

Designing an exit is not a prediction that the relationship will fail.

It is recognition that relationships exist through time.

Paradoxically, a clear exit can make commitment easier. People enter because they know the institution will not become a trap. Investors fund because bankruptcy law creates a process for failure. Employees join because skills remain portable. Customers adopt because data can be exported. Countries cooperate because treaties contain procedures.

The door behind you can make it easier to walk in.

This does not mean every relationship should be easy to abandon. Some value depends on durability. Children need adults whose care is not priced as a daily option. Public goods need contributors who do not defect whenever private incentives shift. Teams need members who stay through unpleasant periods. Democracies need losers of elections to remain inside the constitutional game.

There are commitments whose moral content comes from staying when exit is available.

That is a different kind of loyalty from captivity.

Loyalty without exit is difficult to distinguish from dependence.

Loyalty with exit is a choice repeated through time.

The price of exit therefore tells us something about the quality of the relationship before anyone leaves.

It tells us who has leverage, who bears transition costs, whether retention contains information, and whether commitment is being earned or engineered.

A company can hide the cancel button.

It cannot hide forever what that choice says about the value proposition.
