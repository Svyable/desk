# The Jam Problem

Draeger's, in downtown Menlo Park, California, built its reputation on abundance. This was a grocery store where a shopper could find not one or two brands of olive oil but dozens, not a handful of mustards but something closer to a wall of them, arranged the way a wine shop arranges vintages rather than the way an ordinary supermarket arranges condiments. It was the kind of store where the selection itself was the pitch, an implicit promise that whatever exact thing you wanted, in whatever exact variation, Draeger's would have it. In the late 1990s, two psychologists set up a folding table near the entrance and used that promise to run one of the more quietly influential experiments in the social sciences.

Draeger's was a deliberate choice of location, not an incidental one. Iyengar would later describe, in talks and interviews given after the study made her one of the more visible psychologists writing about choice, just how extreme the store's commitment to variety actually was — a single aisle stocked with something like two hundred and fifty different mustards, another with dozens of vinegars, a cheese counter that treated selection itself as a form of hospitality. A store like that was, in effect, already running an uncontrolled version of the experiment on every customer who walked in, all day, every day. What Iyengar and Lepper added was a controlled version, narrow enough to measure, of a phenomenon the store's entire business model was otherwise simply assuming worked in its favor.

Sheena Iyengar, then at Columbia University, and Mark Lepper, at Stanford, wanted to test something that ran against the grain of how economists had traditionally thought about choice. The standard assumption, going back to classical economic theory, held that more options could only help a rational consumer: a larger menu can never make you worse off, because you can always ignore the options you don't want and pick the one you would have picked anyway. More choice, on this view, is either neutral or beneficial, never actively harmful. Iyengar and Lepper suspected the standard assumption missed something about how actual human beings, with limited time and limited patience for comparison, experience a large menu differently than a small one.

Their experimental setup, published in 2000 in a paper titled "When Choice Is Demotivating: Can One Desire Too Much of a Good Thing?," was elegantly simple. On different Saturdays, the researchers set up a tasting table at the Draeger's Menlo Park store featuring samples of gourmet jams, letting the store's existing weekend foot traffic serve as the subject pool without needing to recruit anyone specially. On some Saturdays, the table displayed twenty-four different jam flavors. On others, it displayed only six. Shoppers who stopped at the table could taste as many samples as they liked and received a coupon offering a dollar off any jam from the store's regular shelf, redeemable whether or not they bought one of the tasting varieties specifically. The researchers then tracked two separate numbers for each condition: how many passing shoppers stopped to look or taste at all, and, among those who stopped, how many went on to actually redeem the coupon and buy a jar.

The two numbers told opposite stories, and the gap between them is the entire point of the experiment. The larger display, with twenty-four flavors, was the better magnet: roughly sixty percent of shoppers who passed the table stopped to look, compared with roughly forty percent at the six-flavor table on the Saturdays it was used. Abundance drew the eye, exactly as a store like Draeger's would have predicted and exactly as its whole retail philosophy assumed. But conversion told a different story entirely. Of the shoppers who stopped at the twenty-four-flavor table, only about three percent went on to purchase a jar. Of those who stopped at the six-flavor table, roughly thirty percent did. A shopper who stopped at the smaller display was, by that measure, roughly ten times more likely to actually walk away with a jam than a shopper who stopped at the larger one. The wide selection pulled in more browsers and then, having pulled them in, sent nearly all of them away empty-handed.

The researchers were careful enough about their design to anticipate an obvious objection: maybe the twenty-four-jam table simply attracted a different, more casually curious crowd, less serious about buying anything in the first place, while the six-jam table happened to draw shoppers already leaning toward a purchase. Running the same table on alternating Saturdays, in the same store, with the same general population of regular Draeger's shoppers passing through, was the design choice meant to rule that explanation out; whatever else varied Saturday to Saturday, it was not the store's customer base. The difference in outcomes tracked the size of the display, not some hidden difference in who happened to walk by that day.

It is worth sitting with how strange this result is against ordinary intuition, because the strangeness is what makes it worth remembering rather than merely noting. If you had asked most shoppers, or most retailers, whether they would rather choose from twenty-four jams or six, a great many would have said twenty-four without hesitation, on the reasonable-sounding theory that more options means a better chance of finding the one you actually want. The jam experiment does not contradict that theory point by point so much as it reveals a cost the theory left out entirely: the cognitive and emotional labor of choosing. Comparing six jams against each other requires a manageable number of pairwise judgments — is the raspberry better than the apricot, is the strawberry better than the raspberry — that a shopper can run through in the time it takes to taste a few samples on a toothpick. Comparing twenty-four jams against each other requires vastly more of that same labor, not because there are four times as many jams, but because there are roughly four times as many jams to weigh against every other jam, and against the mounting, nagging possibility that the perfect flavor is still sitting somewhere on the table, untasted, while the shopper stands there running out of patience and turning toward the next errand on the list.

Part of what made the twenty-four and six numbers a good experimental choice, rather than an arbitrary one, is that both fall within a range a shopper could plausibly browse in full at a sample table before deciding whether to buy. Nobody in either condition was being asked to evaluate an infinite or even an especially large catalog by modern standards; twenty-four jams is a manageable Saturday-afternoon browse, not an algorithmically generated wall of forty thousand SKUs. That restraint is part of why the result unsettled people who read about it. If a fourfold increase in options, from six to twenty-four, both categories still small enough to physically taste every item on the table, could produce a tenfold drop in the rate of actual purchase, the implied cost of choice was not a minor friction showing up only at extreme scale. It was already showing up at a scale most people would have called reasonable, even generous, before the study ran.

The psychologist Barry Schwartz gave this general phenomenon its most widely read popular treatment in his 2004 book The Paradox of Choice, drawing on the Draeger's study and a range of related research to argue that modern consumer societies had, in a great many domains, sailed past the point where additional options make people better off and into a zone where additional options actively make people more anxious, more prone to regret, and less satisfied even with choices that are objectively good. Schwartz's broader claim was psychological as much as economic: every additional option raises the implicit standard against which a final choice gets measured, because a chooser now has to consider not just whether the jam they picked tastes good, but whether one of the twenty-three jams they didn't pick might have tasted better, a comparison that becomes literally unanswerable the moment the choice is made and can quietly poison satisfaction with an outcome that would otherwise have felt perfectly fine.

Schwartz drew a further distinction, borrowed and popularized rather than original to him, between what he called maximizers and satisficers — a maximizer being someone who tries, for any given decision, to identify the single best option among all available ones, and a satisficer being someone who stops searching once they find an option that clears some internal threshold of good enough. The distinction matters here because it predicts, and later research broadly bore out, that the two types experience an expanding menu very differently. A satisficer choosing jam is relatively insulated from overload, because the goal was never to find the best jam on the table, only a jam that will taste fine on toast, a bar the sixth option clears about as reliably as the twenty-fourth. A maximizer is not insulated at all, because expanding the menu from six to twenty-four does not just add options, it adds obligations — eighteen more jams that now, in principle, have to be tasted and weighed before the decision can be called complete, and eighteen more chances for the eventual choice to be quietly haunted by an option that went untried. Modern digital marketplaces, whether they intend to or not, tend to train their users toward maximizing behavior by design, surfacing ratings, rankings, and endless further recommendations that keep implying a better option is still one more click away. A jam table with twenty-four jars eventually runs out of jars. A recommendation feed does not.

None of which means the choice-overload effect is as clean or as universal as its most popular retellings sometimes suggest, and this is a place where the honest version of the story has to resist the neat one. A decade after the original jam study, researchers pooling the results of dozens of subsequent experiments attempting to replicate or extend the choice-overload finding found a much messier picture than the original result implied on its own: in many follow-up studies, across many kinds of choices, additional options produced no measurable drop in satisfaction or purchase rates at all, and the overall effect across the accumulated literature turned out to be far smaller and far less consistent than the striking ten-to-one ratio at Draeger's had suggested. One influential meta-analysis, led by the psychologist Benjamin Scheibehenne along with Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd, pooled results across the accumulated body of choice-overload studies conducted after Iyengar and Lepper's original paper and found the average effect, taken across all of them, to be close to zero — a sobering correction to a finding that had, by then, become a fixture of popular psychology writing and business consulting alike, cited more confidently than the underlying replication record actually supported. This does not mean the original finding was wrong, exactly. It means the effect appears to be real but conditional, showing up more reliably under some circumstances than others — when the options are hard to compare on a single dimension rather than easy to rank, when the chooser has no particular expertise in the category and no clear sense of their own preferences going in, when there is time pressure or an audience watching the decision get made, when the stakes feel high enough that a wrong choice would sting. A wine expert choosing among twenty-four wines they know well may experience none of the paralysis an ordinary grocery shopper experiences choosing among twenty-four jams they have never tasted before. The jam table worked as dramatically as it did partly because jam is exactly the kind of low-expertise, hard-to-rank, one-shot decision where the overload effect tends to bite hardest.

That qualification does not undercut the finding so much as sharpen what it is actually telling us, because the condition under which choice overload reliably appears — unfamiliar options, no personal expertise, no time to develop a considered ranking, real stakes riding on getting it right — describes an enormous and growing share of the choices an ordinary person now faces every single day, in domains that make Draeger's jam wall look almost quaint by comparison. A reader with no particular expertise in contemporary romance fiction, confronting several hundred thousand Kindle titles in the genre with only a cover image and a few reader reviews to go on, is not equipped to rank them the way an expert would. A listener with no specialized knowledge of a niche musical style, scrolling past millions of tracks on a streaming platform, cannot bring the discernment a working A&R executive once brought to a much smaller, pre-filtered catalog. A shopper trying to choose a mushroom foraging guide from among a dozen options with similar covers and similar-sounding titles has no fast, reliable way to tell the guide written by a lifelong mycologist from the one generated in an afternoon. Every one of these is precisely a jam-table decision: unfamiliar terrain, high real stakes, no time to develop expertise before choosing, and a number of options that keeps climbing well past the point where additional choice does anything but raise the cost of choosing at all.

There is a further wrinkle specific to the flood this book has been describing, one that goes beyond anything a jam table could capture and that makes the modern version of the problem harder than the original experiment, not easier. Iyengar and Lepper's shoppers could trust that every jar on the table was, in fact, jam — real fruit, real sugar, made by someone who intended to sell jam and not to deceive anyone about what was in the jar. The chooser's entire cognitive burden was comparison: which of these twenty-four genuine options do I prefer. A reader confronting several hundred thousand self-published titles, or a listener confronting millions of tracks on a streaming platform, faces a second, prior burden the Draeger's shoppers never had to carry at all, which is authentication: which of these options are even what they claim to be, before any question of comparative preference can be asked. Michael Smith's catalog and the AI-generated books that alarmed the mycological societies were not merely additional jam jars crowding an already-long table. They were jars that might not contain jam at all, mixed in among the ones that did, with no reliable label distinguishing one from the other. Choice overload plus an authentication problem is a meaningfully harder condition than choice overload alone, because it does not just raise the cost of comparing options, it raises the cost of trusting the table in the first place.

Line up the three cases this book has already told against that framework and the pattern gets hard to miss. Merriam-Webster gave its word of the year to a term for a texture of content, not a fixed inventory of it, precisely because the flood in question had no meaningful ceiling — a jam table that keeps restocking itself, infinitely, the moment anyone gets close to finishing a comparison. Michael Smith did not need his hundreds of thousands of generated songs to be individually appealing; he needed enough of them, spread widely enough, that the sheer size of the resulting catalog would overwhelm the platforms' capacity to authenticate what they were paying out for, the same authentication burden layered on top of ordinary comparison that this chapter has just described. And Amazon's response to the Kindle flood, a cap of three new submissions a day per account, reads entirely differently once you see it through Iyengar and Lepper's lens: it is not a quality intervention at all, it is a crude, blunt attempt to slow the growth rate of the table itself, on the theory that a smaller, slower-growing pile of jars is at least a more manageable problem than an unbounded one, even before anyone gets around to tasting what is inside them.

Seen this way, the flood described in the previous three chapters is not simply a story about declining quality, or about fraud, or about a publishing platform's content policy falling behind the technology it hosts. It is a story about the denominator in Iyengar and Lepper's experiment scaling up by many more orders of magnitude than the jam table ever tested, while the numerator — the finite, biologically unchanged human capacity to compare options and choose among them — has stayed exactly where it was. Draeger's stacked twenty-four jars on a folding table and produced a measurable collapse in purchases. A streaming platform stacks tens of millions of songs, a bookstore stacks millions of e-books, a search engine stacks results without any practical limit at all, and the same underlying mechanism that discouraged a shopper from buying jam discourages a listener from finishing an album, a reader from finishing a search, a viewer from finishing a scroll through recommendations, at a scale the original researchers had no reason to imagine when they were counting jars sold on a Saturday afternoon in Menlo Park.

This is the part of the argument that is easy to get backward, so it is worth stating with some care. The natural assumption, on first encountering the slop tide, is that abundance itself will eventually generate its own correction — that a marketplace flooded with worthless content will simply teach consumers, through repeated bad experiences, to demand better, the way a bad restaurant eventually closes when enough diners stop coming back. The jam experiment suggests something closer to the opposite. Abundance does not reliably produce more discerning demand. It produces, absent some other intervention, more browsing and less buying, more scrolling and less finishing, more stopping at the table and walking away with nothing at all. The three percent of Draeger's shoppers who bought from the twenty-four-jam table were not shoppers who had been trained by all that abundance into sharper jam-judging skills. They were, more plausibly, shoppers who happened to have a strong enough existing preference, or a high enough tolerance for the comparison labor, to push through the friction everyone else in that ninety-seven percent quietly declined to push through. Left alone, an oversupplied market does not sharpen judgment. It just thins out how many people bother exercising any.

It is worth noticing, too, that the tasting table itself, the very apparatus Iyengar and Lepper used to measure the overload effect, was already a small act of curation layered on top of the store's much larger, uncurated wall of jams. Draeger's did not ask its two researchers to set up shop in front of the full jam aisle, wherever that was and however many flavors it held. The experiment happened at a table someone had deliberately populated with a chosen subset — six or twenty-four, but never the store's complete inventory — samples pre-selected, laid out in a specific order, offered with a specific coupon attached. Even the six-jam condition, the one that performed so much better on conversion, was not really evidence that raw, unaided choice works fine as long as the number stays low. It was evidence that a moderately sized, already-curated selection, presented with a little guidance and a small incentive to act, converts browsing into buying far more reliably than a larger one does. The real contrast the experiment captured was never abundance against scarcity in the abstract. It was unaided abundance against a first, modest layer of somebody else's judgment.

What actually resolved the mismatch at Draeger's, to whatever extent it get resolved at all in that one afternoon experiment, was not a change in the size of the jam selection but a change in what stood between the shopper and the shelf: a sample table, a coupon, a staff member's suggestion, the small, low-cost interventions that let a shopper borrow somebody else's narrowing of the field instead of doing all of it themselves. That is, in miniature, the entire second half of this book's argument, arriving here in advance of the evidence that will support it properly. Some institutions, across some very different domains, have figured out how to intervene between an overwhelming supply and an exhausted chooser in ways that restore rather than merely relocate the labor of discernment — not by pretending to eliminate choice, which nobody actually wants, but by pre-filtering it, vouching for a manageable subset of it, and accepting the real risk of being wrong about which subset deserves the vouching. A tire company built exactly this kind of intervention into a stars-and-forks rating system that outlasted the tires it was invented to sell. A Pasadena grocery chain built it by refusing, on principle, to stock more than a few thousand items when its competitors stock ten times as many. A streaming service built it by paying a standing staff of human editors to do, deliberately and by hand, the narrowing an algorithm alone kept failing to do as well. None of these are jam tables. All of them are, at bottom, responses to the same discovery Iyengar and Lepper made by accident on a Saturday in Menlo Park: that unaided abundance is not a gift a market can simply hand to its customers and walk away from, but a problem that has to be actively managed by somebody willing to taste the jam first.

The mismatch this chapter has been describing did not begin with artificial intelligence, and it is worth remembering that the original study predates the technology this book is otherwise about by more than two decades. Iyengar and Lepper were not writing about content moderation or royalty pools or Kindle submission caps. They were writing about jam. But the psychological mechanism they isolated — that choice imposes a cost distinct from and sometimes larger than whatever value it adds, and that the cost grows faster than the value once the menu passes a certain size — turns out to be exactly the mechanism now playing out, at a scale that would have been unimaginable at a folding table in 1998, across nearly every category of media, information, and goods that generative systems have learned to produce almost for free. The slop tide did not create the jam problem. It simply built, across the entire information economy at once, the biggest jam table anyone has ever set up, and left standing the same open question the original experiment never claimed to answer on its own: what, if not the abundance itself, is actually going to help anyone choose.

That question turns out to have real answers, tested over decades in places considerably higher-stakes than a folding table outside a grocery store. Some of the clearest evidence for what actually works, and what only looks like it works, comes from a domain that treats the business of ranking unfamiliar options against each other as a formal, testable craft rather than a Saturday-afternoon accident: the deliberately blind, carefully structured world of expert tasting, where the whole point is to find out whether trained judgment can survive contact with a table just as crowded, and just as treacherous, as the one at Draeger's.
