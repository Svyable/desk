## A Note on Evidence

*Taste* rests on two kinds of evidence, kept deliberately separate from each other in the writing.

The first is documented history and research: the 1976 Paris wine tasting, Robert Hodgson's judging-consistency experiments, Frédéric Brochet's color and label studies, Semir Zeki's neuroaesthetics work, Pierre Bourdieu's sociology of taste, the Getty kouros dispute, the history of the Michelin Guide, and the recorded facts of the Han van Meegeren forgery case and the Théâtre D'opéra Spatial controversy. Where a figure or finding is disputed, contested, or has shifted between reports, the manuscript says so rather than picking the cleaner number.

The second is recent reporting on fast-moving events: the Michael Smith AI-music-streaming prosecution, Amazon's AI-book policy changes, and the 2023 quiet-luxury fashion cycle. These are the kind of stories that keep developing after a book goes to print. The detailed working source trail, including notes on which claims should be rechecked before any public release, is maintained in `research/README.md` and `research/sources/` beside this manuscript, kept separate from the narrative so the prose can stay readable while every concrete claim remains traceable to a source.

A few distinctions guided the writing throughout.

A study finding that experts disagree with themselves is not the same as a finding that expertise is worthless.

A shared neural response to beauty is not proof that all taste is universal, any more than a shared capacity for language proves all languages say the same thing.

An institution built to manufacture trust, such as a guide or a grocery chain, is not the same as trust itself; institutions can be captured, gamed, or simply outlived by the conditions that once justified them.

A machine trained to imitate human approval is not the same as a machine that has developed judgment, though the difference is easy to lose track of when the imitation is fluent enough.

None of these distinctions are original to this book. Keeping them in view, rather than collapsing them for a tidier argument, was the harder and more important discipline.

## A Small Glossary

**Cultural capital** — Pierre Bourdieu's term for the non-financial assets, such as education, vocabulary, manners, and acquired preferences, that function socially the way money functions economically, including in how they are unevenly inherited.

**Habitus** — Bourdieu's term for the durable, largely unconscious set of dispositions a person absorbs from their social environment, which then shapes what they find tasteful, ugly, appropriate, or embarrassing without their needing to reason it out each time.

**Neuroaesthetics** — The study of the neural basis of aesthetic experience, associated with the neuroscientist Semir Zeki, which looks for what happens in the brain when something is perceived as beautiful.

**Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)** — A method of training AI systems in which human raters compare or rank model outputs, and those preference judgments are used as a training signal to make future outputs more consistent with what raters approved.

**Symbolic violence** — Bourdieu's term for the process by which an arbitrary social hierarchy of taste comes to feel natural and self-evident to the people it disadvantages, rather than being recognized as a contingent social arrangement.

**Thin-slicing** — A term popularized in behavioral science for the way trained judgment can produce fast, often accurate conclusions from very limited information, and the way that speed can occasionally outrun the judge's ability to explain the conclusion.

## Nine Questions Worth Sitting With

These are not exercises with a correct answer. They are prompts for noticing your own judgment at work.

1. Name one thing you have strong taste about. Can you say, honestly, how much of that taste you built yourself and how much you inherited from the people around you growing up?

2. The next time an algorithm recommends something to you, ask what it is actually optimizing for — your long-term satisfaction, or your next few minutes of attention. These are not always the same objective.

3. Think of a time an expert's confident judgment turned out to be wrong. Did the confidence and the correctness feel different from the inside, in the moment, or only in hindsight?

4. Pick a category where you have strong opinions — coffee, film, running shoes, wine — and try to find one serious, well-informed argument for the opposite of what you believe.

5. Notice a recent purchase, book, or piece of media you chose because someone you trust recommended it rather than because an algorithm surfaced it. What did that person's recommendation give you that the algorithm didn't?

6. Consider a field where you have no expertise. What would it cost you, in time and money, to develop real judgment in it? That cost is roughly what the field's paid experts are being paid to save you.

7. Think about a piece of AI-generated content you have consumed without realizing it at the time. Would knowing changed how you felt about it, and if so, what does that tell you about what you were actually responding to?

8. Identify one gatekeeper in your own field or industry. What do they filter for, and who do they plausibly filter out unfairly? Both are usually true at once.

9. Ask what you would need to see, concretely, to change your mind about something you currently believe with confidence. If nothing comes to mind, that may be worth noticing.
