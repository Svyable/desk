# Quiet Money

In the spring of 2023, Gwyneth Paltrow spent eight days in a Park City, Utah courtroom being sued by a retired optometrist named Terry Sanderson, who claimed she had crashed into him on a beginner ski slope at Deer Valley Resort in 2016 and left him with permanent injuries. Paltrow countersued for a symbolic one dollar, testified that Sanderson had in fact skied into her, and was ultimately cleared by the jury. None of that is why the trial became one of the most closely watched fashion events of the year. What made Sanderson v. Paltrow a cultural moment had almost nothing to do with the verdict and almost everything to do with what Paltrow wore to deliver it: a rotating series of cream and gray cashmere sweaters, wide tailored trousers, and unbranded outerwear, day after day, photographed and dissected in real time by fashion writers who treated the courtroom like a runway nobody had announced. Outlets covering the trial identified pieces from The Row, the minimalist label founded by Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen, alongside understated separates attributed to houses like Prada and Celine — clothes that, to an untrained eye, looked almost deliberately forgettable. No visible logos. No recognizable monogram print. Nothing that screamed its own price tag. And that, to the writers covering it and the millions of people reading their coverage, was precisely the point.

The coverage itself became a strange kind of running commentary, a fashion-desk equivalent of a sports broadcast, with outlets like Vogue, Elle, and a swarm of digital fashion accounts publishing near-daily breakdowns of what Paltrow had worn into the courtroom that morning, cross-referencing sweaters and coats against the designer's current-season lookbook to identify the exact piece and its retail price. Social media users, many of whom had no particular interest in the underlying lawsuit, followed the trial primarily as a fashion event, sharing screenshots of Paltrow's outfits with commentary that oscillated between genuine admiration and pointed mockery of a defendant dressing, as more than one commentator put it at the time, like she was attending a First Growth Bordeaux tasting rather than defending herself against a personal-injury claim. That the wardrobe itself became a subject of public debate — was this canny brand management by a defendant known for founding the wellness company Goop, or an unconscious reflex of a woman who simply dresses this way — is itself evidence of how legible the code had become by 2023. A decade earlier, a defendant's cashmere sweater would not have generated newspaper coverage of its own. By the time of the Sanderson trial, enough of the public had absorbed enough of the relevant vocabulary that an unbranded sweater could function as a genuine talking point, parsed and argued over the way an earlier era might have parsed a visible designer logo.

By the time the trial ended, "quiet luxury" had already been circulating as a phrase for months, gaining velocity on TikTok and in fashion trade coverage as a name for a broader shift that Paltrow's courtroom wardrobe seemed to crystallize into a single, widely shared reference point. Some coverage used a slightly different label for the same underlying phenomenon — "old money aesthetic" — leaning into a fantasy of inherited, multigenerational wealth rather than the newly conspicuous kind. Both phrases pointed at the same wardrobe: cashmere in muted neutrals, well-cut trousers, boat shoes and loafers without decoration, cardigans that looked like they belonged to someone's grandfather if the grandfather in question had shopped exclusively in Milan. The style was not new in any absolute sense — a certain kind of understated, expensive dressing has existed for as long as there has been expensive clothing to be understated about — but its sudden prominence as a named, widely discussed trend in 2023 was a reaction to something specific, and the something specific was logos.

For roughly two decades before quiet luxury had a name, the dominant visual language of high-end fashion had moved in almost the opposite direction: louder, more branded, more insistently legible. The 2000s produced an entire aesthetic built around visible logo saturation — Von Dutch trucker hats, Ed Hardy's rhinestone-and-tattoo graphics, head-to-toe monogram from luxury houses whose canvas bags had become as recognizable from across a parking lot as a corporate billboard. Gucci, under creative director Alessandro Michele starting in 2015, pushed the aesthetic further into deliberate, maximalist logomania — interlocking GG prints reworked into streetwear-adjacent silhouettes, worn as loudly as the label's marketing department could manage. The logic of that era was straightforward: a logo is a portable, instantly readable announcement of what something cost and, by implication, what its owner could afford. It worked as a status signal precisely because it required no specialized knowledge to decode. Anyone, rich or not, fashion-literate or not, could look at a monogrammed bag and register roughly what it meant.

None of this vocabulary was actually new; quiet luxury borrowed, whether its 2023 champions realized it or not, from a dress code that predates the phrase by the better part of a century. Old-money American and British elites of the mid-twentieth century — the world of Ivy League campuses, East Coast country clubs, and the specific, understated tailoring associated with houses like Brooks Brothers — had never needed logos in the first place, because their status did not depend on convincing a stranger of anything. A family whose money and social position were assumed, inherited, and confirmed by an address, a surname, and a school tie had no use for a visible brand mark; the entire point of that older aesthetic was that it required insider knowledge to read at all; a stranger looking at a properly worn Shetland sweater or a slightly frayed Nantucket-red pair of trousers would see only plainness, while someone from the same world would recognize, instantly, exactly which decades of unbroken belonging that plainness represented. Logomania, when it arrived decades later, was in some sense a democratizing rupture in that older code — a way for newly wealthy people, entertainers, and aspirational middle-class consumers to buy a visible, purchasable version of a status marker that old money had never needed to purchase because it had never needed to prove itself to anyone. Quiet luxury's 2023 revival was not a wholly new invention so much as a return to the older, harder-to-fake code, dressed up in new branding and marketed, not without some irony, by many of the same fashion houses whose logo-driven products had defined the previous two decades.

That legibility was also the logo's undoing as a reliable signal, because anything legible enough for everyone to read is legible enough for everyone to copy. Counterfeit luxury goods became, over the same decades, a genuinely enormous global industry — international customs and trade organizations have described counterfeit and pirated goods as accounting for hundreds of billions of dollars in global trade annually, with luxury fashion goods among the categories most heavily targeted — and a counterfeit Louis Vuitton monogram, sold on a folding table or a gray-market website, sent almost the same visual signal as the six-thousand-dollar original to anyone glancing at it from ten feet away. Diffusion lines and heavily marketed entry-level products — perfumes, sunglasses, small leather goods bearing a house's most recognizable print at a fraction of its couture prices — extended the same erosion from the top down, letting a much wider swath of consumers buy their way into displaying a logo that used to signal genuine scarcity. By the early 2020s, a status signal that had once separated the wealthy from everyone else had become, through its own commercial success, something close to universally accessible in at least some counterfeit or diffusion-line form. A logo everyone can display, authentically or not, stops functioning as a marker of who actually has money. This is close to the oldest mechanism in the sociology of taste, the one this book examined at length in its account of Pierre Bourdieu's work on cultural capital: distinction requires scarcity, and any signal popular enough to be legible to the masses has already begun the process of losing its value to the group that first claimed it.

What happened next follows a pattern Bourdieu described from a very different vantage point decades before TikTok existed. When a marker of status becomes too widely available, genuine cultural capital does not disappear — it relocates, moving to a register that requires more specialized knowledge to read and is harder, correspondingly, to fake convincingly at a glance. Bourdieu's own research, conducted in France in the 1960s and 1970s, tracked how the reliably wealthy and culturally credentialed classes shifted their signals of taste away from whatever the newly arrived money had learned to imitate, again and again, treating good taste less as a fixed standard than as a moving target that stayed one step ahead of whoever was catching up. Quiet luxury is that same mechanism, running in real time, legible enough now that fashion journalists can narrate it as it happens rather than reconstructing it decades later from survey data. The signal moved from the logo — cheap to copy, easy to read — to a set of much subtler markers: the drape and weight of a fabric, the precision of a seam, the specific and expensive quietness of a garment that announces nothing to anyone who does not already know what they are looking at. A ninety-five-hundred-dollar Loro Piana cashmere baseball cap, a style that circulated widely in fashion media coverage of "stealth wealth" around this period, looks to an untrained eye like a plain gray hat. To someone fluent in the relevant knowledge, it looks like a hat that cost as much as a used car, and the fact that the first group cannot tell the difference is not a flaw in the signal. It is the entire design of the signal.

HBO's Succession, which ran from 2018 to 2023 and followed the Roy family, a fictional media dynasty modeled loosely on real-world moguls, did more than any single television show to popularize this vocabulary before "quiet luxury" had become a widely used phrase for it. The show's costume design leaned deliberately into the same understated register the fashion press would later name: characters worth billions of dollars dressed in cashmere sweaters, unbranded overcoats, and expensive-but-plain separates that never announced their price the way an earlier era's television depiction of wealth — gold jewelry, obvious designer prints, ostentatious furs — would have. Fashion writers covering the show throughout its run repeatedly noted how deliberately its costuming avoided visible branding, treating the absence of a logo as itself the marker of real, inherited money, in contrast to the louder, more legible wealth signals associated on the show with characters positioned as arrivistes or outsiders trying too hard to prove they belonged. The show did not invent quiet luxury as a concept. It gave millions of viewers a weekly, serialized education in how to read it, teaching an audience that had grown up fluent in logo recognition a new vocabulary in which the absence of a recognizable mark was itself the thing worth recognizing.

Fashion-industry data tracked the resulting shift in real commercial terms, not just in magazine trend pieces. Lyst, a fashion-search platform that publishes a quarterly index of what shoppers are searching for and buying across thousands of retailers, identified quiet luxury as one of the defining commercial trends of 2023, and named several previously lower-profile heritage brands — including Loro Piana, the Italian cashmere and wool house — among the year's fastest-rising names in consumer search interest. That is a telling detail in its own right: a brand whose entire historical identity had been built on being recognized only by people who already knew it, patronized quietly for generations by clients who valued exactly that obscurity, was suddenly experiencing a surge in mainstream search traffic driven by people who had just learned its name from a TikTok video or a magazine trend piece. The paradox writes itself. A label prized specifically because most people had never heard of it was becoming famous, on a mass scale, for being the kind of label most people had never heard of — which is a contradiction that cannot hold for very long, and by most fashion journalists' accounts, didn't.

The TikTok side of the trend made the contradiction even sharper, and even more visible in real time. Style creators built entire video series around "how to dress old money," breaking the aesthetic down into a checklist any viewer could imitate regardless of budget: muted color palettes, tailored fits over trendy silhouettes, natural fibers over synthetics, the deliberate avoidance of any visible logo or slogan. Some of this content pointed viewers toward the genuine, expensive originals; a great deal more of it pointed toward fast-fashion approximations, cheap wool-blend sweaters in the right shade of oatmeal, unbranded blazers cut to mimic the silhouette without the tailoring quality that justified the silhouette in the first place. The instructional format itself — a numbered list of rules for looking like money you don't have — is almost a parody of Bourdieu's own argument, since genuine cultural capital, in his account, is precisely the kind of knowledge that resists being reduced to a checklist; a person who actually grew up around this aesthetic did not learn it from a five-point video, and the fact that millions of people were now trying to learn it from exactly that format was itself a sign that the code was already being cracked, imitated, and drained of the specific kind of scarcity that had made it valuable as a signal in the first place.

A parallel case, predating quiet luxury's rise by nearly a decade and running through an entirely different industry, illustrates how flexible and how specific this coding can get. Through the middle and late 2010s, employees at Wall Street banks, private equity firms, and venture capital shops adopted an informal uniform built around fleece and softshell vests — most famously from Patagonia — embroidered not with Patagonia's own logo but with the small, discreet insignia of the wearer's employer: Blackstone, Goldman Sachs, a venture fund most people outside finance had never heard of. Business press coverage of the phenomenon, including a widely discussed 2019 New York Times feature on the trend, described the vest as having become close to a required uniform in certain finance offices, purchased in bulk by firms as branded swag and worn constantly enough that "the finance bro vest" became its own recognized cultural shorthand. What makes the case useful here is how the signal actually worked: the vest itself was not a status object in the way a monogrammed handbag was. It was a plain, practical piece of outdoor gear, its brand meaningful mainly to a customer base of hikers and skiers who had nothing to do with finance. The status signal lived entirely in the small embroidered company name on the chest — a detail invisible or meaningless to almost everyone except the very narrow population of people who could recognize which firms carried the most prestige and compensation, and who therefore knew exactly what it meant to see a stranger on a Manhattan sidewalk wearing one. This is coded legibility functioning in the opposite direction from quiet luxury's blank cashmere — a visible mark, but one interpretable only by an audience already inside the relevant social world — and it demonstrates that the underlying mechanism Bourdieu described is not really about logos versus no logos at all. It is about which audience a signal is built to be read by, and how narrow that audience needs to be before the signal stops working for everyone else.

There is a genuine irony sitting at the center of all this, one that a careful reading of Bourdieu would have predicted before quiet luxury ever had a name, and it is worth stating plainly rather than treating quiet luxury as some final, stable resting place for status signaling. A trend covered this extensively by fashion magazines, TikTok creators, and morning shows is not actually quiet in any meaningful sense — it is, by definition, a mass media phenomenon, discussed, explained, and eventually imitated by exactly the broad audience the aesthetic was supposedly designed to exclude. Fast-fashion retailers moved quickly to sell their own approximations of the look: unbranded cashmere-blend sweaters, wide trousers, muted palettes, all available at a fraction of the price of the Loro Piana or Brunello Cucinelli originals the trend was named after. Within roughly a year of the phrase's peak media saturation, "quiet luxury" had become, functionally, a mass-market aesthetic category available at price points a middle-income shopper could afford, which meant the people who had actually been signaling real wealth through unbranded cashmere before the trend had a name were facing the exact problem the logo generation faced a decade earlier: the signal had become legible, and therefore common, and therefore no longer capable of doing the specific work of separating them from everyone else. Some fashion writers and cultural commentators, by 2024, were already describing quiet luxury itself as played out, overexposed, a costume as recognizable and as easily performed as the logomania it had replaced — the same critique, with different nouns, that a previous generation had made of Von Dutch hats.

This is the part of the story that a purely cynical reading of Bourdieu risks flattening into something too neat, and it is worth resisting that flattening. It would be easy to conclude that quiet luxury simply proves taste is nothing but a status game, endlessly relocating, with no content of its own beyond whatever currently separates the in-group from the imitators — a reading some critics of Bourdieu's work have leveled at his broader theory of distinction, arguing that it reduces all aesthetic judgment to disguised class warfare and leaves no room for anyone to simply, genuinely prefer the drape of well-made cashmere over a synthetic blend. There is something to that objection. People who can tell the difference between a garment cut from a bolt of properly milled fabric and one cut from something cheaper are not always performing status; some of them have simply spent enough time around good clothing, or been trained by a profession that requires it, to notice a real physical difference in how a fabric moves and wears over years of use, independent of any social signaling at all. The honest version of this chapter's argument is not that quiet luxury is pure status theater with zero relationship to genuine craft or quality — expensive, well-made clothing frequently is, in fact, better made, by measurable standards of construction and material, than its cheap imitations. The argument is narrower and, in a way, more interesting: that the specific form the signal takes, the specific vocabulary a status-conscious buyer uses to display their knowledge, moves in a predictable direction whenever the previous vocabulary becomes too widely legible, and that movement itself — not the underlying question of craft quality, which is real — is the part driven by distinction rather than by taste in some purer sense.

Bourdieu had a specific word for the mechanism that makes all of this feel effortless rather than calculated to the people doing it, and it is worth bringing back into the foreground here rather than leaving it as a name-check. Habitus, in his usage, describes the durable, largely unconscious set of dispositions a person absorbs from years of living inside a particular social position — not a set of rules anyone consciously follows, but something closer to an internalized feel for what looks right, sounds right, and fits, acquired the way a native speaker acquires grammar rather than the way an adult acquires a foreign language from a textbook. A person raised inside real, multigenerational wealth does not need a TikTok checklist to know that an unbranded cashmere sweater reads as more sophisticated than a monogrammed bag; they absorbed that hierarchy of taste the way they absorbed table manners, without ever experiencing it as a rule being taught. That is exactly what separates the habitus Bourdieu described from the imitation quiet luxury made available to anyone with a smartphone and a fast-fashion budget: the imitator can learn the checklist, buy the oatmeal-colored sweater, and avoid the visible logo, but the effortlessness itself — the fact that the choice never felt like a choice to the person making it — is much harder to fake, and is often exactly the thing that gives an imitator away to someone who actually has the habitus in question. This is why quiet luxury enthusiasts native to old money circles reportedly found the whole trend faintly embarrassing rather than flattering: watching millions of strangers self-consciously study a set of behaviors that were, for the original wearers, never a set of behaviors at all.

What quiet luxury demonstrates, in the end, is not that discernment about clothing is fake. It is that discernment and status-signaling are tangled together so completely, in practice, that separating them cleanly is close to impossible even for the people doing the signaling. A woman wearing an unbranded cashmere sweater to a courtroom in Park City may have chosen it because she genuinely prefers the way it feels and looks, because her stylist understood exactly what statement an unbranded sweater would make to a national audience watching a defamation trial, or — far more likely, and far more true to how taste actually operates in practice — for some inseparable combination of both motives at once, in which the aesthetic preference and the calculated signal cannot be pulled apart even by the person wearing the sweater. Bourdieu's central insight was never that people are secretly cynical about their own taste. It was that taste, cultivated over years inside a particular social position, comes to feel like pure, unmediated personal preference to the person experiencing it, even as it tracks, with remarkable consistency, the exact contours of that person's class position and the exact signals that position currently requires. Quiet luxury did not invent that mechanism. It simply ran it in full public view, fast enough and visibly enough that anyone paying attention could watch a signal get colonized by the masses and a new one quietly, and briefly, take its place — before the process, inevitably, started again.

Whatever comes after quiet luxury will presumably follow the same logic, though it is hard to say from inside 2026 exactly what shape it will take. Some fashion forecasters have already pointed to a swing back toward maximalism and visible branding among younger shoppers who never lived through the 2000s logomania era firsthand and experience it as retro novelty rather than as the excess quiet luxury was reacting against — a reminder that these cycles do not move in one direction only, and that the next reversal may simply be logos again, worn ironically by people too young to remember why anyone got tired of them the first time. What stays constant across every turn of that cycle is not the specific garment or the specific brand but the underlying arms race Bourdieu described: whatever the current elite is wearing to distinguish itself will, given enough television exposure and enough TikTok tutorials, eventually be worn by everyone, at which point distinction will need a new disguise. Taste, understood this way, is not a fixed target anyone could hit once and be done with. It is a moving argument between the people trying to signal something true about themselves and the much larger group, always catching up, trying to borrow the signal before it means anything at all.
