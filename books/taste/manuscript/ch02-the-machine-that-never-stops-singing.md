# The Machine That Never Stops Singing

Somewhere on a server that Michael Smith paid for, at three in the morning on an ordinary Tuesday, thousands of accounts were listening to songs that no ear had ever needed to hear. The accounts did not sleep. They did not skip tracks out of boredom, did not hum along, did not add anything to a playlist to share with a friend. They opened song after song, let each one run long enough to register as a play, and moved to the next, around the clock, for years, while the man who built the system that ran them lived an otherwise recognizable life in North Carolina. According to federal prosecutors, that system generated more than ten million dollars before it was stopped.

The songs themselves were not the point, which is part of what makes the case such a clean illustration of the argument in the previous chapter. Prosecutors in the Southern District of New York allege that between 2017 and 2024, Smith used artificial intelligence tools to generate hundreds of thousands of songs — a volume no human songwriter, however prolific, could plausibly produce by hand in a lifetime, let alone in seven years while also holding down the rest of a life. He did not need any individual song to be good. He needed a catalog large enough, and a stream count high enough, to convert a royalty system's ordinary machinery into a wealth-transfer engine running quietly under the industry's floorboards. The music was, in the truest sense, incidental. It existed to be counted, not to be heard.

It is worth understanding the resentment such a scheme could plausibly feed on, even without knowing the specific contents of any one musician's bank account, because the underlying economics are widely documented and rarely disputed even by the platforms themselves. Streaming reshaped how recorded music makes money more completely than any shift since the record industry first learned to sell the same song twice, once on a single and once on an album. Musicians who came of age selling CDs out of the trunk of a car at shows, or shipping vinyl to record stores that took them on consignment, found themselves a decade later earning a small fraction of a cent for each individual play of a song on a streaming platform, with meaningful income arriving only at volumes most working musicians never approach. This is not a controversial claim inside the industry; it is the baseline complaint of working musicians across genres, repeated in interviews, congressional testimony, and trade press for the better part of fifteen years, regardless of whether the underlying royalty math is calculated one way or another. A musician who had spent decades on the losing end of that math, and who understood exactly how the pro-rata pool worked because his livelihood depended on it, was not encountering an obscure or theoretical vulnerability. He was looking directly at the seam in a system he had lived inside.

Before the scheme, Smith was a real musician, the kind of working, unglamorous, decades-deep presence in a local music scene that never produces a platinum record but produces a life spent playing, writing, and understanding the business from the inside. He was in his fifties by the time the indictment became public, old enough to have watched the recorded-music industry go through more than one complete upheaval — the collapse of CD sales in the 2000s, the rise of digital downloads, and then the shift to streaming, which restructured how a musician got paid more thoroughly than any single technology since the phonograph. It is worth sitting with that biography for a moment, because the temptation with a case like this is to file it under "AI fraud" and move on, as though the technology did the wrongdoing on its own. The technology did not write the fraud into being. A person who understood exactly how the streaming royalty system worked, because he had spent a career on the wrong end of it, appears to have decided that if the system was going to pay based on volume rather than merit, he would give it volume rather than merit, at a scale the system had never been built to withstand.

To see why that decision worked as well as it did, for as long as it did, it helps to understand what a streaming royalty actually is, because the arrangement is less like a jukebox paying a coin for every song and more like a single, finite pool of money being divided among everyone who put water into the same reservoir. Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, and most of their competitors run what is generally called a pro-rata model. Subscribers pay a monthly fee, and advertisers pay for placements; that revenue, after the platform takes its share, gets pooled and distributed to rights holders roughly in proportion to their share of total streams on the service during that period, not according to a fixed per-stream rate set in advance. If a listener spends an entire month playing nothing but one obscure band on repeat, that listener's subscription fee does not go entirely, or even mostly, to that band; it gets folded into the same pool as everyone else's subscription fee and divided out according to the platform's overall listening patterns that month. This is why so many musicians report earning fractions of a cent per individual stream, and why total plays in the hundreds of thousands can translate into royalty checks that would not cover a month's rent. The system was designed, reasonably enough, on the assumption that the total number of real streams in the pool each month would be driven by real human listening — millions of individual people making millions of individual choices about what to play.

The moment that assumption breaks, the pool becomes a target rather than a mirror. If total real listening in a given period is, hypothetically, ten billion streams, and someone injects an additional five hundred million fraudulent streams into that same pool, the money does not appear from nowhere to cover the difference. The pool is the pool. Every fraudulent stream siphons off a fraction of a cent that would otherwise have gone to a real artist whose real listeners generated a real stream that month. It is a zero-sum transfer disguised as new activity. This is the detail that makes streaming fraud categorically different from, say, a company faking its own product reviews, where the damage is mostly reputational and diffuse. Streaming fraud takes money directly, mechanically, unglamorously out of a fixed pot and redirects it, cent by fractional cent, away from musicians who have no way of knowing it is happening and no practical way to get it back even once they find out.

Prosecutors say Smith did not rely on human listeners to generate the volume his scheme required, because human listeners, even paid or persuaded ones, do not scale reliably and tend to leave traces. Instead, he built and operated networks of bot accounts — automated, non-human listener profiles registered on the streaming platforms, in numbers that at various points approached ten thousand — programmed to stream his AI-generated catalog continuously. Ten thousand accounts, running around the clock, each capable of generating dozens or hundreds of qualifying plays a day, adds up with an efficiency no touring musician's fan base could ever match organically. The songs, in turn, needed to exist in enough variety and be uploaded under enough different names and identities that the streaming platforms' fraud-detection systems, which are generally tuned to notice one song receiving an implausible number of plays from a small number of accounts, would have a harder time flagging any single title or artist as anomalous. Hundreds of thousands of songs, spread across many invented artist names, each pulling a slice of the bot traffic, is a much harder pattern to catch than one song streamed a billion times overnight. The AI generation and the bot infrastructure were not two separate tricks. They were two halves of the same design, each one covering for the weaknesses of the other.

The platforms were not passive victims standing still while this happened, and it is worth giving that some credit before returning to how long it nonetheless took. Spotify, Apple, Amazon, and their peers have all built systems specifically intended to detect and filter streaming manipulation, drawing on patterns like implausible listening velocity, repetitive account behavior, and clusters of accounts with suspiciously similar characteristics. These systems catch a great deal of low-effort fraud, the kind run by amateurs buying a package of "guaranteed streams" from a shady service advertised on social media. What they are less well equipped to catch quickly is a sophisticated, patient operator who understands the detection logic well enough to stay under its thresholds — spreading activity across enough songs, artists, and accounts that no individual signal ever spikes hard enough to trigger an automatic review, while the aggregate quietly compounds into eight figures over the better part of a decade. The case against Smith is, among other things, a case study in how a fraud can be, in a technical sense, unsophisticated — nobody is alleging some novel piece of cryptography or a previously unknown exploit — and still be extraordinarily difficult to catch, simply because it was built at a scale and with a patience that ordinary monitoring was not designed to expect.

Bot networks of the kind prosecutors describe are not, in themselves, a novel invention of the AI era, and it is worth being clear about which part of the scheme was old and which part was new, because conflating them makes the case less instructive rather than more. Manipulating engagement metrics with automated or low-cost human-operated accounts has been a feature of the internet economy since at least the early social media platforms of the late 2000s, when businesses discovered they could buy fake followers, fake likes, and fake views to inflate the appearance of popularity. Streaming platforms inherited the same vulnerability the moment they started paying out real money based on play counts, and stream-farming services — operations that will run a paying customer's chosen tracks through banks of accounts or devices to inflate play counts artificially — have existed in some form since at least the early 2010s, usually applied to a handful of real, previously released songs an artist or label wanted to appear more popular than they were. What generative AI added to that older playbook was the catalog side of the equation: instead of running fraudulent streams against a small, easily audited set of real songs, an operator could manufacture an essentially unlimited supply of distinct-sounding tracks to spread the fraudulent activity across, diluting the signal any single title would otherwise generate and making the entire operation dramatically harder to isolate. The bot farm was the old weapon. The AI-generated catalog was the ammunition that made the old weapon far more dangerous than it had ever been before.

Wire fraud, conspiracy to commit wire fraud, and money laundering conspiracy were the charges federal prosecutors brought, a combination that tells its own story about how the case was understood by the government. Wire fraud, in this context, describes the underlying deception — misrepresenting bot-driven streams as genuine listening activity to induce the platforms and the royalty-collection infrastructure behind them to pay out money they would not have paid had they known the truth. The money laundering conspiracy charge describes what happened after the royalties arrived: an effort, prosecutors allege, to move and disguise the origins of proceeds that had themselves been obtained through the streaming fraud, the kind of second-layer offense that tends to appear once a scheme has been running long enough, and generating enough money, that simply depositing it plainly would attract its own scrutiny.

On March 19, 2026, Michael Smith pleaded guilty to one count: conspiracy to commit wire fraud. A guilty plea to a single conspiracy count, after an indictment listing multiple charges, is a familiar shape in federal cases — prosecutors secure an admission of guilt to the charge that most cleanly captures the criminal agreement at the center of the scheme, in exchange for resolving the matter without a trial, while the defendant avoids the additional exposure of separate convictions on every charge originally filed. As part of the plea, Smith agreed to forfeit $8,091,843.64 — a figure specific enough, down to the last sixty-four cents, that it reads less like an estimate than like the output of an actual accounting of the funds investigators were able to trace and attach to the scheme. Under the terms of the plea, he faces a statutory maximum of five years in prison, with sentencing scheduled for July 29, 2026.

The precision of that forfeiture number is worth pausing on, because it says something about how this kind of fraud eventually gets unwound, which is slowly, forensically, and only partially. Streaming royalties move through a chain of intermediaries — the platforms, the distributors who deliver an artist's catalog to those platforms, performance rights organizations, publishing administrators — each of which takes a cut and each of which keeps its own records. Untangling how much of a given royalty payment traces back to fraudulent streams, as opposed to some genuine listening mixed in alongside it, requires reconstructing that whole chain after the fact, often years after the streams themselves occurred. The eight million dollars and change that Smith agreed to forfeit represents what investigators could trace and prove, not necessarily the full economic damage of a scheme that ran, by the government's own account, for the better part of seven years. Money moves faster than the systems built to trace it, which is a smaller version of the same production-versus-judgment mismatch running through this entire book: it is far cheaper to generate a fraudulent royalty claim than it is to painstakingly audit one after the fact.

It is tempting, and not entirely wrong, to read the Smith case as a story about one greedy man and a clever scheme, closed out neatly by a plea agreement and a forfeiture order. But the more useful way to read it is as a demonstration of what happens to a shared resource once the cost of producing claims on that resource collapses to nearly zero. Before generative AI, someone attempting this kind of fraud at scale would have needed either a very large budget to pay session musicians and studio time for hundreds of thousands of tracks, which would have made the scheme uneconomical, or a much smaller catalog looped and relabeled, which would have been comparatively easy for the platforms to detect as duplicated content. AI tools removed both constraints at once. They made it cheap to generate music that was different enough, take after take, to avoid simple duplicate-detection, while never requiring a single human musician, engineer, or minute of studio time. The fraud was not made possible by bot networks alone — bot networks streaming fraud existed well before generative AI, run against real, previously released songs — but generative AI is what let the catalog underneath the bot networks scale by orders of magnitude, turning what had been a nuisance-level problem for the industry into something that could plausibly extract eight figures from a shared pool before anyone assembled the full picture.

This is where the argument doubles back on itself in a way worth making explicit. The royalty pool that Smith exploited is, in miniature, exactly the kind of commons this book keeps returning to: a resource that works only because most participants are assumed, reasonably, to be acting in good faith, producing real work for real audiences. Commons like that have always been vulnerable to a small number of actors willing to defect from the good-faith assumption at scale, and economists have a long history of describing what happens when they do, generally under some version of the tragedy of the commons — a shared resource degraded or exhausted because no individual actor bears the full cost of their own overuse. The streaming royalty pool adds a modern twist to that old story: overuse here does not mean literally depleting a fixed physical resource, like overgrazing a shared pasture. It means diluting a fixed pool of money with claims that were never backed by genuine listening in the first place, which functions identically from the perspective of everyone whose share gets smaller as a result.

What stands between a commons like that and collapse has never, in practice, been the honor system alone. It has been some combination of monitoring, verification, and enforcement — in other words, judgment applied at the level of the system rather than the individual transaction. Before generative AI, the sheer cost and difficulty of producing enough fraudulent volume to matter did a great deal of that enforcement work by accident, the same way the cost of producing a manuscript once did gatekeeping work for publishers without anyone designing it that way on purpose. Once that accidental filter falls away, the enforcement has to become deliberate: fraud-detection algorithms sophisticated enough to catch a patient operator rather than only an obvious one, legal frameworks clear enough to prosecute the operators those algorithms do catch, and, underneath both, a continuing human willingness to treat "is this real" as a question worth spending money and attention to answer, even when answering it correctly does not show up as a new feature or a quarterly growth number.

There is a question worth sitting with in the space between the scale of what Smith is alleged to have taken and the scale of what he now faces for taking it. More than ten million dollars in alleged royalties, an agreed forfeiture of just over eight million, and a statutory maximum of five years in prison is a ratio that will strike a great many working musicians, the ones whose royalties were diluted a fraction of a cent at a time to make that sum possible, as strange arithmetic. It is not a ratio unique to this case; federal fraud statutes generally calibrate maximum sentences to the type of offense rather than the dollar amount stolen, which is part of why white-collar sentencing has been a subject of recurring public frustration for decades, long before generative AI made any of this possible. Whether five years is an adequate deterrent against a fraud that can be run from a laptop, using tools that get cheaper every year, against a victim pool too diffuse and too individually under-harmed to organize an outcry, is a genuinely open question, and this book is not going to pretend to settle it. What can be said is that the deterrent value of a prosecution like this one depends heavily on operators like Smith believing, correctly, that the platforms and the government are both willing to spend years of investigative effort chasing a fraud that no single victim experienced as more than a few missing dollars.

There is an uncomfortable question sitting just beneath the surface of the Smith case that the plea agreement does not resolve, because plea agreements are not built to resolve questions like it: how much of this kind of fraud is still running, undetected, on these same platforms, built by people more careful than Smith turned out to be, or simply not yet unlucky enough to be caught. Every fraud prosecution that becomes public is, definitionally, a story about a scheme that failed at the one job schemes like this are built to do, which is to remain invisible. It would be a mistake to read one successful prosecution as evidence that the underlying vulnerability has been closed, in the same way it would be a mistake to read one caught shoplifter as evidence that a store has solved theft. The bot networks and the generative tools that made Smith's version of this fraud possible did not become harder to access after his arrest. If anything, both have become cheaper and more widely available in the years since he is alleged to have started.

It helps to picture the operation not as a criminal mastermind's secret lair but as something closer to an unglamorous small business run on rented infrastructure, because that is closer to how schemes of this kind generally function and it makes the failure of detection more understandable rather than less. Generating hundreds of thousands of songs does not require a studio; it requires an account with one or more AI music-generation services, a script to feed them prompts and variations at industrial speed, and cloud storage to hold the output, all of it billed in the same routine, monthly way any legitimate small business pays for software. Running thousands of streaming accounts does not require thousands of physical phones; it can be done with device emulators, virtual machines, and residential proxy networks that make automated traffic look like it is coming from ordinary homes in ordinary cities rather than from a single server rack, a category of service that has existed openly, marketed to a range of legitimate and illegitimate customers alike, for years. None of the individual pieces of infrastructure Smith is alleged to have used were built for fraud specifically, and most of them likely had entirely legal customers using them for entirely legal purposes at the very same time. The fraud was not in any single tool. It was in the assembly, and in the years of patience required to let an operation built from ordinary parts run long enough to become extraordinary.

None of which is a reason for despair so much as a reason to take the underlying mechanism seriously on its own terms, separate from the particular man whose name is now attached to it. The songs Smith is alleged to have generated were never going to move anyone. They were not written from anything, in the sense that a song usually comes from somewhere — a breakup, a road trip, a Sunday morning, a grievance nursed for a decade and finally set to a melody. They were manufactured to be counted by machines that could not tell the difference, played by other machines built for the same purpose, inside a system that had never had to ask, at scale, whether a stream represented a person, because for the entire previous history of recorded music, it reliably had. The machine at the center of this chapter's title never stopped singing, in the sense that it required no rest, no inspiration, and no audience, only enough plausible variety to avoid detection and enough patience to let the royalties accumulate a fraction of a cent at a time. It stopped, in the end, not because it ran out of songs to generate or accounts to run them through, but because enough investigators, at enough platforms and in one U.S. Attorney's office, eventually decided that finding out whether the plays were real was worth the considerable trouble of checking. That decision — to spend real money and real years verifying what should, in a smaller and more honest system, have been self-evident — is itself a form of the taste this book keeps circling back to, applied not to a wine or a painting but to a royalty statement: the discernment to look at an enormous number and ask, before paying it out, whether anyone actually earned it.
