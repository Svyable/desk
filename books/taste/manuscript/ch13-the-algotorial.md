# The Algotorial

Every Monday morning, before most of the world has finished its coffee, Spotify's servers quietly generate somewhere in the neighborhood of six hundred million individual playlists. Each one is called Discover Weekly. Each one is different. Each one is built for exactly one listener, out of that listener's own history — the songs played twice, the songs skipped after four seconds, the artists whose entire catalogs got worked through in a week of insomnia — cross-referenced against the listening patterns of millions of strangers who share some sliver of that same taste. No person at Spotify chooses what goes into any single one of these playlists. There is no editor assigned to your Discover Weekly. There is no pitch meeting where someone argues for including a particular song. An artist cannot submit a track for consideration, cannot call a contact, cannot buy their way in. The playlist is generated, not curated, in the strict sense of a human being deciding.

This is worth sitting with, because Discover Weekly is arguably the single most successful piece of music curation of the streaming era, measured by the plainest metric available: people keep listening to what it gives them. Spotify has said, in various public statements since the feature launched in July 2015, that Discover Weekly consistently drives some of the highest engagement of anything on the platform, and that a meaningful share of a typical user's new listening in a given month traces back to something that first appeared there. The playlist was built by a small team that included an engineer named Edward Newett, drawing on techniques Spotify had absorbed two years earlier when it acquired a Somerville, Massachusetts music-intelligence company called The Echo Nest — a firm that had spent years building tools to analyze audio content and scrape the internet's running commentary about which artists sounded like which other artists. Discover Weekly folded that analysis together with collaborative filtering, the same basic statistical technique Amazon and Netflix had already made famous: find other listeners whose taste graph overlaps with yours, then hand you the parts of their graph you haven't touched yet. It is, in the most literal sense, a machine that has read the room — millions of rooms — and reports back what it heard.

The playlist did not arrive out of nowhere. Spotify had experimented for years with earlier, cruder attempts at algorithmic recommendation — a "Discover" tab that suggested individual tracks, radio-style stations built off a single seed artist — and had watched engagement on those features stay modest. What changed with Discover Weekly was less the underlying math than the format: instead of an endless, algorithmically generated stream, users got a bounded, thirty-song playlist that refreshed once a week and then sat still, inviting the same kind of attention a person might give a mixtape a friend had made for them. That framing turned out to matter enormously. People did not experience Discover Weekly as a machine output to be sampled and abandoned; they experienced it as something closer to a gift, arriving on a schedule, curated — even though nobody had curated it. The choice to make an algorithmic product feel personal, finite, and considered, rather than infinite and disposable, was itself a kind of design judgment, made by humans who understood something about how people relate to being given things. The algorithm supplied the songs. A set of human product decisions supplied the feeling that someone had thought of you.

Somebody has always done this job. For most of the twentieth century, the person who decided what a stranger would hear next was a radio programmer working from a limited playlist and a gut sense of the local market, or a record store clerk who had opinions and wasn't shy about sharing them, or an MTV executive choosing which videos got rotation in the years when rotation could make a career. These were narrow chokepoints — a handful of radio formats, a few dozen influential stations, one cable channel with real reach — and they were narrow in ways that shut plenty of good music out for reasons that had nothing to do with quality: a program director's fear of losing advertisers, a genre's association with a demographic radio sales teams didn't want to chase, a label's inability to get a record physically into the right hands. Streaming did not invent the idea of a gatekeeper choosing what gets heard. It multiplied the number of gates, changed who was allowed to staff them, and — for the first time — put a genuinely comprehensive record of what listeners actually did, rather than what a sales rep claimed they wanted, directly in front of the people making the calls.

A few floors and several product teams away from Discover Weekly sits a very different kind of playlist. RapCaviar, which launched the same year as Discover Weekly, is built and rebuilt continuously by human editors, and it has functioned for the past decade as one of the most influential single distribution points in American hip-hop. Its early editorial lead, a Spotify staffer named Tuma Basa who had previously worked in music programming at Google Play Music, spent years cultivating relationships with artists, managers, and A&R executives, tracking which songs were catching on in specific cities before the streaming numbers caught up, and making calls about which unreleased or barely-released tracks deserved a slot in front of a national audience. By the middle of the 2010s, RapCaviar had grown into something the hip-hop industry itself treated as a genuine gatekeeper — music writers and label executives, in interviews and trade coverage over the following years, routinely described a RapCaviar placement as capable of moving a song's streaming numbers overnight, in some cases functioning as a bigger lever than a radio add or a television performance. Cardi B's "Bodak Yellow," in the late summer of 2017, is often cited in that coverage as an example of how a song without an established radio infrastructure behind it could ride a combination of social-media virality and prominent playlist placement into a number-one single — a route to the top of the charts that simply did not exist in the same form a decade earlier, and that ran directly through a human editor's decision to feature it early. New Music Friday, another marquee Spotify playlist, follows a similar model on a broader scale: a rotating team of genre specialists sorts through the entire week's new releases and decides, track by track, what belongs on a list that a large fraction of the platform's users will see the moment they open the app on a Friday morning. Spotify has said publicly, across various interviews and reporting on its curation operations over the years, that it employs on the order of fifteen hundred people worldwide in editorial and curatorial roles — a number that is, itself, a strange kind of admission. A company built around one of the most sophisticated recommendation engines in consumer technology still pays roughly fifteen hundred human beings to sit and choose songs by hand.

Inside the company, people who work on the platform's recommendation systems have used a specific compound word to describe how these two modes actually relate to each other in practice: algotorial, a fusion of algorithm and editorial. It names something more precise than "human plus machine." It describes a genuinely blended workflow, in which the same listening data that trains Discover Weekly also flows into the tools RapCaviar's and New Music Friday's editors use — heat maps of which songs are gaining momentum in which cities, early streaming velocity on new releases, the same collaborative-filtering signals that route an unfamiliar song toward a receptive listener. The editors are not working blind, guessing at what people want the way a radio programmer in 1975 might have guessed from record sales and gut instinct. They are looking at real-time data about what is actually happening on the platform, and then making a judgment call on top of it — whether to boost a song the data says is starting to move, whether to hold off on an artist whose numbers look promising but whose moment, in the editor's read of the culture, hasn't quite arrived, whether a song fits the specific emotional register the playlist is known for this month. The algorithm supplies the raw material. The editor decides what it means.

The mechanics of that submission process are more bureaucratic than the word "editorial" tends to suggest. Artists and labels pitch unreleased tracks through a tool called Spotify for Artists, filling in a form that asks about genre, mood, instrumentation, and cultural context weeks before a song's release date; editors work through a queue of these pitches alongside the tracks Spotify's own systems have already flagged as gaining early traction, and make their playlist decisions on a rolling weekly cadence tied to Friday's global release schedule. It is less a scene of inspired discovery than a scene of triage — a queue, a deadline, a limited number of open slots on a limited number of playlists, and a person deciding, track by track, which four-minute submissions are worth the scarcest resource the whole system is built around: a listener's attention in the first ten seconds after the app opens. Framed that way, the editorial side of Spotify looks less like a curator's atelier and more like an admissions office, processing far more applicants than it can accept and trying to make defensible calls under time pressure — a structure this book will return to directly in the story of literary agents doing something recognizably similar with query letters.

Not everything the editorial teams stock is chosen for the reasons a listener would assume, and the exceptions complicate any tidy story about human curators as guardians of cultural judgment. Spotify's mood and functional playlists — lists built around states rather than artists, titles like "Peaceful Piano," "Deep Focus," "Ambient Chill" — became, over the second half of the 2010s, a subject of recurring scrutiny from music journalists and artist advocates, who reported that at least some of the tracks populating these playlists came from production-music arrangements in which composers were paid flat, one-time fees to produce mood-appropriate instrumental music under pseudonymous or little-known artist names, music that could then be streamed at volume without generating the same per-stream royalty obligations as a track from an established artist under a standard licensing deal. Spotify has disputed some characterizations of this practice and denied that it deliberately favors such tracks to save money, but the recurring reporting on it — including extended journalism specifically focused on Spotify's internal curation culture — makes clear that at least part of what looks like editorial taste in these functional categories is doing double duty as a cost-management decision. A playlist called "Peaceful Piano" is, on one level, a human judgment about what will help a listener concentrate or relax. It may also be, on another level entirely, a line item.

The distinction that actually matters here is not human versus machine in some abstract sense. It is what each one is structurally built to do well. A recommendation algorithm, whatever form it takes, is fundamentally in the business of exploiting known preference. It looks at what has already happened — what you played, what people like you played — and extrapolates forward. This is an enormously powerful thing to be good at, and dismissing it would be its own kind of dishonesty. Discover Weekly's core trick, at massive scale, is finding you more of what you have already, in some pattern-matched sense, shown you like — not the same songs, but songs that occupy a similar position in a vast, high-dimensional map of taste that no human curator could hold in their head. That is retention. That is personalization. It is also, not incidentally, discovery in a real sense: plenty of listeners have found genuinely new artists, ones they'd never have encountered otherwise, sitting quietly in a Monday morning playlist that no human being would have had the bandwidth to build for them individually. Two hundred and fifty million people cannot each get a personal music consultant. They can each get an algorithm.

What that same algorithm is structurally bad at is the thing a human editor with a feel for a scene does almost by instinct: placing a bet on something the data does not yet support, because the data cannot exist yet. Collaborative filtering needs signal. It needs plays, skips, saves, some trail of behavior to learn from. A song that dropped four hours ago from an unknown artist in Atlanta has none of that. There is no comparison set, no cohort of similar listeners to borrow from, no established pattern to extrapolate. This is sometimes called the cold-start problem in the recommendation-systems literature, and it is not a minor technical wrinkle — it is close to a structural law. An algorithm trained on the past will always be somewhat conservative about the future, because the future, by definition, hasn't generated any training data yet. A human editor who has spent years going to shows, talking to producers, watching what a particular Atlanta scene has been building toward for eighteen months, can hear that same four-hour-old song and recognize, correctly or not, that it is about to matter — not because a dashboard told them so, but because they understand the shape of a cultural moment that hasn't finished happening. That is precisely the kind of judgment this book keeps returning to: fallible, socially embedded, occasionally wrong, and not yet reproducible by a system that only knows what already occurred.

The same problem repeats itself, in different cultural registers, across every region Spotify operates in, which is part of why the company keeps genre and market specialists on staff rather than one central editorial desk making global calls. An editor covering Afrobeats out of Lagos or London is reading a scene shaped by artist collectives, radio DJs in specific cities, and a slang vocabulary that shifts month to month; an editor covering K-pop is tracking a fandom infrastructure — coordinated streaming pushes by fan organizations, comeback schedules planned a year in advance, an entire promotional grammar built around anniversaries and countdowns — that a listening-history model trained mostly on Western pop patterns will misread by default. A purely global, purely algorithmic system, optimized on aggregate listening behavior, tends to flatten these differences toward whatever pattern is statistically dominant in its training data, which is one of the more quietly documented failure modes of large recommendation systems generally: they are good at finding what is popular within a category they already understand, and worse at recognizing an entirely different logic of popularity operating in a scene the training data underrepresents. A local editor does not have that blind spot, because they were never trying to generalize from the whole platform's behavior in the first place. They were only ever trying to understand one place.

There is a wider ecosystem around Spotify's official playlists worth mentioning here, because it shows how quickly a human-judgment bottleneck attracts people trying to route around it rather than earn their way through it. Independent, high-follower playlists not run by Spotify itself — built by outside curators on the platform's open playlist infrastructure — became, over the late 2010s, targets of a small cottage industry of "playlist plugging" services, in which artists or their publicists pay a fee, sometimes to the curator directly and sometimes through an intermediary platform, in hopes of a placement that will look, to any listener who stumbles onto it, exactly like an organic editorial choice. Some of these placements are legitimate — a curator who genuinely likes a submitted track and charges a modest fee to cover the time spent reviewing hundreds of submissions a week is not obviously doing anything different from what a small magazine does when it charges for review copies. Others are closer to the fraud economy this book has already spent a chapter on: playlists inflated with bot followers, streaming numbers padded to make a placement look more valuable than it is, curators who will feature almost anything for a price. Spotify's official editorial layer, whatever its own compromises around cost and mood playlists, is at least nominally insulated from a direct cash-for-placement transaction. The unofficial layer surrounding it mostly is not, and a listener has no reliable way to tell, from the outside, which kind of "curated" playlist they happen to be looking at.

It would be tidy to end there, with the algorithm handling scale and the human handling novelty, each doing its part in some harmonious division of labor. The actual picture inside Spotify is messier, and worth being honest about rather than flattering. RapCaviar's inclusion of a song has, at various points over the past decade, been documented by music journalists and industry analysts as capable of measurably moving an artist's streaming numbers, sometimes dramatically, in the days after a placement — which means the editors making these calls are not neutral tastemakers observing a scene from a safe distance. They are, whether they experience it this way or not, a market-moving institution, the same way Michelin's inspectors turned out to be a market-moving institution for restaurants once enough diners started trusting the stars. A human editor's judgment about what deserves attention becomes, once it is deployed at the scale of a hundred million monthly listeners, something closer to a decision about what gets to exist commercially at all. The instinct that a listener might romanticize as pure cultural sensitivity is also, unavoidably, gatekeeping with real financial stakes attached, exercised by people who are themselves embedded in relationships with labels, managers, and the artists whose careers ride on the outcome.

The algorithm's side of the ledger has its own uncomfortable complication, one that undercuts any easy story about machines being merely neutral and humans merely biased. In 2021, reporting from music-industry outlets and subsequent Spotify disclosures brought wider attention to a program the company had rolled out called Discovery Mode, which let artists or their labels opt certain songs into a lower royalty rate in exchange for a boost in the algorithm's recommendation weighting — a mechanism some critics inside and outside the industry likened to a form of payola, dressed in statistical language rather than a check handed to a radio DJ. The algorithm, in other words, is not simply reading the room and reporting back what it hears. It can be, and reportedly has been, adjusted by commercial arrangements that have nothing to do with what listeners actually prefer. This does not erase the genuine power of collaborative filtering to surface things a listener will enjoy. It does mean that the clean line between "algorithmic and therefore objective" and "human and therefore biased" was never as clean as either side of that argument wants it to be. Both systems are shaped by money, relationships, and the incentives of the company running them. Neither is a pure instrument of taste.

It is tempting, reading about Discovery Mode, to conclude that the algorithm was therefore never really about taste at all, only about revenue extraction with better math. That conclusion moves too fast in the other direction. Discovery Mode adjusted the weighting given to specific, opted-in tracks; it did not rewrite the underlying collaborative-filtering model that still has to place a song in front of a listener who is at least somewhat likely to want to hear it, or that listener leaves the app, and an artist willing to accept a lower royalty rate for a boost still needed a song good enough, or at least well-matched enough to an audience, for the boost to translate into someone finishing the track instead of skipping it after eight seconds. The commercial layer sits on top of a taste-matching layer; it does not replace it. That is arguably the more useful thing to notice than either the boosterish version of this story or the cynical one: almost none of the systems built to manage abundance, whether staffed by algorithms or by fifteen hundred salaried human beings, turn out to be doing only one job. They are doing a taste-related job and an economic job at the same time, in the same gesture, and untangling which one is driving a given decision is often not possible from the outside, and sometimes not fully possible from the inside either.

What the Spotify case actually demonstrates, stripped of both the company's own promotional language and the reflexive skepticism aimed at anything algorithmic, is a working division of labor arrived at not through some grand design but through years of a company discovering, the hard way, what each of its systems was and wasn't good at. Spotify tried, in its earlier years, to lean harder on pure algorithmic playlisting in some editorial-adjacent contexts and found the results thin — technically coherent, sonically plausible, and somehow missing the thing that makes a scene feel alive to the people inside it. It has also, by its own account, tried to formalize and scale its editorial judgment using the same data infrastructure that powers Discover Weekly, on the theory that a human editor armed with better information makes better calls than one working from gut and relationships alone. Fifteen hundred people is not a small commitment for a company whose founding premise was, in large part, that computation could replace the inefficiencies of the old music industry. It is closest to an admission, made in headcount rather than in a mission statement, that certain judgments about what deserves cultural attention still require someone who has actually been in the room.

There is also a cost asymmetry worth naming plainly, because it is the same asymmetry running underneath this entire book. Generating a Discover Weekly playlist for two hundred and fifty million users costs Spotify computing power, which is cheap and getting cheaper every year. Employing fifteen hundred editors costs salaries, benefits, office space, management overhead, and years of relationship-building that cannot be shortened by buying more server capacity — and that expense has to be justified, every fiscal year, against a recommendation system that keeps improving and keeps getting cheaper to run. The fact that Spotify has not shrunk that editorial headcount to zero, in an era of relentless cost-cutting across the tech industry and repeated rounds of layoffs at the company itself, is a small but real piece of evidence that the editorial function is producing something the algorithm genuinely cannot replicate yet, not merely something a nostalgic executive is protecting out of sentiment. Companies eliminate sentimental expenses quickly. They tend to keep the ones that are still making them money.

None of this settles into a permanent arrangement. The line between what the algorithm can do and what only a human editor can do has moved before and will move again — large language models and more sophisticated audio-analysis tools are already being tested, inside Spotify and at competitors, for tasks that used to require a person listening closely, and some of what currently requires an editor's ear may not in five years. The honest description of where things stand in 2026 is not that human judgment has proven permanently superior, and it is not that the algorithm has proven merely mechanical. It is that two systems with genuinely different structural strengths have been stitched together, imperfectly, inside one company, because neither one alone produced a product good enough to keep two hundred and fifty million people paying a monthly subscription. The algorithm will keep getting better at giving you more of what you already, provably, like. Whether anything will get reliably better at recognizing what a scene is about to become before the data exists to prove it — that is still, as of now, a job description with a human being attached to it.
