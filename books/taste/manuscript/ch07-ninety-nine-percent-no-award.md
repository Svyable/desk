# Ninety-Nine Percent No Award

Robert Hodgson did not set out to embarrass the wine industry. He set out to figure out why his own wine kept losing.

Hodgson had spent his career teaching statistics at Humboldt State University before turning his attention full time to Fieldbrook Winery, the small operation he and his wife had founded in 1976 — the same year, as it happened, that a blind tasting in Paris was quietly upending assumptions about where good wine could come from. Fieldbrook produced only around ten different wines in a given year, the kind of scale where a single competition result can meaningfully move a season's sales. Like most small California wineries, Fieldbrook entered its wines into competitions — county fairs, regional contests, the state fair — because a medal on the label sells bottles, and a small winery without national distribution depends on exactly that kind of validation to get noticed at all. What Hodgson noticed, though, was not that Fieldbrook lost. It was that Fieldbrook's results made no sense from one competition to the next. The same wine, from the same vintage, might take home a gold medal in one contest and receive no recognition whatsoever in another held a few weeks later. A statistician does not have to be paranoid to find that pattern worth investigating. He or she just has to have spent a career being trained not to treat a small, noisy sample as a verdict.

Hodgson sat on the advisory board of the California State Fair Commercial Wine Competition, one of the oldest and most established wine competitions in the United States, tracing its roots back to the 1850s. Sometime around 2005 he proposed an experiment to the competition's organizers, and, with the cooperation of the program's management, they let him run it, repeating the design across roughly four consecutive years of the competition through 2008. The design was simple and, in a way, quietly ruthless. Judges at the competition were arranged into panels of four, tasting flights of about thirty wines apiece and scoring each on the standard medal scale — no award, bronze, silver, gold, double gold. Hodgson arranged to have the same wine, poured from the same bottle, inserted into a given panel's flight three separate times, under three different sample codes the judges had no way of recognizing as identical. Between sixty-five and seventy judges were tested this way in a given year. The only question was whether a judge who scored a wine gold on one pour would score the identical wine gold again later the same day.

Most did not. Across the years of the study, Hodgson found that only about ten percent of judges reproduced their own score, within the same medal category, on all three pours of a given wine. That means roughly nine out of ten judges, tasting the exact same wine under the exact same conditions on the same afternoon, rated it differently at least once — moving it, for instance, from bronze to silver, or silver to nothing at all. A further slice of the judges, also on the order of ten percent, produced swings even more dramatic, scoring the same wine bronze on one pour and gold on another, the full spread of the medal scale collapsing onto a single, unchanging liquid. There was a mild asymmetry buried in the numbers that Hodgson thought worth noting on its own: judges tended to be somewhat more consistent about what they disliked than about what they liked, as if a flaw registered more reliably than a virtue did. A minority of judges did show real, repeatable consistency across all three pours — Hodgson's data did not say nobody could do this job well. It said that the competition, taken as a single instrument, was considerably noisier than anyone selling medals to consumers had much incentive to advertise. When he later ran an analysis of variance across every panel in the study, Hodgson estimated that only about half of the medals awarded across the years traced to anything resembling agreement about the wine's actual quality, with the rest better explained by which judges happened to be seated on which panel that day.

It says something about the California State Fair competition's own confidence in its process that it agreed to host this experiment at all, and then let Hodgson keep running it year after year once the first results came back unflattering. The organizers could have quietly declined a repeat invitation once they saw what the data showed; instead the design continued across roughly four consecutive years, with new panels of judges each time, none of whom were told which samples in their flight were repeats. That the competition kept its own reliability under a microscope for that long, in public, is arguably as notable as anything Hodgson found — a rarer form of institutional honesty than most industries manage when an insider proposes to test whether their own credentialing process actually works.

Hodgson published his results in 2009 in the Journal of Wine Economics, in a paper with the dry academic title "An Examination of Judge Reliability at a major U.S. Wine Competition." He followed it with additional analyses over the next several years, widening the lens from individual judge consistency to the competitions themselves. Working with a larger dataset — in one analysis, more than 2,400 wines that had each been entered into at least three separate American competitions — he looked at how a wine that won gold in one contest fared in the others it had also entered around the same time. Roughly 47 percent of those wines picked up a gold medal somewhere. But of those same gold-winning wines, on the order of 84 percent also received no award at all in at least one other competition they had entered, and in some of his more pointed public comments, drawing on comparisons of wines entered widely across the competition circuit, Hodgson put the figure even higher: something close to 99 percent of gold-medal wines, he found, could be expected to win nothing at all if entered into a different competition. The exact percentage moved somewhat depending on which slice of his data was being described in a given talk or interview, and that movement is itself informative rather than a flaw to paper over — it suggests a genuinely noisy underlying phenomenon being measured a few different ways, not a single tidy number being repeated for effect. One example he cited stuck with people precisely because it needed no statistics degree to parse: a winery that entered the same wine in fourteen separate competitions won a gold medal in exactly one of them, and nothing in the other thirteen. Several gold medal winners entered five separate competitions apiece; not one wine among them won five golds. If gold medals were tracking something stable and identifiable about a wine's quality, a wine excellent enough to win gold once should have a meaningfully elevated chance of winning something again elsewhere. Hodgson's numbers suggested the opposite: winning gold at one competition told you almost nothing about what would happen at the next one, a result statistically difficult to distinguish from competitions handing out medals close to at random.

The finding got attention well beyond the wine trade. The Wall Street Journal, the Guardian, and NPR all covered versions of it over the years following the original paper, usually under some variation of the headline that wine competitions amount to a coin flip. Hodgson himself was more careful than most of the headlines, and it is worth being as careful as he was rather than as the headlines were. He was not arguing that wine quality is illusory, or that some wines aren't genuinely better made than others, or that trained tasters can't tell a well-made wine from a flawed one — competitions do reliably screen out wines with actual technical defects, cork taint, oxidation, volatile acidity, the kinds of flaws that any competent taster will flag consistently, pour after pour. What his data undermined was something narrower and, in its way, more consequential: the idea that among the broad, competent middle of well-made wines — the range where almost all commercial competition entries actually sit — trained judges can reliably rank one above another with enough consistency to justify treating a gold medal as meaningful information rather than a largely random outcome generated by which judges happened to be on which panel that day, tasting in which order, at what point in a long flight when palate fatigue was setting in.

Palate fatigue is itself a well-documented phenomenon and part of the likely mechanism behind Hodgson's numbers. Judges at these competitions often taste dozens of wines within a few hours, and the sensitivity of taste and smell measurably degrades across a long tasting session, particularly for tannin and acidity — the very qualities separating a good Cabernet from a great one, or a competent Chardonnay from a memorable one. Order effects compound it: a wine tasted immediately after a particularly powerful or particularly weak neighbor tends to be rated differently than the same wine tasted in different company, an effect wine competitions have historically done little to control for, since randomizing tasting order across enough repeated pours is expensive and slow in a format built to process a few hundred wines over a single weekend. There is also a subtler possibility worth naming, one Hodgson himself gestured toward: that a wine's quality is not always a single fixed point waiting to be correctly measured, but something closer to a range, genuinely sensitive to the taster's momentary state in a way that makes perfect repeatability an unreasonable standard to begin with. A wine tasted first thing in the morning, on a rested palate, may simply present differently — not incorrectly, just differently — than the same wine tasted as the fortieth pour of a long afternoon. If that is even partly true, then some of what Hodgson measured as "inconsistency" might better be described as a real, if inconvenient, fact about how sensory experience actually works: the same stimulus does not always produce the same experience in the same nervous system, and demanding that it should may be asking biology to behave like a machine. That reading does not rescue the medal system Hodgson was examining — a gold medal is still being sold to consumers as if it reflects the wine rather than the hour of the day it happened to be tasted — but it does soften the harsher, more cynical interpretation that circulated in some of the press coverage, in which unreliable scoring was treated as proof that the judges' opinions were meaningless rather than merely momentary.

None of this is a scandal in the sense of anyone behaving dishonestly. Every judge in Hodgson's study believed, in the moment, that they were rendering an honest and consistent verdict. That is precisely what makes the finding unsettling rather than merely disappointing: these were not bad judges caught being lazy. They were, by any reasonable professional standard, good judges, operating inside a format that was quietly asking more consistency of the human palate than the human palate, fatigued and unblinded from its own prior scores, was able to supply.

Hodgson's basic method — hiding a repeated identical sample inside an otherwise normal set of trials to see whether a taster's judgment holds steady — is not a novelty he invented from scratch. Sensory scientists have long used a related design called the triangle test, in which a panelist is given three samples, two identical and one different, and simply asked to identify the odd one out; it is standard practice in food and beverage quality control, precisely because it is one of the few ways to measure discrimination ability without relying on a taster's own unverifiable claim of confidence. What made Hodgson's version pointed was less the technique than the target: he was not testing whether novices could tell an odd sample out of three. He was testing whether some of the most credentialed wine judges in the United States, operating inside a competition with real commercial consequences, could reproduce their own stated opinion of a single wine when that wine reappeared, unannounced, later the same day.

Hodgson was not the only person to find that formal wine expertise and statistical prediction sit in an uneasy relationship. In 1990, the Princeton economist Orley Ashenfelter published what became known, somewhat mockingly at first, as the Bordeaux equation — a statistical model that estimated the eventual quality and price of a Bordeaux vintage using nothing but weather data from the growing season: winter rainfall, average growing-season temperature, and rainfall at harvest. Ashenfelter's model implied that a numbers-driven outsider with a spreadsheet and a weather record could forecast, years in advance and without tasting a drop, roughly how a vintage would be judged once it was bottled and aged. The wine establishment's most influential critic, Robert Parker, reacted with open contempt, reportedly dismissing Ashenfelter's approach as ludicrous. The model nonetheless went on to anticipate, well ahead of the traditional critical consensus, that the 1989 and 1990 Bordeaux vintages would rank among the finest of the century — a forecast that held up as those wines were later tasted, scored, and priced by the very critics who had been skeptical of a weather chart's right to have an opinion about wine at all. Ashenfelter's equation and Hodgson's repeated pours are aimed at different targets — one at predicting quality before the fact, the other at measuring agreement after it — but they share a common thread: both found that the confidence with which the wine establishment asserted its own judgment exceeded what a more disciplined statistical accounting could actually support.

The reception inside the wine competition world was not uniformly grateful. Some competition organizers and industry writers pushed back on Hodgson's design in the years after publication, arguing that his statistical framework — treating each judge's score in isolation, rather than accounting for how panels actually deliberate and average their impressions together before a final medal is assigned — understated how the system performs in practice, since medals are typically the product of panel consensus rather than any single taster's unreviewed number. There is something to that objection; a single judge's noisy score is not necessarily what determines a wine's fate once four scores get averaged and discussed. But averaging noisy inputs together does not automatically produce a reliable output, and Hodgson's later work on cross-competition correlation was aimed at exactly that consensus-level result rather than any individual judge's raw number, and it found the same instability at that higher level too. The debate itself is worth registering rather than resolving neatly one way, because it is a live methodological argument inside a real field, not a settled matter either side has fully won.

Hodgson's finding eventually found its way into a broader argument about judgment made by people well outside the wine trade. In their 2021 book Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment, the psychologist Daniel Kahneman and his co-authors Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein used wine competitions as one of their illustrations of a distinction they argued gets collapsed too often in professional life: the difference between judges who are simply harsher or more generous than their peers on average, a fairly harmless and easily corrected discrepancy, and judges whose rankings scramble unpredictably from case to case even when their overall average matches everyone else's, a much more corrosive problem because it cannot be fixed by a simple adjustment and is far harder to detect from the outside. Hodgson's judges mostly fell into the second category. A panelist who is consistently two points harsher than average is easy to recalibrate; a panelist who swings a given wine from bronze to gold depending on some combination of order, fatigue, and mood is not offering a correctable bias so much as noise dressed up as a verdict, and Kahneman's broader point — made with examples ranging from criminal sentencing to insurance underwriting to medical diagnosis — is that this second, harder-to-see kind of unreliability turns out to be far more widespread across expert professions than most of those professions like to admit about themselves.

None of this unreliability has stopped medals from working, commercially, more or less as intended. Wineries continue entering the same wines in competition after competition because a gold sticker on a bottle reliably moves it off a crowded shelf faster than an unadorned one, regardless of whether that particular gold reflects a judgment that would survive being re-poured to the same panel an hour later. The medal functions as a signal to a shopper standing in front of thirty bottles with no other information and about four seconds to decide, and a noisy signal is still, for a retailer's purposes, better than no signal at all — which is part of why competitions have had so little commercial incentive to fix a problem their own paying customers, the wineries entering wines, rarely ask them to fix. Hodgson found himself in the odd position of being both a beneficiary of that system, as a small winery owner who needed medals to compete for shelf space against much larger producers, and its most persistent internal critic, publishing the data that undercut the very credibility his own bottles depended on.

It is worth pausing on what Hodgson's work does and does not say about expertise generally, because it is easy to over-read a result like this into a broader claim that credentialed judgment is worthless — a claim the data doesn't support and Hodgson never made. Reliability and validity are separate properties, and confusing them is one of the easiest mistakes to make when a result like this first lands. A measurement can be valid — tracking something real — without being reliable, meaning it produces the same reading under repeated identical conditions. A bathroom scale that gives a different weight every time you step on it within the same five minutes is unreliable regardless of whether "weight" is a meaningful, valid concept; the problem is in the instrument's consistency, not in the underlying idea it is trying to measure. Hodgson's judges may well have been detecting something real and valid about each individual wine in each individual pour — genuine, in-the-moment impressions of a genuinely complex beverage — while still being unreliable across repeated pours, because complex sensory judgment made under time pressure, fatigue, and shifting context is simply a noisier instrument than the marketing language of medals and scores implies. The problem competitions have is not that taste is fake. It is that they built an infrastructure of gold seals and ninety-point scores that presents a genuinely noisy measurement as though it had the precision of a thermometer, and consumers, reasonably, take that presented precision at face value because nothing on the label warns them otherwise.

This is where the honest reader has to hold three separate, true things at once, because none of the last three chapters cancels out the other two, however tempting it is to let the most recent one win. The Judgment of Paris shows that trained taste, stripped of prestige and label, can detect something real enough to overturn an entire industry's assumptions about where quality originates — under blind conditions, with the right controls, expert judgment is not merely decoration sitting on top of reputation, and the result held up across repeated rematches over three decades. Brochet's dyed wine and relabeled Bordeaux show that the same trained perception is remarkably vulnerable to being steered by information that has nothing to do with what's actually in the glass — color, a label, a price, a story, all capable of rewriting what an expert believes they are tasting, a pattern that turns out not to be unique to wine at all. And Hodgson's repeated pours show that even when none of that framing is in play — same bottle, same room, same day, no dye, no fraudulent label, nothing to steer anyone — the same trained expert cannot always reproduce their own verdict. Real, and constructible, and poorly calibrated, all at once, in overlapping populations of trained people and the very same glass of wine. Any account of taste that only tells you one of these three things is not simplifying the picture. It is deleting two-thirds of the evidence.

What Hodgson's work points toward, more than a condemnation of expertise, is a case for taking calibration seriously as its own separate discipline — a case for treating "is this person good at judging wine" and "is this person's judgment reliable enough to trust a single reading from" as two different questions requiring two different kinds of evidence. Some professions have built infrastructure around exactly that distinction. Weather forecasters get scored not just on whether they were right but on how well their stated confidence tracked their actual hit rate over the long run, a discipline called calibration testing that has no real equivalent on a wine competition scorecard. Certain medical specialties use blinded, repeated readings specifically to catch the radiologist whose diagnoses drift depending on what case they happened to read immediately before — the same order effect Hodgson's judges seem to have been susceptible to, applied to a very different kind of expert judgment with considerably higher stakes. Wine competitions, for the most part, have not adopted equivalent discipline, in part because doing so would mean admitting, on the label, that a gold medal is a probabilistic statement rather than a certification — a harder thing to sell than the simple, confident sticker consumers have come to expect. Hodgson kept running his tests for years after the first results came out, kept publishing follow-up analyses, and by his own account saw only modest changes in how competitions actually operate. The medals still get printed. The stickers still go on the bottles. Nothing in the years since has produced convincing evidence that the underlying unreliability has meaningfully improved.

That gap — between what an institution claims to certify and what its own internal reliability can actually support — turns out to be a much bigger problem than wine, and it is worth resisting the temptation to file Hodgson's findings away as a colorful anecdote about a quirky retired professor and his backyard winery. The quirkiness is real. So is the finding, replicated across four years, dozens of judges, and thousands of individual pours, none of which have been seriously overturned by anyone in the fifteen-plus years since the first paper appeared. Every domain now facing a rising volume of things to evaluate, human-made or otherwise, inherits some version of Hodgson's finding as a standing risk: that the credential, the score, the medal, the star rating, can drift loose from the reliability needed to justify it, and that nobody finds out until somebody bothers, the way a retired statistics professor with a small winery in Humboldt County eventually did, to quietly pour the same thing twice and see what comes back.
