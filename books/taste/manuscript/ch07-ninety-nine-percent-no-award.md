# Ninety-Nine Percent No Award

Robert Hodgson did not set out to embarrass the wine industry. He set out to figure out why his own wine kept losing.

Hodgson had spent his career as a statistics professor before retiring to Humboldt County in Northern California, where he and his wife ran Fieldbrook Winery, a small operation producing a few thousand cases a year. Like most small California wineries, Fieldbrook entered its wines into competitions — county fairs, regional contests, the state fair — because a medal on the label sells bottles, and a small winery without national distribution depends on exactly that kind of validation to get noticed at all. What Hodgson noticed, though, was not that Fieldbrook lost. It was that Fieldbrook's results made no sense from one competition to the next. The same wine, from the same vintage, the same bottle even, might take home a gold medal in one contest and receive no recognition whatsoever in another held a few weeks later. A statistician does not have to be paranoid to find that pattern interesting. He or she just has to have spent a career being trained not to treat a small, noisy sample as a verdict.

Hodgson sat on the advisory board of the California State Fair Commercial Wine Competition, one of the oldest and most established wine competitions in the United States, dating back to the 1850s. Sometime around 2005 he proposed an experiment to the competition's organizers, and they agreed to let him run it, repeating the design across four consecutive years of the competition. The design was simple and, in a way, quietly ruthless. Judges at the competition were arranged into panels, and each panel tasted wines in flights, scoring each on the standard medal scale — no award, bronze, silver, gold, double gold. Hodgson arranged, without telling the judges, to pour the same wine to the same panel three separate times across different flights within the same competition. Same bottle, same glass, poured under identical conditions the judges had no way to distinguish from any other pour that day. The only question was whether a judge who had scored a wine gold in the morning would score the identical wine gold again that afternoon.

Most did not. Across the years of the study, Hodgson found that only about ten percent of judges reproduced their own score, within the same medal category, all three times a given wine was poured to them. That means roughly nine out of ten judges, tasting the exact same wine on the exact same day under the exact same conditions, gave it a meaningfully different rating at least once — moving it, for instance, from bronze to silver, or silver to nothing at all. A further slice of the judges, also on the order of ten percent, produced swings even more dramatic: the same wine scored a bronze on one pour and a gold on another, the full spread of the medal scale collapsing onto a single, unchanging liquid. A minority of judges did show real consistency, tight and repeatable across all three pours — Hodgson's data did not say nobody could do this job well. It said that the competition, taken as a single instrument, was much noisier than anyone selling medals to consumers had an incentive to advertise.

Hodgson published his results in 2008 in the Journal of Wine Economics, in a paper with the dry academic title "An Examination of Judge Reliability at a major U.S. Wine Competition." He followed it with additional analyses over the next several years, widening the lens from individual judge consistency to the competitions themselves. Working with data across a number of major American wine competitions, he looked at wines that had won a gold medal in one contest and asked how that same wine had fared in other contests it had also been entered into around the same time. If gold medals were tracking something stable about wine quality, a wine good enough to win gold in one competition should have a meaningfully elevated chance of winning something in another. Hodgson's analysis found close to the opposite: the correlation between a wine's results across different competitions was weak enough to be statistically indistinguishable, in his framing, from what you would expect if the competitions were handing out medals at random. He put a specific, startling number on it that traveled widely once journalists picked up the story: of wines that won a gold medal at one competition, on the order of ninety-nine percent failed to win any award at all at another competition they had also entered. A wine that had just been declared one of the best in the country in one room could walk into another room, unaltered, and be told, in effect, that it wasn't worth mentioning.

The finding got attention well beyond the wine trade. The Wall Street Journal, the Guardian, and NPR all covered it in various forms over the years following the original paper, usually under some version of the headline that wine competitions are a coin flip. Hodgson himself was more careful than the headlines, and it is worth being as careful as he was rather than as the headlines were. He was not arguing that wine quality is illusory, or that some wines aren't genuinely better than others, or that trained tasters can't tell a well-made wine from a flawed one — competitions do reliably screen out wines with actual technical defects, cork taint, oxidation, volatile acidity, the kinds of flaws that any competent taster will flag consistently. What his data undermined was something narrower and, in its way, more consequential: the idea that among the broad, competent middle of well-made wines — the range where almost all commercial competition entries actually sit — trained judges can reliably rank one above another with enough consistency to justify treating a gold medal as meaningful information rather than a roughly random outcome generated by which judges happened to be on which panel that day, tasting in which order, at what point in a long flight when palate fatigue was setting in.

Palate fatigue is itself a real, well-documented phenomenon and part of the likely mechanism behind Hodgson's numbers — judges at these competitions often taste dozens of wines within a few hours, and the sensitivity of taste and smell measurably degrades across a long tasting session, particularly for tannin and acidity, the very qualities separating a good Cabernet from a great one. Order effects compound it: a wine tasted immediately after a particularly powerful or particularly weak neighbor tends to be rated differently than the same wine tasted in different company, an effect wine competitions have historically done little to control for, since randomizing tasting order across enough repeated pours is expensive and slow in a way that judging a few hundred wines over a weekend does not comfortably accommodate. None of this is a scandal in the sense of anyone behaving dishonestly. Every judge in Hodgson's study believed, in the moment, that they were rendering an honest and consistent verdict. That is precisely what makes the finding unsettling rather than merely disappointing: these were not bad judges caught being lazy. They were, by any reasonable professional standard, good judges, operating inside a format that was quietly asking more consistency of the human palate than the human palate, fatigued and unblinded from its own prior scores, was able to supply.

It is worth pausing on what Hodgson's work does and does not say about expertise generally, because it is easy to over-read a result like this into a broader claim that credentialed judgment is worthless — a claim the data doesn't support and Hodgson never made. Reliability and validity are separate properties, and confusing them is one of the easiest mistakes to make when a result like this first lands. A measurement can be valid — tracking something real — without being reliable, meaning it produces the same reading under repeated identical conditions. A bathroom scale that gives you a different weight every time you step on it in the same five minutes is unreliable regardless of whether "weight" is a meaningful, valid concept; the problem is in the instrument's consistency, not in the underlying idea it's trying to measure. Hodgson's judges may well have been detecting something real and valid about each individual wine in each individual pour — genuine, in-the-moment impressions of a genuinely complex beverage — while still being unreliable across repeated pours, because complex sensory judgment made under time pressure, fatigue, and shifting context is simply a noisier instrument than the marketing language of medals and scores implies. The problem competitions have is not that taste is fake. It's that they built an infrastructure of gold seals and ninety-point scores that presents a genuinely noisy measurement as if it had the precision of a thermometer, and consumers, reasonably, take that presented precision at face value because nothing on the label warns them otherwise.

This is where the honest reader has to hold three separate, true things at once, because none of the last three chapters cancels out the other two, however tempting it is to let the most recent one win. The Judgment of Paris shows that trained taste, stripped of prestige and label, can detect something real enough to overturn an entire industry's assumptions about where quality originates — under blind conditions, with the right controls, expert judgment is not merely decoration on top of reputation. Brochet's dyed wine and relabeled Bordeaux show that the same trained perception is remarkably vulnerable to being steered by information that has nothing to do with what's actually in the glass — color, a label, a price, a story, all capable of rewriting what an expert believes they are tasting. And Hodgson's repeated pours show that even when none of that framing is in play — same bottle, same room, same day, no dye, no fraudulent label, nothing to steer anyone — the same trained expert cannot always reproduce their own verdict. Real, and constructible, and poorly calibrated, all at once, in the very same population of people and the very same glass of wine. Any account of taste that only tells you one of these three things is not simplifying the picture. It is deleting two-thirds of the evidence.

What Hodgson's work points toward, more than a condemnation of expertise, is a case for taking calibration seriously as its own separate discipline — a case, in other words, for treating "is this person good at judging wine" and "is this person's judgment reliable enough to trust a single reading from" as two different questions requiring two different kinds of evidence. Some professions have built infrastructure around exactly that distinction: forecasters get scored not just on whether they were right but on how well their stated confidence tracked their actual hit rate over time; certain medical specialties use blinded, repeated readings specifically to catch the radiologist whose diagnoses drift depending on what case they read immediately before. Wine competitions, for the most part, have not adopted equivalent discipline, in part because doing so would mean admitting, on the label, that a gold medal is a probabilistic statement rather than a certification — a harder thing to sell. Hodgson kept running his tests for years after the first results came out, kept publishing, and by his own account saw only modest changes in how competitions operate. The medals still get printed. The stickers still go on the bottles. The ninety-nine percent, as far as anyone has since re-measured it, has not obviously improved.

That gap — between what an institution claims to certify and what its own internal reliability can actually support — turns out to be a much bigger problem than wine. Every domain now facing an increase in things to evaluate, human-made or otherwise, inherits some version of Hodgson's finding as a standing risk: that the credential, the score, the medal, the star rating, can drift loose from the reliability needed to justify it, and that nobody finds out until somebody bothers, the way a retired statistics professor with a small winery in Humboldt County eventually did, to pour the same thing twice.
