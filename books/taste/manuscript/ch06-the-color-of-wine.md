# The Color of Wine

Frédéric Brochet was a doctoral student at the University of Bordeaux's Faculty of Oenology in the late 1990s, working under Gil Morrot, when he decided to test something that sounds, described plainly, almost too simple to publish: whether the words wine experts use to describe a wine come from the wine, or from what the experts already believe about the wine before it reaches their mouth.

Bordeaux is not a casual place to ask that question. The city's entire economy, and a meaningful part of France's cultural self-image, rests on the premise that trained tasters can perceive real, describable differences between wines, and that those differences map onto genuine variation in soil, climate, vine age, and winemaking skill — the whole apparatus the French call terroir. Brochet's faculty trained the very experts whose judgment underwrote that premise. He was, in effect, proposing to test his own field's founding assumption from the inside.

The first experiment, run in 1996 and eventually published in 2001 in the journal Brain and Language, was almost absurdly direct. Brochet recruited fifty-four wine experts — enology students, wine journalists, sommeliers, wine buyers, people who did this for a living or were training to. He gave each of them two glasses of wine to describe using the standard vocabulary of wine tasting. One glass held a white wine. The other, the tasters were told, held a red. In fact both glasses held the exact same white wine. Brochet had taken a portion of it and dyed it red with a neutral, flavorless, odorless food coloring — a substance with no taste of its own and no known effect on the wine's actual chemistry, added specifically to remove color as a confound while leaving smell and taste untouched.

If color were cosmetic — if what the tasters were actually perceiving lived in their nose and mouth, independent of what their eyes told them — the descriptions of the two glasses should have converged, since the underlying liquid was identical. They did not converge. They diverged almost completely. The panel described the dyed glass in the vocabulary reserved for red wine: one taster praised its "jamminess," another its note of "crushed red fruit," others reached for descriptors like cherry, blackcurrant, and the tannic structure ordinarily used for red wines. Not one of the fifty-four experts identified the red-dyed glass as a white wine wearing a costume. Brochet and Morrot went further, using a statistical technique to compare the vocabulary the same tasters applied to the actual white wine against the vocabulary they applied to the dyed version, and found the overlap was closer to what you would expect between a white wine and a genuine red than between two versions of the same white. Their sense of smell was, on paper, receiving the same molecules in both glasses. Their brains reported something categorically different, because their eyes had already decided what they were about to taste.

The result unsettles something people assume about wine tasting specifically and expert perception generally: that whatever specialized vocabulary experts have built up — the whole taxonomy of nose, palate, finish, tannin, minerality — describes information arriving through smell and taste, filtered and reported by an unusually well-calibrated instrument. Brochet's dyed wine suggests the instrument is not so cleanly separable from the frame around it. Vision, in this case a single manipulated variable, was overriding olfaction and taste outright, not merely coloring their edges. The experts were not lying, and they were not incompetent; if anything, this is a study about people whose training had made their palates unusually responsive to expectation, because that training had spent years reinforcing the association between color and the vocabulary that goes with it. A novice with no trained associations to draw on might, perversely, have been harder to fool.

Brochet's second experiment, run around the same period and reported in the same body of published work, took a different route to a related conclusion. Rather than manipulating a sensory cue like color, he manipulated a piece of pure information: the label. He took a single, unremarkable mid-range Bordeaux — the kind of wine that occupies the ordinary middle of any wine shop's shelf, priced to be forgettable — and served it to a panel of experts twice, on separate occasions, under two different identities. On one occasion the bottle was labeled as an everyday vin de table, an ordinary table wine, the kind of designation that carries no prestige and invites no particular expectation. On the other occasion the identical wine was presented in a bottle labeled as a grand cru classé, the kind of designation reserved for Bordeaux's most celebrated, most expensive estates.

The panel's ratings were not slightly different. They were close to inverted. Under the humble label, tasters rated the wine as thin, unbalanced, and forgettable — roughly what you would expect a panel of professionals to say about a wine deliberately chosen for its lack of distinction. Under the grand cru label, the same liquid, poured from an identical stock, drew praise: tasters called it complex, well-structured, and worth the attention such a label implies. Woodiness that one taster had flagged as a flaw in the "table wine" version became, in the "grand cru" version, evidence of appropriate oak aging. The wine had not changed in the bottle. The story attached to the bottle had changed, and the story rewrote what people reported detecting.

Put the two results side by side and something uncomfortable comes into focus for anyone who had just finished admiring the Judgment of Paris. In May 1976, nine of France's most credentialed palates, tasting blind, produced a set of rankings that overturned the assumed hierarchy of French versus Californian wine — a result that only makes sense, only counts as evidence of anything, if we believe those palates were tracking something real about the wine in the glass rather than something they had been told about it. Brochet's Bordeaux, twenty years later and a few hundred kilometers away, demonstrates that those same kinds of trained palates can be walked, with total confidence and zero awareness of the manipulation, into describing a white wine as a red one and a mediocre wine as a superb one, purely on the strength of a color additive and a printed label.

Both things are true, and neither one erases the other. That is the uncomfortable part, and it is worth resisting the urge to resolve it into something tidier than it is. One tempting resolution says the Paris tasting proves taste is real and Brochet proves taste is fake, and that the honest conclusion splits the difference — taste is "partly real." That is a way of sounding balanced without actually explaining anything. A more useful way to hold both results is to notice that they were testing different things. The Paris judges were deprived of exactly the cues Brochet weaponized: no labels, no color information distinguishing origin, wines served from unmarked glasses specifically so that expectation could not attach itself to a bottle before the first sip. Under those conditions, whatever the judges were tracking had to come from the wine itself, because nothing else was available to track. Brochet's experiments did the opposite: they supplied a false cue — a dyed color, a false pedigree — and then measured how completely that false cue was allowed to overwrite everything else the senses reported. The Paris tasting shows what trained judgment can do when the context is stripped away. Brochet shows what happens to that same judgment when a false context is supplied instead. Neither experiment ran the other's condition, which is exactly why they don't contradict each other so much as bracket the same open question from opposite sides.

The uncomfortable implication is that most real-world tasting, unlike either Spurrier's carefully blinded room or Brochet's carefully rigged one, happens somewhere in between — with some cues hidden, some visible, some subtly present in ways nobody in the room has bothered to control for. A wine critic reviewing new releases usually knows the producer, the vintage, the price, and the appellation before the glass reaches the table. A sommelier recommending a bottle to a table of diners is operating inside a restaurant that has already signaled, through décor and prices and the weight of the wine list itself, what kind of evening this is supposed to be. None of these professionals are frauds for working under those conditions — it is simply the condition under which almost all real tasting occurs, and Brochet's work suggests that condition is not a neutral backdrop. It is an active ingredient.

There is a broader psychological literature that predates and surrounds Brochet's specific wine experiments, on what researchers sometimes call sensation transference or expectation-driven perception — the general finding that people's reported experience of taste, pain, quality, and even the passage of time shifts measurably based on framing that has nothing to do with the physical stimulus. Brochet's contribution was to run that broader phenomenon through the most self-consciously rigorous tasting culture in the world and get results just as vulnerable to it as anyone else's. The Bordeaux faculty was not testing a population of amateurs prone to suggestion. It was testing people whose entire professional identity rested on the claim that their palate could see past exactly this kind of manipulation, and it could not.

None of this means the vocabulary of wine tasting is meaningless, or that expertise is a kind of elaborate performance with nothing underneath it. Brochet himself continued to work in oenology rather than concluding that the field's core claims were bunk; his experiments are more often read within the field as a caution about methodology — a reason blind tasting protocols exist and get enforced at serious competitions in the first place — than as a debunking of taste itself. If tasting were pure suggestion with no underlying signal, blind conditions would not have produced the Paris result at all; a suggestible palate with nothing to detect would have simply guessed randomly, or default to guessing in favor of the country in the room, rather than converging, as several judges did, on the same specific bottles. Something in the liquid was doing work in Paris. Something outside the liquid was doing work in Bordeaux. The honest position is that both forces are always present, in different proportions, and that a great deal of what separates a reliable taster from an unreliable one is not raw sensitivity but the degree to which they have learned to notice when the label, rather than the glass, is doing the talking.

That distinction — between what a person can perceive and what they can perceive despite being told otherwise — turns out to matter for reasons well beyond wine. It is close to the exact problem now facing anyone trying to evaluate writing, images, or arguments in a world where the origin of a piece of content, human or generated, is no longer visible on its surface the way a red dye or a grand cru label is visible on a bottle. The label problem did not stay in Bordeaux. It became, within a generation, the default condition of nearly all judgment.

Even granting all of that, one more complication was still waiting, and it did not concern expectation or framing at all. It concerned something more basic: whether trained experts, even when nobody is dyeing their wine or swapping their labels, can be trusted to agree with themselves.
