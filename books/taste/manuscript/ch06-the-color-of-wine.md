# The Color of Wine

Frédéric Brochet was a doctoral student in oenology at the University of Bordeaux in the late 1990s, working alongside his advisor Gil Morrot and the respected oenologist Denis Dubourdieu, when he decided to test something that sounds, described plainly, almost too simple to have needed a university laboratory: whether the words wine experts use to describe a wine come from the wine, or from what the experts already believe about the wine before it reaches their mouth.

Bordeaux is not a casual place to ask that question. The city's entire economy, and a meaningful part of France's cultural self-image, rests on the premise that trained tasters can perceive real, describable differences between wines, and that those differences map onto genuine variation in soil, climate, vine age, and winemaking skill — the whole apparatus the French call terroir, the same concept whose stronger claims had just taken a public dent a generation earlier at the Judgment of Paris. The Bordeaux classification system that Brochet's own university trains people to interpret and defend, the 1855 hierarchy of First through Fifth Growths that still shapes which chateaux can charge which prices, rests on the assumption that a trained taster, given the wine alone, can perceive the gradations that hierarchy claims to describe. Brochet's own faculty trained the very tasters whose professional judgment underwrote that premise. He was, in effect, proposing to test his field's founding assumption from the inside, using colleagues and students as his subjects, in the same city, working from the same classification system, that had just watched a foreign panel embarrass a piece of its own mythology a generation earlier.

The first experiment, run in 1996 and eventually published in 2001 in the journal Brain and Language under the authorship of Morrot, Brochet, and Dubourdieu, started with a baseline. Brochet recruited fifty-four students of oenology — twenty-seven men and twenty-seven women, all of them well along in specialized training that most casual drinkers never receive — and gave each of them a glass of red wine and a glass of white wine to describe, using whatever vocabulary came naturally. The results were unremarkable in the way a control condition is supposed to be unremarkable: the white drew descriptors like floral, honey, peach, and lemon, while the red drew raspberry, cherry, cedar, and chicory, a fairly conventional split between the language reserved for white wines and the language reserved for red. This was the baseline the real experiment would be measured against.

A week later, Brochet brought the same panel back and, without telling them, changed the terms of the exercise. He took a portion of the same white wine used in the earlier session and dyed it red, using a neutral additive with no flavor, no odor, and no known effect on the wine's underlying chemistry — a substance chosen specifically to alter color and nothing else, so that anything the tasters reported about smell or taste could only be coming from the wine itself, filtered through their expectations, and not from the dye interfering with the liquid directly. He served this dyed glass alongside the genuine red from the earlier session and asked the panel to describe both.

If color were cosmetic — if what the tasters were perceiving lived in their nose and mouth, independent of what their eyes told them — the language applied to the dyed white should have resembled the language the same panel had applied to real white wine the week before, since the underlying liquid was chemically identical to itself. It did not. The panel described the dyed glass using the same vocabulary they had just used for actual red wine: raspberry, cherry, cedar, the language of red fruit and tannic structure rather than the honeyed, citrus vocabulary they had used for the same wine, undyed, seven days earlier. Brochet and his co-authors ran a statistical comparison of the descriptive terms and found the overlap between the dyed white and the genuine red closer than the overlap between the dyed white and its own undyed self. Their noses were receiving identical molecules in both sessions, a week apart. Their brains reported something categorically different, because their eyes had already decided, the instant the glass reached the table, what kind of wine they were about to taste.

The result unsettles an assumption people make about wine expertise specifically and expert perception generally: that specialized vocabulary — the whole taxonomy of nose, palate, finish, tannin, minerality — describes information arriving through smell and taste, filtered and reported by an unusually well-calibrated instrument. Brochet's dyed wine suggests the instrument is not so cleanly separable from the frame sitting around it. Vision, a single manipulated variable that should have had no bearing on flavor at all, was overriding olfaction and taste outright rather than merely coloring their edges. The oenology students were not lying, and they were not unusually careless; if anything, this is a study about people whose years of training had made their palates unusually responsive to the association between color and vocabulary, because that training had spent years reinforcing exactly that association as a shortcut to fast, confident description. A novice with no trained associations to draw on might, perversely, have had a harder time being fooled, precisely because nothing in their palate had learned yet to expect red wine to taste a certain way once it looked a certain way.

Brochet's second experiment, reported in the same body of published work, took a different route to a related conclusion. Rather than manipulating a sensory cue like color, he manipulated a piece of pure information: the label. He took a single, unremarkable Bordeaux — a middling wine, the kind that occupies the ordinary center of any wine shop's shelf, priced and made to be forgettable — and served it to fifty-seven participants under two different identities in two separate sessions. In one session the bottle was labeled as an everyday vin de table, an ordinary table wine carrying no prestige and inviting no particular expectation. In the other, the identical wine, drawn from the same stock, was presented in a bottle labeled as a grand cru, the designation reserved for Bordeaux's most celebrated, most expensive estates.

The ratings were not merely different. They were close to opposite. Under the table-wine label, the panel's language clustered around weak, short, light, flat, and faulty — a fair description of an unambitious, everyday bottle. Under the grand cru label, the identical liquid drew agreeable, woody, complex, balanced, and rounded, the kind of vocabulary that shows up in tasting notes for wines that cost ten times as much. Brochet's team ran the same statistical clustering technique on the tasting notes from this second experiment that they had run on the color data, and found the same pattern of near-total inversion: participants were not simply grading the grand cru version a bit higher across a shared set of qualities, they were reaching for close to opposite descriptive categories depending on the label, treating the same measurable characteristics — the same oak influence, the same tannic grip, the same finish — as evidence of either fault or craftsmanship purely on the word printed on the glass in front of them. When Brochet asked participants directly whether each wine was worth drinking, forty of the fifty-seven said yes for the grand cru label. Only twelve said yes for the same wine under the table-wine label. The wine had not changed between sessions. The word printed on the bottle had, and the word rewrote what a majority of trained tasters reported detecting, down to specific, opposite adjectives for the same oak influence — a flaw under one label, evidence of skillful aging under the other.

Put the two results side by side and something uncomfortable comes into focus for anyone who had just finished admiring the Judgment of Paris. In May 1976, nine of France's most credentialed palates, tasting blind, produced a set of rankings that overturned the assumed hierarchy of French versus Californian wine — a result that only makes sense, only counts as evidence of anything, if we believe those palates were tracking something real about the wine in the glass rather than something they had been told about it beforehand. Brochet's Bordeaux, roughly two decades later and a few hundred kilometers from Paris, demonstrates that those same kinds of trained palates can be walked, with total confidence and zero awareness of the manipulation, into describing a white wine as a red one and a mediocre wine as a superb one, purely on the strength of a color additive and a printed word.

Both things are true, and neither one erases the other. That is the uncomfortable part, and it is worth resisting the urge to resolve it into something tidier than it actually is. One tempting resolution says the Paris tasting proves taste is real and Brochet proves taste is fake, and that the honest conclusion splits the difference — taste is "partly real." That is a way of sounding balanced without actually explaining anything. A more useful way to hold both results is to notice that they were testing different conditions. The Paris judges were deprived of exactly the cues Brochet later weaponized: no labels, no color information distinguishing origin, wines served from unmarked glasses specifically so that expectation could not attach itself to a bottle before the first sip. Under those conditions, whatever the judges were tracking had to come from the wine itself, because nothing else was available to track. Brochet's experiments did the opposite: they supplied a false cue — a dyed color, a false pedigree — and then measured how completely that false cue overwrote everything else the senses reported. The Paris tasting shows what trained judgment can do when context is stripped away. Brochet shows what happens to that same judgment when a false context is supplied instead. Neither experiment ran the other's condition, which is exactly why the two studies do not so much contradict each other as bracket the same open question from opposite sides.

The uncomfortable implication is that most real-world tasting, unlike either Spurrier's carefully blinded room or Brochet's carefully rigged one, happens somewhere in between — with some cues hidden, some cues visible, and some subtly present in ways nobody in the room has bothered to control for. A wine critic reviewing new releases usually knows the producer, the vintage, the price, and the appellation before the glass reaches the table. A sommelier recommending a bottle to a table of diners is working inside a restaurant that has already signaled, through décor and menu prices and the physical weight of the wine list itself, what kind of evening this is supposed to be. None of these professionals are frauds for working under those conditions; it is simply the condition under which almost all real tasting occurs, and Brochet's work suggests that condition is not a neutral backdrop against which perception operates cleanly. It is an active ingredient in the perception itself.

Brochet's findings did not stay confined to wine research, and they connect to a broader body of work on price and framing that makes his results look less like a quirky one-off and more like a specific instance of something general about how expectation and sensation interact in the brain. In 2008, a team led by the neuroscientist Hilke Plassmann, working with colleagues including Antonio Rangel at Caltech, published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences that ran a version of Brochet's label experiment inside an fMRI scanner. Participants tasted wines while told each one carried a different price, when in fact the same wine was sometimes labeled cheap and sometimes labeled expensive. Told a higher price, participants not only reported the wine as more pleasant; the researchers measured increased activity, correlated with those self-reports, in the medial orbitofrontal cortex — the same reward-related brain region that shows up, for entirely different reasons, in the neuroaesthetics research on beauty perception a few chapters ahead in this book. The finding suggests that Brochet's tasters were not making a simple reporting error, dressing up an unchanged experience in fancier words to seem consistent. The price tag, or the label, appears able to reach further back than language, into the neural machinery that generates the felt experience of pleasure itself, before any vocabulary gets attached to it at all.

There is a broader psychological literature, predating and surrounding both Brochet's and Plassmann's work, on what researchers sometimes call sensation transference or expectation-driven perception — the general finding that people's reported experience of taste, quality, pain, and even the passage of time shifts measurably based on framing that has nothing to do with the underlying physical stimulus. Some of this literature reaches back to marketing research from well before either study, work on how packaging and brand names shift reported taste in blind versus labeled conditions across categories as mundane as margarine and beer, long before anyone had thought to put a wine glass or a soft drink inside an fMRI scanner to watch the effect happen at the level of a single brain region rather than only in a survey response. What the wine and cola experiments add to that older tradition is not the discovery of the phenomenon but a considerably sharper picture of where in the perceptual chain it operates — not at the level of a person consciously deciding to say something flattering about an expensive-looking bottle, but earlier, in whatever combination of memory and expectation generates the raw sensation a person then has no choice but to report honestly, because as far as their own nervous system is concerned, it is honest. Brochet's specific contribution was to run that broader phenomenon through the most self-consciously rigorous tasting culture in the world and get results just as vulnerable to it as anyone else's. The Bordeaux oenology faculty was not testing a population of amateurs prone to suggestion. It was testing people whose entire professional identity rested on the claim that their trained palate could see past exactly this kind of manipulation, and on this particular test, in this particular design, it could not.

The two headline experiments were, in a sense, the dramatic proof of a pattern Brochet had already found sitting quietly inside the ordinary published record of wine criticism, before he ever dyed a glass or swapped a label. A large part of his doctoral work involved feeding years of professional tasting notes — the kind that run in wine magazines and back-label copy, written by people who had no idea they would end up as data — into a piece of French text-analysis software called ALCESTE, designed to find statistical clusters of words that tend to occur together across a large body of text. Run across several separate collections of published tasting notes, the software kept finding the same dominant pattern: the single strongest organizing axis in professional wine vocabulary was not vintage, not region, not even quality, but color. Descriptors for aroma clustered overwhelmingly around objects that happened to share the wine's own color — red wines described by reference to red fruits, red flowers, red spices, white wines by reference to yellow and green ones — a pattern strong enough to show up in the aggregated language of professional critics who had never sat for one of Brochet's rigged tastings and had no dye anywhere near their glass. The lab experiments demonstrated, under controlled conditions, a bias that a separate strand of the same research had already found sitting in plain sight in the accumulated public writing of the wine trade.

A caveat belongs here, because the story of Brochet's dyed wine has been retold so often, in so many popular accounts, that it has picked up a layer of imprecision worth stripping back off. The fifty-four subjects in the color experiment were not working sommeliers or professional critics; they were undergraduates and advanced students in the university's oenology program, people considerably more trained than an average drinker but not yet the credentialed "experts" that headline writers, over the following decade, kept calling them. That distinction matters, and getting it right does not weaken the finding so much as sharpen what it actually shows. These were people early enough in their training that color-based expectation had already taken firm hold of their descriptive vocabulary, well before they'd finished becoming the kind of taster whose judgment a wine competition would later put a medal behind. Brochet himself, in interviews given after the study drew attention, was more measured than the press coverage that followed it, noting that a small minority of tasters — on the order of a few percent, by his own account — did register the underlying white wine correctly despite the color. The finding was never that no one can see through the trick. It was that the overwhelming majority of a trained population, tested under ordinary conditions, did not.

The phenomenon Brochet documented in a Bordeaux lab turns out not to be a peculiarity of wine, or of France, or of any specialized vocabulary at all. In 2004, the neuroscientist Read Montague and colleagues published a study in the journal Neuron that ran a structurally similar test on a much more ordinary pair of beverages: Coca-Cola and Pepsi. Subjects tasted both colas under two conditions, once with no brand information and once knowing which sample was which. Blind, the group split close to evenly, with roughly half preferring Pepsi — a result consistent with decades of blind cola taste tests going back to the original Pepsi Challenge marketing campaigns of the 1970s and 1980s. Once the brand was revealed, the preference shifted hard toward Coke, with roughly three-quarters of subjects now reporting that the Coke sample tasted better, and the fMRI data showed a corresponding change in activity in the brain's reward circuitry, not just in what people said out loud. The physical liquid in the cup had not changed between conditions. The two letters printed on the label had, and that alone was enough to reroute both stated preference and the underlying neural response. Brochet's grand cru label and Montague's Coca-Cola logo are doing recognizably the same thing to two very different populations tasting two very different beverages, which suggests the mechanism is not really about wine snobbery, or French culture, or trained palates specifically. It is closer to a general feature of how brains that already hold an expectation process a sensation that arrives to confirm or contradict it.

None of this means the vocabulary of wine tasting is meaningless, or that expertise is an elaborate performance with nothing underneath it. Brochet himself continued to work in oenology and viticultural science rather than concluding that the field's core claims were bunk; his experiments are more often read within the field as a methodological caution — a reason blind tasting protocols exist and get enforced at serious competitions in the first place — than as a debunking of taste itself. If tasting were pure suggestion with no underlying signal, blind conditions would not have produced the Paris result at all; a suggestible palate with nothing real to detect would have simply guessed randomly, or defaulted to favoring the country in the room, rather than converging, as several judges did across repeated blind tastings over three decades, on many of the same specific bottles. Something in the liquid was doing work in Paris. Something entirely outside the liquid was doing work in Bordeaux. The honest position is that both forces are always present, in different proportions depending on the conditions, and that a meaningful share of what separates a reliable taster from an unreliable one is not raw sensitivity but the degree to which a person has learned to notice when the label, rather than the glass, is doing the talking.

The wine trade's own response to this general problem, developed over decades and mostly independent of Brochet's specific papers, has been to build training around exactly the vulnerability his experiments expose rather than pretend it away. Professional certification bodies such as the Court of Master Sommeliers teach a highly structured method of blind tasting — moving systematically through appearance, nose, and palate in a fixed order, naming specific, falsifiable observations at each stage before allowing oneself to guess at grape, region, or vintage — precisely because an unstructured taste-and-declare approach leaves far more room for exactly the kind of top-down contamination Brochet documented. Candidates for the Master Sommelier diploma, one of the most demanding blind-tasting credentials in the wine trade, spend years drilling this discipline against wines poured with no label in sight, in study groups that exist mainly to practice catching one's own assumptions before they calcify into a wrong answer stated with confidence. The existence of that entire training apparatus is itself a kind of institutional admission: the trade knows its own instrument drifts under the influence of expectation, and has built a corrective practice around that knowledge rather than a denial of it. The correction does not eliminate the vulnerability Brochet found. It manages it, the way a pilot's checklist manages, rather than cures, the fact that human attention is fallible under routine conditions.

That distinction — between what a person can perceive and what they can be talked into perceiving — turns out to matter for reasons well beyond wine. It is close to the exact problem now facing anyone trying to evaluate writing, images, or arguments in a world where the origin of a given piece of content, human-made or generated, is no longer visible on its surface the way a red dye or a grand cru label was visible, once you knew to look, on Brochet's bottles. A reader told a passage was written by a celebrated novelist tends to find more to admire in it than the same reader given the same passage with no name attached — a version of the label experiment that shows up constantly, unstudied and unremarked, in ordinary cultural life. The label problem did not stay in Bordeaux. It became, within a generation, one of the default conditions of nearly all judgment, wine included, well before anyone was asking a machine to produce the thing being judged in the first place.

Even granting all of that, one more complication was still waiting, and it did not concern expectation or framing at all. Brochet's subjects, after all, were being actively deceived — a dye in the glass, a false word on the label, a specific manipulation introduced by an experimenter who knew exactly what he was testing for. Take away the dye. Take away the false label. Give a group of genuine professionals the same wine, honestly presented, with nothing hidden and nothing misrepresented, and ask them only to agree with themselves. That, it turns out, is a separate and in some ways more basic question, and a retired statistics professor with a small winery in Northern California was about to answer it in a way the wine trade did not enjoy hearing.
