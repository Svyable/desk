# Cultivating Taste

Robert Hodgson did not stop judging wine after he published the results described earlier in this book. He kept serving as a judge at the California State Fair Wine Competition, and he kept using its recurring format — pouring flights of hundreds of wines past panels of credentialed tasters over long, tiring days — as a running experiment on the very instrument he had shown to be so unreliable. What changed, in the years after his study became known inside the wine world, was that some competitions began quietly building the same trap he had used to expose the problem into their normal operating procedure: identical wines, poured twice under different labels within the same flight, specifically so organizers could keep a private, ongoing record of which judges' scores held steady across the repeat pour and which did not. Nobody expelled the inconsistent judges or announced the results publicly the way Hodgson had. But the tool he had built to catch the profession's blind spot became, for the people willing to use it, a way of watching their own instrument drift, flight after flight, year after year, rather than trusting it by reputation alone.

That is a small, unglamorous fact, and it is a good place to end this book, because it is the only kind of answer the previous nineteen chapters actually support to the question they keep raising. Taste, across all the evidence this book has gathered, refuses to sit still long enough to be called one thing. It is real enough that nine French judges, tasting blind in a Paris hotel room in 1976, could overturn a hierarchy of national prestige that had stood for a century, using nothing but their own palates. It is constructible enough that Frédéric Brochet could take those same kinds of trained palates and walk them, with total confidence, into describing a white wine as a red one. It is unreliable enough that Hodgson's own credentialed judges could not reproduce their own scores when the identical wine came around again in the same afternoon. It appears to be grounded, at least in part, in a shared neurological reward system that Semir Zeki found lighting up in strikingly similar patterns across people looking at very different paintings, and it is, at the very same time, a learned instrument of social sorting that Pierre Bourdieu spent a career showing reproduces class position from one generation to the next, often most effectively when the people exercising it are least aware that is what they are doing. None of those findings cancels out any of the others. They describe, from five different angles, an actual human capacity that is genuinely powerful, genuinely fallible, genuinely trainable, and genuinely political, all at once — a capacity durable enough that entire institutions have been built for a century or more just to manufacture and sell a trustworthy version of it, and fragile enough that a French wine critic, an American state fair judge, and a Getty Museum curator have each, at different times, gotten a confident verdict badly wrong.

What Hodgson's continued judging adds to that picture is the one ingredient the earlier chapters mostly documented by its absence: feedback. Judgment of Paris was a single afternoon with no rematch built in; nobody scored the nine judges against a later, agreed-upon truth about which wines actually aged better, because there was no mechanism for that comparison to happen automatically. The Getty's kouros sat on display for years while experts disagreed, with no test anyone could run that would settle the matter cleanly, and in a real sense it still sits there unsettled today. Most ordinary exercises of taste share that same structure: a critic pans a book that later finds a devoted readership, a talent scout passes on a band that becomes influential, a reader trusts a five-star average that turns out to be manufactured, and in the overwhelming majority of these cases nobody ever goes back to check the earlier verdict against what actually happened, because there is no institutional habit of doing so and no reward for the extra work involved. Hodgson's repeat-pour trick is interesting precisely because it manufactures, artificially and deliberately, the one thing most taste-exercising professions never get for free: a fast, honest, hard-to-fake signal telling the judge whether their own last verdict was actually consistent with itself.

There is a substantial and much older body of research, running well beyond wine, suggesting that this specific ingredient — structured practice paired with fast, honest feedback — is close to the only reliable way anyone gets measurably better at judgment over time, as opposed to merely feeling more confident in it. The general finding, associated with decades of research on expert performance across fields from chess to medicine, is that raw years of experience alone predict surprisingly little about how good someone's judgment eventually becomes; what predicts improvement is the presence or absence of a tight loop between making a call, finding out relatively soon whether the call was right, and adjusting the next call accordingly. Radiologists who see the confirmed outcome of a diagnosis shortly after making it improve measurably faster than radiologists working in settings where confirmation, if it comes at all, arrives months later and gets filed somewhere they never see it. The clearest large-scale demonstration of the same principle applied to something closer to the informal, everyday judgment this book has been describing came out of a multi-year forecasting tournament run for the American intelligence community and studied most closely by the psychologist Philip Tetlock, whose Good Judgment Project recruited thousands of ordinary volunteers to make probabilistic predictions about world events and then tracked, meticulously, how often each forecaster's stated confidence matched what actually happened. A small number of participants, whom Tetlock came to call superforecasters, turned out to substantially and consistently outperform professional intelligence analysts working with access to classified information the volunteers never saw. What set the superforecasters apart, in Tetlock's account, was not raw intelligence or specialized subject knowledge so much as a specific set of habits: breaking big, vague questions into smaller checkable ones, actively seeking out views that contradicted their own instinct before settling on a number, revising that number in small increments as new information arrived, and — the part most relevant here — keeping score, deliberately and uncomfortably, on their own track record, so that being wrong actually cost them something they could see and correct for next time.

Almost nothing about ordinary cultural consumption in an age of infinite content works this way, and it is worth being specific about why, rather than treating the absence of feedback as an abstract complaint. A recommendation algorithm gets a very fast, very honest signal, but the signal it gets is engagement — did you click, did you keep watching, did you finish the song — which is a real measurement of something, just not of whether the choice was actually good for you in any sense that outlasts the next ten minutes. That is the same conservatism problem this book's earlier chapters describe from two directions: a streaming service's algorithm, optimized on exactly this kind of immediate engagement signal, tends to narrow toward the comfortable and familiar rather than the difficult and rewarding, and a generative model trained through human preference comparisons learns to produce whatever a rater approves of on a fast first look, for reasons this book's chapter on machine taste has already laid out in some detail. Both systems are, in Tetlock's terms, receiving feedback that is fast but not honest about the thing that actually matters — a mirror image of the wine judges' problem, which was honest feedback that mostly never arrived at all. An ordinary reader or listener, meanwhile, usually gets no structured feedback loop whatsoever: nobody tracks whether the book they abandoned after twenty pages would have paid off by page two hundred, or whether the artist they dismissed on first listen was actually doing something they would have valued more on a second, slower pass. The muscle that Judgment of Paris showed can render real, testable verdicts, and that Hodgson showed can drift out of calibration without anyone noticing, is, for most people, simply never exercised against any standard that would let them find out which kind of judge they currently are.

It is possible to say something concrete about what deliberately building that feedback loop back in might look like, without pretending the answer reduces to a checklist, because the honest cases in this book's earlier chapters were never checklist cases either. For an individual reader or listener, the nearest equivalent to Hodgson's repeat pour or Tetlock's forecasting scorecard is simply keeping some private, working record of your own reactions before you know what anyone else thought — writing down, even briefly, what you expect to think of a book before you read the reviews, or how many stars you would have given a restaurant before checking its rating, and then going back, weeks or months later, to see how often your instinct held up and in which specific direction it tended to be wrong. Bourdieu's warning matters here as much as anywhere else in this book: an untrained, unexamined preference is not automatically more honest or more democratic than a trained one; it is simply less aware of where it came from. Deliberately tracking your own calibration does not purify your taste of the class position, the upbringing, or the social circle that shaped it in the first place — nothing does that — but it can at least show you where your snap judgments are reliable and where they are not, in the same modest, checkable way Hodgson's repeat pours showed which judges could hold a line and which could not. Seeking out real disagreement matters for a related reason: a person whose entire cultural diet arrives through a single algorithmically narrowed feed, or through a single social circle that already shares their assumptions, has no equivalent of the panel of nine differently trained French palates in that Paris hotel room, disagreeing with each other in ways specific enough to be checked against an outcome. Genuine exposure to people who are reliably right about different things than you are, and who will tell you plainly when they think you are wrong, functions as an outside check that an isolated feed, however personalized, cannot supply, because personalization is specifically engineered to minimize exactly that kind of friction.

Something similar can be said, more speculatively, about Zeki's neuroaesthetic findings. If beauty really does engage a shared reward circuit across strikingly different kinds of paintings and different viewers, as Zeki's scans suggested, that circuit is still only being fed whatever a person actually spends time looking at, and an algorithmic feed narrows that diet by design, on the entirely reasonable theory that giving people more of what already triggered the reward signal last time is the surest way to keep them scrolling. A record store clerk with strong, argued opinions, a library's new-arrivals shelf organized by a librarian rather than a purchase history, a friend who drags you to a film you would never have chosen — all of these were, without anyone describing them this way at the time, low-tech mechanisms for feeding that same neural machinery something it had not already selected for itself, which is a specific kind of friction a fully personalized feed is built to remove. Losing access to that friction by default, rather than by choice, is one of the quieter costs of letting recommendation do all of the selecting, and one of the more concrete things an individual retains some power to correct simply by choosing, deliberately, to spend part of their attention somewhere a recommendation engine did not send them.

For an institution or a platform, the equivalent discipline is less private and considerably harder to enforce, because it requires treating a gatekeeping role as something closer to a public trust than a proprietary asset — an idea this book's chapter on the newer, paid forms of discernment left open rather than resolved, and one worth returning to directly here. Michelin's anonymous inspectors, rotating restaurants and paying their own bills specifically so no single relationship could quietly bend a verdict, and Spotify's decision to keep paying human editors to sit beside an algorithm that could, on pure engagement numbers, have replaced them years ago, are both, in their own limited commercial way, structural acknowledgments that unaccountable, unexamined judgment degrades over time exactly the way Hodgson's uncalibrated wine judges did — and that the fix is not to abandon human judgment but to build institutional habits that keep checking it against something outside itself. A platform, a magazine, or a newsletter that wants to cultivate rather than merely exercise its own taste has to be willing to do the equivalent of what Hodgson did to his own profession: build in a mechanism, even an uncomfortable one, for finding out when its own verdicts were wrong, and take the finding seriously enough to change something afterward. That is a higher bar than most institutions currently clear, including some celebrated in this book's earlier chapters, and Bourdieu's underlying warning — that the people doing the sorting rarely experience their own sorting as an exercise of power, precisely because it feels to them like simple good taste — applies with undiminished force to every new gatekeeper described in the chapter before this one, not only to the old ones.

Trader Joe's own famous discipline, described earlier in this book, works on close to the same principle, translated into inventory rather than prediction markets. A store that caps itself at roughly four thousand items cannot afford to let a mediocre product linger on the shelf out of habit or sentiment the way a supermarket carrying forty thousand items can; every item is implicitly re-auditioning for its own shelf space against sales data that arrives quickly enough to act on, and a product that stops earning its slot gets rotated out and replaced, repeatedly, as an ordinary operating rhythm rather than a rare, dramatic decision. That is a corporate version of the same discipline Hodgson built for himself and Tetlock's volunteers built through the forecasting tournament: a structure that makes the cost of an uncorrected bad call show up quickly, in a form the people responsible cannot easily ignore or explain away. None of these institutions achieved that discipline by hiring people with unusually good taste and trusting them to keep it forever. They built a system that kept testing the taste against results and gave the people exercising it very little room to hide from what the results said.

None of this is an argument for turning back the clock, and it would be dishonest for this book to end by implying otherwise, because the flood of cheap AI-generated content this book opened by describing has real, underdocumented upsides that a purely nostalgic account of gatekeeping would prefer to skip past. A self-published author with no connections to New York publishing, the specific kind of outsider this book's earlier chapter on the slush pile shows had a genuinely difficult time getting a fair reading from traditional gatekeepers for most of the twentieth century, can now put a finished book in front of readers without first satisfying an agent's sense of what sells, or a query letter's conformity to an unstated house style shaped, as Bourdieu would predict, by the tastes of people who mostly went to the same handful of schools. A musician with no capital and no relationship to a label can record and distribute a song the same afternoon they write it. Cheap AI-assisted tools have made illustration, editing, translation, and basic design available to people who could never have afforded to hire a professional for those tasks before, expanding who gets to attempt creative work in the first place even as it complicates, as this book's later chapters have shown at length, who gets properly credited or paid for it once the attempt succeeds. A visual artist with a physical disability that makes fine motor control difficult, a non-native English speaker who has always had sharper ideas than sentences, a teenager in a town with no art school and no gallery within driving distance — all of these people now have some working version of a tool that used to require years of specific, often expensively acquired technical training just to operate at all, and some real fraction of what pours into this book's opening chapters as undifferentiated slop is, from the vantage point of whoever made it, the first time in their life they have been able to put a mental image in front of other people at all. The old, gatekept world this book's earlier chapters describe was not simply a purer one that generative AI has now polluted; it excluded people for reasons that had little to do with the quality of their work and a great deal to do with the specific, documented biases of the gatekeepers doing the excluding, from publishing's own well-documented preference for manuscripts that resembled what had already sold, to quiet luxury's carefully calibrated system of signals that only the already-initiated could recognize as prestigious in the first place. A society that spends the next decade being nostalgic for that arrangement will have misremembered it.

The honest position, consistent with nearly everything this book has actually found rather than what would make a tidier ending, is that both things are true at once and neither cancels the other: the flood has genuinely democratized who gets to make things, and it has genuinely degraded the average reliability of the signal telling anyone which of those things is worth their limited time, in exactly the way this book's opening chapters on slop, streaming fraud, and the Kindle flood describe in granular detail. Fixing the second problem by re-closing the first one — restoring some earlier generation's gatekeepers to their old monopoly on deciding what counts — would trade a flood for a wall, and this book has spent too many pages documenting what stood behind that wall, and who it kept out, to recommend rebuilding it. The more interesting and more difficult project, which is really what cultivating taste has to mean if the phrase is going to mean anything more than a magazine's lifestyle section, is building new instruments for telling better from worse that carry Hodgson's discipline and Tetlock's scorekeeping into a world that no longer has the luxury of a slow, expensive production bottleneck doing that filtering for free, by accident, the way the twentieth century's economics quietly did for most of the institutions this book has described. That project does not have a finished design yet, and this book has not tried to hand the reader one; what it has tried to do is show, case by case, which components of that design have already been tested somewhere — a repeat pour, a forecasting scorecard, an anonymous inspector, a four-thousand-item ceiling, a newsletter that refuses affiliate money — so that whoever eventually assembles the fuller version is not starting from nothing.

There is a version of this chapter that would end by handing the reader a short list of things to go do differently on Monday morning, and it is worth saying plainly why that is not this chapter. Every taste-testing panel, forecasting tournament, and calibration exercise described in these pages worked, when it worked, because the people involved kept doing it — repeatedly, across years, past the point where it stopped feeling like a novel intervention and became simply how they operated. A single resolution to keep a prediction journal, made while finishing a book about the subject, resembles nothing so much as the New Year's resolution it will almost certainly come to resemble in practice. What actually distinguished Hodgson from an average wine judge, or a superforecaster from an average pundit, was not a better set of intentions but a sustained, unglamorous willingness to keep checking, long after the checking had stopped being interesting, which is a much harder thing to recommend than it is to describe. It is also, not coincidentally, the same quality this book's earlier chapters found lacking in the specific institutions that failed most visibly — the wine competitions that never adopted a repeat pour, the museum that took decades to update a single disputed label, the platforms that chose engagement over difficulty because engagement was easier to measure by the following Monday.

Steven Spurrier, setting up his tasting room at the InterContinental Hotel in the spring of 1976, did not think he was doing anything more consequential than marking an anniversary with a pleasant afternoon of wine and conversation. Nine people wrote numbers on nine sheets of paper, under conditions specific enough that the numbers meant something, and the numbers rearranged an entire industry's sense of where quality could come from, for decades, because of a decision nobody involved understood as historic while they were making it. That is close to the only reassurance this book has to offer at the end of an argument that has otherwise tried hard not to manufacture reassurance where the evidence did not support it: individual, well-conditioned acts of discernment have mattered enormously before, in ways their own participants did not anticipate, and there is no evidence in front of us that they have stopped mattering now, only evidence that they have gotten harder to render carefully and easier to render carelessly, at the exact moment more of them are needed than ever. Whether the next several decades produce more Hodgsons, quietly rebuilding their own instruments after finding them faulty, or simply more noise mistaken for signal because nobody built in a way to tell the difference, is not a question this book, or any book, gets to answer in advance. It is a question being decided, in small, mostly invisible increments, every time someone looks at two things placed side by side and has to decide, honestly, which one is actually better — and then, harder still, has to decide what to do the next time they find out they were wrong.

Somewhere tonight, a person will open a feed, a shelf, or a stream built to hand them the next thing before they have finished deciding what they thought of the last one, and will make a small, unrecorded judgment that nobody will ever check against an outcome, and will move on. There is nothing unusual about that moment; it happens billions of times a day, and it happened, in slower and more expensive forms, for as long as there have been people choosing what to read, hear, and believe. What is unusual, and still new enough that nobody has finished deciding what to make of it, is how much cheaper it has become to fill every one of those moments with something built to be chosen, and how little of what fills them has been tested against anything at all. This book has not tried to settle whether that is a crisis or simply a new and unfamiliar weather system to learn to live inside. It has tried to show what is actually at stake in the choosing, so that the next small, unrecorded judgment — yours, or the next reader's, or the person building whatever platform makes the next round of these decisions for a billion people at once — gets made with some sense of what it is actually testing, even if no one is ever going to hand back a score.
