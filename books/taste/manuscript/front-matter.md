# Taste

*How Human Judgment Becomes the Scarcest Resource in an Age of Infinite Content*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

In 2025, the editors of Merriam-Webster gave their Word of the Year to a term nobody would have chosen to be proud of: slop. Not sludge, not spam, not noise. Slop — the word people had started using, without being told to by any style guide, for the specific texture of AI-generated video, AI-generated music, AI-generated books, and AI-generated arguments that were technically coherent and somehow still nutritionally empty. A British programmer named Simon Willison had popularized the term the year before, in a blog post trying to name a feeling a lot of people already had and hadn't found a word for yet. By the time the dictionary caught up, the feeling had a name, a shape, and a business model.

This book starts from an unfashionable premise: that the flood of slop is not really a story about artificial intelligence. It is a story about supply and demand finally separating, after two hundred years of assuming they moved together.

For most of industrial history, the thing that made a piece of writing, music, medicine, or furniture rare was the cost of making it. A novel took a year. A song took a studio, a band, and a pressing plant. A trustworthy medical opinion took a decade of training. Scarcity of production kept scarcity of judgment mostly invisible, because you couldn't get very much output past the first bottleneck to find out whether the second one — is this any good, is this true, is this worth my remaining hour on Earth — was going to bind next.

Generative systems removed the first bottleneck almost overnight. A man in North Carolina named Michael Smith proved exactly how overnight: prosecutors say that between 2017 and 2024 he generated hundreds of thousands of songs with AI tools and then used networks of bot accounts to stream them billions of times, netting more than ten million dollars in royalties before the Southern District of New York caught up with him. He did not need to write hundreds of thousands of good songs. He needed volume, and generative software will sell you volume by the truckload, indifferent to whether anyone will ever want to hear the two hundred thousandth track.

Once production stops being the constraint, the second bottleneck — judgment — is the only one left standing. This book is about what happens to a civilization that spent two centuries treating that second bottleneck as a background assumption, and has now been handed, all at once, the bill for having never really priced it.

I am calling that second bottleneck taste, in the old, slightly disreputable sense of the word: the capacity to tell better from worse, in circumstances where the difference cannot be reduced to a formula, and where the person making the call will sometimes be wrong. Taste in this sense is not a synonym for elitism, and it is not a synonym for personal preference either, though it gets confused with both. It sits in the uncomfortable space between them — trained, fallible, socially shaped, and still doing real work that formulas cannot yet do.

The book does not treat taste as mystical. Quite the opposite: much of what follows is an attempt to take a famously vague word and drag it toward evidence. There is a chapter about the year French judges, tasting blind, chose Californian wine over their own country's best bottles and revised the entire world wine market by accident. There is a chapter about the psychologist who discovered that expert wine judges cannot reliably reproduce their own scores when the same wine is poured for them twice. There is a chapter about the art historians who took one look at a $10 million Greek statue and felt, before they could say why, that something was wrong — and were right, against the museum's own scientific tests. There is a chapter about the neuroscientist who found that beauty, however different the paintings or the cultures, appears to activate the same patch of the human brain. None of these stories resolves into a tidy formula for good judgment. Together they sketch its actual, contradictory shape: partly learned, partly biological, partly a matter of social position, and stubbornly resistant to being fully automated even by systems that are, on other measures, extraordinarily capable.

The second half of the book follows that shape into institutions built specifically to manufacture and sell trustworthy judgment at scale: a tire company that invented restaurant criticism to sell more tires; a Pasadena grocery chain that makes more money per square foot than any competitor by refusing to stock more than four thousand items; a streaming service that pays roughly fifteen hundred human editors to do a job its own recommendation algorithm cannot. Each is a working answer to the same question this book keeps returning to: when anyone can produce anything, who gets paid to say what is actually worth your attention, and why do we trust them.

The last part turns toward what is coming rather than what has already happened. Generative systems are not just flooding the world with content; they are increasingly being trained, through human feedback, to imitate the judgment they are also busy overwhelming. That loop — machines learning taste from the very evaluators whose job the machines are making harder — is one of the strange, underexamined mechanisms of the years just ahead. I have tried to describe it honestly rather than dramatically, including the places where automated curation is already better than human curation, which are more numerous than most defenders of human judgment want to admit.

None of this is a nostalgia project. The flood of cheap content has genuine, underrated upsides, several of which appear in these pages. But a society that spends the next decade unable to tell a curator from a spammer, a considered opinion from a fluent one, or a real photograph from a plausible one, will have made itself poorer in a currency it forgot it was still spending. This book is an attempt to look at that currency directly, while there is still time to decide how we want to mint it.
