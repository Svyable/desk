# The Slop Tide

The people who run the Merriam-Webster dictionary do not choose a Word of the Year to be clever. They choose it by watching what word millions of people actually typed into the dictionary's own search bar, over and over, at a rate that could not be explained by curiosity about spelling. In 2025 that word was slop. Not a coinage the editors invented and hoped would catch on. A word that had already caught on, in kitchens and comment sections and group chats, long before anyone with a title like lexicographer got around to noticing.

Slop was doing a specific job that no existing word did quite as well. Spam had always meant unwanted; junk had always meant worthless; fake had always meant untrue. Slop meant something narrower and, in its way, more unsettling: content that was none of those things, exactly. It was often perfectly readable. The image was often perfectly rendered, the video perfectly lit, the argument perfectly structured with an introduction and three supporting points and a conclusion that restated the introduction. It had the shape of something somebody had made. What it lacked was the sense that anybody had meant it — that a specific person, at a specific moment, had cared enough about a specific true thing to put in the work of saying it well. It was food-like. It filled the mouth. It did not nourish.

The word did not originate with the dictionary, and it did not originate in 2025. A British software developer named Simon Willison had been writing about it more than a year earlier, in the spring of 2024, on a blog he had kept for years as a working log of his own projects and observations about the software industry. Willison did not discover a new phenomenon so much as he named an old feeling accurately. People had been drowning in mediocre, machine-produced text and images for a while by then — on social media, in search results, occasionally in their own inboxes — without a compact way to say what was wrong with it that didn't sound either paranoid or snobbish. "AI-generated content" was accurate and useless, a phrase built for a press release. "Slop" did the work in one syllable. It borrowed the image, deliberately, from the word's oldest sense: the watery, low-grade feed poured into a trough for animals that will eat whatever is put in front of them because they have no other choice. The metaphor was not subtle, and it was not meant to be. It was aimed at machines producing without judgment, but it landed just as hard on the audiences being fed as if they, too, had no other choice.

There is a precedent for this kind of lag between a phenomenon and the word that finally pins it down, and it is worth pausing on because the precedent ran for decades rather than months. "Spam," as a term for unwanted, mass-produced junk, did not originate with computers. It came from tinned meat, and then from a Monty Python sketch in which a group of Vikings drown out every other word of a restaurant's conversation by chanting the product's name, until the dialogue itself becomes unintelligible under the noise. Early internet users borrowed the word in the 1980s and 1990s for exactly that experience — a signal being buried under a flood of repetitive, low-cost, unwanted material sent by someone who paid almost nothing to send it and did not care whether any individual recipient wanted it. Naming the problem did not solve it. It took the better part of two more decades of spam filters, blacklists, federal legislation like the CAN-SPAM Act of 2003, and constant arms-race engineering between senders and platforms before email inboxes became survivable again, and even now the fight has never fully ended, only reached a rough equilibrium. Willison's choice of "slop" to describe the AI equivalent was not an accident of vocabulary. It was a deliberate echo, a bet that the new flood would need the same kind of sustained, unglamorous institutional response the old one eventually got, and that pretending otherwise would waste the years in between.

By the time the dictionary made it official, "slop" had stopped being a clever observation on a niche blog and had become descriptive of daily life for a very large number of people who had never read Willison's post and had never needed to. A parent scrolling a video platform to find something for a six-year-old to watch. A shopper on a marketplace site trying to tell a real product review from a fabricated one. A student handing in an essay that read like a student's essay because it had, in fact, been produced by something trained to sound like a slightly above-average student. A job seeker's cover letter, an author's book description, a musician's promotional bio, a company's quarterly blog post about "leveraging innovation" — texture, at this point, more than content. Fluent. Structured. Correct at the level of the sentence and often hollow at the level of the paragraph.

In 2025, a group of researchers at Cornell University published findings on how much of this material was actually accumulating across parts of the open web — the ordinary, ungated internet of articles, images, and forum posts that search engines still crawl and that other machine-learning systems are, in turn, trained on. Their conclusion was not that AI-generated material had replaced human writing outright. It was narrower and, in the context of what came after, more consequential: that AI-generated "slop" was beginning to overwhelm meaningful portions of that open web, filling categories of content — product listings, low-stakes articles, image search results — fast enough to change what a person, or another machine, would actually encounter when it went looking. The internet had always contained noise. What the Cornell researchers were describing was something closer to a shift in the ratio, the moment when a lake that had always had some sediment in it started silting up in earnest.

The detail that made this finding land harder than an ordinary complaint about internet quality was who else was drinking from that lake. Search engines crawl the open web to answer queries; increasingly, so do the very language models producing the slop in the first place, since each new generation of these systems is trained in part on fresh text scraped from the internet after the previous generation was released. A web filling up with fluent, ungrounded machine output is not just an inconvenience for a human being trying to find a real recipe or a real review. It is a feedback loop, in which the tool doing the polluting is also the tool that has to keep drinking the water afterward. Researchers studying this problem had already given it a bloodless technical name — model collapse, the gradual degradation that can occur when a system trains too heavily on its own outputs rather than on the messier, more varied record of things people actually wrote and meant. Whether that degradation becomes a serious constraint on the technology's future is still an open question the field has not settled. What is not in question is the underlying image: a system built to imitate human judgment, increasingly forced to learn from a world in which its own unjudged output makes up a larger and larger share of the available evidence.

There is an asymmetry buried in all of this that is easy to state and hard to fully absorb. The systems producing slop can scale without limit, in the narrow sense that matters commercially: run more servers, rent more compute, and the output multiplies accordingly, with no meaningful ceiling imposed by human fatigue, training time, or the number of studios available on a given afternoon. The systems that have to receive and evaluate that output cannot scale the same way, because they are, for now, still mostly people, and people have exactly twenty-four hours in a day and a finite amount of attention within them. A reader can read only so many books before dying. A listener can listen to only so many songs before falling asleep, going to work, or simply losing interest. This is not a new fact about human beings; it has been true since before writing existed. What is new is that, for the first time, the supply side of nearly every attention-based market has been decoupled from any similarly binding constraint, which means the old, comfortable assumption that supply and demand would naturally find some sane equilibrium — that nobody would bother producing vastly more content than an audience could plausibly absorb, because production cost would stop them first — has quietly stopped holding. Production no longer polices itself. Something else has to.

None of this, on its own, would be a book. Complaints about the quality of mass media are older than mass media itself; there were essays fretting about the death of serious reading being written when the technology under suspicion was the paperback. What makes the current version of the complaint different is not that the content is bad. Plenty of pre-AI content was bad. What is different is the volume at which bad, mediocre, and passable content can now be produced, and the fact that the volume increased by orders of magnitude in a period best measured in months rather than decades. And that change in volume, once you sit with it, turns out to be a change in something much older and much less discussed than content quality. It is a change in which of two very old bottlenecks is doing the binding.

Here is the premise the rest of this book is built on, and it is worth stating plainly once, because everything that follows is really just this claim examined from different angles and different centuries. Making anything of value — a book, a song, a medical diagnosis, a chair, a legal argument — has always required two separate kinds of labor. The first is producing it: writing the pages, recording the take, running the tests, cutting the wood. The second is judging it: deciding whether the thing that got produced is any good, whether it should be published, released, prescribed, sold, or believed. For almost the entire history of industrial civilization, the first kind of labor was so expensive, so slow, and so limited in supply that it functioned as a natural rationing device on the second. You could only judge what had already been made, and only a finite amount of anything could be made in a given year, because making things required capital, time, trained hands, and physical materials that did not multiply for free.

The pattern is old enough to predate every technology this book is actually about. Before Johannes Gutenberg's press reached wide use in Europe in the mid-fifteenth century, a single book might represent months of a scribe's labor, which meant that judgment about which texts deserved that labor — which manuscripts a monastery or a wealthy patron chose to have copied — mattered enormously, because copying was so expensive that almost nothing got copied by accident. The press did not eliminate that judgment step; it relocated it. Once a text could be set in type and struck off by the hundreds, the expensive decision moved from "is this worth a scribe's months" to "is this worth a printer's paper, ink, and the risk of unsold stock sitting in a warehouse," a calculation still costly enough that most printers remained highly selective for centuries afterward. Each subsequent leap in production technology — steam-powered presses in the nineteenth century, offset printing in the twentieth, digital typesetting at its end — made producing a book cheaper and faster than the leap before it, and each time, the industry's actual gatekeeping simply moved to whatever step remained expensive: distribution, marketing, shelf space in a finite number of physical stores. The bottleneck kept migrating. It never actually disappeared, because there was always some remaining scarce resource for it to hide inside.

A publisher in 1965 received manuscripts through the mail, a few dozen a week if the house was a serious one, because writing and typing a full manuscript took a person the better part of a year and most people who started one did not finish it. A record label in 1975 could release only as many albums as its studios, pressing plants, and promotion budgets allowed, because a professional recording required booked studio time, session musicians, and physical vinyl manufactured in a factory with finite capacity. A hospital could produce only as many trained diagnosticians as its medical schools and residency programs could turn out, at a pace measured in years per doctor, because that was how long it took to build the judgment those diagnoses actually required. In every one of these systems, the editors, the A&R representatives, the hospital credentialing committees existed and did real work, exercising real discernment, rejecting things, being wrong sometimes in both directions. But their workload was capped by a mechanism upstream of them that nobody had designed for that purpose and that nobody thought of as a filter, because it did not look like one. It looked like the ordinary cost of doing business. The scarcity of judgment was real the entire time. It simply never had to announce itself, because the scarcity of production was doing the rationing first, and doing it for free.

What generative artificial intelligence did, starting in earnest in the early 2020s, was remove the first bottleneck almost completely, for an enormous range of goods, in the space of a few years. It became possible to produce a plausible song, a plausible book chapter, a plausible product photograph, a plausible legal brief, a plausible customer review, at a marginal cost close to zero and at a speed that had no meaningful floor. The systems did not have to be excellent to change everything. They had to be adequate, and cheap, and fast, and available to anyone with an internet connection and no particular expertise. The typing bottleneck, the studio bottleneck, the years-of-training bottleneck: all of them, across wildly different industries that had never had reason to think of themselves as facing the same problem, buckled at roughly the same time, for roughly the same reason.

Once that happens, the second bottleneck — the one that had been quietly doing its job, uncelebrated, since before the printing press had fully matured into an industry — is the only one left standing. It cannot be rationed by production cost anymore, because production no longer costs anything worth mentioning. It has to be rationed by something else, or it will not be rationed at all, and the result of a resource going unrationed is not that everyone gets enough of it. The result is that the resource collapses under demand it was never built to serve, the way a bridge built for foot traffic collapses when someone drives a truck across it — not because trucks are evil, but because the bridge's capacity was always finite and had simply never been tested.

That second, previously invisible bottleneck is what this book is about, and it deserves a name sturdier than "quality control" or "curation," both of which sound like corporate functions rather than what they actually are: an old, human, fallible, culturally specific capacity to tell better from worse in situations where the difference cannot be settled by a ruler or a stopwatch. The name for that capacity, in English, has existed for centuries, and it is a word that makes a lot of serious, well-meaning people uncomfortable the moment it is used sincerely. The word is taste.

It is worth being precise about what that word does and does not mean here, because "taste" carries a great deal of unhelpful baggage into any conversation it enters, and clearing that baggage out now will save a great deal of trouble later. Taste is not a polite synonym for elitism. Historically, claims about good taste have absolutely been used to sort people by class, and that history is real and matters and gets its own examination later in this book — the sociologist Pierre Bourdieu spent a career demonstrating how thoroughly a person's aesthetic preferences track their social position, and there is no honest way to talk about taste without acknowledging that his critique landed. But the fact that taste has been misused as a weapon for social sorting does not mean it reduces to nothing but a weapon for social sorting, any more than the fact that intelligence testing has a shameful history of misuse means that some people are not, in fact, better than others at solving certain kinds of problems.

Nor is taste simply a fancy word for preference. Preference is symmetrical and needs no defense: if you like the sweeter wine and I like the drier one, we are both simply reporting true facts about our own nervous systems, and neither of us is in error. Taste claims something riskier. It claims that, within a domain, some choices, performances, and judgments are more skillful, more resonant, more durable, or more likely to reward attention than others — not universally and not eternally, but reliably enough, within a community of people who have put in the time to develop calibrated judgment, that the claim can be tested and can turn out to be wrong. A sommelier who says a wine is flawed is not merely disclosing a personal reaction. She is making a claim that other trained palates can check, and sometimes overturn, the way a chess player's claim that a move is a blunder can be checked by other chess players and sometimes overturned. That testability, and that fallibility, are the whole point. Taste in this sense is not an assertion of superior status. It is a skill, developed through exposure, feedback, and error, that gets better with practice and can also go wrong, be captured by fashion, be fooled by a convincing fake, or simply miss something that a later generation sees clearly. It sits in the uncomfortable middle ground between preference, which cannot be wrong, and objective fact, which cannot be argued with. Almost everything interesting about human judgment lives in that middle ground, and almost none of our public conversation about artificial intelligence has had a good way to talk about it.

It is also worth saying plainly that taste, in this sense, is not the exclusive property of any credential. A line cook who has burned enough sauces to know at a glance when one is about to break has taste. A teenager who has listened to enough music on cheap earbuds to recognize a cliché chord progression before the second verse arrives has taste. A grandmother who has planted enough tomatoes to know, by the smell of the vine, which fruit to leave on it another week has taste. None of them would use the word, and none of them would necessarily be able to explain their judgment in language a philosophy department would accept, but their judgment is real, trainable, and frequently more reliable within its own domain than a credentialed expert's judgment outside it. The word has spent a long time being associated with people who went to the right schools and can name the right wines, and that association has done real damage to how usable the concept is. Stripped of the snobbery, what is left is simply this: practiced, feedback-corrected discernment, wherever it happens to live.

For as long as production was expensive, this whole category of skill could stay mostly implicit — practiced by editors and critics and doctors and master craftsmen, discussed in trade publications and dinner parties, but never forced into general public attention, because the volume of things requiring judgment never outran the supply of people equipped to judge them. The slop tide changes that arithmetic completely. When a marketplace can be flooded with tens of thousands of AI-written books in categories that used to see a few hundred genuine submissions a year, when a streaming platform can receive more machine-generated songs in a week than human musicians uploaded to it in its first several years combined, when a job platform can be buried under cover letters that all read like the same slightly-too-smooth voice, the question is no longer whether good judgment exists somewhere in the system. Good judgment has existed all along, in the editors and the reviewers and the human listeners who still know a real thing when they encounter one. The question is whether that judgment, which was built and staffed and funded for a world of scarce production, can survive contact with a world where production is functionally infinite — and if it cannot, in its current form, what has to change so that something can.

That is not a rhetorical question posed for effect. It is the actual, unresolved situation a great many institutions found themselves in during the first half of the 2020s, often without the vocabulary to describe what was happening to them. A streaming platform built its royalty payouts on the assumption that most uploaded music represented real effort by real musicians hoping for real listeners; it discovered, the hard way, that a system built on that assumption could be gamed at industrial scale by someone willing to skip the musicians and the listeners both. A self-publishing platform built its openness on the assumption that most submitted manuscripts represented a writer's honest attempt to say something, however good or bad the result; it discovered that assumption could be exploited by someone who had never intended to write anything at all, only to generate something that looked enough like writing to be sold. Neither platform was naive in any obvious sense. Both had simply been built for a world in which the cost of producing enough fake material to matter was, itself, prohibitively high — a world, in other words, that no longer existed by the time the fraud showed up.

What comes next in this book follows two of those situations in close, specific detail, because the abstractions above are easy to nod along with and hard to actually feel until you watch them play out in the life of one particular man in North Carolina, and in the inboxes of one particular team of people trying to keep a bookstore that sells everything from becoming a bookstore that sells nothing worth reading. The slop tide is not a metaphor happening somewhere else, to someone else's industry. It has already reached specific royalty statements and specific bank accounts and specific readers holding a book they paid for and cannot finish, and it did that well before most of the people it affected had a word as good as "slop" to describe what they were looking at.

The dictionary caught up eventually. The rest of us are still catching up to what the flood actually costs, and to whom.
