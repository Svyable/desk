# The Judgment of Paris

Steven Spurrier did not expect anyone to care about the results.

He ran a wine shop on the Rue Royale in Paris called Caves de la Madeleine, a narrow, well-regarded place a few blocks from the Place de la Concorde, and he taught a wine school next door with an American colleague named Patricia Gallagher. It was the spring of 1976. The United States was about to turn two hundred years old, and Gallagher thought the bicentennial deserved some kind of gesture from the two of them — something a little playful, a little instructive. Spurrier had recently traveled through California, visiting wineries in the Napa Valley that were, at the time, mostly unknown outside a small circle of American enthusiasts and almost entirely unknown in France. He had tasted some things there he found genuinely impressive. He proposed a tasting: French wines against Californian wines, judged blind, by French judges, with nobody in the room from either country's press until it was already over.

He was not trying to prove anything about California. If he had been asked to bet on the outcome, he would have bet on France, and he said so afterward, more than once. The tasting was conceived as a kind of educational entertainment — a way to mark the anniversary, flatter the judges with a rare comparative exercise, and give his American guest something to write home about. He booked a private room at the InterContinental Hotel in Paris for May 24, 1976. He and Gallagher selected the wines themselves: six white Burgundies from producers like Bâtard-Montrachet and Meursault-Charmes against six California Chardonnays, and four red Bordeaux from classified estates — among them Château Mouton-Rothschild, Château Haut-Brion, and Château Léoville Las Cases — against six California Cabernet Sauvignons. Spurrier and Gallagher had bought or been given most of the American bottles on Spurrier's California trip and had them shipped over, since almost no retailer in Paris carried them.

The nine judges were, by design, some of the most credentialed palates in France. Pierre Tari, general secretary of the Institut National des Appellations d'Origine. Aubert de Villaine, co-owner of Domaine de la Romanée-Conti, arguably the most prestigious wine estate on earth. Michel Dovaz, a wine writer and instructor. Odette Kahn, editor of La Revue du Vin de France. Pierre Bréjoux, an inspector for the same appellation institute. Claude Dubois-Millot of the Gault-Millot restaurant guide. Christian Vanneque, at the time the head sommelier of the three-star restaurant La Tour d'Argent, one of the most storied dining rooms in Paris. Raymond Oliver, chef and owner of Le Grand Véfour. Jean-Claude Vrinat, owner of Taillevent. Spurrier and Gallagher rounded out the panel as the only non-French participants, but by prior agreement their scores were recorded separately and did not count toward the official results — this was to be a verdict rendered by the French establishment on its own terms.

The wines were served blind, in a genuinely blind format: brown paper had been wrapped around some bottles, and all the wines — French and Californian alike — were poured from unmarked glasses so that no judge could tell, by sight or by any decanting cue, which country a given pour came from. Each judge scored each wine on a twenty-point scale and wrote brief tasting notes. There was no coordination beforehand about how the scoring should run, which would later become a minor point of contention, but nobody in the room believed they were doing anything other than an honest side-by-side comparison of good, and in most cases very expensive, wine.

The only journalist in the room was George Taber, a young Time magazine correspondent who happened to be finishing up an assignment in Paris and had heard about the tasting through Spurrier. Nobody else from the press had bothered to show up; a comparison between French wine and California wine did not, in May 1976, strike most editors as a story with an ending anyone needed to know. Taber came because he had nothing better scheduled that afternoon.

The whites went first. As the scores came in, something started to go wrong, from the French judges' point of view, and it kept going wrong. Odette Kahn, tasting one wine she found flabby and clumsy, said aloud that it was clearly not a French wine — it had to be one of the Californians. She was tasting a 1973 Bâtard-Montrachet, one of the more expensive white Burgundies in the room. Judges kept making confident, specific claims about which wines were French and which were Californian, and kept being wrong, sometimes badly wrong, in ways that were only checked against the paper trail after all the scores were locked in. When Spurrier tallied the final numbers, the highest-scoring white wine in the room, by a real margin, was a 1973 Chardonnay from Chateau Montelena, a winery at the northern end of the Napa Valley that had only replanted with Chardonnay a few years earlier and was still, commercially speaking, an obscurity. Its winemaker, Mike Grgich, a Croatian immigrant who had trained under André Tchelistcheff — one of the most influential figures in postwar California winemaking — had made the wine in a converted stone building that had once housed a failed winery from the 1880s. Behind it came a Meursault-Charmes from Domaine Roulot, and behind that, in third, a second California Chardonnay from Chalone Vineyard. Four of the top five whites, and six of the top ten, were Californian.

The reds followed the same pattern, though the margin was tighter. The top-scoring red was a 1973 Cabernet Sauvignon from Stag's Leap Wine Cellars, another Napa producer, this one founded only two years earlier by a former Bay Area philosophy instructor named Warren Winiarski, who had left academia to make wine in a former prune orchard. Its nearest competitor, close behind, was Château Mouton-Rothschild, one of the five estates classified as a First Growth in the 1855 Bordeaux classification that still, a century and a quarter later, functioned as the industry's closest thing to a permanent aristocracy. Third was Château Haut-Brion, another First Growth. The margin between Stag's Leap and Mouton-Rothschild was a fraction of a point, well within the range that a different panel, or the same panel on a different day, might have reversed. But first was first, and the room had scored it that way with its own numbers, un-coerced, on the basis of taste alone.

Some of the French judges asked to see their score sheets again once the wines were unmasked. Odette Kahn reportedly demanded her sheet back and never allowed the results to be published in her own magazine. The organizers had not planned for a controversy; they had planned for an afternoon of tasting notes and a pleasant item in Gallagher's newsletter. What they got instead was a result that undid, in about two hours, a set of assumptions about where wine quality could and could not originate — assumptions that had been treated as close to physical law by nearly everyone in the room.

Taber went back to his hotel and filed a short item for Time, published on June 7, 1976, under the headline "Judgment of Paris." It ran to a few hundred words, tucked into the magazine's back section, and Taber later admitted he'd chosen the phrase almost as a throwaway — an allusion to the Greek myth in which the Trojan prince Paris is asked to judge a beauty contest among three goddesses, a judgment that, in the myth, sets off consequences far larger than the contest itself seemed to warrant. The item did not get much attention in France at the time; several of the French papers ignored it, and some of the judges spent years afterward insisting, not unreasonably, that a single afternoon's blind tasting under time pressure was a strange instrument to use for ranking an entire nation's winemaking tradition against another's. Wine judged in isolation is not wine judged with food, at leisure, over an evening, they pointed out, and there is truth in the complaint — a fast blind flight measures something real but not everything a bottle of wine is for.

None of that stopped the story from becoming, over the following two decades, one of the most consequential magazine items in the history of the wine trade. American wine writers picked it up. California producers who had spent years being politely patronized by importers and sommeliers now had a citable, third-party, French-judged result to point to. Robert Mondavi, already the most prominent evangelist for Napa Valley's ambitions, used the tasting for the rest of his career as proof that California belonged in the conversation with Bordeaux and Burgundy rather than beneath it. Investment that had been trickling into Napa Valley through the 1970s turned into a much larger wave through the 1980s and 1990s; land prices in the valley, which had been affordable enough in 1976 that a philosophy instructor could start a winery in a prune orchard, began a climb that would eventually make Napa Cabernet some of the most expensive agricultural real estate in the country. In 2006, on the tasting's thirtieth anniversary, Spurrier and Taber organized a rematch using older vintages of some of the same wines, judged again by a mixed panel; a Californian, this time a Cabernet from Ridge Vineyards' Monte Bello vineyard, again finished first among the reds.

What actually happened in that room at the InterContinental deserves to be stated carefully, because it is easy to overstate and easy to understate in opposite directions. It was not a comprehensive verdict that California wine had surpassed French wine in some permanent, general sense; it was nine specific, credentialed people, tasting a specific set of bottles, on a specific afternoon, under blind conditions, and reaching numerical judgments that did not track the assumed hierarchy of prestige. It is also not evidence that taste is arbitrary or that expertise is theater — quite the opposite. The judges were not guessing randomly; they were applying real, trained discrimination to unfamiliar wines and simply getting the country of origin wrong while still identifying, with some consistency, which specific bottles they preferred. Kahn was confident the flabby Bâtard-Montrachet was Californian. She was wrong about the nationality. She was not wrong that something about the wine, tasted blind, did not meet her standard that day.

That is the fact the Paris tasting establishes, and it is worth sitting with before moving on to complicate it, because the complication in the next chapter will be real and will not cancel this one out. Under conditions engineered to remove reputation, brand, price, and label from the equation — conditions that are hard to construct and easy to sabotage, which is part of why blind tastings remain rare relative to how often they are demanded — a room of serious experts produced a ranking that the wine world's existing hierarchy had not predicted and would not have permitted if the hierarchy alone were doing the judging. Prestige did not choose the winner. Somebody's palate did. That distinction — between taste as inherited status and taste as an exercisable, testable skill that can occasionally embarrass the institutions built on top of it — is the reason the Judgment of Paris still gets taught in business schools, cited in wine economics papers, and reenacted, however imperfectly, whenever someone wants to ask whether a given hierarchy of quality actually corresponds to anything real or is simply doing what hierarchies do, which is protect their own membership.

The tasting also demonstrates something specific about the mechanics of confidence. Several of the judges were not tentative in their wrongness; they were certain, in the moment, of judgments the paper trail later proved false. That is not a flaw unique to these nine people. It is closer to a structural feature of expert judgment under blind conditions generally, and it is the detail that the next chapter will pull on until the picture gets considerably less comfortable. The Paris tasting proves that taste, freed from its usual scaffolding of price tags and famous names, can still do real work. It does not yet tell you how sturdy that work is, how much of it survives contact with a label, a color, or a story about where the wine came from. That question was sitting in the room in 1976 too. Nobody had thought to ask it yet.
