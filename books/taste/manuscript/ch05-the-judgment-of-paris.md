# The Judgment of Paris

Steven Spurrier did not expect anyone to care about the results.

He had come to wine sideways, the way a lot of the people who end up defining a field do. Educated at private school and then the London School of Economics, he joined London's oldest wine merchant, Christopher's, as a trainee in 1964, learning the trade from the bottom in an industry that still ran largely on inherited palates and inherited customers. In 1970 he moved to Paris and talked an elderly woman out of her small wine shop tucked into a passage off the Rue Royale, a few blocks from the Place de la Concorde. He renamed it Caves de la Madeleine, stocked it with wines most Parisian shops didn't bother with, and encouraged customers to taste before they bought — a small heresy in a city where wine retail still ran on reputation rather than the glass. A couple of years later he opened L'Académie du Vin next door, France's first private wine school, and hired an American named Patricia Gallagher to help run it. By 1976 the two of them had spent years teaching Parisians, many of whom considered themselves already expert, how to taste analytically rather than reverently.

It was Gallagher's idea, more or less, to do something to mark the American bicentennial. Spurrier had recently spent time in California, touring the Napa Valley at Gallagher's urging, tasting through wineries that were, at that point, almost entirely unknown in France and not much better known outside a small circle of American enthusiasts. He came back impressed by some of what he'd tasted, though not so impressed that he doubted how a comparison with France would turn out. He proposed a tasting: a handful of the California wines he'd found most promising, set blind against some of France's most respected bottles, judged by French professionals, with no press invited until it was over. It was conceived less as provocation than as a teaching exercise dressed up as an anniversary gift — a way to give his American colleague's countrymen an occasion, and give the French judges something genuinely novel to taste. If he'd been asked to bet money on the outcome beforehand, by his own later account, he would have bet on France without much hesitation.

Spurrier and Gallagher selected the wines themselves, and the selecting was not a formality. Spurrier had spent roughly two weeks driving through Napa and Sonoma, tasting through cellars most of which had no export business at all and no expectation that a French wine merchant would ever knock on their door, let alone buy a case for a tasting back home. Because almost no Parisian retailer carried serious California bottlings in 1976, most of what he eventually poured in Paris had come out of that trip directly — bought a bottle or two at a time, in some cases carried back personally rather than shipped, since there was no established import channel to rely on. The obscurity of the source material is part of what made the outcome land as hard as it did: these were not, for the most part, wines any French judge in the room had tasted before, or in several cases had even heard of. For the white flight they chose four French white Burgundies from respected producers — a Meursault-Charmes from Domaine Roulot, a Bâtard-Montrachet, a Puligny-Montrachet Les Pucelles from Domaine Leflaive, and a Beaune Clos des Mouches from Joseph Drouhin — against six California Chardonnays, including bottlings from Chateau Montelena, Chalone Vineyard, Spring Mountain Vineyard, Freemark Abbey, Veedercrest, and David Bruce. For the red flight they chose four Bordeaux from classified estates, including Château Mouton-Rothschild and Château Haut-Brion, both ranked among the five First Growths in the 1855 Bordeaux classification that had functioned, for over a century, as the closest thing the wine world had to settled law, alongside Château Montrose and Château Léoville Las Cases. Against them went six California Cabernet Sauvignons, among them Stag's Leap Wine Cellars, Ridge Vineyards' Monte Bello bottling, Heitz Cellars' Martha's Vineyard, Mayacamas, Clos Du Val, and Freemark Abbey's red.

The nine official judges were, by design, some of the most credentialed palates in France. Pierre Tari, general secretary of the Institut National des Appellations d'Origine, the body charged with policing France's own appellation system. Aubert de Villaine, co-owner and manager of Domaine de la Romanée-Conti, arguably the single most prestigious wine estate on earth. Odette Kahn, editor of La Revue du Vin de France, the country's leading wine publication. Pierre Bréjoux, an inspector for the same appellation institute Tari represented. Michel Dovaz, a wine instructor and writer. Claude Dubois-Millot of the Gault-Millot restaurant guide. Christian Vanneque, head sommelier at La Tour d'Argent, one of the most storied dining rooms in Paris. Raymond Oliver, the chef-owner of Le Grand Véfour. Jean-Claude Vrinat, owner of Taillevent. Spurrier and Gallagher sat in on the tasting as well, but by prior agreement their own scores were recorded separately and excluded from the official tally — this was to be a verdict rendered by the French wine establishment about itself, using its own trained instruments, with no foreign thumb on the scale.

The tasting took place on May 24, 1976, in a private room at the InterContinental Hotel in Paris. The wines were poured blind in the most literal sense available: unmarked glasses, no labels visible, nothing to signal country of origin by sight. Each judge scored each wine on the 20-point scale standard to French wine evaluation at the time and wrote brief tasting notes as they went — a scale considerably coarser than the 100-point system Robert Parker would later popularize through the 1980s and 1990s, partly in response to the same growing American appetite for authoritative, numerical wine criticism that this tasting helped stimulate. There was no formal calibration exercise beforehand to align how the panel would use the scale, which would later become a point of methodological criticism, but nobody in the room that afternoon believed they were doing anything other than an honest comparative tasting of good, expensive wine among colleagues who took the exercise seriously.

The only journalist present was George Taber, a young Time correspondent based in Paris who had, by his own later account, initially turned down the invitation before deciding he had nothing better scheduled that afternoon and might as well see what an amateur curiosity like this looked like in practice. No other outlet had bothered to send anyone; a side-by-side of French and Californian wine did not, in the spring of 1976, strike most editors as a story with an ending worth staffing.

The whites went first, and the trouble — from the French judges' point of view — started almost immediately. Judges kept making confident, specific claims about which glass held which country's wine, and kept being wrong in ways nobody could check until the paper trail was unsealed. Odette Kahn, tasting one wine she found flat and clumsy, announced that it had to be Californian; the glass in front of her held a 1972 Bâtard-Montrachet, one of the more expensive white Burgundies in the room. When Spurrier and Gallagher totaled the scores, the highest-rated white wine in the tasting, with an average of 14.67 out of 20, was the 1973 Chardonnay from Chateau Montelena, a small winery at the northern end of Napa Valley that had only replanted with Chardonnay a few years earlier and remained, commercially speaking, an obscurity even within California. Its winemaker, Mike Grgich, a Croatian immigrant who had trained under André Tchelistcheff, one of the most influential figures in postwar California winemaking, had made the wine in a converted stone building that had housed a failed nineteenth-century winery. Second was the Meursault-Charmes from Domaine Roulot, at 14.05. Third, at 13.44, was a second California Chardonnay, from Chalone Vineyard, ahead of the remaining French entries and two more Californians. Of the ten whites tasted, three of the top four finishers were Californian, in a flight the organizers themselves had expected France to dominate without much contest.

The reds followed a tighter but similarly unsettling pattern. The highest score, 14.14, went to the 1973 Cabernet Sauvignon from Stag's Leap Wine Cellars, a winery only two years old at the time, founded by Warren Winiarski, a former University of Chicago political philosophy instructor who had left academia to plant vines in a converted prune orchard in the Napa Valley. Close behind, at 14.09, came Château Mouton-Rothschild's 1970 vintage — a First Growth, and by reputation one of the small handful of the greatest red wines France produces in any given year. Third, at 13.94, was Château Montrose's 1970, another respected Bordeaux estate, narrowly ahead of Ridge Vineyards' 1971 Monte Bello at 12.14. Haut-Brion, the other First Growth in the flight, finished behind Mouton, and Château Léoville Las Cases came in at 11.18, trailed by Heitz Cellars' Martha's Vineyard at 10.36. The margin separating Stag's Leap from Mouton-Rothschild was a fraction of a point, well within the range a different panel, or the same panel on a different afternoon, might have reversed. But the room had scored it that way, on its own numbers, without coercion, on the basis of taste alone, and first was first.

The scoring itself was unglamorous. Spurrier and Gallagher collected the written sheets after each flight and tallied the averages themselves, by hand, at a side table, while the judges moved on to the next set of glasses uncertain of anything except their own opinions. Nobody in the room learned who had won until both flights were finished and the totals were added up — which meant the confident, specific misidentifications happened in real time, unwitnessed by anyone who could have corrected them, and only became visible as a pattern once the numbers were already fixed on paper. Several of the French judges asked to see their own score sheets again once the wines were unmasked, as if rereading their handwriting might change what it said. Odette Kahn reportedly demanded her sheet back afterward, and the results never ran in her own magazine. Nobody had planned for a controversy. Spurrier and Gallagher had planned an afternoon of tasting notes and a pleasant item for the Académie's newsletter. What they got instead was a set of numbers that undid, in roughly two hours, an assumption about where wine quality could and could not originate — an assumption that had operated, for nearly everyone in that room, with something close to the force of physical law.

Spurrier himself paid a social price for organizing an event he hadn't expected to embarrass anyone. By several later accounts, a number of his French colleagues in the wine trade were cool toward him for a period afterward, unhappy that a British merchant living in their city had staged the exercise that produced the result, whatever his intentions had been going in. He kept running Caves de la Madeleine and the Académie du Vin for years afterward, and remained, until his death in 2021, one of the most respected wine educators in the English-speaking world — but the 1976 tasting, which he had organized mostly as a bicentennial courtesy, ended up defining his professional biography more than anything else he did across a career that included decades as a working critic and, later, years spent chairing judging panels for major British wine competitions. He seemed, in interviews given long after the fact, more amused than embittered by the disproportion between the size of the gesture and the size of what it set off.

Taber went back to his hotel and filed a short item that ran in Time's June 7, 1976, issue, in the Modern Living section, under the headline "Judgment of Paris" — four paragraphs, tucked well back in the magazine. He later said he had reached for the Greek allusion almost as a throwaway: in the myth, the Trojan prince Paris is asked to judge a beauty contest among three goddesses, a seemingly minor arbitration that sets off consequences far larger than the contest itself would suggest. The item drew little notice in France at the time. Several French papers ignored it outright, and a number of the judges spent years afterward arguing, not unreasonably, that a single blind flight tasted quickly under time pressure was a strange instrument for ranking one nation's winemaking tradition against another's. Bordeaux, in particular, is built to be tasted old; its tannins are meant to soften and its fruit to develop complexity over a decade or two, and a young 1970 vintage tasted alongside California wines built to show well on release was, by this argument, competing at a disadvantage baked into the format rather than a fair test of which country made better wine. There is real substance to that complaint, and it deserves to survive here rather than be argued away, because it points at something true about blind tastings generally: they measure a wine's performance under one specific, artificial set of conditions, at one specific age, and a different set of conditions can produce a different verdict. Aubert de Villaine himself, decades later, would describe the tasting's design as imperfect while still acknowledging that it had exposed something real about how his own palate, and his colleagues', had performed that day.

For the winemakers themselves, the win translated almost immediately into something more concrete than vindication. Mike Grgich left Chateau Montelena within a year of the tasting and, in 1977, co-founded his own label, Grgich Hills Estate, trading in part on a reputation the Paris result had just handed him for free. Warren Winiarski kept building Stag's Leap Wine Cellars into one of Napa's most consistently respected producers; in 1996, twenty years after the tasting, the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History accepted a bottle of the winning 1973 Cabernet into its permanent collection, listing it years later among the objects it considers to have helped make America. Neither man had entered the tasting expecting anything beyond the novelty of being included. Both spent the rest of their careers being introduced, at least in part, by what had happened in a hotel room they didn't attend.

None of that stopped the story from becoming, over the following two decades, one of the most consequential magazine items in the history of the wine trade. American wine writers picked it up and kept running with it long after the French press had moved on. California producers who had spent years being politely patronized by importers and sommeliers now had a citable, French-judged, statistically documented result to point to whenever the conversation turned to whether Napa belonged in the same sentence as Bordeaux or Burgundy. Robert Mondavi, already the most visible evangelist for the valley's ambitions, referenced the tasting for the rest of his career as evidence that California deserved a seat at the table rather than a footnote beneath it. Investment that had been trickling into Napa through the 1970s turned into a much larger wave through the 1980s and 1990s, and land that had been cheap enough in 1976 for a former philosophy instructor to buy a former prune orchard began a climb that would eventually make Napa Cabernet vineyard land among the most expensive agricultural real estate in the United States. The psychological opening the tasting created did not stay confined to California, either. Over the following two decades, wine regions with even less standing in 1976 than Napa had — the Barossa Valley in Australia, Chile's Maipo and Colchagua valleys, South Africa's Stellenbosch — found international critics and buyers somewhat more willing to taste their bottlings on the merits rather than dismiss them by geography alone, a shift in appetite that had many causes but that the Paris result had helped make thinkable in the first place.

The result also proved durable in a way a single lucky afternoon would not have. In 1986, on the tenth anniversary, two more comparative tastings were organized — one by the French Culinary Institute in New York, with Spurrier again involved, and a separate one run by Wine Spectator — this time limited to the red wines, since the whites from 1976 were considered past their prime a decade on. Eight judges blind-tasted nine of the original ten reds; Freemark Abbey's bottling could not be sourced in time. California wines again finished ahead of the French entries, with Clos Du Val's Cabernet topping the red flight. In 2006, on the thirtieth anniversary, Spurrier and Taber organized a further rematch using older vintages of some of the same labels, judged this time by a mixed panel in London and California simultaneously; a Californian wine again finished first among the reds, this time Ridge Vineyards' Monte Bello. Whatever had happened in that room at the InterContinental in 1976 was not, it turned out, an isolated fluke of one afternoon's palate fatigue or one unlucky flight order. It kept reproducing, with different judges, different vintages, and a decade or three of additional aging thrown in.

The story also outgrew its own facts, the way durable stories tend to. By the 2000s enough embellished, secondhand, and simplified versions of the afternoon were circulating that George Taber, who had watched it happen from four paragraphs away, wrote a full-length book in 2005 — Judgment of Paris: California vs. France and the Historic 1976 Paris Tasting That Revolutionized Wine — largely to put the documented version back on record before it disappeared under its own legend. Three years later, a loosely fictionalized film called Bottle Shock dramatized the tasting for a wider audience, inventing a rivalry and a romantic subplot that had no basis in what actually happened and, in the process of making the story more cinematic, getting a number of its specifics wrong. Taber was blunt about the gap in interviews around the film's release, cautioning audiences that whatever impression Bottle Shock left them with should have no bearing on the documented record he'd spent a book trying to preserve. It is a small, telling irony that a story about the difference between what people believe and what a blind test actually shows would itself generate a layer of comfortable fiction that had to be corrected by the one witness who'd been in the room.

There is a reason the French wine establishment took the result harder than a mere ranking might seem to warrant, and it has to do with what French wine culture had built its authority on for over a century. The concept of terroir — the idea that a wine's character comes from an irreproducible combination of a specific patch of soil, climate, and slope, tended by generations who understood that patch better than any outsider could — was not just a marketing claim in 1976. It was close to a national credo, formalized in law through the appellation d'origine contrôlée system that men like Pierre Tari and Pierre Bréjoux spent their careers administering. If terroir was truly irreproducible, then a nine-year-old vineyard in a converted prune orchard in California had no business producing anything capable of beating a First Growth Bordeaux with a hundred and twenty years of institutional pedigree behind it, blind tasting or not. The Judgment of Paris did not disprove terroir — nobody has seriously argued that soil, slope, and climate are irrelevant to what ends up in the glass — but it did puncture the stronger version of the claim, the version holding that pedigree alone guaranteed superiority regardless of who was doing the actual work in a given vintage. Aubert de Villaine, whose own Domaine de la Romanée-Conti sits near the summit of that entire pedigree system, was among the more measured of the French judges in later years, generally describing the tasting as a useful and clarifying shock rather than an insult requiring a rebuttal.

What actually happened deserves to be stated carefully, because it is easy to overstate in one direction and understate in the other. The tasting was not a comprehensive verdict that California wine had surpassed French wine in some permanent, general sense; it was a specific, credentialed panel, tasting a specific set of bottles, under blind conditions, on specific afternoons repeated at ten-year intervals, reaching numerical judgments that did not track the assumed hierarchy of prestige. Nor is it evidence that taste is arbitrary, or that expertise is theater — if anything the opposite. The judges were not guessing at random; they were applying real, trained discrimination to unfamiliar wines and getting the country of origin wrong while still converging, with some consistency across repeated events and different panels, on many of the same specific bottles. Kahn was confident the Bâtard-Montrachet in front of her was Californian. She was wrong about its nationality. She was not wrong that something about the wine, tasted blind that day, fell short of what she expected from a bottle that expensive.

That is the fact the Paris tasting and its several rematches establish, and it is worth sitting with before the next chapter complicates it, because the complication to come will be real and will not cancel this one out. Under conditions engineered to remove reputation, brand, and label from the equation — conditions that are difficult to construct honestly and easy to sabotage, which is part of why genuinely blind tastings remain rarer than the demand for them would suggest — a room of serious professionals produced rankings that the wine world's existing hierarchy had not predicted and would not have permitted if hierarchy alone were doing the judging. Prestige did not choose the winner. Somebody's palate did, repeatedly, across a decade and then three. That distinction — between taste as inherited status and taste as an exercisable, testable skill capable of embarrassing the very institutions built on top of it — is the reason the Judgment of Paris still gets taught in business schools, cited in wine-economics journals, and re-enacted, however imperfectly, whenever someone wants to ask whether a given hierarchy of quality corresponds to anything real or is simply doing what hierarchies generally do, which is protect their own membership.

The tasting also demonstrates something specific about the mechanics of confidence under uncertainty. Several of the judges were not tentative in their wrongness; they were certain, in the moment, of country-of-origin judgments the paper trail later proved false, even while their underlying preferences among the actual bottles held up reasonably well on replay. That gap — between confidently misreading a wine's identity and still, in the same breath, ranking its quality in a way that survives a decade of scrutiny — is not a flaw unique to these nine people. It looks more like a structural feature of expert judgment operating under blind, unfamiliar conditions generally, and it is the detail the next chapter is going to pull on until the picture gets considerably less comfortable. The Judgment of Paris shows that trained taste, stripped of its usual scaffolding of price tags and famous names, can still do real, durable work. It does not yet tell you how sturdy that same instrument remains once the scaffolding is quietly put back — once a label, a color, or a familiar story about where the wine came from is standing in the room along with the glass. That question was sitting in the InterContinental too, in May 1976. Nobody in the room had thought yet to ask it.
