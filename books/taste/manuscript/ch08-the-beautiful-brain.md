# The Beautiful Brain

Semir Zeki spent the first part of his career studying how the visual cortex of the macaque monkey processes color and motion, work that helped establish some of the basic architecture of how primate brains build a picture of the world out of raw retinal signal. Born in 1940 and holding both British and French nationality, Zeki spent essentially his entire career at University College London, where he became Professor of Neurobiology in 1981 and later served for over a decade as deputy to Richard Frackowiak inside the Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology — one of the labs where brain-imaging analysis as a discipline was more or less invented during the 1990s, developing the statistical methods that let researchers make sense of the noisy blood-flow signal an fMRI scanner actually produces. Zeki himself was among the researchers who mapped what came to be called area V4, a region of the visual cortex central to color perception, and helped clarify how the brain manages to see a red apple as reliably red under a candle, under fluorescent light, and under an overcast sky, even though the actual wavelengths reaching the eye differ substantially in each case — a property called color constancy. It was, by the standards of neuroscience, a respected and fairly conventional research program, the kind that produces careful papers about receptive fields and gets cited in textbooks without ever reaching a general audience.

What made Zeki unusual was what he did with that foundation afterward. Beginning in the late 1990s, he started arguing that the same tools neuroscience had developed to study vision and perception could be turned on a question that had, until then, mostly belonged to philosophers and art historians: what happens in the brain when something strikes a person as beautiful. He gave the resulting field a name, neuroaesthetics, founded a laboratory built around the question at University College London, and spent the following two decades trying to make the term mean something more rigorous than a repackaged version of old aesthetic theory with a brain scanner bolted on.

Zeki was not shy about the ambition of what he was proposing. He argued, across a series of papers and a widely read 1999 book called Inner Vision, that the entire history of art could be productively reread as a long, unwitting experiment in the biology of the visual brain — that painters, working centuries before anyone could image a living human brain, had nonetheless been finding, through trial and error and their own private sense of what worked, ways of engaging visual mechanisms that neuroscience was only now getting around to describing formally. It was a large claim to build a career on, and it drew exactly the kind of resistance a large claim generally does.

The proposition invited skepticism from multiple directions at once, and it is worth taking the skepticism seriously before describing what Zeki found, because the objections were not frivolous. Art historians had spent a century and a half building frameworks for why the Sistine Chapel ceiling or a Rothko canvas produces the response it does, frameworks built out of history, culture, technique, and biography rather than blood flow. Reducing that response to a pattern of neural activation risked sounding like exactly the kind of category error people worry about when neuroscience wanders into the humanities' territory — explaining a symphony by measuring air pressure. And beauty itself has always seemed like the textbook case of something irreducibly cultural: Renaissance ideals of the human body look nothing like eighteenth-century ones, Tang dynasty conventions for landscape painting share almost no visible vocabulary with French Impressionism a millennium later, and any given museum visitor can walk past a canvas that moves a stranger to tears while feeling nothing in particular. If beauty is that plastic, does it even make sense to go looking for a single, common brain mechanism underneath it? Some philosophers working in aesthetics have continued to argue, since Zeki's program got underway, that a field built on this premise risks flattening exactly the historical and cultural specificity that makes any individual artwork worth studying in the first place — a fair objection to keep in view rather than dismiss.

Zeki's answer was to go looking anyway, on the theory that cultural variation in what people find beautiful does not automatically rule out a shared underlying mechanism for the experience of finding something beautiful, whatever the something happens to be. The distinction matters and is worth sitting with for a moment: a claim that all human beings share a similarly organized visual cortex does not require that all human beings agree on what image belongs in front of it. Zeki's research program set out to test the narrower, more defensible version of the claim — not that people would converge on the same beautiful objects, which no one seriously expected, but that the brain's response to whatever each individual already considered beautiful might route through the same neural territory regardless of the object or the person's cultural background.

The best-known test of that idea was a study Zeki ran with his colleague Tomohiro Ishizu, funded by the Wellcome Trust, a major British medical research charity, and published in 2011 in the journal PLoS ONE under the title "Toward a Brain-Based Theory of Beauty." The design started outside the scanner. Twenty-one volunteers, deliberately recruited from a range of cultural and ethnic backgrounds rather than a single homogeneous pool, were shown a series of paintings — spanning genres and periods, portraits, landscapes, abstract work — and separately played a series of musical excerpts. For each piece, in both modalities, the volunteers rated what they had just seen or heard on a simple three-point scale: beautiful, indifferent, or ugly. This step mattered because it meant the researchers were not imposing an external definition of beauty on the participants. They were letting each person define, for themselves, what belonged in each category, and only afterward asking what the brain did in response to that person's own judgment.

The volunteers then went back through the same paintings and musical excerpts a second time, this time while lying inside an fMRI scanner, which measures brain activity indirectly by tracking blood oxygenation, a proxy for which regions of neural tissue are working harder at a given moment. Because each participant had already sorted the material into their own beautiful, indifferent, and ugly categories, the researchers could look specifically at what happened in the brain at the moment a person was looking at, or listening to, something they personally had called beautiful, and compare it to what happened when the same person encountered something they had called ugly or indifferent — controlling, in effect, for the enormous cultural and personal variation in which specific paintings or pieces of music ended up in which bin.

The finding that made the study widely cited well outside neuroscience circles was this: across the volunteers, and across both paintings and music — two entirely different sensory channels, one visual, one auditory, processed through largely separate early sensory pathways in the brain — the activity that tracked most consistently with a person's rating of beauty was located in the medial orbitofrontal cortex, a region toward the front of the brain, above and behind the eyes, long associated in other research with reward, pleasure, and value-based decision-making. Zeki and Ishizu reported that this was, in fact, the only cortical area where activity reliably tracked the experience of beauty across both modalities, with the overlap between the visual-beauty and musical-beauty activation patterns in that region close to complete. The more beautiful a participant had rated a given painting or musical passage, the more activity researchers measured in that region when the same participant later encountered it in the scanner. This held whether the stimulus was a piece of visual art or a piece of music, and it held across participants from noticeably different cultural backgrounds who had, in many cases, rated entirely different objects as beautiful in the first place. Two people looking at completely different paintings, each finding their own painting beautiful, showed activation converging on the same general neural neighborhood — not because they agreed on beauty's content, but because whatever criterion each of them had privately applied appeared to route through a shared piece of biological machinery once it arrived.

It's worth being precise about what that convergence does and does not establish, since a finding like this is easy to inflate into something grander than the data supports. The study did not find that all beautiful objects share physical properties, or that beauty can be detected directly from a brain scan without first asking the person what they think, or that culture and training play no role in shaping aesthetic judgment — the opposite is baked into the design, since the participants' own culturally and biographically shaped ratings were the whole basis for sorting the stimuli in the first place. What the study found was narrower and, arguably, more interesting: that once a person has, for whatever combination of reasons, arrived at the judgment "this is beautiful," the resulting brain state draws on much the same reward circuitry regardless of whether the trigger was a Renaissance portrait or a Bach prelude, and regardless of whether the brain doing the judging grew up in London, Lagos, or Tokyo. The verdict is personal and culturally inflected. The currency the verdict gets paid in, at the level of the brain's reward system, appears to be shared.

Zeki extended the same design, a few years later, to a category of experience that has nothing to do with sensory perception at all. In 2014, working with Ishizu, John Paul Romaya, and — notably — the mathematician Michael Atiyah, one of the most decorated figures in twentieth-century mathematics and a winner of the Fields Medal, Zeki published a study in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience examining what happens in the brain of a working mathematician confronted with an equation. Fifteen mathematicians were given sixty mathematical formulae to study at leisure and rate on a scale running from minus five, ugly, to plus five, beautiful. Two weeks later, they returned and rated the same sixty formulae again, this time inside an fMRI scanner. The formulae that came out on top, rated beautiful with unusual consistency both outside the scanner and during it, included Euler's identity — a compact equation relating five of mathematics' most fundamental constants, e, i, pi, 1, and 0, in a single line, which mathematicians have singled out as exceptionally elegant for generations, sometimes comparing it to a line of poetry for the amount of meaning it compresses into so little space — along with the Pythagorean identity and the Cauchy-Riemann equations. At the other end of the scale, the mathematicians in the study rated one of Srinivasa Ramanujan's infinite series and Riemann's functional equation as the ugliest of the sixty, formulae that are not considered incorrect or unimportant, simply graceless by the aesthetic standards of people who spend their working lives immersed in this kind of notation. The result echoed the art-and-music study almost exactly: activity in the same field of the medial orbitofrontal cortex correlated with how beautiful a mathematician rated a given formula, in response to a stimulus that was never sensory to begin with, just symbols on a page evaluated by a highly trained mind for their internal elegance and economy. A proof and a painting, on this evidence, are being weighed by the same piece of neural machinery, however different the paths by which each reaches it.

The consistency of that signal across such different triggers — paintings, music, and now abstract mathematical structure — has led Zeki to argue, in papers and public talks over the years since, for something like a common neurobiological basis for aesthetic experience broadly construed. It is worth being careful here, since the popular press that covered the 2011 study sometimes reached for a cruder version of the claim than Zeki himself was making — some coverage described the region as a "beauty center" in the brain, language that overstates what a single, if consistent, correlation can support. The orbitofrontal cortex is involved in plenty of judgments that have nothing to do with beauty; it is a general hub for reward and subjective value, and what these studies show is that aesthetic judgment appears reliably to recruit that hub rather than that the hub exists for aesthetic judgment specifically. The finding is narrower, and more interesting, than a beauty center would be: a general-purpose reward system, evolved for reasons having nothing to do with Renaissance portraiture or Euler's identity, gets called into service whenever a person — of any culture, confronting any kind of material — reaches the private verdict that something in front of them is beautiful.

Not everyone who has since tried to replicate or extend the finding has come away agreeing that the picture is as clean as the 2011 paper suggested. A meta-analysis published in 2021, pooling results across dozens of separate fMRI studies of beauty judgments involving faces and visual art, found the evidence for a single, consistent "beauty center" considerably weaker and messier than the strong version of Zeki's claim implies — activation across studies turned out to be more scattered across regions than a single, reliably recruited hub would predict, and some researchers in the field have argued that the appeal of a tidy, singular answer has outrun what the accumulated data can actually support. This does not erase what Zeki and Ishizu found in their own more narrowly designed study, which controlled its comparisons unusually carefully by using each participant's own ratings as the baseline; it does mean that the field as a whole, once other laboratories with different designs and different stimuli went looking for the same signal, has produced a noisier and more contested picture than the original result on its own would suggest. That tension is worth leaving exactly where it sits rather than resolving in either direction. Science that studies something as subjective as beauty is unusually hard to pin down with total confidence, and a responsible reading of this research holds the original finding and the subsequent skepticism in mind at the same time, rather than picking whichever one makes for a cleaner story.

There is a more general methodological caution worth raising here, one that applies to a great deal of fMRI research well beyond neuroaesthetics and not just to Zeki's specific claims. Critics of brain-imaging research have long warned against what is sometimes called reverse inference: the temptation to look at a scan, see activity in a region already associated with some mental state from prior research, and conclude that the mental state in question must be present, when in fact most brain regions participate in more than one kind of cognitive process and a single area lighting up is rarely proof of any one specific experience. The medial orbitofrontal cortex is a genuine hub for registering subjective value and reward across an enormous range of contexts — food, money, faces, music, and, in Zeki's studies, beauty — which is exactly why finding it active during an aesthetic judgment is interesting, but also exactly why that activity alone cannot, by itself, prove that beauty is a distinct, separable kind of experience rather than one more flavor of the brain's general-purpose reward response. Zeki's own studies were reasonably careful about this, using each subject's own beautiful-versus-ugly ratings as an internal comparison rather than relying on the region's activity in isolation, which is part of why the finding has held up better than many single-region imaging claims tend to. But the broader caution is worth keeping in mind for any research, in this field or another, that reduces a rich subjective experience to a single dot lighting up on a brain map.

The findings sit alongside a wider, sometimes contentious literature in what has become a genuine subfield of cognitive neuroscience, with other researchers proposing related but distinct accounts of aesthetic response — some emphasizing the role of processing fluency, the ease with which the brain handles a given stimulus, as a contributor to perceived beauty; others focused on symmetry, prototypicality, or the brain's response to a pattern of expectation set up and then resolved, particularly in music. Not every researcher in the field agrees with every claim Zeki has made, and neuroaesthetics as a discipline has faced its own internal debate over how much any of this actually explains about why a particular Vermeer moves one viewer to tears and leaves another cold, or why an entire generation's musical taste can shift within a decade in ways no fMRI scanner run today could have anticipated. But the core empirical result — that self-reported beauty, across different sensory categories, different cultural backgrounds, and even a nonsensory category as abstract as mathematics, tracks activity in a specific and already well-characterized reward region rather than scattering unpredictably across the brain — has held up reasonably well under scrutiny and remains one of the more frequently cited findings in the field.

Zeki's brain-imaging work is not the only line of evidence suggesting something biologically universal sits underneath aesthetic judgment, even if it is the most direct look at the reward circuitry involved. A separate body of research, running back to the 1990s work of the developmental psychologist Judith Langlois and her colleagues, found that infants only a few months old — well before they could have absorbed any specific culture's beauty standards from magazines or movies — looked measurably longer at faces that adults independently rated as attractive than at faces adults rated as plain. Later research extended the finding across cultures, finding a meaningful degree of agreement between observers from different countries and ethnic backgrounds about which faces they considered attractive, more agreement than a purely culturally constructed standard would predict. Neither the infant studies nor the cross-cultural attractiveness studies use anything like Zeki's scanning methodology, and facial attractiveness is a considerably narrower category than beauty in general — but the two literatures point in a compatible direction, suggesting that whatever biological substrate Zeki's fMRI work locates in the orbitofrontal cortex may sit on top of some inherited, pre-cultural sensitivity to certain patterns, faces included, rather than the whole apparatus of taste being learned from nothing.

There is a natural echo here to an experiment several chapters back in this book, though it comes from a different research question entirely. When researchers scanned people tasting wine they were told was expensive rather than cheap — the same wine in both cases — the region that tracked their reported increase in pleasure was, again, the medial orbitofrontal cortex, the identical territory Zeki's volunteers were lighting up when they looked at a painting they privately considered beautiful. That overlap does not mean beauty and price manipulation are the same phenomenon; one reflects a person's own considered aesthetic judgment, arrived at honestly, and the other reflects a person's judgment being quietly redirected by a number someone else supplied. But both appear to cash out, at the level of the brain's own accounting system, in the same currency — which suggests that whatever this reward circuitry is actually doing, it does not distinguish especially well between a pleasure that a person's own history and genuine engagement with an object earned, and a pleasure that a label or a price tag simply told the brain to expect. The machinery that registers "this is beautiful" and the machinery that can be fooled into registering "this must be better" turn out to be uncomfortably close neighbors, a fact worth remembering the next time either a critic or a marketer claims to be appealing to nothing but pure aesthetic judgment.

There is something worth sitting with in the fact that a two-thousand-year-old philosophical question — whether beauty is a property of objects in the world or a response manufactured entirely inside the observer — turns out to have a partial empirical answer, and the answer is neither of the two options philosophers spent those two thousand years arguing about. Beauty is not simply sitting out there in the painting, waiting to be correctly perceived, the way Renaissance theorists of proportion and harmony sometimes implied. But it is also not simply arbitrary, a private hallucination with no more in common across observers than the specific objects that happen to trigger it. There is a third possibility sitting between those two, and Zeki's research is some of the better evidence for it: a shared biological reward mechanism, evolved presumably for reasons having little to do with Renaissance portraiture, Bach, or Euler, that gets recruited, again and again, whatever the specific cultural material feeding into it happens to be.

It is worth remembering, too, how recent this entire mode of inquiry actually is. Zeki was building his early color and motion maps of the visual cortex in the 1970s using methods — recording from individual neurons in anesthetized animals — that could not have told anyone anything about subjective experience at all, only about the wiring. The technology that let him and others ask what beauty does to a living, awake, opinionated human brain did not exist in any usable form until functional imaging matured through the 1990s, which means the entire empirical literature this chapter describes has been possible for less than three decades, against a philosophical conversation about the nature of beauty that runs back at least to Plato. Measured against that timeline, neuroaesthetics is closer to its opening chapters than its conclusions, and some of the tension between Zeki's original claims and the more cautious meta-analyses that followed is exactly what an early-stage science ought to look like from the inside — bolder claims made first, on real but limited evidence, followed by a slower, messier process of other laboratories checking whether the claims survive contact with different designs, different populations, and different statistical assumptions.

Which raises the obvious question the study cannot answer on its own, and was never designed to. If the reward signal underneath beauty is this consistent across cultures — if a volunteer raised in one cultural tradition and a volunteer raised in an entirely different one show their brains lighting up in roughly the same place when each, independently, decides something is beautiful — why do actual tastes vary so enormously by culture, era, class, and generation? Why did Georgian aristocrats build houses that look nothing like the houses fashionable a hundred years later, and why does a teenager's music collection routinely embarrass the person that teenager becomes at forty? The universal machinery Zeki found sits underneath an extraordinary amount of variation in what gets fed into it, and neuroscience, by its nature, is not well equipped to explain why one specific object rather than another ends up occupying the beautiful slot in a given person's mind at a given moment in history. That explanation, when it comes, tends to look less like a brain scan and more like a sociology of who gets to decide what counts as good taste in the first place — who is taught to recognize it, who benefits from being recognized as someone who already has it, and how those judgments quietly reproduce the social order they claim only to be describing. The hardware may be shared. What gets loaded onto it, and who does the loading, is a different story, and it is not one a scanner can tell.
