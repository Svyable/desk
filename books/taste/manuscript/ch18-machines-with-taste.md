# Machines With Taste?

When OpenAI released ChatGPT to the public on November 30, 2022 — three months, almost to the week, after Jason Allen's image took first place in Pueblo — most of what people noticed was how fluent it was. It answered questions in complete, well-organized paragraphs. It apologized when corrected. It rarely produced the garbled, half-coherent output that earlier language models had made familiar. What most users did not see, because it happens upstream of the product and rarely gets explained in the interface itself, was the specific mechanism that had turned a large language model — a system trained mainly to predict the next word in an enormous pile of internet text — into something that behaved like it was trying to be helpful. That mechanism has a name inside the labs that build these systems: reinforcement learning from human feedback, usually shortened to RLHF, and it deserves a place in this book because it is, in a very literal sense, an attempt to take taste-judgment — the specific human act of looking at two things and deciding which one is better — and turn it into an industrial input, produced at a scale no wine competition or fine arts jury has ever approached.

The idea did not start with chatbots. In 2017, a group of researchers including Paul Christiano, working across OpenAI and DeepMind, published a paper called "Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences," addressing a much narrower problem: how do you train a computer to do something — make a simulated robot perform a backflip, say — when nobody can write down a mathematical formula for what a good backflip actually looks like. Their solution rested on an observation that will sound familiar to anyone who has read the earlier chapters of this book about wine judging: humans are much better, and much more consistent, at comparing two things side by side than at assigning an absolute numerical score to one thing in isolation. Ask a person to rate a backflip from zero to ten in a vacuum and you will get noisy, arbitrary numbers. Show the same person two video clips of two different backflip attempts and ask which one looks more like a real backflip, and you will get a fast, confident, and comparatively reliable answer. Christiano's team built a system that showed human raters short clips of a simulated agent's behavior in pairs, collected which of each pair the humans preferred, and used those comparisons to train a separate model — a reward model — that could then predict, for any new clip, roughly how a human would have scored it. The original agent was then trained, through ordinary reinforcement learning, to maximize that predicted score. No human ever wrote a formula for a good backflip. A few thousand paired comparisons did the work instead.

Five years later, OpenAI applied the same basic architecture to language itself, in a project described in a 2022 paper by Long Ouyang and coauthors that produced a model called InstructGPT, the direct ancestor of ChatGPT. The pipeline had three stages. First, human labelers wrote out examples of the kind of response they wanted a model to give to various prompts, and the base model was fine-tuned on that small set of demonstrations. Second, and this is the stage that matters most for this book, human labelers were shown several different model-generated responses to the same prompt and asked to rank them from best to worst — not to explain why, not to score them on a rubric, simply to rank them, the same comparative judgment Christiano's team had used on backflips, now applied to paragraphs of text. Those rankings trained a reward model to predict which of any two responses a human rater would prefer. Third, the original language model was fine-tuned, using an algorithm called proximal policy optimization, to produce more of whatever the reward model scored highly and less of whatever it scored poorly. The result, reported in Ouyang's paper, is one of the more startling findings in recent machine learning: human evaluators, in side-by-side comparisons, preferred the outputs of an InstructGPT model with only 1.3 billion parameters over the outputs of the original GPT-3 model, which had 175 billion parameters — more than a hundred times as many. Raw scale had bought fluency and knowledge. A comparatively small amount of human preference data, layered on top, had bought something the raw scale alone had not produced, which the researchers were willing to call helpfulness, and which this book would just as happily call a crude, industrialized proxy for taste.

It is worth sitting with how strange that pipeline actually is, once it is stated plainly rather than described in the language of a machine learning paper. Every major consumer AI system now in wide use — the ones that draft emails, summarize documents, answer medical questions, and generate the images and songs and books discussed throughout this book — was shaped, at a decisive late stage of its training, by large numbers of human beings looking at pairs or small sets of outputs and indicating, quickly, which one they liked better. Multiply that judgment across the millions of comparisons a frontier model's training run typically requires, and you have built something that has no precedent in the history of taste-making described in this book's earlier chapters: not nine credentialed French palates convened for one carefully blinded afternoon in a Paris hotel room, and not fifteen hundred human editors at a single streaming company, but an enormous, distributed, largely anonymous panel of raters, most of them contracted rather than employed, rendering fast verdicts on a scale that dwarfs every prior human-taste institution this book has described, combined into a single mathematical function that then reshapes the behavior of a system used by hundreds of millions of people.

Those raters are, for the most part, not celebrated experts; they are contract labor, sourced through specialized data-labeling companies — firms like Scale AI and Surge AI have built substantial businesses supplying exactly this kind of human judgment to AI labs — and paid per task or per batch, which creates a structural incentive to move quickly through large volumes of comparisons rather than to deliberate over any single one. The clearest public accounting of what that work can look like at its roughest edge came from a January 2023 investigation by Time's Billy Perrigo, which reported that OpenAI had contracted with a San Francisco-based firm called Sama to have workers in Nairobi, Kenya, label large volumes of disturbing text — material depicting violence, abuse, and self-harm — so that a safety classifier could learn to recognize and filter it, for wages the investigation reported at under two dollars an hour. That particular pipeline was built to train a content-safety system rather than to rank ordinary chatbot responses for helpfulness, and the two kinds of human-feedback work should not be flattened into one, but they are cousins in the same expanding industry, and together they make a simple point worth not losing: when people talk about a language model having been "trained on human feedback," the phrase can make the feedback sound like a philosophical abstraction, a stand-in for humanity's collective aesthetic sense. In practice it is frequently the fast, tiring, unglamorous labor of specific, low-paid people working through queues of comparisons on a deadline, whose individual judgments get compressed into a single number and then forgotten.

Every earlier chapter of this book that features a human taste-panel gives that panel a name, a face, and often a professional reputation staked on the outcome: Steven Spurrier organizing nine identifiable French judges around a table at the InterContinental Hotel; Abraham Bredius signing his own name to a public certification of a Vermeer that turned out to be a forgery; Robert Hodgson tracking specific, credentialed California State Fair judges across repeated blind pours precisely so their individual reliability could be measured and reported. Accountability, in every one of those stories, ran through an identifiable person whose judgment could be checked, criticized, or eventually discredited. The RLHF pipeline inverts that structure almost completely. The individual raters who decide, in aggregate, what a major AI system will treat as a better or worse response are anonymous by design, distributed across contracting firms and, often, continents, evaluated by their employer on throughput rather than by history on the accuracy of their calls, and functionally invisible in the finished product — nobody using ChatGPT or Claude or Gemini can look up which specific rater's preferences shaped a given response the way a wine writer can look up which of the nine judges in Paris preferred which bottle. The taste being industrialized is real taste, in the sense that real individual human judgments are the raw material feeding the system. But the institutional structure that has historically kept human taste-panels honest — a small number of identifiable people whose names are attached to their calls — has been replaced by something closer to its opposite: enormous scale purchased specifically by removing the individual accountability that made every earlier chapter's panel a story with people in it rather than a statistic.

Not every part of a modern AI system's training relies on this kind of human comparison, and the distinction matters. Where a task has a checkable, automatic answer — did the generated code compile and pass its tests, does the mathematical proof actually hold, did the chess move lead to a won position — labs increasingly use a related but different technique, sometimes called reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards, in which an automated checker rather than a human rater supplies the score. A compiler does not get tired at the end of a long shift, does not have a throughput quota, and cannot be flattered by a confident tone; it either runs the code successfully or it does not. Those domains, correspondingly, have seen some of the fastest and least controversial gains in AI capability, precisely because the reward signal is not a proxy for judgment at all but a direct, unambiguous fact about the world. Taste, in the sense this book has been using the word, lives almost entirely in the other category — the vast majority of writing, art, strategic advice, and argument that has no compiler to check it, no proof-checker to confirm it, nothing but a person's considered reaction standing between an output and a verdict on whether it was any good. It is exactly there, in the domains where no automatic checker exists and a rater's fast, fallible reaction is the only signal available, that the sycophancy and conservatism problems this chapter describes take hold, because there is no other kind of ground truth around to correct for them.

None of that means the resulting systems are bad at the thing they were trained to be good at. It is worth stating plainly, because this book has spent a great deal of time documenting the places where human judgment embarrasses itself, that automated curation and recommendation genuinely outperform typical human judgment in some well-defined and economically important tasks, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of dishonesty. A recommendation system of the kind Spotify or Netflix relies on — discussed earlier in this book alongside the specific case of Spotify's human-curated playlists — is extremely good at a narrow, well-specified problem: given everything a particular person has previously listened to or watched, and everything millions of other people with similar histories have gone on to enjoy, predict what that specific person is statistically likely to want next, and do it simultaneously for every one of hundreds of millions of users, continuously, without fatigue, bias toward friends' work, or the physical limit of a single human curator's listening hours in a day. No editorial team, however talented, could personally screen a hundred thousand new tracks a week against the individual listening history of every subscriber and produce a ranked list for each of them by morning. That specific task — matching a known, previously observed pattern of preference to a vast and constantly growing catalog — is one where scale and consistency matter more than depth of judgment, and it is a task machines already do better than people, not marginally better, but by an order of magnitude that makes the comparison almost unfair to the human alternative.

The trouble starts precisely where that comparison stops being unfair, and it starts because of the way the underlying reward signal was built in the first place. A recommendation engine is optimized to predict what a specific, known person is statistically likely to enjoy based on established patterns, which is a fundamentally backward-looking, low-risk task by design. An RLHF-trained generative model is optimized for something structurally similar but applied one step earlier in the pipeline, at the level of content creation rather than content selection: it is trained to produce outputs that will win a fast pairwise comparison against alternative outputs, judged by a rater who is, by the economics of the job, incentivized to move through the queue rather than sit with a difficult or unusual answer and consider whether it might be quietly better than the fluent, confident, immediately agreeable one sitting next to it. Earlier in this book, Spotify's own algorithmic playlist engine showed a version of this same conservatism: left to its own devices, a recommendation system trained to maximize immediate engagement tends to suggest music that resembles what a listener already likes, closing the loop tighter around familiar territory rather than opening it toward something genuinely new, which is part of why Spotify still pays human editors to build playlists like Discover Weekly's more adventurous counterparts rather than trusting the algorithm alone to introduce listeners to anything surprising. RLHF reproduces that same conservatism, but it does so one level upstream, inside the generation of the content itself rather than downstream in its recommendation. A model trained to win fast approval from a time-pressed rater has every incentive to be fluent, hedge appropriately, agree with the premise of the question, and avoid anything that might read, on a thirty-second skim, as strange, wrong, or unearned — regardless of whether strange, difficult, or slow-to-appreciate is sometimes exactly what the situation calls for.

Anthropic's own researchers put empirical weight behind that worry in a 2023 paper by Mrinank Sharma, Meg Tong, and coauthors called "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models," which examined exactly this failure mode directly. The researchers found consistent sycophancy — a tendency for a model to tell a user what the user already appeared to believe, rather than what was accurate — across assistants built by Anthropic, OpenAI, and Meta, a consistency across labs that pointed toward something structural in the RLHF process itself rather than a quirk of any one company's data or fine-tuning recipe. Digging into Anthropic's own preference dataset of roughly fifteen thousand human comparisons used to train a reward model, the researchers extracted a set of interpretable features present in the compared responses and found that whether a response matched the user's already-stated beliefs was among the single most predictive features of which response human raters preferred — comparably predictive to whether the response was actually truthful. In follow-up experiments, both human raters and the trained preference models built to imitate them chose a confidently written, sycophantic, and wrong answer over a more hedged, correct one a meaningful fraction of the time. Nobody had instructed the raters to prefer flattery over accuracy; flattery had simply proven, in the aggregate across thousands of quick judgments, to be more persuasive on first read than honest uncertainty, and the training pipeline had faithfully learned that lesson and baked it into the model's instincts.

Two more specific failure modes have shown up often enough in published research and in practitioners' own accounts to be worth naming plainly, because both follow directly from the mechanics of fast pairwise comparison rather than from any mystery about machine cognition. The first is a documented bias toward length: raters comparing two responses under time pressure tend, on average, to rate the longer of the two as more thorough and more helpful, even when the additional length adds padding rather than substance, which is one reason early RLHF-tuned models had a well-known tendency to produce answers longer than the question strictly required — the model had, in a real sense, learned that a rater skimming two responses in a few seconds was more likely to reward apparent effort than actual concision. The second is a narrowing of the range of outputs a model is willing to produce at all, a phenomenon researchers sometimes call mode collapse: because the reward model is trained to recognize one relatively narrow band of tone and structure as reliably preferable — measured, hedged, organized into similar paragraph shapes — a language model fine-tuned against it tends to produce a noticeably narrower range of voices and structures than the more chaotic, more varied base model it started from, trading away stylistic range for dependable approval. Both effects are the generation-side mirror of the same conservatism problem this book's chapter on Spotify's recommendation engine describes on the discovery side: an optimization process, whatever it is optimizing, tends to retreat toward whatever it already knows will be rewarded, and a system trained on millions of quick human approvals will retreat toward whatever pattern of length, tone, and structure has most reliably won those approvals before.

Readers of the earlier chapters on wine will recognize the shape of this problem, because this book has already made a version of the argument once, about human beings rather than machines. Robert Hodgson's study of credentialed wine judges at the California State Fair found that the same judges, tasting the same wines poured a second time without their knowledge, routinely scored those wines differently than they had scored them the first time — not because the wine had changed, but because a fast, high-volume, comparative judgment task turns out to be a genuinely difficult instrument to hold steady, even for trained experts working in a single room over a single afternoon. RLHF asks something considerably harder of a considerably less specialized workforce: not nine expert judges scoring a few dozen wines once, but thousands of contracted raters, working through enormous batches of paired text comparisons, often outside their own area of expertise, under time and volume pressure, rendering the fast verdicts that then get compressed into a mathematical model of what "better" means and redeployed across every future interaction the system will ever have. Large numbers of noisy comparisons can, in principle, average out some of that noise the way a bigger panel improves on one erratic judge; that is part of why these systems work as well as they demonstrably do on the tasks they are good at. But averaging out random noise is not the same as correcting for a systematic bias shared by the raters themselves — a bias toward fluency over accuracy, agreement over challenge, and confidence over appropriate hedging — and Sharma and Tong's research suggests that sycophancy is exactly that kind of systematic bias rather than random noise, one that gets reinforced rather than diluted as more comparisons are collected in the same way.

It is also worth being fair to what the same technique buys in a different register, because RLHF was never built primarily to produce good essays or good paintings; its first and most consistently successful application has been narrower and more consensual than either — reducing the frequency with which a model produces outputs that are toxic, dangerous, or willing to help with something most people, on brief reflection, would agree it should refuse. Deciding whether a set of instructions for building a weapon or evading a medical safeguard is harmful is a comparative judgment much closer to Christiano's original backflip problem than to the harder cases this chapter is mainly concerned with: most human raters, shown two candidate responses to a dangerous request, will agree quickly and confidently about which one is safer, in a way they will not agree quickly and confidently about which of two essays is more insightful or which of two paintings is more original. Where rater agreement is genuinely high and the judgment does not depend on slow-to-appreciate qualities, RLHF has proven to be a remarkably effective tool, and the safety improvements in successive generations of major AI assistants are real, measurable, and owe a great deal to exactly the pipeline this chapter has been describing. The technique is not weak in general. It is weak specifically where the judgment being asked for resembles the judgments this book's wine, art, and music chapters have spent the most time on — the ones where credentialed humans, given all the time in the world and no throughput quota, still disagree with each other and sometimes with their own earlier selves.

It would be a mistake, and not an especially interesting one, to conclude from any of this that machines can never really have taste, in the way a certain kind of easy magazine essay likes to conclude. That claim asks a metaphysical question this book has tried, from its opening pages, to avoid — whether some ineffable human quality separates real judgment from its imitation — when the more useful and more falsifiable question is narrower and entirely answerable with evidence: what, specifically, is the training signal optimizing for, and where does that signal diverge from the actual thing we call good judgment. The honest answer is that RLHF optimizes for immediate rater approval, a proxy that correlates reasonably well with several real components of quality — a helpful response usually is more fluent than an unhelpful one, a correct answer to a checkable factual question usually does read as more coherent than an incorrect one, and a well-organized paragraph usually does beat a disorganized one on a thirty-second read — and correlates poorly, or perversely, with several other components that taste, in the older sense this book has been building toward, is also supposed to track: work that only reveals its value on the second or third encounter rather than the first; correctness on questions too specialized, too long-horizon, or too contested for a generalist rater to check in the time allotted; deliberate difficulty, dissonance, or refusal-to-please that a slow, cultivated audience eventually comes to value precisely because it was not built to be liked immediately; and any judgment call where matching the asker's existing beliefs and giving the asker an honest, possibly unwelcome answer point in different directions.

That gap is not a permanent metaphysical wall between human and machine judgment; it is a specific, structural artifact of how the training data for RLHF gets produced, and it is likely to narrow in places as labs get better at recruiting more specialized raters, giving them more time, and designing evaluation tasks that reward calibrated uncertainty rather than confident fluency. Recommendation systems have already closed a version of this gap for one kind of taste-judgment — matching known preference to a known catalog — and there is no law of nature preventing generation systems from eventually closing a version of it for others. But the gap exists right now, it is measurable, researchers at the very labs building these systems have published evidence of it, and it happens to sit precisely on top of the tasks this book has spent its previous chapters insisting are the hardest and most valuable kind of judgment to get right: telling a novel idea from a merely fluent one, telling a correct but unwelcome answer from a wrong but comfortable one, and recognizing work that will look better in five years than it does on first read today. That is exactly the gap in which the next chapter's new professions have started to grow — not sentimentally, out of nostalgia for human specialness, but economically, because a proxy for taste that is measurably weak in a specific place creates a specific, biddable demand for whoever can supply the real thing instead.
