# The Opera Spatial Problem

The Colorado State Fair runs every August in Pueblo, a steel town on the Arkansas River about two hours south of Denver, and most of what happens there is exactly what a person would expect from a state fair: 4-H kids leading steers around a show ring, a midway smelling of funnel cake and diesel, a competitive pie table, a building full of quilts pinned with judges' ribbons that have been awarded, in more or less the same format, since the fair's founding in 1872. Tucked inside that same fine arts building, alongside the amateur painting and photography entries, is a smaller and newer category called Digital Arts/Digitally-Manipulated Photography, part of the fair's "emerging artist" division for non-professional entrants. In most years it draws a modest number of submissions and generates no controversy whatsoever. In 2022 it produced a story that made it, briefly, into the Associated Press, the Washington Post, Smithsonian Magazine, and a great many aggrieved corners of Twitter and Reddit.

The entry was a large, ornate image titled Théâtre D'opéra Spatial — Space Opera Theater — depicting two robed, faceless figures with their backs to the viewer, standing in a grand hall of the kind that might belong to a cathedral or an opera house or a spaceship, gazing into a luminous, circular opening that filled the far wall with light. It had the compositional confidence of a nineteenth-century academic painting and the strange gloss of something that had never touched a brush. It won first place in its category. It came with a $300 prize. And the man who submitted it, a Pueblo West resident named Jason M. Allen who ran a small tabletop-game company called Incarnate Games, had not painted it, photographed it, or drawn it in any conventional sense. He had built it with Midjourney, an AI image generator that in the late summer of 2022 was still running as an invitation-only tool inside a Discord server, by typing and retyping descriptive text prompts, refining the wording the way a person might refine an order at a very specific restaurant, watching the software return four rough options at a time and pushing the phrasing further with each round. Allen later said the final image was the product of roughly 624 prompts and revisions, run over the course of about eighty hours, before he exported the composition he wanted, cleaned it up in Adobe Photoshop, and used a separate program called Gigapixel AI to upscale it to a resolution large enough to print and hang on a fairground wall.

The two jurors who judged the digital arts category that year said afterward that they had not known, at the time of judging, that Midjourney was an AI image generator; they had assumed it was some kind of advanced photo-editing software, in a category built around exactly that kind of tool. One of them, a local author and art historian named Dagny McKinley, said the piece had struck her immediately for its composition and its evocation of something like Renaissance grandeur, and that she and her co-juror had judged it, as they judged everything else in the room, on the finished image in front of them. Both said, once the method became public and reporters started calling, that they would have given it first place regardless.

Allen posted his win to social media with evident pride, framing it as proof that AI-assisted art belonged in the same conversation as art made by hand, and the backlash arrived within a day. Digital artists on Twitter and Reddit — people who spend hours manually manipulating photographs and building illustrations pixel by pixel inside programs like Photoshop, the very category Allen's category was named for — argued that it was not a fair fight and never could be: a tool that could generate a finished, exhibition-quality composition from a paragraph of English had no business competing against, let alone beating, work that required weeks of manual technical skill to produce by hand. Some of the reaction was measured; a great deal of it was not, running from accusations that Allen had cheated to broader, angrier anxiety about what a tool like Midjourney meant for every working illustrator's income and self-conception, sentiments that would recur, at much larger scale, in the arguments over AI-generated books and AI-generated music that fill earlier chapters of this book. Allen pushed back on the cheating charge specifically and, on that narrow point, had a real argument to make. He had disclosed his method to the fair's organizers on the entry form before the judging took place, writing "Jason M. Allen via Midjourney" as the stated medium; nobody had hidden anything from anyone with actual authority over the category. His deeper claim was that 624 rounds of prompting and rejection constituted real curatorial and creative labor — closer, he argued, to what a photographer does when selecting and adjusting a shot from among many exposures than to pressing a single button and taking whatever appeared. The category was called Digitally-Manipulated Photography, a name that had already, for years, accepted heavy computer involvement as compatible with the human authorship a state fair ribbon was meant to certify. What the category's rules had not done, because nobody writing them in previous years had needed to, was define how much of that manipulation a machine was allowed to perform on its own initiative before the result stopped being the entrant's work and started being the software's.

Allen's win was not the only institutional collision of that particular autumn. A little over two months later, on November 9, 2022, the art-sharing platform DeviantArt launched its own AI image generator, called DreamUp, built on the same underlying Stable Diffusion technology that had opened to the public that August. DeviantArt's early policy made every piece of art already posted on the platform, going back years, available by default for DreamUp to train on and imitate, with an opt-out that had to be requested per work or, for a whole account, through a web form that could take up to ten days to process. The backlash was immediate and much larger than the one Allen had triggered, since it implicated not one contest entry but the platform's entire back catalog of working artists' portfolios. A professional illustrator named Steven Zapata told Artnet News the rollout was "tone-deaf" and fundamentally out of step with the community DeviantArt had spent years cultivating. Within days, facing an exodus of furious longtime users, DeviantArt reversed the default, excluding all existing work from AI training unless an artist specifically opted in. Two institutions, a state fair board and a two-decade-old art website, spent the same season discovering that a policy written for one set of assumptions about how images get made had quietly stopped matching reality, and both responded the same way: not by resolving what an AI-assisted image actually is, but by inserting a disclosure or a consent mechanism in front of it and hoping the mechanism would hold until someone smarter figured out the underlying question.

The Colorado State Fair answered that question, in the practical way institutions usually answer questions like it, by rewriting the rule rather than resolving the philosophy behind it. Beginning with the 2023 fair, every entrant in the fine arts competition was required to disclose whether their work had been created with the help of an artificial intelligence generator, and fair officials began discussing whether digital art assisted by AI tools deserved a separate category of its own, judged on its own terms rather than measured against work made without that assistance. Allen, for his part, was unhappy with the new disclosure requirement, telling the Denver Post that singling out AI-assisted work amounted to a "discriminatory mark" against it — a complaint that inverted the artists' original grievance almost exactly: where they had argued the categories treated AI-generated and hand-made work as equivalent when they should not be, he now argued the fair was treating them as separate when, in his view, they should not be either. Both objections could not be fully right, and the fair's actual response — disclosure first, a possible new category later — was less a philosophical answer than an attempt to buy time to figure out which objection mattered more, using the oldest tool institutions have for that purpose: paperwork.

A second, more consequential institution took up a version of the same question the following year, with more lasting effect. In 2023, Allen applied to the United States Copyright Office to register Théâtre D'opéra Spatial, seeking the same legal protection any painter or photographer routinely receives over an original work. The Copyright Office's Review Board denied the application. Its reasoning turned specifically on how much of the image's actual content — its composition, its lighting, the particular arrangement of forms that make a viewer's eye move where it does — had been generated by Midjourney's model in response to Allen's prompts, rather than directly controlled by Allen's own hand. The office's long-standing position is that copyright protects the "traditional elements of authorship" produced by a human being; where a machine, rather than a person, generates those elements, the office has held, the work lacks the human authorship copyright law requires, whatever human curation surrounded the process of getting there. Allen's own account of his method — the 624 rounds of prompting, revising, and selecting — became, in front of the Copyright Office, not evidence of authorship but evidence against it, a paper trail documenting exactly how much of the final image had emerged from Midjourney's own internal choices rather than Allen's. The Review Board's letter denying registration put the distinction plainly: Allen's prompts, in its reading, described broad ideas and instructions, while Midjourney itself generated the specific expressive details — the brushwork, the exact fall of light through the circular window, the particular arrangement of drapery — that copyright law treats as the actual substance of authorship. Because Allen refused to disclaim that machine-generated content and register only whatever remained, the office concluded, the work as a whole could not be registered.

Allen sued. On September 26, 2024, he filed Allen v. Perlmutter in the United States District Court for the District of Colorado, naming the Register of Copyrights, Shira Perlmutter, as defendant and asking the court to overturn the office's refusal. His argument on paper was the same one he had been making since the state fair controversy two years earlier, now dressed in litigation: that an iterative process spanning hundreds of prompts and deliberate artistic choices amounts to substantial human direction over the image, whatever tool ultimately rendered the pixels. The government's answer echoed the Review Board's original reasoning almost exactly — that Allen's prompts specified what he wanted in general terms and Midjourney supplied the expression itself, which is the part the statute actually protects. As of August 2026, the case had finished summary-judgment briefing in front of the district court with no ruling yet issued, a genuinely open question sitting, at this writing, on a judge's desk rather than in a settled body of law.

It sits there alongside a related but not identical precedent that had, by then, already closed. A computer scientist named Stephen Thaler had spent years arguing an even more radical version of Allen's claim: that an AI system he called DABUS, short for "Device for the Autonomous Bootstrapping of Unified Sentience," should itself be listed as the author of an image titled A Recent Entrance to Paradise, with Thaler owning the copyright as the machine's owner rather than its author. The Copyright Office rejected that application in 2019; a district court and then the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, ruling on March 18, 2025, upheld the rejection, affirming that the Copyright Act requires a human author and that a work generated with no human control over its expression cannot qualify. The Supreme Court declined to hear a further appeal on March 2, 2026, letting the D.C. Circuit's ruling stand as the closest thing American law now has to a settled position on fully autonomous AI authorship. But Thaler's case and Allen's case are not the same question, however often they get mentioned in the same sentence. Thaler conceded, as a matter of strategy and belief, that no human had exercised any expressive control at all. Allen has never conceded anything of the kind; his entire legal position is that 624 rounds of prompting and selection is expressive control, exercised by a human being, merely delivered through an unusual instrument. Resolving that a machine cannot be an author, which is what Thaler's case settled, says nothing about how much human steering of a machine is enough to make the human one — which is the actual, harder, still-open question sitting in the Colorado docket.

Allen never treated any of this as a reason to retreat. He had come to Midjourney the way a great many early users did in 2022, looking for artwork to illustrate his tabletop games rather than setting out to make a statement about the future of art, and he stayed, after the controversy, exactly where the controversy had put him: a minor public figure for AI-assisted image-making, giving interviews, selling prints of Théâtre D'opéra Spatial, and continuing to build images with the same iterative, prompt-heavy method that had won him the ribbon in the first place. He did not apologize for the win, disclaim the process, or concede that the fair's judges had been misled about anything that mattered. Whatever a court eventually decides about the copyright, Allen's own account of his summer in 2022 — hour after hour narrowing a description toward a single mental image he could not paint or photograph himself, rejecting outcome after outcome until one finally matched what he had been picturing — describes an experience that felt, to him, indistinguishable from the ordinary frustration and satisfaction of making something. Whether that felt experience constitutes authorship in the sense copyright law is willing to recognize is precisely the question nobody, including the judge with the motion now sitting on their desk, has yet had to answer for a case built quite this way.

Set beside the rest of this book, the entire episode looks at first like something new arriving too fast for anyone to have written the rules in advance, and in one narrow sense — the speed — it is. But strip away the word "AI" for a moment and the shape of the dispute is one this book has already told twice. State fairs are, among other things, nineteenth-century machinery for solving a very old problem: how do you take something as subjective as "this pie is better than that pie" or "this quilt shows more skill than that quilt" and turn it into a defensible, repeatable procedure that a community will accept as fair, complete with named judges, transparent categories, and a ribbon that means something because everyone agreed in advance on what it would take to earn one. The digital arts category was a late graft onto that older machinery, built to compare manual skill exercised through software tools like Photoshop against manual skill exercised with a brush, on the assumption that both still required a human hand performing the work being judged. Nobody drafting that category's rules in whatever year it was created had needed to specify how much of the manipulating a machine was allowed to do on its own, because until 2022 the question had never actually come up. The rule was not badly written. It was written for a world that had not yet produced Midjourney.

The dispute is not even the first time a new image-making machine forced fine art institutions to decide, in public and under pressure, whether a mechanical process could produce real authorship. When photography itself was young, in the second half of the nineteenth century, the dominant view among painters and critics was that a camera merely recorded what was already in front of it, with no more claim to artistry than a mirror; the London-based Photographic Society, founded in 1853 and later granted royal patronage, spent decades lobbying for photography's inclusion in exhibitions on anything like equal footing with painting, and mostly lost that argument for most of the century. Alfred Stieglitz, in New York, took up the same fight a generation later, founding a group called the Photo-Secession on February 17, 1902, built specifically around the claim that what made a photograph significant was not the mechanical fact in front of the lens but the photographer's deliberate manipulation of exposure, printing, and composition to express a subjective vision — a claim strikingly close to the one Jason Allen would make, a hundred and twenty years later, about deliberate manipulation of a text prompt. Stieglitz published a journal, Camera Work, and mounted exhibitions specifically to argue that photography was a technical, manual craft in the same sense a paintbrush was a technical, manual object: a tool through which authorship passed, not a replacement for it. It took decades, and it was never fully resolved so much as gradually absorbed; museums eventually built photography departments, camera clubs eventually got their own categories at fairs very much like Colorado's, and the argument that a mechanical image-making device could not produce real art quietly stopped being made by anyone whose opinion mattered, not because someone won a decisive debate but because enough photographs accumulated that the position became untenable to hold. AI image tools are being asked, in a fraction of that time, to survive the identical argument — that the device did the work, not the person operating it — with the added complication that a camera cannot generate an entire finished composition from a sentence, and a photographer cannot produce six hundred candidate images an hour to choose among.

That is closer to what happened, decades earlier and with far higher stakes, to Abraham Bredius, the Dutch art historian whose 1937 certification of a forged Vermeer — a Christ and the Adulteress that Han van Meegeren had spent years learning to fake convincingly enough to fool exactly the kind of expert eye Bredius had spent a career training — briefly made van Meegeren rich and permanently damaged Bredius's reputation once the fraud came out at trial a decade later. Bredius was not a fool, and his expertise was not fake; it was real expertise, trained on the actual population of forgeries and genuine Old Masters that had passed in front of him for fifty years, and van Meegeren had studied that expertise closely enough to build a fake that satisfied every test it was equipped to apply. The categories a connoisseur's trained eye is built to sort — genuine seventeenth-century brushwork versus a later imitation — simply had not yet needed to include "convincing enough to fool Bredius specifically," because nobody had built that particular forgery before. Allen's case runs the same structural problem in the opposite moral direction. He did not deceive anyone; he told the fair exactly what tool he had used, before judging, in writing. What failed was not his honesty but the category's imagination — a rule built to sort human manual effort from other human manual effort, encountering, for the first time, an entrant that fit the letter of the category and broke its unstated assumptions anyway.

The Getty Museum's kouros offers a still closer parallel, because its authenticity remains formally unresolved to this day, decades after the museum paid roughly nine million dollars for a nearly seven-foot marble youth in 1985 on the strength of scientific tests suggesting its surface had aged for centuries, only to have several of the most respected classical art historians in the world take one look at the statue in person and feel, with a confidence the science had not prepared them to have, that something about it was wrong. The Getty's own wall label today still hedges between "Greek, about 530 B.C." and "modern forgery," a hedge that has survived scientific analysis, expert testimony, and nearly four decades of scholarly argument precisely because the categories being asked to resolve the question — ancient or fake, ourselves competent to tell the difference or not — turned out not to have a clean answer waiting underneath them. That is the discomfort Allen's copyright appeal shares with the Getty's disputed label, at a much smaller scale and over far less money: an institution built to render a clean, binary verdict — authored or not, genuine or not — discovering, once a genuinely novel case reaches it, that the verdict it is being asked to render does not actually reduce to the categories the institution has on hand.

Michelin and Trader Joe's, elsewhere in this book, represent the opposite outcome: institutions that got ahead of exactly this kind of ambiguity by building procedural machinery — anonymous inspectors rotating through restaurants on an expense account, a strict and enforced ceiling on how many items a single store is allowed to stock — sturdy enough that the judgment behind it rarely needs to be litigated case by case. The Colorado State Fair and the United States Copyright Office are now doing that same institutional work in real time, under public attention, for a technology whose capabilities are changing faster than either institution's normal rulemaking cycle was ever built to track. By the time Colorado's 2023 disclosure requirement took effect, Midjourney itself had already moved several versions past the one Allen used, needing far fewer than 624 prompts to produce comparable results; a rule written to catch up with one moment in the technology's development was, by the time it was enforced, already describing a tool that no longer quite existed. Whatever the courts eventually decide about Allen's copyright — and as of this writing they had not decided — the more durable fact is procedural rather than legal: institutions whose entire social function is to say, credibly, that this one is better than that one, are now being forced to rewrite their own operating rules faster than they are used to rewriting anything, because the thing they were built to judge no longer holds still long enough to be judged the old way twice.

None of that answers the question the state fair judges, the copyright examiners, or Allen's critics on Twitter were actually asking, which was never really about pixels or prompts. It was the same question a Bordeaux faculty asks about a dyed glass of wine, a museum asks about a marble youth, and a fair board asks about a quilt: how much of what we are praising lives in the object, and how much of it lives in the story we have been told about how the object came to exist. Midjourney did not invent that question. It only asked it fast enough, and publicly enough, that an institution built to answer questions like it roughly once a year discovered it needed an answer within a news cycle — and discovered, at the same time, that the software producing the problem was also, quietly, being trained on the very human approval it was busy making harder to trust.
