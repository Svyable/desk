# Chapter 10 — Getty kouros primary-source audit

Checked September 11, 2026 against Getty's own technical and institutional publications.

## Why this audit exists

Chapter 10 currently uses the Getty kouros as a case about expert pattern recognition colliding with apparently objective scientific authentication. That is a good case for a book about taste, but the familiar popular retelling is tidier than the primary record.

The manuscript's strongest overreach is the sentence-level implication that **intuition ultimately proved more accurate than the science**. The object's authenticity has not been resolved cleanly enough to support that verdict.

The correction is more interesting: connoisseurship and laboratory evidence identified different kinds of uncertainty, the scientific interpretation itself evolved as the surface was studied more deeply, and even a deliberately multidisciplinary colloquium failed to close the case.

## What Getty's own record establishes

### 1. The acquisition was heavily studied before purchase

Getty's museum history says the previously unknown kouros underwent roughly two years of study before its 1985 purchase. The museum says stylistic evidence pointed overall toward authenticity and that the scientific evidence strongly supported the same conclusion at the time.

Primary institutional source:
https://www.getty.edu/publications/resources/virtuallibrary/0892364769.pdf

This matters because the episode is not well described as careless curators ignoring science. The institution commissioned substantial review and still ended in uncertainty.

### 2. The surface-science story became more complex than 'dedolomitization proved age'

Frank Preusser's contribution to *The Getty Kouros Colloquium* reports that the marble was identified as dolomitic material broadly consistent with Thasos. Early studies of the 10–50 micrometer surface alteration layer emphasized calcite produced by dedolomitization.

Later analysis using multiple techniques found the layer to consist mainly of **calcium oxalate monohydrate (whewellite)**, with smaller contributions from iron-oxide-rich soil and calcite. Getty scientists could produce whewellite on dolomitic marble using oxalic-acid treatment, but they reported that they could not reproduce the complexity of the kouros alteration layer.

Primary technical source:
https://www.getty.edu/publications/resources/virtuallibrary/0892362634.pdf

Safe inference: the original scientific case did not simply 'fail.' Its explanatory model changed, and artificial alteration became more plausible in some respects without a successful reproduction of the observed surface.

Too strong: the lab test was debunked and therefore the connoisseurs were proven right.

### 3. The multidisciplinary meeting did not settle authenticity

Getty's 1993 proceedings collect nineteen papers from scholars and scientists addressing authenticity. Getty's later museum history calls the international symposium **inconclusive**.

That is the key evidentiary endpoint for the book.

An inconclusive symposium is not a failure of expertise. It is evidence that the object generated multiple partly independent lines of evidence whose likelihoods were difficult to calibrate.

### 4. Getty itself later still leaned toward authenticity

The museum's published collection history says that, after renewed suspicions and the inconclusive symposium, Getty believed the weight of available evidence supported authenticity.

That assessment cannot be treated as neutral: the institution owned the object and had invested heavily in it. But it is important counterevidence to the popular narrative that later scholarship straightforwardly vindicated the skeptics.

The chapter should preserve this asymmetry:

- some connoisseurs had immediate negative reactions;
- some experts judged the statue authentic;
- scientific testing initially supported authenticity strongly;
- later technical work complicated the surface-weathering mechanism;
- the formal multidisciplinary review did not reach consensus;
- Getty itself subsequently continued to argue that the balance of evidence favored authenticity.

No honest version of that sequence yields 'intuition won.'

## Editorial correction required before merge

Keep the chapter's larger argument but revise any wording equivalent to:

> intuition, in this case, appears to have been more accurate than the science

That sentence claims a ground truth the historical record does not supply.

A stronger replacement argument would be:

> The unsettling lesson is not that the eye beat the laboratory. It is that neither form of expertise could cash its confidence into a decisive authentication. The connoisseurs detected stylistic tension that laboratory work could not erase; the laboratory found a surface history that intuition could not explain away. The unresolved object exposed where each method's uncertainty lived.

Also avoid presenting quoted gut reactions from later popular retellings as though they are themselves the primary evidentiary record unless their original source is retrieved.

## What this case can support in *Taste*

The Getty kouros is a good example of **uncertainty localization**:

- trained visual judgment can notice incompatibilities before it can verbalize them;
- scientific measurement can constrain material history without automatically proving object history;
- provenance documents provide another evidence lane and should not inherit certainty from either style or chemistry;
- institutional incentives can affect how ambiguous evidence is weighted without making the evidence itself fraudulent;
- a mature decision process needs methods whose failure modes differ.

That is a better fit for the book's thesis than a victory story about gut instinct.

Taste is valuable when it notices the thing the metric did not measure.

It becomes dangerous when the noticing itself is mistaken for a verdict.
