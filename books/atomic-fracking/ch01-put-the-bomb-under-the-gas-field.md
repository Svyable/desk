# 1. Put the Bomb Under the Gas Field

On December 10, 1967, somebody put a nuclear explosive 4,240 feet under New Mexico and called it a drilling experiment.

The sentence sounds like satire because history has been edited by survival. We remember the technologies that reproduced. Their ancestors acquire inevitability. The failures become jokes, if they are remembered at all. A rotary drill belongs in an oil field because rotary drills won. Hydraulic fracturing belongs there because hydraulic fracturing won. A nuclear bomb belongs in a silo, a submarine, a bomber, a test range, a museum.

Not under a natural-gas reservoir.

Except it did.

Project Gasbuggy was a joint experiment of the Atomic Energy Commission, El Paso Natural Gas Company, and the U.S. Bureau of Mines. The proposition was brutally simple. Some gas reservoirs contain enormous quantities of hydrocarbons trapped in rock that does not surrender them easily. Break enough rock and the gas can move. Conventional wells had a radius of influence. Explosives had another.

The atomic age had supplied an explosive with an almost embarrassing amount of influence.

The U.S. Geological Survey's contemporary report describes a 26-kiloton design-yield device detonated at 12:30 Mountain Standard Time in Rio Arriba County. It sat in the Lewis Shale, about forty feet below the contact with the gas-bearing Pictured Cliffs sandstone. The report's first judgment was wonderfully narrow: early indications were that the explosive had “performed satisfactorily.”

Of course it had.

The bomb was the part of the system America already knew how to make work.

That is the first mistake this story can correct.

A frontier technology is almost never one technology.

It is a stack of permissions imposed by physics, engineering, economics, biology, regulation, culture and time. Passing one layer can make the whole thing look solved precisely when it is not. Gasbuggy did not need to prove that a nuclear explosive could release energy underground. The country had an extensive empirical literature on that. The useful uncertainty was downstream. Would the explosion create the right fracture network? Would gas flow through it? Would the gas be usable? Could wells recover it economically? Would customers accept it? Would regulators permit it? Would the public tolerate an energy industry whose completion crews arrived with nuclear devices?

These are not footnotes to feasibility. They are feasibility.

## The hole

The location was remote enough that the map itself begins to sound like an alibi: northwestern New Mexico, about fifty-five air miles east of Farmington. The explosive went more than four thousand feet down. Instruments spread outward to record what the ground did when the device fired.

USGS seismologists were there because a peaceful nuclear explosion remains a nuclear explosion to the Earth. Their 1968 open-file report records surface seismic measurements at intermediate distances. That document is useful partly because it has none of the retrospective weirdness the project later accumulated. It is a technical report about an event that its authors treated as an engineering experiment.

This is one of the privileges of reading old frontier science. You get to encounter the impossible before history decided it was foolish.

Project Plowshare had been formally established by the Atomic Energy Commission in 1957 to investigate peaceful applications of nuclear explosives. Excavation. Underground storage. Resource extraction. The same physical fact that made nuclear weapons terrifying—the concentration of extraordinary energy into a compact device—could be redescribed as an industrial capability.

Language did some work here. A bomb could become an “explosive.” A crater could become excavation. A blast cavity could become storage. Fractured rock could become reservoir stimulation.

But it would be too easy to treat the vocabulary as mere propaganda. Engineers really did have a problem that large underground explosions could address. The United States and Soviet Union both investigated peaceful nuclear explosions. The question was not invented by a public-relations department. It emerged from an actual engineering temptation: if you already possess a device capable of moving millions of tons of earth or shattering huge underground volumes, what else can you do with it?

Gasbuggy was where that temptation met natural gas.

And something happened that makes the story harder than a morality play.

The gas flow improved.

Lawrence Livermore's retrospective history says production increased six to eight times over previous rates. If the experiment had simply failed to fracture the formation, Plowshare gas stimulation would be an amusing museum piece. The bomb would have been too crude, the rock would have refused, everyone would have gone home.

Instead, the physical mechanism earned another experiment.

This is the dangerous kind of failure: the kind that works enough to continue.

## The gas

The nuclear device used at Gasbuggy had been designed with residual radioactivity in mind. That sentence deserves to sit by itself.

The designers knew that a gas reservoir stimulated by a nuclear explosion had a peculiar product-quality problem. The explosion could not merely fracture the rock. It had to do so without turning the recovered gas into something commercially impossible.

Livermore's own historical account is blunt about the outcome: Gasbuggy produced an undesirably high concentration of tritium in the gas.

Now the word *success* becomes unstable.

The explosive fired. Success.

The formation fractured. Success.

Production increased. Success.

The produced gas carried radioactive contamination. Problem.

Economic viability remained unproved. Problem.

The experiment had crossed the threshold from “nuclear fracking is impossible” to “nuclear fracking creates a much more interesting argument.”

That is where this book lives.

A lot of technology history is told as a tournament between machines. Steam versus diesel. Horse versus automobile. Film versus digital. Nuclear gas stimulation is more revealing because the rival that eventually mattered was not required to beat the nuclear explosive at energy density. It only had to solve the commercial problem better.

Hydraulic fracturing did not have to be more spectacular.

It had to be less nuclear.

## Rulison

The second gas-stimulation experiment was Project Rulison in Colorado in September 1969. It used another underground nuclear explosive. Again the technical problem was attacked as an engineering problem. Again gas could be liberated. Again gas quality refused to remain a secondary detail.

A technology does not sell its mechanism. It sells the state of the world after the mechanism has operated.

This is obvious when written plainly and astonishingly easy to forget inside a program.

Imagine a team whose job is to increase permeability. Their charts can improve while the product dies.

Imagine a device laboratory whose job is to reduce residual contamination. Its design can improve while economics dies.

Imagine an energy company whose reservoir model says recoverable gas has increased. Its resource estimate can improve while public acceptance dies.

No participant has to be irrational. The system can fail through locally successful work.

That is a more useful explanation than hubris.

Hubris is a personality defect. The feasibility trap is an institutional geometry.

## Rio Blanco

The third and final natural-gas-reservoir stimulation experiment was Project Rio Blanco in 1973. This time three devices were detonated nearly simultaneously at different depths in one emplacement well in Colorado.

Three.

If Gasbuggy is the image that sticks—the bomb beneath the gas field—Rio Blanco is the moment the program becomes almost architectural. The intention was not simply to make a cavity. The geometry mattered. Multiple explosions could, in principle, create a connected fractured system through which gas could migrate and be recovered.

Livermore's retrospective account says the device design was acceptably clean, but only small amounts of gas were obtained; later tests exposed problems with the site that made production testing impractical.

This is the second correction to the easy version of the story.

The program did not merely discover “radioactive gas” and stop. Engineers responded to contamination. Device design changed. The system moved. Then another constraint asserted itself.

That pattern is the book.

Frontier technologies do not usually meet one wall. They enter a corridor of walls.

Solve heat, discover materials.

Solve materials, discover manufacturing.

Solve manufacturing, discover cost.

Solve cost, discover regulation.

Solve regulation, discover demand.

Solve contamination, discover geology.

A single demonstration can be genuinely impressive and still tell you almost nothing about whether the corridor has an exit.

## The public was part of the machine

Technical histories sometimes treat public acceptance as an external force that interrupted engineering. That framing is convenient and wrong.

If the technology produces a consumer fuel, the consumer is in the system boundary.

If it requires federal licensing, the regulator is in the system boundary.

If it creates a site requiring long-term institutional controls, future governments are in the system boundary.

If it depends on repeated nuclear detonations beneath producing regions, politics is in the system boundary.

Calling these things “nontechnical” does not make them unreal.

This becomes especially important when evaluating strange or suppressed technologies. A familiar rhetorical move goes like this: *The technology worked, but politics killed it.* Sometimes that statement is even true in the narrowest sense. But “worked” has been quietly defined as passing the experimenter's preferred metric.

Project Plowshare gives us a documented place to examine the trick.

Gasbuggy can be described as a technical success because it increased gas production. It can be described as a commercial failure because the technology never established economic viability and produced contaminated gas. Those sentences do not contradict each other. The contradiction appears only if success is treated as a single variable.

That mistake is everywhere in frontier science.

A propulsion device produces thrust, therefore spacecraft.

A material has an unusual isotope ratio, therefore manufacture.

A laboratory task produces an above-chance result, therefore operational intelligence.

A sensor records an anomaly, therefore an anomalous craft.

A model passes a benchmark, therefore generalized intelligence.

The missing layers are where reality charges interest.

## The ledger underground

There is a temptation, looking backward, to make Plowshare ridiculous because we know it lost.

Resist it.

The people involved had access to a new physical capability of unprecedented scale. They asked what else it could do. That is exactly what engineers are supposed to ask.

The error, where there was error, is more subtle and more contemporary: physical capability has glamour. System viability is bookkeeping.

A nuclear explosive can shatter rock in an instant. The costs arrive for decades.

Gasbuggy's surface was eventually cleaned and restored. Equipment contaminated during production testing had to be decontaminated. The underground source did not cease to exist because the experiment ended. Department of Energy legacy-management responsibilities survived the technology that created them.

That gives us another metric.

A machine has a half-life after its program dies.

Not a radioactive half-life, though here the pun is unavoidable. An institutional half-life. Monitoring. Land restrictions. Records. Liability. Sampling. Signs. Future drilling constraints. The technology continues consuming attention after it stops producing value.

Most technology comparisons ignore this tail because it does not fit in the demonstration.

The demonstration is where the cameras are.

The tail is where the bill is.

## The Soviet mirror

The Soviet Union pursued its own peaceful nuclear explosion program, including applications connected to resource extraction and underground engineering. That parallel matters, but not because two governments trying something proves the idea was good.

Convergent state interest proves interest.

It can also tell us that the engineering temptation was not uniquely American. Two nuclear powers looked at devices developed for war and independently saw tools. The shared premise was physical: enormous controlled underground energy release might perform industrial work that conventional machinery could not perform as cheaply or at the same scale.

The later chapters will compare those programs carefully. The comparison has to resist a common failure in anomalous research: once two countries investigated the same strange subject, researchers treat convergence as confirmation of the underlying phenomenon.

No.

Rivals can share errors. Rivals can react to each other's programs. Rivals can fund low-probability options because the cost of ignoring a genuine breakthrough appears larger than the cost of investigating it. Intelligence competition can manufacture parallel curiosity.

What convergence establishes is that the question survived more than one bureaucracy.

That is interesting enough.

## The bomb worked

The deepest lesson of Gasbuggy may be contained in that sentence.

The bomb worked.

It is almost useless as a conclusion.

It tells us that the most mature component in the experiment performed the job it was already known to perform. The consequential uncertainty lived elsewhere: fracture geometry, reservoir behavior, radionuclide production and transport, gas cleanup, economics, licensing, public tolerance, and the rate at which conventional alternatives would improve.

The spectacular component hijacked attention.

This still happens.

When a frontier claim contains one extraordinary mechanism, we stare at the mechanism. If it can be made to work, we assume the world downstream will arrange itself.

But a technology is the downstream.

A fusion reaction is not a power plant.

A quantum bit is not a useful computer.

A rocket engine is not a transportation economy.

An anomalous laboratory effect is not an intelligence service.

And a nuclear explosion under a gas field is not a gas industry.

Project Gasbuggy's real value may be that it lets us say this without guessing.

They put the bomb underground.

They fired it.

The rock broke.

More gas came out.

And that was nowhere near enough.
