# ATOMIC FRACKING

## The Day America Tried to Turn Nuclear Bombs into Drilling Tools

### Sven Hardy Benson

**Status: Drafting**

This is a book about a technology that crossed the line from ridiculous to physically demonstrated and still failed.

That distinction matters. We are unusually bad at discussing frontier technologies because we collapse at least four questions into one: *Can it happen? Can it work? Can it work economically? Can it become a product that society will permit?* Project Plowshare's natural-gas experiments force those questions apart with unusual violence.

The United States did detonate underground nuclear explosives in attempts to stimulate natural-gas production. That is documented history. The blasts fractured rock and, in important respects, changed gas production. That does not establish that nuclear gas stimulation was commercially viable, environmentally acceptable, safe in every relevant sense, or secretly perfected later.

This manuscript marks evidence in four classes.

**Class 1 — documented.** Government technical reports, laboratory histories, monitoring records, contemporary program documents, and physical site records.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete.** Serious historical reporting, participant recollections, and technical interpretation for which important underlying records or denominators remain missing.

**Class 3 — disputed.** Claims of concealed continuation, suppressed commercial success, undisclosed contamination, or other allegations not independently established by the public record.

**Class 4 — conjecture.** The book's own synthesis: especially the idea that Plowshare exposes a recurring “feasibility trap,” in which a spectacular proof of physical possibility makes institutions slow to notice that deployment has already failed on a different axis.

A patent is evidence that somebody filed a patent. A classified document is evidence that somebody classified a document. A successful detonation is evidence that an explosive detonated. None of these facts gets promoted into evidence of efficacy by rhetorical momentum.

The strangest thing about this story is that it does not need embellishment.
