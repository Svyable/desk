# Chapter 14 — Plastics Are Not the Point

Whenever the deep-time detectability of our civilization comes up, somebody mentions plastic.

The image is intuitive. We have made a material unknown to most of Earth history, produced it in enormous quantities, dispersed it from mountain snow to abyssal sediments, and watched it fragment into particles small enough to enter organisms. Surely the plastic layer will be our fossil.

Maybe.

The confidence usually arrives before the chemistry.

“Plastic” is not one substance. Polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, polyvinyl chloride, polyethylene terephthalate, nylon and thousands of formulations differ in backbone chemistry, crystallinity, additives, pigments, fillers and degradation pathways. Ultraviolet light embrittles some. Oxygen attacks others. Heat, pressure, hydrolysis, microbial activity and mechanical abrasion change the rates. Burial removes sunlight but introduces new temperatures, fluids and reactions.

On archaeological timescales, plastics can be maddeningly persistent. On geological timescales, persistence is a different question.

A bottle that survives centuries in a landfill does not automatically survive fifty million years as a bottle. It may become a carbon-rich film, hydrocarbon mixture, altered polymer, mineral-replaced void or nothing diagnostically recognizable. Additives may outlast the polymer or leave their own metal and halogen anomalies. Pigments may transform. Chlorinated or fluorinated compounds may have different fates from hydrocarbons.

This does not make plastics useless as technosignatures. It moves the target from object to transformation product.

Oklo already trained us for that move.

The original plutonium is gone. Many original fission products are gone. The reactor geometry is altered. Yet descendants and relationships preserve process. Plastics may have a similar future. A deep-time “plastic horizon” might be recognized less by intact polymer chains than by peculiar carbonaceous particles, additive assemblages, unnatural monomer-derived biomarkers, halogen patterns or sediment textures.

The right research question is not “will plastic survive?” It is “what does each class of industrial polymer become under realistic burial histories, and can any of those products be distinguished from natural organic matter?”

That is an experimental question.

High-pressure, high-temperature maturation studies can simulate some diagenetic pathways. Long-term environmental studies can constrain oxidation and biodegradation. Natural analogues such as fossil resins, bitumens and kerogens show how organic molecules transform over millions of years. Analytical techniques—pyrolysis gas chromatography, Raman spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, synchrotron methods—can identify structures after macroscopic form is gone.

A technosignature program could deliberately age representative industrial materials and map their descendant chemistry.

This is one of several places where the search for hypothetical ancient technology could produce practical science even if the hypothesis is false. We need to know the long-term fate of plastics for environmental reasons. We need to understand landfill diagenesis, microplastic burial, additive mobility and carbon cycling. The same work calibrates deep-time detectability.

The most important conceptual shift is away from novelty as a binary.

A molecule can be synthetic today and become ordinary chemistry later.

Polyethylene is a long hydrocarbon chain. Break it enough times and the fragments enter a chemical world full of hydrocarbons. Polystyrene begins with aromatic rings that are hardly alien to geology. PET contains ester linkages that can hydrolyze. PVC contains chlorine, but chlorine is ubiquitous in minerals and fluids. Fluoropolymers may leave more unusual fluorinated products, yet fluorine itself is natural.

The artificial signal often lies in arrangement, purity, distribution and combination, not elemental ingredients.

This resembles metallurgy. Iron is natural. High-purity steel is manufactured. Nickel and chromium are natural. A particular alloy composition with trace elements controlled to narrow tolerances may be artificial. After corrosion and metamorphism, those elements can separate into natural-looking minerals. The original combination dissolves.

How long does the combination remain legible?

That depends on diffusion length, redox state, fluid flow and temperature. A cold, dry lunar environment can preserve a manufactured alloy far longer than an active terrestrial soil. A deep anoxic sediment may preserve organics better than an oxidizing surface. Salt may entomb objects while flowing enough to distort geometry. Amber can preserve biological structures exquisitely because chemistry and isolation align.

Preservation is a property of object plus environment.

The Anthropocene literature has another candidate class: novel minerals and mineral-like materials.

Human industry has created compounds that either do not occur naturally or occur in radically different contexts. Concrete hydrates, slags, synthetic crystals, pigments, abrasives, ceramics and semiconductor materials expand the mineralogical diversity of the near-surface environment. Some researchers have described an explosion of anthropogenic mineral species and mineral-like compounds associated with mining and manufacturing.

Minerals may be better deep-time archives than polymers because crystal structures can persist or transform into diagnostic pseudomorphs. But the same caution applies. Synthetic minerals can have natural analogues. Slag textures can resemble volcanic glass. High-temperature ceramics can resemble metamorphic phases. A compound unknown in nature today might later be discovered in a rare fumarole or meteorite.

“Never seen naturally” is provisional.

Oklo is again the perfect antidote to overconfidence. Before 1972, self-sustaining terrestrial fission was technologically familiar and naturally hypothetical. Then the geological instance was found.

Any technosignature defined as “nature cannot make this” should be phrased as “no known natural process under these conditions makes this at the observed abundance.” The latter can survive discovery of new nature. The former cannot.

There are stronger forms of novelty.

Stable-isotope enrichment far beyond known natural fractionation can be difficult to mimic. Nature fractionates isotopes through mass-dependent chemical and physical processes, nuclear reactions, radioactive decay and sometimes mass-independent photochemistry. Human centrifuges and electromagnetic separators can produce extreme enrichments in selected isotopes. An isotope that is naturally rare can become nearly pure.

If such a material were buried and preserved, its artificiality could be obvious.

But deep time attacks purity through diffusion, mixing and decay. The best archive might be a mineral inclusion that sealed the enriched material away from exchange. Alternatively, the complementary depleted reservoir could survive nearby, strengthening the separation inference through mass balance.

The most powerful artificial marker may therefore be not a synthetic substance but a *partition* that natural processes struggle to make.

Technology separates.

Ore beneficiation separates valuable minerals from gangue. Metallurgy separates metals from oxygen and sulfur. Petroleum refining separates hydrocarbon fractions. Isotope enrichment separates nearly identical atoms by mass. Semiconductor fabrication separates dopants to extraordinary spatial precision. Pharmaceuticals separate enantiomers. Data storage separates magnetic or charge states into patterns.

Nature separates too. Crystallization, evaporation, diffusion, chromatography in porous media, biological membranes, isotope fractionation and sediment sorting are all separation processes.

The question becomes one of degree and architecture.

A natural crystal can achieve chemical purity greater than industrial products. Evaporites can make astonishingly clean salts. Biological systems synthesize enantiopure molecules. Uranium isotopes fractionate slightly in nature. So purity itself is not agency.

But some separation patterns combine precision, disequilibrium and spatial arrangement in ways that have narrow natural alternatives.

A wafer-scale array of alternating isotopically enriched layers would be extraordinary while intact. After geological alteration, perhaps only diffusion-broadened bands remain. If those bands repeat at uniform spacing inside a material introduced into host rock, the pattern could remain diagnostic long after function vanished.

This raises the possibility of an “information fossil.”

Information is often proposed as a universal technosignature because technology manipulates structured states. A radio message can contain sequences unlikely under natural stochastic processes. An engineered genome can contain watermark sequences. A digital storage device contains vast low-entropy patterns.

Deep time destroys fine structure. Thermal noise, radiation, chemical reaction and physical damage erase bits. But coarse information can survive as repeated geometry, nonrandom isotope layering, patterned dopant profiles or manufactured compositional sequences.

How much pattern is required before agency becomes the best explanation?

Crystals repeat. Sedimentary laminations repeat. Liesegang bands repeat. biological tissues repeat. Orbital forcing creates cycles. Reaction-diffusion systems make spots and stripes. Columnar basalt makes regular polygons. Natural pattern generation is one of the great traps of intuitive archaeology.

A robust information fossil would need structure whose complexity is not simply regularity.

This is why a prime-number radio pulse attracts SETI attention. Not because primes are magical, but because an intentionally selected nonperiodic mathematical sequence is less likely to emerge from simple oscillation. An analogous geological pattern might involve compositional coding with long-range correlations not generated by known growth processes.

This moves us into Class 4 territory quickly. We have no such fossil. The point is methodological: if we want universal technosignatures, we should think about transformations of information, not just longevity of materials.

Plastics are path-dependent. Another civilization may never invent polyethylene.

Information processing is more universal to technology, but its physical implementations are diverse and fragile.

Energy use is universal, but natural backgrounds are enormous.

Material separation is universal, but geology does it too.

Every candidate trades universality against uniqueness and preservation.

Oklo occupies a revealing corner of that triangle. Fission is universal physics. The isotope products are preservable. But uniqueness is weak because nature can make a reactor.

Atmospheric CFCs might occupy another corner. Certain fully fluorinated industrial compounds could be hard to make naturally and detectable remotely, but their universality is low because alien technology need not produce them, and atmospheric lifetimes may be short relative to civilization age.

A megastructure around a star could be durable and technologically obvious, but its prevalence is unknown and natural dust can mimic infrared excess or odd transits.

No perfect signature appears.

That is not discouraging. Forensics works by combining imperfect evidence.

A detective does not need fingerprints to be the only patterned residue humans can leave. Fingerprints become powerful because of individuality, context and chain of custody. DNA becomes powerful because genotype probabilities can be quantified and sample contamination assessed. Ballistics can link marks to mechanisms without being infallible.

Technosignature science needs the same maturation: from “this looks artificial” toward likelihoods under competing generative processes.

Plastics can contribute to that portfolio for our own civilization. Their strongest role may be temporal correlation with other human markers.

Suppose a future section contains a carbon-isotope excursion, combustion particles, a plutonium daughter anomaly, a mass extinction pulse, industrial metal ratios and altered polymer-derived molecules all beginning in the same narrow interval. Each marker has its own preservation pathway and possible false positives. Their synchronized onset is much harder to explain naturally than any one.

This is the principle Schmidt and Frank emphasized: multivariate concurrence.

Oklo's false-positive power also comes from concurrence—but in the opposite direction. A natural reactor model predicts the uranium, fission products, neutron captures and geology together. The bundle is real; the agent is natural.

Therefore even a bundle is not enough unless the competing models are compared.

That is the book's bar.

A prior-industry claim should not be declared because ten strange things occur together if one natural process predicts the same ten things.

At Oklo, fission predicts many.

At the PETM, large carbon release predicts many.

At an impact boundary, impact physics predicts shocked minerals, spherules, iridium anomalies and ejecta together.

At a large igneous province, volcanism predicts lava, mercury anomalies, gas release, warming and ocean chemistry changes together.

The artificial model needs residuals that the coherent natural model does not swallow.

Plastics could be such a residual if their descendants are demonstrably synthetic and syngenetic with the ancient layer.

That “if” contains most of the research program.

First, characterize modern polymer geological transformation.

Second, identify durable molecular or mineral descendants.

Third, establish natural abiotic production limits.

Fourth, develop contamination-resistant sampling.

Fifth, search independent ancient sections.

Sixth, require temporal concurrence with other markers before escalating the claim.

This is slower than finding a weird particle and naming a civilization after it.

It is also how a weird particle becomes evidence instead of content.

Plastics are not the point.

The point is learning what technology becomes after geology has had enough time to stop caring what we called the original thing.
