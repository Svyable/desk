# Chapter 5 — Xenon Remembers the Water

The famous three-hour heartbeat of Oklo comes from a very small witness.

In 2004, Alex Meshik, Charles Hohenberg, and Olga Pravdivtseva used selective laser extraction and ion-counting mass spectrometry on a uranium-free aluminous hydroxy phosphate associated with reactor zone 13. The mineral held extraordinary concentrations of fission xenon and krypton—up to about 0.03 cubic centimeters at standard temperature and pressure per gram, according to the paper—and an isotopic pattern unlike ordinary trapped xenon.

From that pattern they reconstructed a reactor that ran for about thirty minutes, stopped for roughly two and a half hours, then started again.

The number is memorable enough to have become part of Oklo folklore. The measurement is stranger than the folklore.

Xenon should be an awkward archive. It is a noble gas. The uranium minerals that generated the fission products did not simply lock up a neat record at the instant of fission. Instead, the useful information came from the different chemical and radioactive lives of xenon's precursors. Tellurium and iodine isotopes produced in fission decay toward stable xenon on different timescales. During hot reactor operation, those parents and daughters did not all move or escape in the same way. When the system cooled, some of the eventual xenon could be retained in the phosphate phase.

The clock is therefore not a row of xenon atoms laid down every three hours. It is a decay-and-migration problem.

Meshik and his colleagues varied two times in their model: how long the reactor remained active and how long after shutdown it took before xenon began to be retained. The measured isotope ratios picked out an operating interval of about half an hour and a cooling interval of about 150 minutes. The proposed physical mechanism was groundwater. Water moderated neutrons and helped sustain criticality. Fission heated the zone. Enough water converted to steam that moderation weakened and the reactor became subcritical. Cooling allowed liquid water to return, and the sequence began again.

A nuclear geyser is almost too good an image. It is also where caution should begin.

The 2004 result did not measure a geyser erupting two billion years ago. It inferred an intermittent operating regime from isotope fractionation in a particular mineral and then proposed water-to-steam feedback as the mechanism that made the timing physically sensible. Contemporary commentary already separated those two claims. Marc Caffee, a nuclear geochemist quoted by *Physical Review Focus*, called the cycling case strong while saying he was less persuaded by the geyser detail.

That distinction has aged well.

A 2026 review by Hiroshi Hidaka and colleagues returns to the xenon result with a useful reminder about scale. The celebrated cycling signature came from what they describe as a “tiny and specific” uranium-free aluminous hydroxy phosphate in RZ13. The review notes that isotopic work on RZ13 has covered only a few samples and explicitly identifies replication of the xenon signature in similar and different minerals as an open target for new measurements.

That makes the strongest counterargument unusually concrete. The issue is not that the xenon result is vague. It is that it is precise in one place.

RZ13 is also not a generic Oklo reactor zone. Hidaka and colleagues compile several ways in which it differs from better-characterized zones. They estimate that only about 74.9 percent of its fissions came from thermal-neutron-induced uranium-235 fission, compared with roughly 87.7 to 95 percent in the other zones they tabulate. Fast-neutron uranium-238 fission contributed about 17.9 percent. Their compiled operating duration for RZ13 is about 0.242 million years, shorter than the 0.60 to 2.24 million years listed for the comparison zones. The core reached extremely high uranium concentrations and severe uranium-235 depletion.

Those numbers do not refute the cycling model. They make careless generalization harder.

It is tempting to say that “the Oklo reactors” switched on for thirty minutes and off for two and a half hours for hundreds of thousands of years. The evidence is narrower. A specific isotope archive in a particularly unusual reactor zone supports that cadence. Whether other zones carried the same pulse, a different pulse, or no comparable recoverable pulse is still an empirical question.

That narrower statement is more interesting for this book.

The result shows how much operational history can survive when almost everything recognizable as equipment is absent. No control rod survived because there was no control rod. No instrument log survived because there was no instrument. The useful record sits in the relationship among fission yields, radioactive precursor half-lives, chemical mobility, mineral growth, neutron moderation, and later retention.

Two billion years later, the reconstruction is possible because those processes were not interchangeable.

This is the deeper form of a process fossil. A physical system can erase its original actors while preserving enough mismatched clocks to reconstruct behavior.

The same idea matters for any serious search for ancient technology, but it is easy to abuse. If a returned Martian sample someday contains noble-gas isotopes suggesting intermittent neutron irradiation, the exciting sentence will be the one about duty cycle. The useful work will begin immediately after it.

Where did the gas reside? Which minerals trapped it? When did those minerals form? Which precursor elements were mobile? What neutron spectrum produced the fission inventory? Could groundwater, seasonal recharge, thermal cracking, hydrothermal circulation, impact heating, or later alteration create the same fractionation? Does the pattern recur in adjacent samples? Does it change systematically across the supposed reactor geometry?

One beautiful grain is a discovery. A mapped gradient is an argument.

Oklo makes that difference hard to ignore because the natural alternative is already spectacular. A uranium ore body accumulated enough fissile material, encountered water, went critical, generated heat, changed its own moderating environment, shut itself down, and apparently did it again. Nothing about that sentence requires intention.

The cycling model therefore sharpens one of the book's central problems. Feedback is not evidence of design merely because the feedback regulates something engineers also regulate.

An engineered reactor and a natural uranium body can both have power affected by moderator density. What distinguishes them is not the abstract presence of control. It is the physical origin of the controlling relationships and the other residues those relationships predict.

For Oklo, a groundwater feedback predicts heat, fluid movement, mineral alteration, and isotope transport tied to the local ore and fracture system. An engineered reactor would generally add other kinds of organization: transported or processed fuel, deliberately separated materials, structural components, shielding, cooling architecture, chemically distinct waste streams, or spatial relationships that local mineralization does not naturally generate. Deep time may destroy most of those objects, but it would have to destroy all of their correlated consequences as well before the two stories became indistinguishable.

The xenon result is useful because it demonstrates both sides of that standard at once.

First, geology can preserve an operational clue far more specific than “something nuclear happened here.” It can preserve a candidate duty cycle.

Second, an operational clue that looks machine-like can emerge from a natural feedback loop.

The right response is not to distrust regularity. It is to demand the reservoir that sets the rhythm.

For the RZ13 model, the candidate reservoir is water and heat. The thirty-minute interval is not supposed to be an arbitrary schedule. It is the time over which criticality heats the system enough to remove effective moderation. The longer dormant interval is the cooling and recharge side of the same feedback. If that story is right, future measurements should not merely recover the same xenon ratios. They should connect the isotope timing to mineralogy, neutron spectrum, thermal history, fluid pathways, and position inside the zone.

That is why the 2026 return to Oklo is encouraging. The next generation of work is not treating the 2004 number as scripture. Researchers are asking whether modern microbeam and mass-spectrometric methods can find the same kind of record in additional minerals and reactor zones, and whether krypton and rare-earth isotope systems tell a compatible story.

The famous cycle may survive that scrutiny. It may broaden into a family of different operating regimes across Oklo. It may turn out that RZ13 was unusually cooperative as both reactor and archive.

Any of those outcomes would improve the book.

The important fact is already secure enough: a mineral can remember more than composition. Under favorable circumstances, it can preserve enough nuclear kinetics to make ancient behavior inferable.

But behavior inferred from one archive is not yet a universal biography.

Before we ask whether a pattern was made by somebody, we should first find out how many rocks remember it.
