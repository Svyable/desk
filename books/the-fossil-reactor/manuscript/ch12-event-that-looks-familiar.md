# Chapter 12 — The Event That Looks Too Familiar

Fifty-six million years ago, long after the Oklo reactors had gone quiet and long before humans existed, Earth's carbon cycle lurched.

The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum—PETM—is preserved in marine and terrestrial sediments as a rapid warming event accompanied by a large negative excursion in carbon isotopes. Carbon depleted in carbon-13 entered the ocean-atmosphere system. Temperatures rose substantially. Ocean chemistry changed. Biogeographic ranges shifted. Extinctions occurred in some groups, notably benthic foraminifera. Weathering and hydrology changed. The event is one of the most intensely studied natural analogues for rapid carbon release into Earth's climate system.

It also looks, at a sufficiently low resolution, like us.

That resemblance is why the PETM appears in discussions of the Silurian hypothesis. Industrial civilization is also releasing isotopically light carbon and warming the climate. If a future geologist had only a coarse record of the Anthropocene, some of the broad signals would rhyme with the PETM.

The dangerous word is *therefore*.

Therefore the PETM was industrial? No.

Therefore industrialization is geologically undetectable? Also no.

The PETM is useful because it gives us a real false-positive laboratory. We know natural Earth systems can generate a package of signals that overlaps with expected industrial effects. The task is to identify which dimensions separate the causes.

Timing is one candidate, but timing is treacherous in deep time.

Modern fossil-fuel carbon is being released on a timescale of centuries. Many estimates for the main PETM carbon release are longer, though the exact onset duration remains debated because sedimentation, bioturbation, incomplete sections, orbital tuning and age-model uncertainty blur sharp events. A future geologist looking at the Anthropocene after tens of millions of years might not resolve our first two centuries cleanly either.

Sediment is a low-pass filter.

Imagine a deep-sea accumulation rate of a few centimeters per thousand years. A century-scale industrial pulse may occupy millimeters before compaction, then be mixed by burrowing organisms across centimeters. The apparent duration expands. Hiatuses can remove the onset. Chemical diffusion can smooth concentration peaks. Dating uncertainty can make globally synchronous events look diachronous.

Speed is evidence, but not always decisive evidence.

Source magnitude and isotope composition help. Different carbon reservoirs carry different carbon-13 signatures. Volcanic carbon, biogenic methane, thermogenic methane, organic carbon oxidation and fossil-fuel combustion can produce different combinations of isotope excursion and total carbon release. Researchers use carbon-cycle models, boron isotopes, carbonate dissolution, temperature proxies and other measurements to constrain PETM sources.

None requires industry.

Large igneous province activity associated with the North Atlantic Igneous Province is temporally relevant. Magmatic intrusions into organic-rich sediments can release thermogenic carbon. Methane hydrates, permafrost-like reservoirs, peat or other organic pools may have contributed through feedbacks. The debate is about proportions and triggers inside a natural system, not whether a refinery was hiding in the Eocene.

The temptation to invoke industry usually enters through resemblance plus imagination rather than an unexplained residual in the data.

That distinction is important. A hypothesis becomes scientifically interesting when it solves a problem competing models struggle with and makes risky predictions. “An unknown civilization could have caused this” is easy to append to almost any abrupt event. Unless it predicts independent residues, it explains by permission rather than mechanism.

Oklo again supplies the standard.

The natural-reactor model did not win because scientists preferred nature. It won because it explained missing uranium-235, fission-product isotopes, neutron-capture effects and geological setting together. An industrial PETM model would need equivalent reach.

What would it predict?

If the hypothetical civilization burned fossil carbon at planetary scale, perhaps mining disturbances or combustion-derived particles would accompany the isotope excursion. If it manufactured metals, metal fluxes and isotope patterns might shift. If it used nuclear power or weapons, radionuclide descendants might occur in unusual combinations. If it produced persistent synthetic chemicals, transformation products might survive in protected sediments. If it altered ecosystems through agriculture and transport, biotic changes might have peculiar selectivity beyond climate forcing.

Then those markers would need to be found, independently, in well-dated PETM sections.

They have not been.

This absence is not mathematically absolute because preservation is incomplete and an unknown technology may not resemble ours. But each added rescue—perhaps they used no metals, perhaps no fossil fuels, perhaps no persistent synthetics, perhaps no nuclear processes, perhaps all artifacts were recycled—makes the industrial explanation less testable and therefore less useful.

There is a philosophical asymmetry worth naming. Natural explanations are constrained by processes we can observe, model or infer from other geological settings. A hypothetical civilization can be assigned almost any technology needed to evade a failed prediction. That flexibility is not a strength. It is a penalty.

A model with unlimited design freedom can fit any residue after the fact.

The way to discipline it is to specify a technological class before examining the anomaly.

For example: carbon-based industrialization using large fossil-energy flux, widespread metallurgy and chemical manufacturing, lasting between one hundred and ten thousand years. Now predictions can be generated. The model may be too anthropocentric to cover all possible civilizations, but at least it can lose.

Science needs hypotheses that can lose.

The PETM also complicates one of the most popular Anthropocene markers: the carbon-isotope excursion itself.

Fossil fuels are depleted in carbon-13 because they derive from ancient biological carbon that underwent fractionation during photosynthesis. Burning them lowers the carbon-13/carbon-12 ratio in atmospheric carbon dioxide. This Suess effect is measured today. In a future rock record, a sharp negative excursion will be one of our fingerprints.

But negative excursions recur in Earth history because the planet contains large reservoirs of isotopically light carbon. Methane is especially light. Organic carbon oxidation is light. The carbon cycle has several ways to mimic the sign of industrial fossil-carbon release.

A marker can be perfectly real and still nonunique.

This is the central problem in biosignatures too. Oxygen is real when detected. The question is source. Methane is real when detected. The question is source. Phosphine, if securely detected, is real. The question is source.

The same discipline should govern technosignatures. Detection and attribution are separate achievements.

The PETM's value grows when we compare dimensions that industry might alter differently.

Nitrogen is one. Modern humans convert enormous quantities of atmospheric nitrogen into reactive forms through the Haber-Bosch process and cultivation of nitrogen-fixing crops. This changes nitrate fluxes, eutrophication and nitrogen isotope patterns. Yet natural climate transitions also reorganize nitrogen cycling. Again, a nitrogen anomaly would support a package rather than carry the claim alone.

Metals are another. Modern mining has moved lead, copper, zinc, mercury, rare earths and other elements through the environment at rates and distributions that can overwhelm natural background locally. But volcanic ash, hydrothermal activity, wildfire and erosion also produce metal anomalies. Particle morphology, alloy associations and isotope ratios might separate industrial sources better than concentration alone.

Combustion particles provide a stronger avenue. High-temperature fossil-fuel combustion produces spheroidal carbonaceous particles and fly ash with characteristic morphologies and compositions. These particles have been studied as markers of industrialization. Their long-term preservation over tens of millions of years is not guaranteed, but a globally synchronous distribution of combustion-derived particles would be harder to explain with one local wildfire or volcano.

Yet even spherical particles are not unique. Micrometeorites, volcanic spherules and impact ejecta exist. Mineral chemistry matters.

Plastics are dramatic because they are unquestionably technological at production. Their geological future is less clear. Some polymers degrade relatively quickly; others persist. Burial can protect materials from ultraviolet light and oxygen but expose them to heat and pressure. Over millions of years, original polymers may crack into hydrocarbons, cross-link, carbonize or disappear. Additives, pigments and unusual molecular structures may be more or less durable. A “plastic horizon” in deep time may not contain recognizable bottles.

This is why synthetic compounds need taphonomy before they become search markers.

The PETM also teaches a social lesson about analogy. We use it as an analogue for modern warming because it provides a natural experiment in carbon release, not because it duplicates the Anthropocene. The same event can be scientifically useful without being causally identical.

Oklo is a natural analogue for repository processes without being a repository. The PETM is a climate analogue for anthropogenic carbon release without being industrial. Ancient ocean anoxic events can illuminate biogeochemical responses without being pollution episodes. Large igneous provinces can illuminate atmospheric chemistry without being factories.

Analogy becomes dangerous only when similarity is promoted into identity.

This matters beyond ancient-civilization speculation. In UAP debates, a sensor signature may be compared to a known aircraft, plasma phenomenon, balloon or glare artifact. Similarity helps generate hypotheses; it does not identify the object. In medicine, symptom clusters can resemble known syndromes without sharing etiology. In intelligence analysis, a pattern of activity can resemble an adversary operation without proving attribution.

The epistemic move is the same.

What features are shared? What features differ? Which model predicts the differences?

For the PETM, one difference may be the specific suite of technological novelty expected from humans. Our current perturbation includes radionuclides from weapons and reactors, synthetic organic chemicals, plastics, vast concrete production, industrial aluminum, species transport across biogeographic barriers and unmistakable manufactured objects in young sediments. If a future record preserves even a subset of those alongside the carbon pulse, our event will become easier to distinguish from the PETM.

A hypothetical prehuman civilization could have a different suite. That weakens our ability to exclude all conceivable industry but not our ability to test specified forms.

This leads to a useful hierarchy of claims.

The weakest claim is existential: some technological civilization of unknown type could in principle have existed during a poorly sampled interval. Deep time makes that difficult to falsify absolutely.

A stronger, testable claim specifies when, where, duration and technological effects. “An industrial civilization caused the PETM” is much stronger. It predicts residues.

A stronger claim still identifies an anomaly that natural PETM models fail to explain and that follows naturally from industry.

We do not have that.

The lack matters.

It should not be dismissed with “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.” Absence becomes evidence when detection should have been likely. The harder part is estimating “should.” Preservation, sampling density and technology class determine it.

Suppose industrial nuclear activity occurred globally for one hundred thousand years during the PETM and produced large quantities of long-lived transuranics. Depending on the isotopes, many would decay away by now, but daughter anomalies or unusual isotope ratios could remain. If no such anomalies appear in carefully targeted sections, the model loses probability.

Suppose instead a small regional civilization used only solar and biomass energy for three centuries. Its global geological signal might be negligible. The lack of evidence would tell us little.

These two hypotheses are often rhetorically blended into “could there have been a civilization?” They are scientifically different.

The PETM forces scale into the conversation.

Industrial civilization as a geological force is detectable because it is large. If a civilization is too small to perturb the geological record, geology may be the wrong instrument for finding it. Archaeology would be better, if sites survive. If all archaeology is gone, the hypothesis may fall below recoverability.

That is not a failure of science. Not every historical event is reconstructable.

The desire for a definitive answer can tempt us to overinterpret weak traces because the alternative—permanent uncertainty—is emotionally unsatisfying.

Oklo provides relief because it shows what deep-time certainty can look like when the signal is strong enough. We know natural reactors operated. We do not need intact reactor geometry or written records. Nuclear products make the case.

The PETM currently shows the opposite. We know a dramatic Earth-system event happened. We have substantial evidence for natural carbon-cycle and volcanic mechanisms. We do not have a technological residue package.

So the scientifically interesting use of the PETM is not as a candidate lost civilization.

It is as a stress test for our ability to tell one from climate.

That is more useful, and stranger in the end.

Because if our own industrial layer survives fifty-six million years from now, the geologists who read it will have the PETM for comparison.

Their job will be to explain why ours is different.
