# Chapter 3 — The Planet Learns to Concentrate Uranium

Oklo did not begin with neutrons. It began with oxygen.

That sentence is necessarily simplified, but the simplification points in the right direction. Uranium's behavior in the crust depends strongly on oxidation state. Under reducing conditions, uranium in the tetravalent state is relatively insoluble. Under oxidizing conditions, hexavalent uranium forms soluble complexes and can move with groundwater. Once the surface environment of Earth became sufficiently oxidizing, uranium could be mobilized, transported and later precipitated when those fluids encountered reducing conditions.

The Franceville Basin is therefore a nuclear story nested inside a planetary-atmosphere story.

This is easy to miss because the temporal scales are so different. A neutron chain reaction feels instantaneous. Atmospheric oxygenation feels geological. Yet without the older planetary transition, the younger ore geometry may not exist. A reactor zone is the local expression of a planet's prior chemical evolution.

For technosignature work, this matters enormously. A residue can only be interpreted against the world capable of producing it.

Take oxygen itself. On Earth it is tightly connected to photosynthesis, but oxygen is not an unambiguous biosignature in every atmosphere. Photochemistry can create false positives under some planetary conditions. Methane can be biological, geological, or both. Nitrous oxide has stronger biological associations but still requires context. The lesson from biosignatures has increasingly become the lesson Oklo teaches in rock: no molecule gets to testify without its environment being cross-examined.

Uranium is especially good at forcing this discipline because its isotopes preserve both universal physics and local history.

The half-lives are universal. The decay of uranium-235 relative to uranium-238 means ancient natural uranium was more fissile everywhere. But natural reactors did not appear everywhere. The necessary concentration and moderation conditions were local. A planet-wide parameter changed the possibility space; basin-scale geology selected the actual sites.

This is a useful model for thinking about technology in deep time. An industrial civilization, if one existed, would not leave evidence independently of geology. Its materials would be sorted by depositional environments, redox fronts, erosion, diagenesis, metamorphism and plate tectonics. Some residues would be concentrated. Others would be dispersed below detectability. A dump might become an ore body. A city might become sedimentary noise. A mine might be preferentially erased because the host rock itself was later recycled.

We should not ask what a civilization would “leave behind” as though the leaving were passive. We should ask what geological processes would edit, amplify, relocate or destroy the initial signal.

Oklo provides examples at every stage.

The uranium concentration preceded criticality. Fission then created products with different chemistries. Some remained astonishingly localized over immense periods; others migrated. Later thermal events overprinted parts of the record. Mining in the twentieth century physically removed portions of the original configuration. The object we study today is not the pristine reactor. It is a palimpsest whose nuclear signals are strong enough to survive several editors.

That word—*palimpsest*—is used too freely in archaeology, but here it earns its keep. Isotope systems can record processes that no longer have an intact physical structure. A ruined city still looks like a ruined city for some interval. A two-billion-year-old reactor zone does not have to look like a reactor. Its identity is reconstructed from effects.

The same would be true of sufficiently old technology.

This is where discussions of ancient industrial civilizations often go wrong in two opposite directions. Skeptics sometimes imagine that the absence of recognizable artifacts after tens or hundreds of millions of years decisively ends the question. Enthusiasts imagine that because artifacts can disappear, almost any unexplained anomaly can stand in for them. Both positions skip the actual work.

Deep time does erase many objects. It does not erase everything equally.

Durable evidence is not simply the hardest material. It is the signal best coupled to a preservable geological archive. A thin global isotope excursion can outlive a steel building. An extinction turnover can outlive a concrete dam. A peculiar ratio in a stable mineral can outlive a machine that caused it. Conversely, an artificial molecule that seems durable on a laboratory bench may be destroyed, metabolized, hydrolyzed or transformed before burial.

So the search strategy changes. Instead of imagining future archaeologists digging up our laptops, imagine isotope geochemists encountering a stratigraphic interval that refuses to behave like neighboring intervals.

What would make them suspicious?

Not simply a carbon isotope excursion: Earth has many. Not simply warming: Earth has done that. Not simply heavy-metal enrichment: volcanism and hydrothermal systems do it. Not simply uranium depletion: Oklo does it. Not simply extinction: impacts, climate shifts and ecological cascades do it.

Suspicion should grow when several independent systems shift together in a configuration for which a single known natural forcing performs badly.

The Franceville Basin helps because it demonstrates the opposite case. There *is* a coherent natural forcing. Atmospheric oxygenation helps uranium move. Sedimentary redox structure helps it concentrate. Ancient isotope abundance makes criticality feasible. Groundwater moderates neutrons. Reactor operation creates fission products. Geochemistry sorts those products afterward.

The explanation is not simple. It is integrated.

That distinction matters. “Natural explanation” does not mean a hand-wave toward nature. It means a causal chain with enough specificity to compete against the anomaly.

The more specific the natural chain becomes, the less room remains for technological authorship at Oklo—not because technology is taboo, but because it becomes unnecessary.

The planet did not merely host the reactor.

Over hundreds of millions of years, it assembled the preconditions.
