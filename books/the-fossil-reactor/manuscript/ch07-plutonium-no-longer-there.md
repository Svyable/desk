# Chapter 7 — The Plutonium That Is No Longer There

Some of the most useful evidence at Oklo is evidence for material that has vanished.

Plutonium-239 has a half-life of about twenty-four thousand years. That is long by human standards and short beside two billion years. Any plutonium-239 created while the Oklo reactors were operating is gone now. Yet reactor physics says it must have existed. Uranium-238 captures a neutron, becomes uranium-239, beta decays to neptunium-239, then beta decays again to plutonium-239. In an operating reactor, some of that plutonium can fission; some can capture another neutron; some survives until shutdown and later decays.

We do not need an intact atom of ancient plutonium-239 to infer the pathway.

That is a profound point for deep-time searches because many candidate technosignatures have shorter physical lifetimes than the interval we want to probe. The naive response is to discard them: if the marker decays, it cannot help. Nuclear forensics says otherwise. A vanished nuclide can leave daughter products, altered isotope ratios, capture products, mineral damage, or mass-balance effects that remain.

The search target becomes a genealogy rather than a relic.

Plutonium is especially evocative because it is tightly associated with human nuclear technology. In modern discourse, a lump of plutonium is almost definitionally artificial, though trace natural plutonium can occur through neutron-capture processes and primordial or cosmogenic contexts complicate the absolute statement. Human reactors manufacture plutonium in quantity because uranium-238 is abundant and neutron fluxes are intense. Weapons programs made the element politically radioactive before most people knew what it was chemically.

Oklo made plutonium without a state.

This does not trivialize plutonium as a technosignature. It specifies the question. Quantity, isotope composition, spatial distribution, associated fission products, host minerals and geological feasibility determine how surprising the plutonium pathway is.

Suppose future explorers find plutonium-239 on another world. If the material is fresh relative to its half-life, an active or recent production mechanism is required. On a geologically dead body with no obvious neutron source, a concentrated deposit could be extraordinarily interesting. But “plutonium exists” still would not complete the argument. Spontaneous fission, cosmic-ray interactions, natural reactors, nearby nucleosynthetic deposition, or contamination by the explorers themselves would have to be considered at the relevant abundance.

Now push the discovery one hundred million years into the past. Plutonium-239 itself is gone. A search that knew only to look for plutonium would fail. A search that knew the decay chain could look for excess uranium-235 produced by plutonium decay, altered daughter ratios, or correlated fission-product patterns if the plutonium participated in a reactor.

The problem is that daughter products are rarely unique. Uranium-235 existed already. An excess may be tiny or erased by mixing. The more generations of transformation separate the present measurement from the original material, the more important independent lines of evidence become.

This is why the most durable technosignature may be relational.

An isotope in one mineral is ambiguous. A set of isotope anomalies whose spatial and chronological relations fit a common source process can be far harder to naturalize. The vanished plutonium at Oklo is credible because it sits inside a reactor system already established by uranium depletion, fission products, neutron-capture signatures and reactor modeling. We are not discovering a lone daughter and inventing plutonium to explain it.

This difference is easy to lose in anomaly hunting.

An unexplained ratio invites a menu of exotic parents. If the menu is unconstrained, almost any desired history can be reverse-engineered. A good reconstruction does the opposite. It narrows possible parents using known decay schemes, measured abundances, mineral closure ages, reactor conditions and mass balance.

In other words, the dead isotope is not a blank check.

There is a larger astrobiological analogy. Most life does not fossilize. Soft tissue disappears. DNA degrades. Proteins hydrolyze. Yet paleobiology reconstructs organisms from mineralized structures, chemical biomarkers, isotopic fractionation, trace fossils, ecological patterns and descendants. The evidence of vanished biological matter survives in transformations.

Technology should be expected to leave a similarly indirect record at great age.

A polymer may hydrolyze but leave characteristic carbon skeletons or additives. A fluorinated compound may transform into persistent fluorine-bearing products. Semiconductor structures will not remain functional, but unusual dopant concentrations or refined elemental assemblages might enter sediments. A reactor's short-lived isotopes disappear, but daughter systems and fission products remain. An industrial atmospheric gas may be destroyed, but the climate perturbation, oxidation products, or sediment chemistry it caused can persist longer.

This has an uncomfortable consequence: preservation can amplify ambiguity.

The original artifact may have been unmistakable. Its descendant may be shared with natural processes.

A stainless-steel pressure vessel is obviously manufactured while intact. After oxidation, transport, dilution and metamorphism, its nickel-chromium-iron signature may be indistinguishable from natural mineralization unless spatial context or trace-alloy patterns survive. A kilogram of separated plutonium is technologically obvious. Two billion years later, its decay products may no longer be.

So evidence does not simply weaken with time; it can change category.

Class 1 evidence for an object can become Class 2 inference about a process, then eventually a small likelihood shift among several models.

Oklo lets us watch this happen in reverse. We begin with present minerals and reconstruct a process so well understood in modern laboratories that we can infer invisible historical stages with confidence.

That confidence depends on nuclear data.

Half-lives, neutron cross sections, fission yields and decay schemes are measured independently. Reactor calculations can estimate plutonium production from neutron capture in uranium-238 under modeled spectra. The ancient system is therefore not free to have any history we like. Its inferred plutonium inventory has to be compatible with neutron fluence, uranium burnup and daughter products.

This is one reason nuclear technosignatures are attractive in principle. Nuclear transformations obey unusually rigid bookkeeping.

Chemical systems also obey conservation laws, but biological and geological pathways can rearrange molecules through enormous reaction networks. Nuclear isotope patterns often provide fewer plausible routes. If a sample contains a specific suite of activation products whose production cross sections require a narrowly defined neutron spectrum and flux, the source mechanism can be constrained strongly.

The agency question remains separate.

Natural neutron sources include spontaneous fission, alpha-neutron reactions, cosmic rays and, in rare circumstances, self-sustaining fission. Artificial systems can produce thermal, epithermal, fast or pulsed neutron fields with engineered geometries. The discriminating evidence may therefore be less “neutrons happened” than the spectrum, dose, spatial geometry and material associations.

A reactor is not the only technology that leaves nuclear descendants. Particle accelerators activate surrounding materials. Nuclear explosions produce characteristic fission and activation products, fractionate refractory and volatile elements, and distribute fallout according to atmospheric and geological processes. Medical and industrial isotope production creates unusual inventories. Fusion systems, if deployed at scale, would activate structural materials in recognizable ways. Advanced propulsion could leave neutron or gamma activation in nearby surfaces.

Each technology has a residue genealogy.

One could imagine a field of “technogenic nuclear taphonomy”: the study of how artificial nuclear signatures decay, migrate, react and fossilize. It would combine nuclear forensics with geochronology and repository science. We already possess most of the disciplines separately. What is missing is the habit of asking what their signals look like after ten million, one hundred million or one billion years.

The answer would rarely be a smoking gun. Smoking guns rust.

But some daughter systems could remain strange enough to justify escalating the artificial hypothesis.

Consider a hypothetical deposit with fission products indicating sustained neutron irradiation but no uranium ore capable of supporting natural criticality. Suppose the host rock was originally poor in uranium, yet the fission products cluster in repeated geometric lenses with uniform burnup characteristics. Suppose isotopic mass balance implies that enriched fissile material once existed there, while surrounding rocks contain complementary depleted tails. Suppose the age relations show the material was introduced after the host rock formed rather than concentrated by the same hydrothermal system. Suppose no natural process accounts for the enrichment scale.

Now the technological hypothesis begins doing real explanatory work.

None of those conditions is present at Oklo in the necessary form. The reactor zones are part of a uranium mineral system. Their existence follows from ancient isotope abundance and basin geochemistry. The vanished plutonium is one expected stage in that system.

That is what a false positive should look like when it is well understood: not a superficial resemblance to technology, but a complete natural pathway that recreates a supposedly technological process.

Oklo does more damage to weak technosignature claims than any skeptic saying “nature is weird.”

It shows exactly how weird.

Nature can mine in the loose sense of concentrating uranium through fluid flow. It can enrich a fissile fraction in time through differential radioactive decay—not spatially separating isotopes, but allowing the ancient natural ratio to be higher. It can moderate neutrons with water. It can breed plutonium from uranium-238. It can fission that plutonium. It can cycle power through thermal feedback. It can immobilize some waste in minerals for geological ages.

Put badly, that sounds as though nature built a nuclear industry.

Put accurately, it means the physical functions humans later arranged deliberately can arise piecemeal in one favorable geological system without any planning at all.

This distinction is the book's recurring fight.

Functional similarity is not historical identity.

A beaver dam and a landslide can both block a river. A crystal and a machined component can both be geometrically regular. A lightning strike and a power supply can both generate high voltage. A natural reactor and a power plant can both sustain fission.

Agency has to be inferred from features that arise because of selection, manufacture, communication, or operational constraint—not merely because the same physical outcome occurred.

The vanished plutonium helps clarify what those features might be. Human nuclear systems often leave separation histories. We mine ore from one place, mill it, convert it chemically, enrich uranium, fabricate fuel, move it, irradiate it, cool it, perhaps reprocess it, and dispose of waste somewhere else. Each step decouples materials that geology initially colocated.

That spatial decoupling could be a deeper technosignature than the reactor itself.

Natural reactors tend to occur where ore, moderator and geometry coincide. Engineered reactors can occur where humans carry fuel. A two-billion-year-old fission signature in a geological setting that could never have concentrated enough fissile material naturally would be more interesting than one inside a world-class uranium deposit.

Likewise, complementary waste streams matter. Isotope separation conserves atoms. Enriched product implies depleted tails. Chemical purification creates slag, raffinate and secondary waste. Mining moves overburden. Manufacturing rejects off-spec material. Technology rearranges the planet spatially.

Deep time may erase individual facilities while preserving some of those mass-balance asymmetries.

This is one of the places where the Silurian question becomes experimentally useful rather than merely provocative. Instead of asking “Were there industrial civilizations?” ask “What spatial isotope relationships would be difficult for known ore-forming systems to create?” Then look.

The answer might be none strong enough to justify a search. That result would still improve the field.

Or the answer might identify neglected archives: old stable cratons, ancient evaporites, sedimentary basins with exceptional mineral closure, or lunar material that preserves terrestrial ejecta from deep time.

The Moon deserves a brief detour because it lacks plate tectonics, oceans and a thick active biosphere. Earth's surface is aggressively recycled. Material blasted from Earth by large impacts can, in principle, reach the Moon. Researchers have discussed whether ancient terrestrial rocks might survive there. If technology ever existed on Earth in deep time—a hypothesis for which we have no evidence—the lunar surface could conceivably preserve ejecta or impact-melt residues that Earth later erased.

This is Class 4 conjecture, not a proposed discovery program with demonstrated yield. The flux, provenance and survival of useful material would be severe constraints. But the logic follows from preservation science: search the archive that edits least.

Oklo itself survived not because Earth edits gently, but because the local geology was favorable enough and the nuclear signal strong enough.

The plutonium did not survive.

Its story did.

That is the difference a deep-time science of technology will have to learn to exploit.
