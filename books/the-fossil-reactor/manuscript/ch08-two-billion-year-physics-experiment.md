# Chapter 8 — A Reactor as a Two-Billion-Year Physics Experiment

Oklo acquired a second scientific life after geologists and nuclear chemists had already made it famous.

The reactors became an experiment in whether the laws of physics have changed.

This sounds grander than the evidence permits, so it is worth proceeding slowly. The fine-structure constant, usually written alpha, is a dimensionless number that sets the strength of electromagnetic interactions. Physicists have long asked whether quantities we call fundamental constants are truly constant across cosmic time. Astronomical spectra, laboratory atomic clocks, the cosmic microwave background, nucleosynthesis and geophysical records all offer different baselines.

Oklo offers something unusual: a naturally operating neutron reactor from roughly two billion years ago whose products can preserve information about nuclear resonance energies.

The key isotope in much of this work is samarium-149.

Samarium-149 absorbs low-energy neutrons extraordinarily efficiently because it has a resonance near the energies relevant to thermal reactor neutrons. The effective neutron-capture cross section depends sensitively on the resonance energy. If nuclear and electromagnetic parameters had been different enough two billion years ago, the resonance could have shifted, changing how much samarium-149 survived irradiation.

So researchers work backward. Measure samarium isotopes in reactor-zone samples. Estimate the ancient neutron spectrum, fluence, temperature, contamination and reactor composition. Infer the effective capture cross section during operation. Compare that with what modern nuclear data predict. Then ask how large a shift in resonance energy could be hidden inside the uncertainties.

That chain is why Oklo constraints are both impressive and model-dependent.

The easy popular version says something like “Oklo proves the laws of physics have not changed for two billion years.” It does not. Different analyses have obtained different ranges because assumptions about reactor temperature, neutron spectrum, sample contamination and the relation between resonance shifts and underlying constants matter. The 2006 work by Christopher Gould, Evgeny Sharapov and Steve Lamoreaux used detailed Monte Carlo reactor models for reaction zones RZ2 and RZ10 and emphasized that prior disagreements were partly driven by unrealistic or differing neutron spectra. Their proposed bound on fractional change in alpha was extremely small, but explicitly conditional on the model and on treating alpha as the varying parameter.

This is an unusually clean example of epistemic layering.

Class 1: Oklo operated. Samarium isotopes were exposed to a neutron field. Modern samarium has a well-measured low-energy resonance. Reactor-zone isotope ratios can be measured.

Class 2: particular reconstructions of the ancient neutron spectrum, reactor temperature and effective samarium capture cross section.

Class 3 would begin if someone claimed that one favored fit demonstrates a nonzero change in alpha despite alternative branches or contamination uncertainties not being resolved.

Class 4 includes broader attempts to map any inferred resonance change onto speculative theories in which constants vary cosmologically.

None of these classes is insulting. They specify how much weight a claim can carry.

The fine-structure-constant work matters to this book because it shows how much information can be extracted from a geological system after the physical apparatus has vanished.

Imagine trying to infer whether a nineteenth-century laboratory's spectrometer calibration had drifted using only rust from the building. That sounds absurd. Oklo does something conceptually adjacent at a vastly larger scale: nuclear products inside ancient rock constrain properties of microscopic interactions billions of years ago.

The trick is that nature supplied a resonance with extreme sensitivity.

This is a general strategy in historical science. Look for systems where a small change in the parameter of interest produces a large change in an observable residue. Paleoclimatologists look for proxies sensitive to temperature or precipitation. Geochronologists use isotope systems with useful half-lives and closure properties. Paleomagnetists use minerals that lock in magnetic direction. Oklo physicists use neutron resonances.

A deep-time technosignature search should look for the same kind of leverage.

The wrong question is: what artificial objects are indestructible?

Almost none are, on the timescales that matter.

The better question is: what technological processes could push a natural system into a state whose descendants retain a sensitive memory of that push?

Nuclear processes are obvious candidates because isotope ratios can be extremely sensitive. But there are others.

Industrial nitrogen fixation changes the amount and movement of reactive nitrogen. Fossil-fuel combustion shifts carbon isotope ratios because fossil organic carbon is depleted in carbon-13 relative to many inorganic reservoirs. Large-scale mining exposes minerals to oxidation and erosion. Certain persistent organic compounds may create molecular fossils. Massive production of alloys redistributes trace metals into combinations and particle morphologies uncommon in natural settings. Global agriculture changes pollen distributions, sedimentation, erosion and species ranges.

Most individual effects are not unique. The leverage comes from combinations and from proxies that respond nonlinearly or specifically.

Oklo's samarium resonance is an extreme case of specificity. A resonance energy is not a vague environmental indicator. It is a feature of nuclear structure. Yet even here, translating the measured isotope ratio into a statement about alpha requires reactor modeling. The lesson is not that sensitive proxies eliminate inference. They move uncertainty to the calibration model.

That movement matters because it is easy to confuse precision with directness.

A result can report a bound at parts per hundred million and still depend on assumptions that dominate the interpretation. This is not a contradiction. The measurement may be extraordinarily precise while the mapping from measurement to underlying parameter remains conditional.

Technosignature claims are vulnerable to the same confusion.

A mass spectrometer might measure an isotope anomaly to parts per billion. That does not mean the probability of artificial origin is known to parts per billion. An atmospheric retrieval might detect a molecule at high statistical significance. That does not make its source unique. A radar track can have precise range and Doppler measurements while object identity remains uncertain.

Precision lives at one layer. Attribution lives at another.

Oklo forces the layers apart because the physicists did not simply measure samarium and announce new physics. They built reactor models, compared neutron spectra, examined temperature estimates, worried about contamination, and argued about which solution branches were physically plausible.

The disputes are instructive.

Some earlier Oklo analyses produced a non-null branch in which the resonance energy appeared shifted. Other work argued that gadolinium data, contamination treatment, or more realistic spectra disfavored that interpretation. The details are technical, but the sociology is familiar: an anomaly appears; a dramatic interpretation becomes possible; improved modeling changes the posterior landscape; the field does not resolve itself by insisting that extraordinary claims are forbidden. It resolves itself by making the forward model better.

This is exactly how a responsible search for ancient technology should behave.

If an anomalous layer resembles industrial activity, the first response should not be to suppress the possibility because it sounds sensational. Nor should the anomaly be branded evidence of a civilization. Build the natural forward models. Quantify volcanic carbon release. Model sedimentation and bioturbation. Search for impact tracers. Measure metal ratios. Look for synthetic compounds or unusual mineral phases. Date the onset and duration. Test whether the anomalies are globally synchronous. Ask what the industrial hypothesis predicts that the volcanic one does not.

As the models improve, some dramatic branches should die.

That is success.

The fine-structure-constant episode offers another warning: a geological archive can constrain physics only if we understand the archive as a physical system, not a black box.

Oklo was not a standardized laboratory reactor. Reaction zones differed in uranium content, geometry and water history. Some samples were altered. Reactor temperatures are reconstructed indirectly. Neutron spectra include thermal and epithermal components. The ancient system cannot be rerun.

Yet this mess does not make the experiment useless. It makes uncertainty accounting central.

Deep-time technosignature research will be even messier because the hypothesized source process may be unknown. We may not know what fuel an alien civilization used, which materials it manufactured, whether it had our industrial pathway, or whether it would generate our favored markers. Schmidt and Frank stressed a version of this: the most uniquely human industrial markers may not be universal markers of industrial civilization.

Plastics are diagnostic of us because we made plastics. Another technological species might not.

Nuclear fission is more universal in principle because uranium and nuclear physics do not depend on human chemistry, but the technological pathway still could differ. A civilization might use fusion, stellar energy, geothermal gradients, antimatter, or something beyond our present engineering. Even if it uses fission, it may breed thorium, reprocess aggressively, operate fast reactors, or consume nearly all long-lived waste.

The most universal technosignatures may therefore be thermodynamic or spatial rather than material: energy throughput, disequilibrium, heat rejection, unnatural concentration gradients. Unfortunately, those are often less unique because planets and stars also move enormous energy.

This is where Oklo again becomes useful. It sits precisely at the boundary between universal physics and local history.

The neutron cross sections are universal. The ore deposit is local.

The decay constants are universal. The groundwater path is local.

Fission yields are universal. Product retention is local.

A technosignature inference must separate those two layers. Which part of the observation follows from physics everywhere, and which part requires a specific history that might contain agency?

At Oklo, the specific history is natural and well supported. The universal physics then lets the site become a laboratory for questions much larger than itself.

There is a lovely reversal here. At first, Oklo was strange because a natural object looked technological. Later, physicists used that natural object as if it were a laboratory instrument.

The difference is that no one mistakes the instrument for an engineer.

This is what mature anomaly science looks like. The initial surprise is metabolized. The site becomes useful. It stops being a curiosity and begins constraining other questions.

A future technosignature discovery should aspire to the same fate.

If we ever find an ancient layer that seriously raises the possibility of technology, the best outcome will not be a permanent mystery. It will be an object that can be measured until the dramatic interpretation either collapses or becomes part of an explanatory model strong enough to make new predictions.

Perhaps the layer will turn out to be a new natural geochemical process. That would be a discovery.

Perhaps it will reveal an unknown type of impact chemistry. Also a discovery.

Perhaps it will contain mutually reinforcing signatures for which no natural process performs well. Then the technological hypothesis will deserve to rise.

The crucial thing is that each stage should create better experiments.

Oklo's fine-structure-constant work did not begin with an ancient-physics mystery and end with a slogan. It began with measurable isotope systems and became a decades-long argument about what they can constrain.

That is a much better model for frontier science than certainty on first contact.

A rock can carry a question for two billion years.

We should be willing to spend more than one news cycle answering it.
