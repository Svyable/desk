# Chapter 2 — When the Ore Went Critical

A reactor is not a substance. It is a relationship.

Enough fissile nuclei must be present. Neutrons released by fission must, on average, induce enough additional fissions before escaping or being absorbed by something unhelpful. The geometry matters. The energy distribution of the neutrons matters. Water can matter because hydrogen slows fast neutrons, increasing the chance that uranium-235 will absorb them and fission. Neutron poisons matter. Temperature matters. The reactor is the state of the whole arrangement.

That is why Oklo is more interesting than a rich uranium deposit.

At the time the reactors operated—commonly placed around 1.8 to 2.0 billion years ago—the isotopic composition of natural uranium was different from today's because uranium-235 decays faster than uranium-238. Reconstructions typically put ancient natural uranium above 3 percent uranium-235 around the relevant era. That does not automatically create criticality. It makes criticality possible under conditions that would be much harder for modern natural uranium.

The Franceville Basin supplied the rest through geology rather than engineering. Uranium was mobilized under oxidizing conditions and concentrated where reducing environments caused it to precipitate. Rich ore lenses formed. Water moved through the system. Some zones crossed the neutron-economy threshold.

The phrase “crossed the threshold” deserves attention because it gives this book its second useful analogy. Technological systems often look categorically different after a threshold even when their ingredients existed separately beforehand. A mine is not a reactor. A computer is not a network. Methane plus oxygen is not a biosignature until disequilibrium, flux and context make it one. Deep-time inference fails when we classify ingredients instead of reconstructing systems.

At Oklo the system announced itself by what it did to nuclei.

Uranium-235 was consumed. Uranium-238 captured neutrons, creating nuclides that could eventually lead to plutonium-239, which itself could fission. Fission products accumulated. Strong neutron absorbers altered the chain reaction. Heat changed groundwater behavior. The chemistry of the host rock influenced which products stayed put and which moved. In other words, the reactor wrote simultaneously into nuclear, thermal, hydrological and geochemical channels.

This multi-channel character is what makes the natural-reactor interpretation robust.

A simplistic technosignature hunt might privilege signatures that feel “too advanced for nature”: enriched or depleted isotopes, unusual molecules, smooth geometric objects, narrowband radio emission. But sophistication is a property of an explanation, not a residue. Nature is perfectly capable of producing structures or patterns that look designed if the state space is large enough and our intuitions are weak enough.

The correct question is not: *Can nature make this one thing?*

It is: *Can one natural process account for this whole correlated bundle at the observed place, time and scale?*

Oklo passes that test for natural criticality. The ancient uranium abundance explains why fission was more achievable. The sedimentary and redox setting explains uranium concentration. Groundwater provides a plausible moderator. Fission and capture explain the isotope patterns. Thermal feedback plausibly helps explain cycling. Long-term geochemistry explains differential retention.

Notice what is absent: intention is unnecessary.

That absence should not be treated as philosophical preference. It is model economy. A technological hypothesis would have to explain all of the same nuclear evidence while adding mining, design, construction, operation and disappearance without leaving independent evidence for those activities. When a geological model already predicts the observed reactor zones, technology adds entities without explanatory gain.

This is where “absence of evidence” becomes more subtle than the cliché allows. If a proposed cause would normally generate additional traces, their absence can matter. An engineered two-billion-year-old reactor might not preserve walls, pipes or tools. But engineering would likely alter ore geometry, material purity, spatial regularity, waste distribution, access pathways, associated extraction signatures or regional geochemistry in ways that could, in principle, differ from an ore body that went critical in place. The longer the time interval, the harder those auxiliary signatures are to preserve. Yet the hypothesis still makes a probabilistic commitment.

A natural model makes commitments too.

The deposit should look geologically coherent with the basin. Uranium transport should fit known redox processes. Reactor zones should align with ore structures rather than arbitrary architectural plans. Mineral alteration should track thermal and fluid history. Isotopic burnup should fit plausible neutron spectra. If those pieces failed badly, “natural reactor” would weaken.

That is a more productive way to handle extraordinary possibilities than arguing over whether extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. Ask what else must be true if the claim is true.

At Oklo, what else is true is remarkably geological.

The natural-reactor story also unsettles another lazy intuition: that sophisticated outcomes imply sophisticated control. Human reactors require elaborate systems because humans want particular power levels, safety margins, fuel utilization and outputs. Nature only has to wander into a self-sustaining region of parameter space. It can be inefficient. It can stop and restart. It can poison itself. It can waste fuel. It can operate for geological intervals without ever becoming an engineered machine.

Selection without a selector appears throughout science. Rivers optimize nothing yet carve efficient drainage networks. Crystals form ordered lattices without blueprints. Evolution produces machinery-like biological structures without foresight. Critical phenomena generate abrupt transitions from continuous parameter changes. Oklo belongs to this family of reminders.

The deposit did something we associate with technology because technology later learned to exploit the same physics.

The order of authorship runs the other way.

Physics invented the reactor. We eventually copied it.
