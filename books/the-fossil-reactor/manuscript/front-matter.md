# The Fossil Reactor

## Oklo, Deep Time, and the Problem of False Technosignatures

**Sven Hardy Benson**

There is a particular kind of scientific mistake that becomes more tempting as an idea becomes more exciting: confusing rarity with authorship.

In 1972, technicians in France found uranium from Gabon that did not have the isotopic composition uranium was supposed to have. Uranium-235 was missing. Not all of it, but enough to trigger the sort of accounting problem nuclear states take seriously. The answer was stranger than diversion and older than any state. Roughly two billion years earlier, parts of the Franceville Basin had assembled the ingredients for self-sustaining nuclear fission. Water moderated neutrons. Uranium ore reached criticality. Fission products accumulated. The reactors stopped. Geology buried the evidence and, astonishingly, preserved enough of it for isotope chemists to reconstruct what happened.

Oklo is often introduced as a curiosity: nature built a reactor before humans did. That sentence is true and much too small.

Oklo is also a calibration object for one of the hardest questions in astrobiology and deep-time archaeology: what does technology look like after nearly everything recognizable as technology is gone?

A nuclear reactor leaves signatures that feel technological because our species learned to make them technologically. Depleted uranium. Fission-product isotope patterns. Neutron-capture products. Plutonium once existed in the system. Noble gases record operational history. Some reaction zones appear to have cycled because water boiled away and returned. If a geochemist encountered an unfamiliar ancient layer carrying only selected pieces of that pattern, how quickly would the word *artifact* enter the conversation?

This book is about the discipline required before it does.

It is not a book arguing that an industrial civilization existed on Earth before ours. There is no credible evidence for that claim. Gavin Schmidt and Adam Frank's 2018 “Silurian hypothesis” paper was explicitly framed as a detectability exercise, not an assertion of lost industry. Their useful question was methodological: if a civilization had existed millions of years ago, what traces would remain, and how would we distinguish them from ordinary Earth-system events?

Oklo makes the problem harder in exactly the right way. It shows that nature can produce a signature—sustained uranium fission—that, encountered without geological context, would be easy to call technological. It also shows the opposite: once a rare process happens, isotope systems can preserve astonishingly specific operational information across geological time. The xenon work at Oklo has been interpreted as evidence for reactor cycles of roughly half an hour active followed by about two and a half hours dormant. Whether every detail of that reconstruction survives future refinement is less important than the existence of the archive itself. Rock can remember process.

The thesis of this book is therefore double-edged:

**deep time can preserve technological-looking residues far better than intuition suggests, but the same planet that preserves them can also manufacture false positives. A serious technosignature requires a conjunction of traces whose joint explanation is harder to naturalize than any one anomaly.**

That rule applies on Earth, Mars, icy moons, exoplanets, and perhaps one day to returned samples from worlds we have not yet visited. It also applies backward, to our own future fossil record. What would remain of us after ten million years? A hundred million? Two billion? Not skyscrapers. Not satellites. Not the objects on which archaeology normally depends. Perhaps unusual isotope ratios. Plastics transformed into unfamiliar carbonaceous matter. Persistent synthetic molecules in lucky depositional settings. Mining disturbances blurred by tectonics. Extinction patterns. A pulse of carbon. Metals redistributed into strange stratigraphic combinations. Transuranic daughters, for a while.

And every one of those traces would sit in a world capable of making some version of it without us.

So the real subject here is not lost civilizations. It is attribution under deep uncertainty.

## Evidence classes used in this book

**Class 1 — verified/documented:** measured isotope anomalies, mapped reactor zones, peer-reviewed geochemical and nuclear-physics results, documented discovery history, published technosignature and Anthropocene literature.

**Class 2 — credible but incomplete:** reconstructions that depend on reactor models, uncertain original geometry, altered samples, inferred temperatures, inferred cycling behavior, or incomplete preservation.

**Class 3 — disputed/unconfirmed:** claims that specific unexplained geological events were caused by prior industrial civilizations, or that isolated isotope anomalies establish technology without an exclusionary geological case.

**Class 4 — speculative synthesis/open conjecture:** how a future deep-time technosignature search should combine isotope systems, mineralogy, stratigraphy, molecular fossils, and spatial structure; what extraterrestrial false positives may resemble; what evidence would actually force an artificial-origin hypothesis upward rather than merely keep it alive.

The point of the four classes is not bureaucratic neatness. It is to prevent the book's most seductive possibility from eating the evidence that constrains it.

The rocks get the first word.
