# Chapter 16 — The Isotope Signature of Somebody Else

Oklo turns a local geological puzzle into a question about other worlds almost by accident.

If a civilization uses nuclear physics, can it change a planet's isotopes enough for somebody far away—or somebody much later—to notice?

The first answer is disappointing: usually not.

A reactor is small compared with a planet. Human isotope-separation plants process tiny fractions of Earth's elemental inventories. Nuclear weapons have left globally measurable fallout, but most individual artificial radionuclides decay quickly on astronomical timescales. An astronomer light-years away cannot take a core sample from our lake sediment. The strongest terrestrial nuclear fingerprints are often local or stratigraphic, not remotely visible.

Technosignature science therefore has to distinguish remote signatures from archaeological ones.

Oklo is an archaeological signature. Its power comes from touching the material.

Atmospheric technosignatures are different. They must alter gases enough to change a spectrum. Researchers have proposed industrial pollutants such as chlorofluorocarbons and, more recently, fully fluorinated gases including nitrogen trifluoride and sulfur hexafluoride because these compounds have strong technological associations and potentially useful spectral features. Sara Seager and colleagues argued in 2023 that NF3 and SF6 are attractive candidates in part because known terrestrial biology does not make fully fluorinated molecules of those classes.

The proposal is conditional, not a detection. An alien civilization does not have to manufacture our industrial gases. A telescope also needs the right wavelength coverage and sensitivity. Atmospheric photochemistry and lifetime matter. Clouds and stellar spectra matter. But the exercise shows how technosignature science formalizes possibility: choose a technological process, calculate planetary abundance, model spectra, ask whether an instrument could detect it.

Nuclear technology offers analogous possibilities, though direct atmospheric fission products may be poor long-term remote markers.

A more interesting 2025 proposal by David Catling, Joshua Krissansen-Totton and Tyler Robinson looked at deuterium. If a long-lived civilization powered itself using deuterium-deuterium fusion and drew the fuel from planetary water for geological timescales, it could gradually lower the planet's deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio. Under their modeled assumptions, sufficiently sustained fusion use could push ocean D/H below values expected from ordinary planetary evolution and even below the local interstellar medium, creating a potential long-lived technosignature.

This is a strange and elegant idea because it resembles Oklo from the opposite direction.

Oklo tells us that isotope anomalies can look technological while being natural.

The fusion proposal asks whether technology could create a planet-scale isotope anomaly that remains after the machinery is invisible.

The entire argument then lives or dies on false positives.

Planetary D/H ratios vary naturally. Atmospheric escape preferentially removes lighter hydrogen and can enrich deuterium. Cometary and asteroidal delivery affects inventories. Interior exchange matters. Formation history matters. Water loss matters. A low D/H value would not mean “fusion” by itself.

The proposed technosignature becomes interesting only if the measured ratio falls into a region that natural planetary models have difficulty reaching, while other observations constrain the escape and formation histories.

This is the Oklo rule again: an anomaly earns agency only after a natural generative model is pushed hard.

The fusion-D/H idea also exposes a feature of mature technologies that ordinary pollution signatures miss. A civilization could be environmentally clean and still consume an isotope reservoir.

Waste heat may be minimized but not eliminated. Pollutants may be captured. Materials may be recycled. Radio emissions may be narrow and encrypted. Yet sustained energy extraction can change reservoirs simply because fuel is finite and isotopically distinct.

This is a universalizing move in technosignature theory. Instead of guessing alien products, look for conservation-law consequences.

Oklo is built from the same move. The uranium-235 deficit is a mass-balance consequence of fission. You do not need to know how a reactor “looks” to notice that fissile nuclei were consumed.

Could other industrial energy systems leave similar inventory scars?

Hydrogen fusion could change D/H. Helium-3 fusion, if fuel were mined from a planetary atmosphere or regolith at enormous scale, could alter helium isotope ratios locally or globally. Fission changes uranium and plutonium inventories but probably not enough for remote detection unless the civilization processes a substantial fraction of planetary accessible uranium. Antimatter production would consume vast energy rather than a naturally abundant feedstock. Stellar engineering could alter elemental distributions on much larger scales.

The more dramatic the inventory change, the more energy the civilization has usually processed.

That creates a connection to Kardashev-style thinking without relying on giant structures. Energy use writes into reservoirs.

But reservoir signatures have a natural background, often larger than the technological effect. Earth has a D/H history because water came from sources, exchanged with rocks and atmosphere, and experienced escape. Uranium isotope ratios vary slightly naturally because of redox-dependent fractionation and nuclear processes. Carbon isotopes vary through biology and geology. Nitrogen isotopes vary through atmospheric and biological cycles.

A civilization does not write on blank paper.

It writes into a palimpsest already covered in planetary fractionation.

The remote observer faces a harder version of the Oklo problem because spatial context is unresolved. A spectrum integrates huge areas and altitudes. You may know a gas abundance but not where it came from. Planetary mass, radius, stellar ultraviolet spectrum, atmospheric pressure and surface composition help, but the inverse problem remains underdetermined.

This is why biosignature researchers developed contextual frameworks rather than treating oxygen as a yes/no test. NASA-supported exoplanet biosignature work has emphasized false positives, false negatives and Bayesian-style integration of planetary context. Technosignature science is moving in the same direction.

Oklo should be part of that intellectual toolkit because it is a real case in which a seemingly technological nuclear process has a natural planetary explanation.

What would an Oklo-like false positive look like on an exoplanet?

We almost certainly could not see a local natural reactor directly with current telescopes. But imagine a world with extensive uranium deposits and water where natural criticality occurs more frequently than on Earth. The energy output would still be tiny on planetary scales. Atmospheric fission products might be negligible. Yet a future probe sampling the surface could find reactor residues and mistake them for industry if the geological possibility were not considered.

Mars presents a closer version of that scenario. Its ancient hydrology and geochemistry differ from Earth's, and its uranium distribution is not identical. If a returned Martian sample ever showed fission signatures, a natural reactor hypothesis would have to be tested before technology entered the conclusion. Oklo provides the existence proof that planets can do this.

This is the broader value of terrestrial weirdness in astrobiology.

Every unusual natural process we understand becomes a false-positive library for other worlds.

Hydrothermal vents teach us about abiotic chemical gradients.

Serpentinization teaches us natural hydrogen and methane production.

Photochemistry teaches us abiotic oxygen possibilities.

Impact chemistry teaches us high-temperature organic synthesis and destruction.

Lightning teaches us nonbiological nitrogen fixation and complex chemistry.

Oklo teaches us natural nuclear industry without industry.

The word library is important because false positives are not nuisances to eliminate after discovery. They are training data collected beforehand.

A mature search program should deliberately catalogue extreme natural processes that mimic life or technology.

This is common in medical diagnostics. A good test is evaluated not only on people with the disease but on conditions likely to produce false positives. If a cancer marker also rises during inflammation, the clinical interpretation changes. Sensitivity without specificity creates panic.

Technosignature studies need the planetary equivalent of differential diagnosis.

Oklo belongs on the differential for ancient fission.

Large igneous provinces belong on the differential for industrial warming and metal anomalies.

Impacts belong on the differential for global spherule layers and extinctions.

Stellar flares belong on the differential for radio bursts and atmospheric chemistry changes.

Dust clouds belong on the differential for unusual transit light curves and infrared excess.

The more sensational the proposed technology, the more aggressively the differential should be built.

But there is a complementary risk: natural explanations can become an infinite dumping ground.

If every anomaly is answered with “some unknown natural process,” technology becomes unfalsifiable in the other direction. A valid natural alternative needs a mechanism and, ideally, independent predictions.

Oklo's natural explanation meets that standard magnificently.

Ancient uranium abundance was high enough.

Uranium was geologically concentrated.

Water could moderate neutrons.

Fission products are present.

Neutron absorbers record exposure.

The site geometry is an ore body, not an imported fuel depot.

Models reproduce criticality under plausible conditions.

That is not a hand-wave.

A remote exoplanet false positive should be held to the same standard when data permit. If an atmosphere contains a candidate industrial gas, model natural synthesis quantitatively. If the proposed natural pathway requires implausible fluxes or absent precursors, it should lose probability. If a low D/H ratio is attributed to unusual planetary formation, ask whether the planet's mass, stellar history and other isotope systems support that formation history.

Agency should not be forbidden. It should be forced to compete.

This makes technosignature science less about finding “alien stuff” and more about model comparison under sparse data.

The philosophical consequence is subtle. Technology is not a substance category.

There is no periodic-table box labeled artificial.

Technology is a history imposed on matter.

The same uranium nucleus can sit in untouched ore, a natural reactor, a centrifuge cascade, a weapon or a power reactor. Its identity does not change with agency. Its relationships do.

That is why technosignatures are often relational: isotope ratios, spatial concentrations, temporal modulation, disequilibrium, geometric organization, correlated waste streams.

Remote astronomy sees fewer relationships than field geology, so it must choose relationships that survive compression into light curves and spectra.

Temporal behavior is one. Industrial atmospheric gases might change over decades. Artificial illumination might track a rotating nightside. A laser could be narrowband and pulsed. Radio emission could carry modulation. Waste heat could show a nonnatural temperature distribution.

Isotope ratios may eventually become another as telescopes improve, though most stable-isotope separations in exoplanet atmospheres are difficult measurements even for biosignature work.

Local probes will always be more powerful.

This suggests that technosignature confidence may rise in stages with exploration technology. A telescope detects an odd atmospheric ratio. A future interferometer maps it. A robotic probe samples aerosols. An orbiter maps isotope gradients. A lander analyzes minerals. Eventually the anomaly moves from remote inference toward forensics.

Oklo is what the final stage looks like: the evidence is in hand, the context mapped, the physics modeled.

If we ever reach that stage on another world, the strongest habit we can carry with us is not skepticism or belief.

It is the insistence that agency explain the whole system better than the planet can.

The planet gets a defense attorney.

Oklo is Exhibit A.
