# Chapter 1 — The Missing Uranium

The story begins with accounting, which is how many good mysteries begin once governments own the instruments.

In 1972 uranium hexafluoride produced from ore mined at Oklo, in what is now Gabon, reached the French enrichment system. Natural uranium is not chemically uniform in the ordinary sense—ore bodies vary in mineralogy, impurities, age and history—but its isotopic composition is famously stable on human scales. The fraction of uranium-235 in ordinary natural uranium is about 0.72 percent today. Some Oklo material came in lower. The discrepancy was not a philosophical puzzle. Fissile isotope accounting is the kind of discrepancy a nuclear enterprise is built to notice.

The first important fact about Oklo is therefore epistemic rather than nuclear: the anomaly appeared because somebody knew what should be boring.

Mass spectrometry had made “natural uranium” into a numerical expectation. Against that baseline, a small departure became loud. Later work found reactor-zone ore much more strongly depleted in uranium-235, along with isotopic patterns in neodymium, ruthenium and other elements characteristic of fission and neutron capture. Nature had not merely fractionated uranium by some unknown geochemical trick. Parts of the deposit had consumed fissile material.

The surprise was historically conditioned. Paul Kuroda had argued in 1956 that natural uranium deposits could, under the right ancient conditions, have sustained chain reactions. The key was isotope history. Uranium-235 has a shorter half-life than uranium-238. Go far enough back in time and natural uranium contains a larger fraction of the fissile isotope. Roughly two billion years ago, the proportion was in the neighborhood of a few percent—closer to the enrichment of fuel used in some human reactors than modern natural uranium is. Add sufficiently rich ore, a neutron moderator such as water, favorable geometry, and low enough concentrations of neutron-absorbing contaminants, and “natural reactor” stops being an oxymoron.

That is not the same as saying reactors should have been common. Geological possibility is not geological frequency. The Franceville Basin had an unusual conjunction of uranium concentration, redox history, sedimentary structure and groundwater conditions. Oxygen matters because soluble oxidized uranium can be transported and later concentrated when it encounters reducing conditions. The rise of oxygen in Earth's atmosphere and surface environments changed the mobility of uranium. In broad terms, planetary evolution helped prepare the ore bodies that later went critical.

The phrase “nature built a reactor” conceals this chain of prerequisites. Nature did not decide to arrange fuel rods. A basin evolved into a configuration in which ordinary physical laws produced a neutron economy above the critical threshold for some intervals. The distinction matters because the same rhetorical compression will return later when people speak about technosignatures. We are tempted to translate outcome into intention.

The isotope evidence is stronger than the metaphor.

Fission rearranges nuclei in patterned ways. Uranium-235 depletion is one clue. Fission-product distributions are another. Neutron capture changes isotopic abundances in ways that depend on flux and spectrum. Samarium carries especially useful scars because samarium-149 has a large neutron-capture cross section. Neodymium isotopes can be separated conceptually into naturally occurring background and fission-derived components. Xenon, if trapped, preserves another archive. A single odd ratio might admit several explanations. A family of ratios generated by the same neutron field is harder to explain away.

That is the first rule this book steals from Oklo for technosignatures: *conjunction beats astonishment*.

Suppose a future Mars sample contains uranium anomalously depleted in uranium-235. The exciting sentence would be obvious. Someone used it. But that conclusion would be scientifically premature until the sample also carried the expected fission products, neutron-capture signatures, geologic setting and spatial structure. Even then, a natural criticality model would have to be tested. Oklo is the reason.

The irony is delicious. The clearest known case of ancient nuclear activity on Earth is not evidence for ancient technology. It is the reason ancient nuclear activity, by itself, cannot be treated as evidence for ancient technology.

There is another lesson in the discovery. Detection thresholds are cultural artifacts before they are scientific ones. Had uranium not been processed by a nuclear industry obsessed with material balance, the anomaly might have remained inside rock for longer. The geological record does not volunteer interpretations. It requires a monitoring system with enough precision, enough expectations and enough institutional reason to care.

Technosignature science often imagines the distant observer: a telescope looking for atmospheric chemicals, a radio array looking for narrowband emission, an infrared survey looking for waste heat. Oklo reminds us that some signatures become visible only after a civilization invents the bookkeeping category that makes the anomaly meaningful.

Before nuclear physics, depleted uranium was just uranium ore with an unrecognized isotope ratio. Before isotope geochemistry, the rock did not look like a reactor. Before reactor physics, a fission-product pattern had no narrative.

Evidence can predate the concepts necessary to see it.

This is not mystical. It is ordinary science at its best. Instruments turn expectation into numbers. Numbers turn deviation into a question. Models compete to explain the deviation. Independent isotope systems narrow the possibilities. Eventually the explanation becomes stranger than the anomaly first appeared.

At Oklo, the missing uranium was not missing.

It had been burned two billion years before anybody learned how to ask where it went.
