# Back Matter — Evidence, Counterevidence, and a Field Guide to Endings

The Deletion Economy is an argument about a pattern, not a claim that every old thing should disappear.

Preservation matters. Repair matters. Reuse matters. Containment can be wiser than removal. A functioning old system can be safer than a rushed replacement. A dam can remain useful. A building can deserve adaptation. A material can be recovered rather than destroyed. The practical question is not whether subtraction is virtuous. It is whether an ending has been designed as deliberately as a beginning.

The research notes accompanying this manuscript prioritize regulators, public oversight bodies, standards organizations, original research, and first-party technical material. Numbers that describe inventories, funding, or regulatory programs should be read as time-bounded observations rather than permanent constants. The source ledger records the dates and contexts that matter.

A useful field guide for any asset, product, institution, or system begins with seven questions:

1. What remains when productive use ends?
2. Who legally and economically owns that remainder?
3. What does a safe and useful residual state look like?
4. What information will a future remover need?
5. What money is reserved before the productive cash flow disappears?
6. What evidence proves that the ending is complete?
7. What design change today would make that ending cheaper, safer, or unnecessary?

Those questions work on a well, a building, a battery, a dataset, a spacecraft, a mine, a software application, and many things this book never mentions.

That is the point.

The deletion economy is not a sector. It is the missing half of the asset lifecycle.

The future will still be built. The societies that build best will be the ones that know, before they begin, how the thing ends.

— Sven Hardy Benson
