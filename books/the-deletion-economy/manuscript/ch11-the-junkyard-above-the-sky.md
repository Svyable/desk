# Chapter 11 — The Junkyard Above the Sky

Nothing in orbit is still.

A dead satellite may be useless, silent, and completely beyond human control, yet it can travel around Earth again and again at thousands of miles per hour. A piece of rocket body that has not served a mission in decades can cross paths with hardware launched last week.

The junkyard above the sky is not a pile.

It is traffic.

That difference makes orbital debris one of the most technically elegant deletion problems humans have created.

On Earth, gravity helps garbage become local. A broken chair sits where it falls. A derelict building occupies one parcel. A mine is attached to geology. An abandoned well has coordinates.

Orbital debris inhabits trajectories.

Objects circle the planet, and those trajectories intersect a shared environment used by weather satellites, communications systems, Earth observation, science missions, navigation infrastructure, human spaceflight, and a rapidly expanding commercial space sector.

The European Space Agency's 2025 Space Environment Report described about 40,000 objects tracked by space-surveillance networks at the end of 2024, with roughly 11,000 active payloads, while models estimate far larger populations of smaller debris. ESA explicitly describes Earth's orbital environment as a finite resource and argues that mitigation alone is insufficient in some regions: active debris removal is needed to reduce long-term collision risk.

This is a remarkable sentence for an economy built around launching things.

The space industry has reached the point where addition requires subtraction.

That transition happens in every mature system.

Roads need abandoned vehicles removed. Ports dredge accumulated sediment. Cities demolish unsafe structures. Computer systems archive and delete. Forest managers remove fuels in some landscapes. Industrial plants shut old units before new ones can be integrated.

Capacity is not only what you build.

It is what you stop allowing to occupy the system.

Orbit makes this literal because collision risk can create more debris.

Two objects hit. They fragment. The fragments create new collision opportunities. Under some conditions, a sufficiently dense debris environment can generate self-sustaining growth in the population of dangerous objects even without future launches, a dynamic associated with what is commonly called Kessler syndrome.

This is the deletion economy's nightmare case: the waste reproduces.

Most waste is passive. Orbital debris can create more orbital debris through physics.

Prevention therefore has exceptional value.

Space-debris mitigation standards focus on reducing release of mission-related debris, preventing accidental explosions, designing spacecraft for post-mission disposal, limiting long-lived objects in protected orbital regions, and reducing collision risk. NASA maintains orbital-debris mitigation requirements, and international guidelines encourage operators to plan end-of-life behavior before launch.

The logic should now be familiar.

The disposal plan belongs in the design review.

A spacecraft without a credible ending is an asset with an unfunded spatial liability.

The analogy to an orphan well is stronger than it first appears.

A spacecraft begins useful and owned. It has a mission, an operator, telemetry, propulsion, and people watching it. Over time fuel runs low. Components fail. The company can be acquired. The mission ends. If disposal is not completed while control remains, the object can become an orbital orphan.

Ownership may still be legally identifiable.

Operational parenthood is gone.

The object remains in a shared environment where everyone else bears some collision risk.

This creates an intergenerational commons problem.

A satellite can provide benefits for a few years and leave a piece of hardware in orbit much longer. Future operators did not receive the original service, but they inherit the conjunction warnings.

Time separates the benefit from the congestion.

Once again, the answer begins by bringing the ending forward.

Reserve propellant. Design reliable disposal capability. Passivate energy sources to reduce explosions. Choose orbits and mission architectures with end-of-life behavior in mind. Track the object. Maintain contact long enough to complete disposal.

These steps are far cheaper than rendezvousing later with an uncontrolled object.

The economics of active debris removal make that asymmetry stark.

Launching one satellite into orbit may be routine enough to fit inside a commercial schedule. Removing one dead satellite can require another spacecraft to find, approach, characterize, match motion with, capture or otherwise interact with a target that may not have been designed for servicing, then move it into a safer trajectory or controlled reentry.

Addition starts with a cooperative payload.

Deletion may start with an uncooperative target.

This is the same design failure seen in buildings glued together and software without migration paths. The future remover was not invited to the original architecture.

Active-debris-removal companies therefore face a strange salvage problem.

On Earth, scrap often has material value. A steel structure can be cut and recycled. Copper wire can be sold. An old device contains recoverable metals.

In orbit, the material can be valuable in theory and worthless in practice because bringing it home costs more than the scrap. The immediate economic value comes from reducing collision risk and preserving access to orbital regions.

Absence is the product.

The customer is difficult to identify.

Who should pay to remove a dead upper stage launched decades ago? The original state? The launch provider? Current satellite operators who benefit from lower risk? An international fund? Insurers? Governments purchasing a public good?

The physics are easier than the invoice.

This makes orbital debris a leading example of a deletion market waiting for market design.

There are companies developing rendezvous, inspection, servicing, life extension, and debris-removal capabilities. But a commercial capability becomes a large industry only when someone pays repeatedly for the outcome.

Regulation can create demand by requiring disposal or remediation. Insurance can price risk. Governments can procure removal of priority objects. Operators can pay into collective funds. Licensing systems can incorporate end-of-life performance.

Each mechanism has tradeoffs.

A fee on launches may be simple but can poorly reflect risk if every mission pays the same. Risk-based pricing could reward safer designs but depends on models and data. Requiring operators to remove their own satellites encourages responsibility but does little for historical debris whose creators are gone or unable to act.

The orphan problem returns.

We need a system for legacy objects and another to avoid making more.

The same dual structure appears in mines and wells. Society pays for yesterday's orphaned liabilities while strengthening assurance for today's operators.

The deletion economy has two businesses: cleanup and prevention of future cleanup.

They should not be confused.

Space also adds a geopolitical complication. Under international space law, ownership and jurisdiction over launched objects do not become casual salvage rights just because an object is defunct. A company cannot simply tow another country's satellite because it looks abandoned.

Removal can require consent and coordination.

This is deletion with sovereignty attached.

The technical image of a robotic servicer gripping a dead satellite is therefore incomplete. Behind the robot sit licensing, liability, diplomatic questions, data sharing, military sensitivities, and rules around proximity operations.

An orbital debris company is partly an aerospace company and partly an institution operating in a politically charged commons.

This is not a reason the market cannot grow.

It is a reason institutional competence will be a competitive advantage.

The best deletion companies often sit at awkward boundaries: engineering and law, chemistry and waste regulation, software and privacy, ecology and public consent.

The difficulty of the boundary is part of the moat.

Orbital debris further illustrates the value of standard interfaces.

Future spacecraft could include grappling fixtures, fiducial markers, cooperative navigation aids, standardized servicing ports, or other features that make inspection, refueling, repair, relocation, or disposal easier. Some missions already explore servicing-friendly design.

This is design for disassembly without gravity.

A small feature added at manufacture can save an enormous future rendezvous problem.

The economics resemble tow hooks on cars. Most vehicles will never need to be dragged from an extraordinary location, but a standardized attachment point makes recovery cheaper when they do.

The deletion economy likes hooks.

Anything that gives the future a safe place to grab the past has value.

Buildings need lifting points and accessible fasteners. Data systems need deletion interfaces. industrial plants need isolation valves. Financial contracts need successor obligations. Spacecraft need disposal and servicing interfaces.

A system with no safe place to grab is a system designed to become an orphan.

There is also an information lesson in orbit.

Tracking is deletion infrastructure.

Before anyone can avoid or remove an object, they need to know where it is with sufficient accuracy. Space-surveillance networks catalog many objects, operators exchange conjunction data, and models estimate populations too small to track individually.

The invisible debris matters enormously because objects too small for routine tracking can still damage spacecraft at orbital velocities.

This creates a risk floor that removal can reduce but not eliminate.

The deletion economy must be careful with perfection language.

Orbit will not be cleaned like a kitchen floor. The goal is to maintain collision risk within sustainable bounds, reduce the most consequential derelict objects, improve post-mission disposal, and avoid fragmentation.

The endpoint is managed congestion.

That is a more realistic model for many global problems.

We will not remove every molecule of anthropogenic CO2. We will not restore every ecosystem to a pre-industrial state. We will not recover every plastic particle or unexploded munition. We will not delete every obsolete file from every forgotten backup.

Civilization needs deletion priorities.

Which removals reduce the most risk per dollar? Which objects are most likely to generate additional harm? Which interventions create option value? Which legacy liabilities can be contained safely instead of physically removed?

Orbital-debris modeling can help prioritize high-mass objects in crowded regions or objects with elevated collision potential. Similar risk-ranking logic appears in Superfund cleanup, mine reclamation, infrastructure inspection, and cybersecurity vulnerability management.

Deletion at scale becomes portfolio optimization.

The world has more unwanted stock than it can remove immediately.

Prioritization is not surrender.

It is how an industry becomes rational.

There is one more reason orbital debris belongs in a book about future industries.

Space is still young.

Most of the infrastructure stock that will exist in orbit by mid-century has not yet been launched. That means the deletion economy can influence the design of the system before legacy overwhelms it.

Earth-based infrastructure rarely gets this luxury. Cities inherit centuries of pipes. Mining regions inherit nineteenth-century workings. Governments inherit software built before the web. Water systems inherit lead installed by people who never imagined current standards.

Space can choose differently.

Every satellite licensed today can carry an ending requirement. Every constellation can budget disposal reliability. Every launch can price future congestion. Every servicing architecture can plan for failure.

We know the orphan pattern in advance.

If we repeat it in orbit, we cannot claim surprise.

That may be the most hopeful lesson in the book.

The deletion economy does not have to begin after the mess.

It can begin on the launchpad.
