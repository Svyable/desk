# The Things We Forgot to End

There is a ceremony for putting almost anything important into the world.

A mayor cuts a ribbon at a bridge. A utility synchronizes a new generator to the grid. A drilling crew celebrates first production. A founder rings a bell. A satellite separates from its launch vehicle and the control room applauds. A family carries boxes through the front door of a new house. Even software has a launch.

We are less practiced at the opposite ceremony.

There is no brass band when the last barrel comes out of an oil well and somebody has to decide who will pay to fill the hole. Nobody rings a bell when a forty-year-old payroll system is finally turned off after three replacement attempts. A nuclear plant can spend decades making electricity and years making almost nothing except work for people whose job is to make the plant cease to be a nuclear plant. A satellite that once justified a launch can become a fast-moving piece of metal sharing an orbital highway with everything launched afterward.

The ending is not the absence of work. Often it is the most concentrated work the asset will ever require.

That is the subject of this book.

I began with a question that sounds almost too ordinary to carry an economic argument: what happens to useful things when they stop being useful?

The answer kept opening into larger rooms.

An oil well becomes a plugging problem. A mine becomes a water and land problem. A reactor becomes a decommissioning problem. A dam becomes a safety, sediment, fish, power, recreation, and property problem. A building becomes hundreds or thousands of tons of material whose next destination depends on how it was assembled. A lead service line is valuable right up until the moment we understand that its material is the problem. A molecule designed to resist heat, water, or grease can remain stubborn long after the product it improved has been discarded. A database can keep copies after the interface says delete. A software system can become so embedded in an institution that nobody can easily replace it, even when every engineer involved agrees that it should go.

In each case the useful life and the responsible life are different lengths.

The machine may finish its job before society finishes with the machine.

Economics has long had words for depreciation, residual value, externalities, sunk costs, liabilities, remediation, closure, reclamation, decommissioning, waste, abandonment, and disposal. Engineering disciplines have their own vocabulary. Environmental law has another. Cybersecurity has another. The terms are not missing.

What is easy to miss is that they describe versions of the same problem.

We have built an economy optimized to notice addition.

More homes. More roads. More wells. More capacity. More accounts. More devices. More storage. More satellites. More chemical performance. More throughput. Gross domestic product is a flow measure, not a morality play, but the cultural habit around it is unmistakable: a crane on the skyline looks like progress. A demolition excavator does not. A new factory is a photograph. The unglamorous work of taking down an obsolete factory is usually described as cleanup.

Sometimes cleanup is exactly the right word. But the word can hide productive sophistication.

Consider what it takes to remove a structure without bringing down the one beside it; to plug a well so fluids and gases stop using the bore as a path; to dismantle radioactive equipment while protecting workers and the public; to remove an invasive predator from an island without destroying the native animals you are trying to save; to clear a field of explosives after maps have been lost and vegetation has grown back; to erase data across modern storage systems with enough assurance that “gone” has a technical meaning; to retire software while preserving every function that depended on it.

These are not empty activities. They require knowledge, machinery, judgment, insurance, measurement, regulation, and people who know what can go wrong.

Subtraction can be a high-skill industry.

The argument becomes more interesting when money enters.

When an asset is profitable, everyone can see the cash coming out of it. The future bill attached to its ending is harder to see. It may arrive twenty, forty, or eighty years later. The company may have changed owners. The original executives may be dead. The technology may be obsolete. The regulator may have been reorganized. The community may still be there.

Time turns responsibility into a credit problem.

A promise to clean up something in 2060 is partly an engineering plan and partly a claim about who will still be solvent in 2060. This is why financial assurance keeps appearing in industries that otherwise have little in common. Nuclear regulators require mechanisms intended to support eventual decommissioning. Mining rules can require reclamation bonds. Oil and gas regulators use bonding and other forms of financial assurance. Waste programs use closure and post-closure requirements. Product rules can move end-of-life costs back toward producers.

The instruments differ. The intuition is the same: if the ending is certain, funding it should not depend entirely on the future generosity of whoever happens to be standing nearby.

That sounds obvious after the fact. It is less obvious at the beginning, when an industry is young and all attention points toward growth.

The first generation of an industry tends to ask whether something can be built. The second asks whether it can be scaled. A mature industry eventually has to ask whether it can be retired.

That last question will become more important, not less.

A richer civilization owns more stuff. A more digital civilization stores more state. A more chemically sophisticated civilization creates more substances that do not exist in ordinary natural cycles. A spacefaring civilization places more hardware in orbit. A decarbonizing civilization will retire large amounts of old energy infrastructure while building new infrastructure at the same time. An aging civilization inherits bridges, dams, pipes, buildings, reactors, mines, refineries, cables, platforms, landfills, servers, and records created under assumptions that may no longer hold.

Abundance accumulates.

Eventually accumulation creates a market for selective absence.

I am calling that market the deletion economy. The term is intentionally broad. It includes literal deletion of information, but it is mostly about physical and institutional removal: decommissioning, reclamation, remediation, dismantling, plugging, deconstruction, clearance, take-back, eradication, retirement, and the design choices that make those acts possible.

There are two mistakes I want to avoid from the beginning.

The first is romanticizing removal. Taking things away can be destructive. A dam may be obsolete as a power asset and still support a community's water supply. A building may be inefficient and still contain enormous embodied energy and cultural value. Eradication campaigns can harm non-target species. Remediation can move contamination from one place to another and call the first place clean. Carbon removal can be used rhetorically to delay emissions cuts. “Delete everything” is not a serious data-governance strategy. The book will keep returning to the question: removed from where, removed by what method, and removed into what next state?

The second mistake is treating the deletion economy as a consolation prize for failure. Prevention usually dominates cleanup. The cheapest orphan well is a well whose closure was funded while it was producing. The easiest hazardous chemical to destroy is one never dispersed through millions of products. The safest piece of orbital debris is a spacecraft that completed a reliable disposal maneuver. Designing the ending early is part of creation done properly.

The strongest version of the deletion economy may therefore be almost invisible.

It is the refundable deposit that keeps a future liability from becoming an orphan. The fastener that allows a component to be separated rather than shredded. The data lineage that tells an engineer where every copy went. The modular replacement that lets a critical system retire in pieces instead of one terrifying weekend. The material passport that turns a demolished building into a parts inventory. The contract that survives a change in ownership. The boring reserve account that nobody celebrates because, decades later, it simply pays the bill it was created to pay.

That is a different picture of progress.

Progress is usually drawn as a line going up and to the right. More production. More capability. More output.

But there is another axis. A civilization can become better at finishing what it starts.

It can learn to make useful things and also remove them cleanly; to profit from assets without hiding the funeral costs; to use shared environments without treating them as permanent storage; to build systems that can be reversed, dismantled, migrated, and retired.

The future will still contain spectacular acts of addition. New factories, medicines, networks, spacecraft, homes, energy systems, and machines will matter enormously.

Some of the most valuable companies beside them may be the ones whose product is that, afterward, something difficult is finally gone.
