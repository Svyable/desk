# A Successful Obsolescence

There is a kind of technological defeat that should count as a victory.

West Ford demonstrated passive orbital scatter communication. Then active satellites made the architecture unattractive.

This outcome is easy to narrate as waste: why launch hundreds of millions of copper dipoles if satellites were about to solve the same problem more elegantly? That question commits hindsight fraud. In the late 1950s, the future reliability, cost, capacity, and military usefulness of active satellite communication were not settled facts. Competing architectures deserved experiments because uncertainty was real.

The better criticism is not that West Ford explored the wrong branch. It is that exploring a branch with global externalities requires a different burden than exploring one inside a laboratory.

Experimental value and environmental permission have to be evaluated together.

Once the belt proved capable of supporting communication, the relevant comparison shifted. Active satellites could concentrate capability into maintainable, controllable assets. They could amplify, switch, and evolve. The communications system no longer had to treat orbit itself as the reflecting medium.

West Ford then became a successful obsolescence: a technology whose experiment produced knowledge while the operational system failed to justify continuation.

This is a useful category for frontier programs. The pressure to defend past spending often converts experimental success into a demand for deployment. But demonstration should sometimes be the end. A prototype can answer the question for which it was built and still lose the next procurement decision.

The opposite error also occurs. Because West Ford did not become an enduring network, later storytellers can imply it was technologically foolish. Lincoln Laboratory's performance record says otherwise. The project sits in the uncomfortable middle where serious engineering met a rapidly changing technical landscape.

Institutions need a way to honor that middle. Otherwise every experiment becomes either a triumph that must scale or a failure that should be forgotten.

The environment cannot afford that binary. High-externality experiments should be designed with explicit stop conditions, cleanup assumptions, and rival architectures in view from the beginning. Technical success should not automatically raise the political cost of stopping.

West Ford stopped, in part, because a better machine arrived.

Sometimes progress looks like proving you can do something and then deciding not to build the world around it.
