# The Copper Sky

The most useful fact about Project West Ford is still the one least compatible with the joke: it worked.

The copper belt became a real microwave scatter medium. Messages crossed the continent through it. Engineers learned from the deployment. Then active satellites improved quickly enough to make the system obsolete.

The astronomers who opposed it were not vindicated by a ruined sky. They were vindicated by history's decision to take their category of concern seriously. Near-Earth space became recognized, however imperfectly, as a shared domain in which one actor's technically successful system could impose conditions on others.

This is a more mature ending than villainy.

If West Ford had been a complete technical failure, nobody would need to wrestle with it. If it had devastated astronomy, the moral would be easy. If active satellites had not arrived, perhaps the pressure to build permanent belts would have become much harder to resist. Instead the episode sits in the narrow region where every side was partly right and the future resolved the immediate dispute by changing the technology.

That is exactly why it should be remembered.

The program demonstrates that infrastructure choices are arguments about what the environment is allowed to become. A natural ionosphere was unreliable, so engineers proposed a designed scattering layer. The decision treated orbit not simply as a route through which spacecraft traveled but as a material substrate for communications.

We now make similar moves with far more capable machines. Orbital shells become network layers. Radio spectrum becomes a managed computational resource. The night sky becomes the background against which thousands of moving reflectors operate. None of this is equivalent to West Ford in mechanism. All of it inherits the same political fact: once infrastructure occupies a shared environment densely enough, engineering choices become environmental choices.

The copper needles mostly left. The Westford antenna remained and found new scientific uses. Satellite communications won. Space law matured. Astronomers became organized participants in infrastructure debates. A strange military experiment that did not become a system still changed the questions people knew to ask.

There are worse outcomes for a failed future.

The sky did not become copper. For a few years, enough of it did to teach us that the sky could be designed.
