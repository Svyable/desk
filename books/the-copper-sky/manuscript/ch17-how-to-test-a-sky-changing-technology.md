# How to Test a Sky-Changing Technology

A planetary-scale experiment should be designed so that failure teaches us something and success does not trap us.

West Ford offers a surprisingly good starting point because the program had both virtues and defects. It tested a specific technical proposition. The deployment was designed to be temporary. The system's performance could be measured. Astronomical effects were monitored. The technology was not automatically scaled after demonstration.

Those are strong features.

The weak point was legitimacy. The groups affected by an alteration of orbit had limited ability to evaluate the experiment before deployment, particularly where national-security classification narrowed access to information. International consultation was immature. Cleanup predictions depended on a deployment whose first attempt had already shown that dispersion behavior could surprise engineers.

A modern high-externality experiment should therefore separate five questions before launch, though I resist turning them into a branded framework. What exact proposition requires field scale? What smaller experiment has already failed to answer it? Which environmental quantities are predicted to change, with uncertainty rather than one expected value? What independent observers can measure those changes? What happens if the system technically succeeds but the environmental assumptions fail?

The last question is the most neglected. Engineers plan for mission failure because mission failure is obvious. We need equal planning for the case where the machine works and the world around it responds badly.

The design should include defeat conditions. A claim about harmlessness should be stated in measurable terms before the result is known. A cleanup model should identify tail risks and population behavior. Independent measurement should not depend entirely on data supplied by the operator. If a project alters a shared scientific environment, affected scientific communities should help define the monitoring plan without receiving a veto over every use of the domain.

This sounds bureaucratic until one remembers that the alternative is to discover governance through controversy after deployment.

West Ford's great historical gift is that it remained small in duration relative to what its architecture imagined. We can study the belt as a near miss without pretending catastrophe occurred.

The next sky-changing technology may not be so forgiving.
