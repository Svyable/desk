# May 1963

The second West Ford experiment launched in May 1963 into a sky already crowded with Cold War meaning.

Sputnik had been up less than six years. Human spaceflight was new. Telstar had already shown the appeal of active communications satellites. The United States had conducted the Starfish Prime high-altitude nuclear test the previous year, producing artificial radiation effects that damaged satellites and deepened international suspicion about large unilateral experiments in near-Earth space.

Into that atmosphere came copper.

Lincoln Laboratory's history says the West Ford belt formed and closed over about forty days, eventually reaching a density on the order of a few dipoles per cubic kilometer. The number of pieces was enormous, but their separation was also enormous. This is why both the engineering and public arguments were so counterintuitive. Hundreds of millions sounds dense. Orbital volume made it sparse.

The ground system had to find usefulness in that sparsity. At Camp Parks in California and the Westford site in Massachusetts, narrow beams illuminated a common region of the belt. A receiver collected the tiny scattered return. The arrangement was not broadcasting to the belt in the loose sense. It was geometry disciplined enough to make a diffuse population behave like a communications path.

The 1963 experiment succeeded before its controversy could settle. Astronomers worried about radio and optical interference and about precedent. American officials defended the project's temporary nature and scientific safeguards. International critics saw a country treating near-Earth space as experimental territory before any widely accepted governance regime had matured.

All sides had a point they could support.

The actual belt appears not to have catastrophically ruined astronomy. Physics Today reported in 1964 that the National Academy of Sciences' Space Science Board found no significant interference with optical or radio observations from the experiment. That finding matters because retrospective environmental history becomes dishonest if it converts feared harm into observed harm.

But absence of major observed interference does not make the governance concern disappear. The astronomers were also arguing about permission, precedent, and cumulative behavior. Those concerns cannot be tested solely by asking whether one experiment measurably damaged one observing campaign.

A technically successful experiment can reveal that a feared mechanism was overstated while simultaneously revealing that the institution deciding whether to conduct the experiment was underdeveloped.

May 1963 is therefore not best remembered as the month America littered orbit with needles. It is the month an argument about infrastructure arrived before the rules for having the argument.
