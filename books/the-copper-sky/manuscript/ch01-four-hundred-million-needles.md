# Four Hundred Million Needles

Nineteen kilograms of copper is not much. It is less than a checked suitcase allowance on some airlines. In Project West Ford, that small mass was cut into roughly 480 million pieces of wire, each 0.7 inch long and 0.0007 inch in diameter, and sent toward a circular polar orbit about 2,200 miles above Earth. The pieces were meant to spread until the average distance between neighboring dipoles was measured in hundreds of meters. The engineering trick was to make almost nothing useful by having an absurd amount of it.

That is why West Ford is easy to retell badly. Hundreds of millions of copper needles in orbit sounds like satire with an Air Force contract number. The joke arrives before the problem. In 1963 the problem was not absurd: the United States wanted long-range military communications that could survive circumstances in which the ordinary communications system had stopped being ordinary.

Submarine cables could be cut. High-frequency radio depended on a natural ionosphere whose behavior varied with solar conditions and could itself be disturbed by nuclear detonations. Active communications satellites existed, but they were not yet the mature global military network they would become. Lincoln Laboratory was looking for another path—a communications medium that did not need an onboard power supply, a transponder, or a single spacecraft that could fail.

Walter E. Morrow and colleagues proposed making the medium itself orbital. Ground transmitters would illuminate a belt of resonant copper dipoles. Each wire would scatter almost nothing. In aggregate, enough scattered microwave energy could reach a distant receiver to carry information. The proposed system required 60-foot antennas, transmitters delivering roughly 20 to 40 kilowatts of average power, low-noise maser receivers, and a four-megahertz signaling scheme. The famous needles were the cheapest-looking part of a fairly serious machine.

This matters because the cartoon version of West Ford quietly supplies its verdict in advance. A bizarre-looking technology must have been foolish; a technology that later lost to something better must have failed. Neither follows.

The first West Ford launch, on October 21, 1961, did fail: the dipoles did not disperse as planned. The second, on May 8, 1963, used an improved dispensing system. Over about forty days a belt formed. Lincoln Laboratory's later history puts its density at roughly five dipoles per cubic kilometer and reports communication rates up to 20,000 bits per second while the belt was still relatively dense. Voice, data, and teletype crossed the continent by scattering radio energy from pieces of copper with no electronics in them at all.

The experiment had demonstrated the physics. It had not settled whether the architecture deserved a future.

That distinction is the book's real starting point. Active satellites with transponders were improving at the same moment. They could concentrate capability in machines that could be upgraded, steered, replaced, and eventually made far more useful than a passive belt. West Ford's success therefore arrived with an unusually sharp counterexample to the idea that technical success decides technological history. A system can work and still be the wrong system.

The objections do not become trivial because a better architecture won. Astronomers had reason to care about hundreds of millions of artificial scatterers placed into a domain they also used for observation. Other governments had reason to notice that one country was conducting a planetary-scale experiment in a space no country owned. The designers, for their part, had reason to point out that they had deliberately chosen tiny passive elements and an orbit intended to make the experimental belt temporary. The strongest version of the West Ford story has to allow all of those facts to be true at once.

So the useful questions are less theatrical than the needles. What did the system actually do? What risks did its designers understand? Which risks were outsiders entitled to reject even if the engineering worked? And what changes when a technology alters a shared environment before the institutions for governing that environment have caught up?

West Ford lasted briefly. The argument did not.
