# Mega-Constellations, Sixty Years Early

West Ford is not a miniature version of today's satellite constellations. It is an ancestor of the argument.

The differences are fundamental. Modern communications satellites are active, addressable, tracked spacecraft capable of enormous data throughput. Operators can maneuver many of them, update software, deorbit them, replace them, coordinate spectrum, and model conjunctions. West Ford's needles were passive resonant scatterers with no individual control.

The similarity is architectural scale. Both approaches improve communications resilience by making orbit populous enough to function as infrastructure.

That shared move produces a familiar political pattern. The operator evaluates mission benefits against modeled impacts. Astronomers and other users evaluate cumulative change in the observing environment. Regulators work with rules often written for fewer objects. International effects arise even when authorization is national.

The number of objects becomes a social fact before it becomes a physical hazard.

West Ford's critics worried about precedent because they could imagine permanent belts following a successful experiment. That future did not occur. Another future did: active satellite constellations whose object counts can reach into the thousands and planned systems into much larger numbers.

The analogy should not be used lazily. Copper needles and maneuverable satellites differ in brightness, radio behavior, collision cross section, lifetime, utility, and controllability. A historical case becomes useless when resemblance replaces analysis.

The better use of West Ford is to ask old questions with modern numbers. Who bears the burden of proving tolerable interference? How should cumulative effects be modeled when each new deployment is individually compliant? What consultation is owed to scientific communities? What disposal claims deserve confidence? How do we treat technological architectures whose value increases with scale while some externalities also increase with scale?

West Ford's answer was improvised because the institutions were young. Modern space governance has treaties, licensing, debris standards, spectrum coordination, and a much larger technical literature. Yet the political asymmetry remains. Infrastructure can be deployed faster than international consensus can be renegotiated.

The copper belt passed quickly through history. The argument stayed in orbit.
