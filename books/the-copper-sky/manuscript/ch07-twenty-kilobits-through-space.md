# Twenty Kilobits Through Space

Twenty thousand bits per second is unimpressive until the competing system is silence.

Lincoln Laboratory's retrospective account gives West Ford peak data rates up to 20,000 bps during the denser early phase of the belt. As the scatterers dispersed, performance fell. The details of modulation, receiver sensitivity, antenna gain, orbital geometry, and signal spread belong in the deeper technical pass of this manuscript, but the headline is enough to kill one lazy interpretation: the belt was not a fantasy that failed immediately upon contact with physics.

The communication path existed.

That makes the project's demise more interesting. Engineers often tell histories in which the best-performing technology wins. West Ford reminds us that a system can meet its experimental objective and still lose almost instantly because another architecture changes the relevant comparison.

Passive orbital scatter demanded enormous ground capability. Active satellites put amplification and electronics in orbit. As spacecraft reliability improved and satellite communications accelerated, the trade shifted. The passive belt offered survivability through dispersion but gave up flexibility, efficiency, and control. An active satellite could relay signals with far more useful link economics and without requiring hundreds of millions of free-flying resonant elements.

West Ford's technical success therefore did not accumulate into strategic inevitability. It became evidence about a branch of the design tree that the military did not need to follow.

That is what experiments are supposed to do, although organizations rarely celebrate it this way. A good experiment can make itself obsolete. If it proves a principle and clarifies the costs, a rival architecture may become easier to justify.

The phrase failed project is too crude for this kind of outcome. West Ford succeeded as an experiment, failed to become enduring infrastructure, and helped define problems that later space governance would take seriously. Those are three different legacies.

The result also disciplines modern analogy. It is tempting to look at the scale of today's satellite constellations and say we are repeating West Ford. The better analogy is structural, not moral. Both episodes ask what happens when communication reliability is obtained by turning orbital abundance into infrastructure. But active satellites are controllable machines with propulsion, telemetry, service lifetimes, and discrete operators. West Ford's dipoles were passive material.

The similarities matter because scale changes a commons. The differences matter because mitigation depends on the mechanism.

Twenty kilobits through space was enough to show the world that the weird system worked. It was not enough to make the weird system win.
