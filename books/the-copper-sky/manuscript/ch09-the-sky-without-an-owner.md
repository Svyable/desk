# The Sky Without an Owner

Near-Earth space had users before it had mature rules.

That sequence is normal for frontiers and dangerous for commons. Technical capability arrives first because capability has a budget, a sponsor, and a mission. Governance arrives later because governance requires agreement among actors who do not share the same mission.

West Ford made the lag visible. The United States could launch the experiment. Astronomers in other countries could object. Governments could debate the precedent. No single institution had obvious authority to decide whether hundreds of millions of passive scatterers constituted an acceptable use of orbital space.

The legal framework that would become the 1967 Outer Space Treaty was still forming. Later historical work has connected the controversy around West Ford to broader efforts to establish consultation norms and principles for activities that could affect other users of space. The causal chain should not be overstated: treaties emerge from many pressures, and West Ford was one episode among nuclear testing, reconnaissance, sovereignty disputes, scientific cooperation, and superpower competition. But it supplied a concrete case in which environmental modification and scientific access could no longer be treated as abstractions.

The phrase freedom of space contains its own conflict. Freedom to use a domain can become freedom to alter it in ways that constrain others. The first user experiences openness. The hundredth user experiences congestion.

West Ford arrived when orbital scarcity still sounded absurd. The needles occupied negligible physical volume in any ordinary sense. Yet the controversy anticipated a future in which visibility, radio quiet, collision risk, debris persistence, and spectrum would all become shared constraints.

A commons is not defined only by crowding. It is defined by interdependence among uses.

Astronomers did not need to own orbit for West Ford to matter to them. A military communications experiment could affect their measurements. Other countries did not need to own the belt's orbital altitude for the experiment to become diplomatic. The relevant fact was coupling.

This is why ownership language has always been insufficient in space. A satellite can be national property while its effects propagate through a domain nobody owns. Light crosses borders. Radio emissions cross borders. Debris crosses borders. Orbital shells are physical trajectories, not fenced parcels.

West Ford forced the early space age to confront the difference between owning an object and altering a shared condition.

We are still working on that sentence.
