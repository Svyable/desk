# Back Matter

## Working chronology

1958 — Walter E. Morrow and colleagues at MIT Lincoln Laboratory develop the orbital dipole-scatter concept that becomes Project West Ford.

October 21, 1961 — First West Ford launch attempt; deployment does not produce the intended dispersed belt.

1961–1963 — Astronomical and international criticism intensifies; scientific organizations press for review and safeguards around experiments affecting the space environment.

July 9, 1962 — Starfish Prime high-altitude nuclear test alters the near-Earth radiation environment and damages satellites, shaping the political context in which later American space-environment assurances are heard.

May 1963 — Second West Ford experiment successfully deploys a large dipole population. The belt forms over subsequent weeks.

1963–1964 — Communications experiments demonstrate useful microwave scatter performance between California and Massachusetts. Scientific organizations assess optical and radio interference.

1964 — Proceedings of the IEEE publishes an historical perspective on the West Ford program. Physics Today reports the Space Science Board conclusion that the experiment had not significantly interfered with optical or radio astronomy.

By early 1966 — Lincoln Laboratory later reports that the main dipole belt has largely cleaned itself from orbit.

1960s onward — Active communications satellites supersede passive orbital scatter as the practical military and civil architecture.

1967 — Outer Space Treaty enters the legal history of space governance. West Ford is one of several contemporary controversies later historians examine when tracing consultation and shared-use norms.

## Evidence classes

Class 1 — verified/declassified/documented: Project West Ford existed; MIT Lincoln Laboratory conducted the work for U.S. national-security communications purposes; the 1961 deployment failed; the May 1963 deployment substantially succeeded; the belt supported measured communications experiments; official Lincoln Laboratory history reports peak data rates up to about 20 kbps; astronomers objected; contemporary scientific bodies reviewed interference; active satellites superseded the architecture.

Class 2 — credible but incomplete: exact internal military decision chains; the relative weight of cable vulnerability, ionospheric disruption, jamming, and nuclear-effects concerns in specific procurement decisions; detailed diplomatic negotiations attributable directly to West Ford; complete inventory and lifetime distribution of all residual clumps from both launch attempts.

Class 3 — disputed/unconfirmed: claims that West Ford created a permanently dangerous ring around Earth; claims that the experiment measurably ruined astronomy; claims assigning any particular surviving catalog object to a specific needle population without orbital-catalog support; claims that the project alone caused specific Outer Space Treaty language.

Class 4 — speculative synthesis: West Ford as an early prototype of orbital-commons politics; its architectural resemblance to modern abundance-based networks; the idea that governability helped active satellites become the more institutionally legible architecture; the proposed use of West Ford as a design test for future sky-changing experiments.

## Contradiction and incentive audit

MIT Lincoln Laboratory has an institutional incentive to remember West Ford as successful technical innovation. Its histories are strongest for engineering chronology and measured performance and should not be treated as neutral accounts of political controversy.

Astronomical critics had legitimate scientific and institutional interests in preserving observing conditions. Contemporary warnings sometimes discussed future permanent belts and improved detector sensitivity, which should not be retrospectively rewritten as measurements of damage from the actual temporary 1963 belt.

Cold War U.S. officials had strategic and reputational incentives to emphasize temporariness, predictability, and military necessity. Soviet criticism had incentives to portray American space activity as reckless. Neither propaganda context determines the underlying physical claims.

Modern space-environment advocates may use West Ford as an ancestor of constellation controversies. The analogy is historically productive but mechanically limited. Passive dipoles and active maneuverable satellites require separate impact models.
