# Walter Morrow's Strange Redundancy

Walter E. Morrow is easy to reduce to the man who wanted to put needles in space. That is unfair to the engineering problem and also to the weirdness of his answer.

The West Ford concept emerged at MIT Lincoln Laboratory in the late 1950s, when the laboratory was already entangled with radar, air defense, communications, and the fast-growing technical demands of the Cold War. Morrow's proposal treated orbit not primarily as a place for machines but as a place for a medium.

That is a subtler distinction than it sounds. A machine has parts whose failure can be localized. A medium can be robust because it is diffuse. The ocean does not stop being an ocean because one cubic kilometer becomes inaccessible. A radio-reflecting belt made of enormous numbers of passive elements offered the same seduction. Destroy some of it, and the rest remains. Jam one path, and geometry may still provide another. Remove power, and there is no onboard electronics package to fail because nothing onboard requires power.

Cold War systems engineering was full of redundancy, but West Ford made redundancy material. It was not two transmitters instead of one. It was abundance as infrastructure.

That choice also moved cost and risk out of the obvious place. A powered satellite concentrates engineering complexity in the spacecraft. West Ford transferred some complexity to deployment, orbital dynamics, terminal antennas, tracking, and the consequences of dispersion. The individual element became wonderfully simple because the system around it became strange.

This tradeoff would later recur throughout technology. Make the edge cheap and numerous, then pay for coordination. Distribute the function, then discover that the distribution has become an environment. The vocabulary changes—swarms, constellations, sensor networks—but the architectural temptation is stable.

West Ford is useful because it lets us inspect that temptation before the modern language arrived. The tiny wire had no software. It could not be hacked. It could not misbehave in the contemporary sense. Yet the system could still produce externalities, diplomatic friction, scientific interference concerns, uncertain cleanup, and governance problems at planetary scale.

The absence of intelligence in each component did not make the aggregate simple.

Morrow's redundancy was technically serious precisely because it refused elegance. It imagined reliability emerging from quantity and geometry. The same idea now appears in orbital constellations whose individual satellites are replaceable enough that the network, not the spacecraft, is the real product.

The West Ford needles were not proto-Starlink satellites. That analogy becomes misleading if pushed. But they belong to the same architectural family: orbital abundance used to turn a fragile link into an infrastructural layer.

The difference is that Morrow's layer was almost literally a layer.
