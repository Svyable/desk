# The Copper Sky

## Project West Ford, Artificial Ionospheres, and the First Fight Over Who Gets to Engineer Orbit

Sven Hardy Benson

In 1963 the United States put hundreds of millions of hair-thin copper dipoles into orbit. They were not an accident. They were the communications medium.

The experiment was called Project West Ford. Its premise was severe and, by Cold War standards, not irrational: long-distance military communications depended heavily on submarine cables and an ionosphere that could be disturbed by solar activity, jammed, or altered by nuclear war. MIT Lincoln Laboratory proposed a third path. If nature's ionosphere could not be trusted, perhaps a thin artificial belt of resonant metal could provide a passive, distributed reflector no single enemy target could destroy.

The strange part is not that anyone imagined this. The strange part is that it worked.

Lincoln Laboratory's own history says the 1963 belt supported data rates as high as 20,000 bits per second while dense enough for useful scatter communication. The stranger part came next. Active communications satellites improved so quickly that West Ford became obsolete almost as soon as its technical case had been demonstrated.

That would already make a good engineering history. But the needles also provoked astronomers, international criticism, questions about contamination, and an argument that has become ordinary only because sixty years have passed: does a nation have the right to alter a shared orbital environment merely because it can?

This book follows that collision. It treats West Ford neither as proof of American recklessness nor as a misunderstood triumph. It was a serious military communications experiment, a technically real system, a governance shock, and an early demonstration that the environmental consequences of infrastructure do not wait for the infrastructure to become commercially successful.

The historical record is uneven. Where official MIT, NASA, scientific-journal, and archival records are available, I use them. Where later accounts disagree about quantities, debris persistence, diplomatic influence, or institutional intent, I mark the uncertainty rather than smooth it away. The purpose is not to make the past cleaner. West Ford matters because it was messy in exactly the way modern orbital systems are messy: technically impressive, strategically motivated, globally externalized, and hard to judge with a single metric.
