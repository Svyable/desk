# The Debris That Was Supposed to Leave

West Ford's designers expected the dispersed dipoles to leave orbit. That expectation was not invented after criticism; decay was part of the safety case.

The physics favored removal for isolated wires with extreme area-to-mass ratios. Solar radiation pressure could alter their orbits until atmospheric drag finished the job. Lincoln Laboratory's historical account says the useful belt thinned progressively and that by early 1966 the removal process was almost complete.

Almost is where environmental history begins.

Some material did not behave as ideal isolated dipoles. Clumps associated with imperfect dispersion could have very different area-to-mass ratios and therefore different orbital lifetimes. Modern catalogs have tracked objects associated with the West Ford launches long after the operational experiment ended, though historical accounts vary in how confidently individual remnants are attributed and how the surviving population should be characterized.

This is an important place not to dramatize beyond the evidence. West Ford did not leave hundreds of millions of intact needles circling Earth indefinitely. Most of the intended belt dispersed and decayed. Nor does the existence of cataloged remnants by itself demonstrate significant present hazard.

But the gap between expected removal and long-tail persistence matters.

Environmental forecasts are distributions disguised as sentences. The sentence says the material will decay in a few years. The distribution contains deployment failures, clumps, outliers, modeling assumptions, and the possibility that a small fraction behaves unlike the nominal case.

For a system containing an enormous number of elements, tails deserve attention. If one in a million behaves unexpectedly, the phrase one in a million describes hundreds of objects.

Space debris engineering now formalizes concerns that West Ford encountered in primitive form: post-mission disposal, lifetime, trackability, fragmentation, collision probability, conjunction assessment. The modern apparatus is more sophisticated because the domain is more crowded and the objects are far more consequential.

Yet the old lesson survives. Cleanup promises must be evaluated at the population level, not only by the expected behavior of one representative component.

The copper sky mostly cleaned itself out.

Mostly was good enough for the communications experiment. It is not a universal standard for changing a shared environment.
