# Starfish in the Background

Project West Ford entered orbit less than a year after Starfish Prime.

On July 9, 1962, the United States detonated a high-altitude nuclear device over the Pacific. The test produced electromagnetic effects and injected energetic particles into Earth's magnetosphere. Several satellites were damaged or degraded in the aftermath. The event became a spectacular demonstration that near-Earth space could be altered by military action in ways that crossed program boundaries.

West Ford was not Starfish. Copper dipoles were not an artificial radiation belt. The mechanisms, risks, and intended purposes were completely different.

But politically they shared a sky.

For astronomers and foreign critics, assurances about a temporary communications belt arrived after a recent demonstration that the United States could underestimate the persistence and consequences of a large space-environment experiment. Contemporary criticism often placed the two episodes in the same moral frame even when the technical comparison was poor.

This is how trust works in technological governance. Risk assessments are evaluated not only against equations but against the recent error history of the institution presenting them.

Engineers sometimes regard that as unfair contamination. A new program should be judged on its own physics. True. But the credibility assigned to uncertain predictions necessarily depends on whether the forecasting institution has recently been surprised.

Starfish therefore mattered to West Ford without becoming evidence about West Ford's dipole dynamics. It changed the prior on institutional confidence.

That distinction is useful for every controversial classified or national-security technology. Adjacent secrecy and adjacent failure can rationally alter scrutiny without proving a specific allegation. We should neither erase context nor let context substitute for mechanism.

The temptation to merge stories is strong because narratives want coherence. A government explodes a nuclear weapon in space, then places hundreds of millions of wires into orbit: the montage writes itself. Science has to separate them again. What particles? What lifetime? What interaction? What observed harm? What prediction failed?

Epistemic discipline is not the refusal to see patterns. It is the refusal to let a pattern do work that only evidence can do.

West Ford survived that discipline better than its reputation suggests. Its predicted main belt decay was broadly real. Its astronomical interference was less severe than feared. Its political controversy was still legitimate.

The sky remembers programs separately even when history shelves them together.
