# The Vacuum Engineers

## Hal Puthoff, Zero-Point Energy, and the Physics at the Edge of Empty Space

Sven Hardy Benson

### Author's note

This book is about a dangerous kind of intellectual border: the place where a real physical effect acquires a technological mythology before anyone has built the machine.

The quantum vacuum is not nothing. Casimir forces are measured. Vacuum polarization is part of modern quantum electrodynamics. General relativity permits mathematically exotic geometries. Those are facts. They do not, by themselves, give us a propulsion system, a free-energy machine, a traversable wormhole, a warp drive, or a laboratory method for changing inertia.

Hal Puthoff is useful precisely because his career crosses the border repeatedly. He published on stochastic electrodynamics and zero-point fields, proposed a polarizable-vacuum representation of gravity, wrote about metric engineering, and later authored a Defense Intelligence Reference Document on advanced space propulsion based on vacuum engineering. Around that record accumulated stronger claims: vacuum-energy extraction, inertia modification, antigravity, and technological interpretations of unidentified aerospace phenomena. Some are published hypotheses. Some are disputed extrapolations. Some are folklore.

The method here is simple and intentionally unfriendly to romance. Every claim is assigned to one of four classes: documented fact; credible but incomplete evidence; disputed allegation; speculative synthesis. A government-funded report proves that a government funded a report. A paper proves that an argument entered the literature. A patent proves filing. A Casimir experiment proves a force in a specified geometry. None of those facts are silently promoted into an engine.

The wager of the book is that the fringe becomes more interesting, not less, when the paperwork and the physics are forced to occupy the same room.
