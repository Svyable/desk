# Who Gets Asked Next Time

The most useful question Fernald leaves us is not whether we would do Fernald again.

Of course we would not do it the same way.

No modern university is likely to send a parent a two-paragraph letter about cereal and blood tests while omitting the radioactive tracer, recruit institutionalized boys with outings, and treat silence as permission under a protocol that has passed contemporary pediatric review. Declaring victory over that exact scenario requires very little moral imagination.

The harder question is where convenience is becoming invisible now.

Who lives in a system that can be experimentally controlled?

Who has data that already exist?

Who cannot easily leave?

Whose refusal costs access to care, work, status, freedom, education, or attention?

Which technical detail do experts currently consider too harmless or too confusing to disclose?

Which population receives the burdens because it is assembled, measurable, compliant, or cheap?

Those questions are Fernald's surviving instrument.

The modern research environment is in many ways safer. Institutional review boards, federal regulations, pediatric protections, consent rules, adverse-event monitoring, privacy law, trial registration, and professional norms create checks that the boys at Fernald did not have. It would be foolish to write as though nothing changed.

It would be equally foolish to assume bureaucracy solved power.

Forms can become ritual. Review boards can miss social pressure. Commercial platforms can conduct experiments outside traditional biomedical institutions. Data research can affect people without touching their bodies. International studies can migrate toward jurisdictions where recruitment is cheaper. Gene and cell therapies can place desperate patients in relationships where hope overwhelms uncertainty. Artificial-intelligence systems can experiment on attention at enormous scale.

The surface changes.

Convenience remains recognizable.

Fernald offers four tests.

First, restore the omitted noun.

What fact would change how an ordinary participant describes the study? At Fernald it was radioactive. In another context it may be genomic, military-funded, commercially sponsored, behaviorally manipulative, permanently stored, linked to identity, or shared with law enforcement. The expert's belief that a fact is low-risk does not settle whether the participant considers it material.

Second, price the reward in the subject's world.

A benefit cannot be evaluated outside deprivation. Fifty dollars means something different to different people. Access to a physician may be more powerful than cash. A day outside may be more powerful than either if the institution controls movement.

Third, ask what happens after no.

This may be the best test of voluntariness. Does the person lose care? Anger a supervisor? Miss a privilege? Disappoint an authority? Face repeated persuasion? Become less eligible for something else? If refusal is formally allowed but institutionally punished, the consent form is describing the wrong system.

Fourth, ask why this population.

Scientific necessity is a strong answer.

Convenience is an answer that requires more work.

These tests are not a grand ethical framework. They are questions forced on us by a specific archive.

That is how historical cases should be used. Not as morality plays that prove our superiority, but as stress tests for present habits.

The boys of the Science Club do not need us to exaggerate their dose. They do not need a claim that every scientist intended to deceive them. They do not need a secret weapons program hidden inside the oatmeal.

The documented case is enough.

Seventeen boys in 1946.

Fifty-seven in the calcium series from 1950 to 1953.

Letters to parents that did not mention the radioisotope.

A Science Club offering milk, baseball, beach trips, and dinners.

A researcher discussing how boys who objected might be induced to change their minds.

A later federal review concluding that physical radiation harm was extremely unlikely and that the ethical problems remained serious.

That final pairing is the part worth carrying forward.

The body can emerge uninjured from an experiment while the person has still been treated wrongly.

Science learned something from those breakfasts.

We should too.
