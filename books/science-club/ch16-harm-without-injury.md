# Harm Without Injury

The easiest lawsuit injury to understand is a damaged body.

A burn. A tumor. A fracture. A poisoned organ.

Fernald forces a more difficult idea: a person can be wronged even when the challenged experiment probably did not cause physical injury.

This distinction is not sentimental. It is built into ordinary law and ethics. Unauthorized touching can matter even if no wound results. Privacy can be violated without bodily damage. Deception can wrong a person even when the lie does not shorten a life. Taking control of another person's body for an undisclosed purpose is not made ethically neutral by competent execution.

Research ethics sometimes loses this point because risk assessment is so central.

Risk is measurable enough to organize review. How likely is harm? How severe? Is the risk minimal? Is there direct benefit? Can risk be reduced? These are essential questions.

But if ethics becomes only actuarial, the subject disappears into expected value.

Suppose the Fernald radiation dose had been exactly zero through some hypothetical perfect tracer. The experiment could still have been wrong if children were recruited unfairly, guardians were misled about purpose, and objections were overridden through dependency.

The moral claim does not require ionizing radiation at all.

This is why the federal committee's low-dose conclusion should be placed near the center of the book rather than buried defensively in an appendix. It prevents the reader from using injury as a shortcut.

Former subjects later described anger, betrayal, and uncertainty after learning what the Science Club had involved. The uncertainty itself has a peculiar cost. Once a person discovers that a hidden exposure occurred, later illnesses can become psychologically entangled with it even when causation is scientifically unlikely. Every symptom acquires a possible origin.

That does not prove the exposure caused the symptom.

It shows why concealment has a long half-life.

A person deprived of information at the time may spend decades trying to reconstruct a risk that could have been disclosed in a sentence.

The resulting distrust can spread beyond the original institution. If prestigious universities, state officials, and federal programs once participated in ethically deficient research, later assurances from similar institutions face a credibility tax.

Public-health systems encounter that tax whenever they ask communities to trust technical risk estimates.

This is not an argument that mistrust is always scientifically justified. A historical wrong does not make a modern vaccine unsafe or a current radiation standard false. It means trust cannot be demanded as though history never happened.

Institutions often respond to scandal by emphasizing that nobody was physically harmed. Scientifically, that may be exactly the right correction to exaggerated claims. Morally, it can sound like evasion if the complaint is about being used without adequate disclosure.

The better response holds both propositions at once.

The available evidence indicates the doses were low and unlikely to have caused physical harm.

The consent and selection practices were still deficient.

That combination is not contradictory.

It is the point.

The distinction also matters for compensation. Legal systems prefer injuries that can be causally linked and monetized. Ethical wrongs are harder. How much is an undisclosed low-dose experiment worth? How does one value being selected because one's institutional circumstances made resistance easier? What is the monetary equivalent of decades-late discovery?

Settlements answer these questions pragmatically, not philosophically.

They end disputes.

Fernald's later class settlement should therefore not be treated as a scientific verdict that radiation caused disease. Nor should the existence of a settlement be dismissed as mere nuisance payment. It belongs to another category: institutional response to contested legal claims grounded in a documented ethical history.

This four-part separation is worth preserving.

Exposure is one question.

Physical injury is another.

Ethical wrong is another.

Legal liability is another.

Popular retellings routinely collapse them.

A serious book cannot.
