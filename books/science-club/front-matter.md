# Science Club

*Radioactive Tracers, Fernald, and the Children Chosen for Convenience*

Sven Hardy Benson

There are stories from the atomic age that arrive already dressed as horror stories. This one does not need the costume.

The boys were not strapped beneath a reactor. Nobody needed to tell them a bomb might go off. The doses used in the Fernald nutrition studies were low. The federal committee that later reconstructed the work judged it extremely unlikely that the children were physically harmed by the radioactive iron and calcium they received. If the only moral instrument available is a Geiger counter, that fact can make the story look smaller.

It makes it larger.

A low dose strips away the easiest argument. It leaves the machinery exposed: who was available, who was convenient, who could say no, what a parent was told, what a child understood, what a scientist believed counted as negligible, and how a respectable institution could translate dependency into experimental efficiency.

In 1946, seventeen young male residents of the Walter E. Fernald State School were exposed to radioactive iron in a nutrition study. Between 1950 and 1953, fifty-seven boys took part in a series of calcium-tracer experiments. They belonged to something called the Science Club. The name is almost too neat for a book, which is why I have kept it.

The point here is not to turn every researcher into a villain from the future. That is lazy history and bad ethics. The point is to reconstruct the decision in its own vocabulary and then ask which parts of that vocabulary deserve to survive.

A parent can sign a form and still not have been told the thing that would have mattered to the decision. A child can volunteer when the available rewards reveal how little he normally receives. A dose can be medically small while the selection of the subject is morally enormous.

Fernald is a book about radioactive tracers. More than that, it is a book about convenience.
