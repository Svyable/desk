# 6. Time Without Generations

A bacterium dividing every twenty minutes teaches you almost nothing about the temporal meaning of life.

It teaches you what one lineage can do in abundance.

The deep biosphere is interesting because abundance disappears. Carbon is scarce. Electron acceptors arrive slowly. Hydrogen leaks out of rock at rates measured against geological processes. Temperatures can be high enough to increase maintenance costs while transport remains miserly. In some deep marine sediments, estimates of biomass turnover have stretched toward hundreds or thousands of years.

That is not one cell patiently waiting ten centuries and then splitting on a Tuesday. Population-level turnover estimates integrate complicated mixtures of active cells, dormant cells, death, repair, cryptic cycling and local hotspots. But the existence of ecosystems operating at such low average power forces a biological question we rarely need on the surface.

What is the minimum continuous work required to remain alive?

A cell is an unstable arrangement of matter. Proteins misfold. DNA suffers damage. Membranes leak. Ion gradients dissipate. Reactive chemistry happens whether metabolism is convenient or not. If a cell stops all work, thermodynamics eventually collects it.

So the lower energy limit for life is not zero.

The difficult region is between obvious growth and obvious death. A cell can allocate energy to maintenance rather than reproduction. It can reduce transcription, repair selectively, recycle damaged components, change membrane composition, form a spore or enter another dormant state. Different lineages solve scarcity differently.

The surface bias appears again in our instruments. Many assays are optimized to detect growth or metabolic rates large enough to measure over days. Deep life may force experiments lasting months or years to distinguish a slow signal from background. That creates an institutional problem as much as a technical one. Grant cycles, student projects and laboratory schedules are not designed for organisms that refuse to hurry.

This mismatch can generate two opposite errors.

The first is to call something dead because it does not grow on our schedule.

The second is to call something alive because a molecule associated with life persists after metabolism has ceased.

ATP turns over. RNA usually degrades faster than DNA. Membrane lipids can preserve signatures. Isotope incorporation can reveal assimilation. But none is a universal life meter. The strongest studies combine methods and ask mechanism-specific questions.

The deep biosphere also complicates evolution. Natural selection requires differential reproduction. If generation times are exceptionally long, evolutionary change through ordinary replication proceeds slowly. Yet geological time is generous. A lineage dividing once per century still experiences ten million generations in a billion years. Slow is not static.

Dormancy further scrambles the arithmetic. A spore can preserve a lineage through long intervals and then re-enter active metabolism. Horizontal gene transfer can import functions from elsewhere. Periodic fracture opening can suddenly deliver nutrients to a community that spent centuries near maintenance.

The environment may therefore be better described as pulsed than uniformly starved.

This matters when interpreting genomic homogeneity in a system like *Desulforudis audaxviator*. Low diversity is compatible with slow turnover, but it can also reflect bottlenecks, selection or colonization history. The genome does not contain a simple clock for how often the cells divided in that particular fracture.

The fascination with “million-year-old microbes” often comes from collapsing this distinction. Scientists have reported revival claims from ancient salt crystals, ice and sediments, some convincing, some disputed, some vulnerable to contamination. The age of the enclosing material is never enough. A microbe could enter later through microfractures. DNA can move. Fluids can migrate. A sample can be contaminated after collection.

The correct question is not “how old is the microbe?” but “what evidence constrains the last biological exchange between this population and another?”

That is much harder.

Life is an ongoing process, not a dated artifact. A lineage can be ancient without any living individual being ancient. A population can occupy old water while continuously replacing itself. A spore can be individually old but metabolically almost absent for most of that interval.

The deep biosphere thus erodes one of the simplest cultural metaphors for life: life as activity.

An oak tree is visibly doing things. A bird is doing things faster. A bacterium in broth turns the medium cloudy overnight. We infer life from motion, consumption, reproduction and response.

What if the dominant mode of life in the universe is maintenance?

Imagine a cold rocky planet whose surface became hostile two billion years ago. A deep aquifer remains liquid. Radiolysis and water-rock chemistry supply a trickle of hydrogen. A sparse microbial population repairs itself, divides rarely and leaves no atmospheric signature. It could outlast forests, oceans and civilizations while being almost impossible to detect remotely.

That scenario is speculative. Its components are not.

Earth already contains organisms living at power densities that make surface life look wasteful. The question is how far the curve can be pushed before chemistry is merely preserving remnants rather than sustaining metabolism.

That boundary is experimentally difficult because life near the edge looks like noise.

And noise is where contamination lives too.

The deepest lesson may be that the search for minimal life requires maximal methodological discipline. The slower the organism, the more patient the experiment must become. The weaker the signal, the more aggressively the researcher must measure every background source.

A century-scale cell is not miraculous.

It is expensive to prove.
