# 8. Stone Eating Stone

There is a category error hiding in the phrase “microbes eat rock.”

They do not chew granite.

They exploit reactions at mineral surfaces. They transfer electrons. They alter pH. They excrete ligands. They precipitate minerals, dissolve minerals, change oxidation states and modify the microscopic environment where water meets stone. The rock is not dinner. It is reactant, scaffold, battery, shelter and archive.

This distinction matters because geomicrobiology lives in the borderland where biological and geological causation become difficult to separate.

Iron is a good example. Microorganisms can oxidize ferrous iron or reduce ferric iron. But iron also changes oxidation state abiotically. Sulfur cycles biologically and geochemically. Methane can be microbial, thermogenic or abiotic. Carbonate can precipitate because metabolism changed local chemistry or because the fluid simply became supersaturated.

The more ancient the sample, the harder the attribution.

That is why modern deep environments are so useful. They let us watch processes that might leave ambiguous traces in old rocks.

A biofilm in a fracture can concentrate metals, alter isotopes, generate sulfide and change mineral textures. If we understand the process in situ, we gain a library of plausible biosignatures. But “plausible” is not “unique.”

The dream of astrobiology is a biosignature no abiotic process can mimic.

Nature has been uncooperative.

Morphology can fool us. Carbon isotope fractionation can arise through multiple pathways. Methane is not uniquely biological. Organic molecules can form without life. Even complex mineral structures can emerge through self-organization.

The correct strategy is therefore combinatorial.

A single ambiguous signature is weak. Several independent signatures that require the same biological story are stronger. Spatial relationships matter. A chemical anomaly attached to a fracture wall is more informative if microscopy localizes cells there and isotopes show fractionation consistent with the observed metabolism.

Deep biosphere work has another advantage: the habitat itself can preserve the crime scene.

Mineral veins can seal. Fluid inclusions can trap ancient chemistry. Reaction fronts can record where oxidized and reduced fluids met. Secondary minerals can preserve isotopic patterns after cells disappear.

This makes subsurface rock both habitat and archive.

The archive is imperfect. Later fluids overprint earlier signals. Heating resets isotopes. Recrystallization erases textures. Fractures reopen. Organics degrade. Yet in some settings, mineral systems can preserve evidence far longer than loose sediment at a surface exposed to radiation and weather.

That point matters on Mars.

If Mars ever had a deep biosphere, the best evidence might not be a surviving cell. It could be a mineralized fracture with a suite of chemical and isotopic anomalies formed when water still circulated.

Searching such a target requires geological context, not merely a life detector.

The rover must know what vein it is in, what rock it cuts, whether the fracture predates or postdates alteration, what temperatures were involved, and whether abiotic redox processes predict the same pattern.

The deep biosphere therefore collapses the distinction between geology and biology.

A microbiologist alone can misread the rock. A geologist alone can miss the organism.

This is one reason some of the best work in the field feels methodologically dense. Noble gases constrain fluid histories. Stable isotopes constrain reaction pathways. metagenomics identifies metabolic potential. microscopy localizes cells. thermodynamic calculations test whether reactions pay enough energy. mineralogy constrains possible abiotic mechanisms.

Each method is a partial witness.

The phrase “stone eating stone” captures the circularity of the system. Rock-water reactions make hydrogen. Cells use hydrogen and alter sulfur. Sulfide reacts with minerals. New mineral phases change porosity. Porosity changes fluid access. Fluid access changes microbial habitat.

The ecosystem modifies the geology that defines the ecosystem.

At the surface, biology does this loudly. Roots split rock. reefs build limestone. oxygen changes minerals globally. Underground the feedback may be microscopic but persistent.

A billion years of weak effects can be geologically significant.

That possibility invites overreach too. Not every unusual mineral texture is microbial. The burden rises with age because biological alternatives become harder to test directly. Deep-time biosignature claims should therefore be strongest where a proposed mechanism predicts multiple features not easily reproduced abiotically.

This is less satisfying than finding a fossil cell.

It is also how the real problem is shaped.

Life below the surface does not merely occupy geology.

It participates in it.
