# 4. Nuclear Power for a Bacterium

Radioactivity enters biology wearing a black hat.

We associate it with mutation, cancer, sterilization, broken DNA and warning signs on steel doors. All of that is warranted. Ionizing radiation deposits energy violently enough to damage the molecular machinery of life.

But an energy source does not care about our narrative categories.

When radioactive decay occurs in water-bearing rock, the energy can split water molecules. Radiolysis produces reactive species including molecular hydrogen and oxidants. In deep environments where photosynthetic carbon is scarce and fluid circulation is slow, that hydrogen can accumulate. If an organism can pair it with a suitable electron acceptor, radioactive decay has become part of a food web—not by feeding radiation into a cell, but by maintaining chemical disequilibrium in the surrounding environment.

Li-Hung Lin and colleagues quantified this possibility in South African subsurface fracture waters. In work published in 2005, they reported hydrogen concentrations in some deep fracture waters reaching extraordinarily high levels and argued that radiolysis of water by uranium, thorium and potassium decay in the host rock was sufficient to account for the observed hydrogen inventory. Their calculations indicated that radiolytic hydrogen could be a widespread energy source for deep lithoautotrophic communities.

The phrase “nuclear power for microbes” is irresistible and slightly dangerous.

The useful translation is simpler. Radioactive decay is one member of a family of geological processes that can continuously convert stable materials into metabolically useful redox pairs.

Serpentinization can do something analogous through rock-water reactions involving ultramafic minerals. Ferrous iron can be oxidized, hydrogen generated, methane produced abiotically or biologically, sulfur species transformed. The deep subsurface is full of slow batteries.

The battery metaphor is better than the reactor metaphor because it emphasizes gradients.

Life does not need energy in the abstract. It needs a pathway down a free-energy gradient that can be coupled to work. A mixture at equilibrium is chemically boring even if it contains enormous total energy. A tiny disequilibrium, renewed steadily and accessible to enzymes, can be biologically valuable.

This is why energy accounting matters more than lists of ingredients.

Popular habitability discussions often resemble grocery inventories. Water? Check. Carbon? Check. Nitrogen? Check. Sulfur? Check. Minerals? Check. Therefore perhaps life.

But a pantry is not a metabolism.

The relevant question is whether the environment supplies electron donors and acceptors in combinations that yield enough usable free energy, at sufficient rates, under the actual temperature, pressure, pH and concentration conditions, to pay for cellular maintenance and occasional synthesis.

Radiolysis is interesting because it can run without sunlight and without active volcanism. Long-lived radionuclides provide a geologically persistent source. Water and radioactive elements are common planetary materials. The mechanism therefore expands the set of worlds and epochs in which some chemical energy can exist below a hostile surface.

That expansion has limits.

Radiolytic production rates depend on radionuclide abundance, water content, mineral geometry, porosity and the efficiency with which reactive products escape recombination and become available to metabolism. A calculation showing positive energy is not a demonstration of a viable ecosystem. Transport can kill an elegant energy budget. So can salinity. So can temperature. So can a missing electron acceptor.

And life can consume a gradient faster than geology replenishes it.

This is where the deep biosphere forces a useful inversion. Instead of asking how much energy life can obtain, ask how little it can survive on.

Surface organisms generally evolved in environments where fluxes are comparatively generous. Photosynthesis captures an enormous planetary energy stream. Oxygen enables high-yield metabolism. Food arrives in packets. Deep organisms often face the opposite condition: energy is not absent but drips in.

At extremely low power, selection should favor repair, recycling, dormancy, small cell size, slow synthesis and metabolic flexibility. A microbe that divides every few hours in the laboratory is not the relevant benchmark.

That also means laboratory cultivation can lie by omission.

If a subsurface organism is adapted to a world in which energy arrives over years or centuries, dumping it into a nutrient-rich flask may fail to reproduce its ecology even if it grows. Conversely, failure to culture an organism does not imply inactivity in situ. The laboratory can be too rich, too warm, too oxygenated, too fast.

The radiolysis story also carries a caution for Mars.

A 2021 study led by Jesse Tarnas used martian meteorite compositions to estimate whether radiolysis in water-bearing Martian crust could generate enough redox energy to support sulfate-reducing microbes analogous to those in Earth's deep subsurface. Their calculations suggested that, where groundwater exists, some Martian rocks could in principle support substantial microbial densities by terrestrial deep-biosphere standards.

That sentence contains three traps.

First, “could support” is an energy calculation, not a detection.

Second, martian meteorites are samples from particular source regions and histories, not a random census of the entire crust.

Third, the largest uncertainty is not necessarily whether metabolism is energetically possible. It is whether life ever originated on Mars or arrived there, persisted, accessed liquid water, and escaped subsequent extinction.

Physics can give life a budget without giving a planet life.

The distinction matters because habitability research can become a machine for producing false positives in the public imagination. Every time a paper identifies water, organics or chemical energy, the result is translated one rung upward: ingredient becomes habitat, habitat becomes life, life becomes aliens.

The deep biosphere lets us reverse that ladder.

Here on Earth we have actual organisms. We can measure their geochemistry. We can sequence their genomes. We can test contamination. We can calculate energy flux. Even with all of that, the causal chain remains difficult.

That should discipline what we say about a planet we have barely drilled.

Radiation can destroy biology near a surface and help sustain chemistry deeper down. The same physical process can therefore be both enemy and subsidy depending on geometry and dose.

The planet does not care which story is cleaner.
