# 11. The Biomass Nobody Sees

How many cells live beneath the continents?

The honest answer is a range wrapped around a model.

Cara Magnabosco and colleagues assembled cell counts and diversity measurements from continental subsurface sites around the world and, in 2018, estimated roughly 2 to 6 × 10^29 cells. They used heat flow, depth, surface temperature and lithology to estimate the volume that could remain within known thermal limits for life. Their work also helped revise global prokaryotic biomass estimates downward from older, much larger numbers.

That history is important because it shows science behaving correctly.

Discovery inflated the plausible world. Better measurement then cut it down.

A hidden biosphere invites extrapolation because almost all of it is unsampled. Boreholes are expensive. Mines are geographically biased toward ore. Oil and gas wells target particular formations. Ocean drilling samples narrow cylinders through an enormous seafloor. The database is therefore not a random survey of Earth.

Any global number must carry those biases into the model.

The question is not whether the estimate is “right” in a final sense. It is whether the assumptions are explicit enough to improve.

This distinction is often lost when biomass numbers become rhetoric. “There are more cells underground than…” makes a good factoid, but the interesting scientific content lies in how a few liters of fracture water become a planetary estimate.

You need a depth distribution. A thermal cutoff. Assumptions about rock porosity. Decisions about whether to include sediments and aquifers. A conversion from cells to carbon. Treatment of regions with no data. Correction for methodological differences in cell counting.

Each choice can move the answer.

Yet even a broad range has conceptual force.

If the continental subsurface contains on the order of 10^29 microbial cells, deep life is not a curiosity at the margin of the biosphere. It is a major planetary reservoir of biological matter, even if its total metabolic throughput is slow.

This decoupling between abundance and activity is easy to miss.

A rainforest may contain less total microbial biomass than an enormous subsurface volume yet transform carbon far faster per unit time. A deep community can be massive in aggregate while each cell operates near maintenance power.

The planet therefore has at least two biological tempos layered on top of each other.

Fast surface cycling responds to seasons, weather, fires, blooms and predation. Slow subsurface cycling responds to groundwater movement, burial, fracture opening, radioactive decay, mineral alteration and tectonic history.

The tempos interact, but not symmetrically.

Surface carbon can be buried into deep sediments. Deep methane can rise. Groundwater can transport cells and metabolites. Hydrothermal circulation connects crust and ocean. But some fracture systems remain isolated enough that local geochemistry dominates for long periods.

This partial isolation is what makes the deep biosphere relevant to planetary resilience.

If surface photosynthesis stopped tomorrow, much deep life would eventually feel the consequences because electron acceptors, organic matter and hydrology are connected across scales. But not every community would stop on the same schedule. Radiolytic and water-rock energy sources could continue.

The biosphere would not turn off as one machine.

That matters for interpreting mass extinctions too. Surface catastrophes erase macroscopic diversity dramatically. Microbial refugia belowground may survive impacts, radiation events and climate swings that devastate the surface. This does not mean the subsurface is an untouched ark. Heat pulses, anoxia changes, groundwater chemistry and nutrient collapse propagate downward. But depth provides physical buffering.

The deep biosphere may therefore be less a separate biosphere than the planet's biological flywheel.

A flywheel stores momentum not by doing more work but by changing more slowly.

This metaphor should not become a claim that deep microbes reseed every extinction. Evidence for specific recolonization pathways is case-dependent. The point is structural: a spatially dispersed, shielded microbial reservoir reduces the chance that surface catastrophe equals total sterilization.

For exoplanets, that is bad news for remote detection.

A world can maintain a significant biosphere below ground and present little atmospheric evidence. Oxygen can disappear while anaerobic communities persist. Methane production may be too small or too efficiently consumed to alter the sky.

The absence of a remote biosignature does not imply sterility.

It may imply the biosphere is somewhere the telescope cannot see.
