# 12. The Repository Has Residents

If you want to know whether engineers believe in the deep biosphere, look at what they are willing to insure against for one hundred thousand years.

High-level nuclear waste disposal is one of the few human projects forced to take geological time literally. The basic strategy in countries pursuing deep geological repositories is to isolate spent fuel or vitrified waste hundreds of meters underground behind multiple barriers: waste form, metal canister, swelling clay or other engineered buffer, and host rock.

Then biology arrives and refuses to stay in its lane.

Microorganisms can influence corrosion, gas production, redox conditions, mineral alteration and the chemistry of radionuclides. They can consume sulfate, produce sulfide, reduce metals, form biofilms and modify interfaces between canister, bentonite and groundwater.

This does not mean microbes will eat through a repository.

It means a safety case that ignores microbiology is incomplete.

Sweden's spent-fuel program has studied microbial activity in granitic groundwater and bentonite because sulfate-reducing bacteria can produce sulfide, which matters for copper corrosion in the KBS-3 disposal concept. Bentonite is designed partly to suppress microbial activity by swelling to low pore spaces and low water activity around the canister. The engineering question is not whether bacteria exist somewhere in the rock. It is whether repository conditions permit enough metabolically active cells, in the relevant location, for enough time, to change corrosion rates materially.

That is a deep-biosphere question translated into regulatory language.

The translation is useful because it forces quantitative humility.

A microbiology paper can report that a lineage is present. A safety assessment asks how much sulfide it can produce per unit time, how transport works through compacted clay, what substrate limits metabolism, how temperature evolves after emplacement, whether corrosion products inhibit or stimulate activity, and whether the effect is already bounded by conservative engineering assumptions.

Presence becomes flux.

This is exactly the discipline astrobiology needs.

A habitat can contain cells without those cells controlling chemistry. A genome can encode sulfate reduction without sulfate reduction dominating the local sulfur budget. An organism can survive without reproducing. The repository engineer does not care that a microbe is philosophically interesting. The engineer cares whether it changes failure probability.

Recent laboratory work continues to probe microbiologically influenced corrosion under repository-relevant conditions. Experiments with bentonite-associated communities can accelerate corrosion of steel under some nutrient-rich or flow-through conditions and identify sulfate reducers, fermenters and nitrate reducers associated with localized attack. But translating an accelerated laboratory system to a sealed repository is not automatic. Nutrient replenishment, hydrology, temperature, material choice and density of compacted clay can differ drastically.

This is the distinction between demonstrating a mechanism and demonstrating operational significance.

The deep biosphere is full of mechanisms that are real and effects that remain context-dependent.

Repository science also turns “life independent of the surface” into a practical accounting problem. A microbe hundreds of meters underground may depend on carbon introduced during construction. Tunnels bring oxygen. Concrete changes pH. Steel supplies a reactive surface. Excavation alters groundwater flow. Human engineering creates a new ecosystem.

The repository is therefore not dropped into a static natural biosphere.

It perturbs one.

That perturbation can awaken metabolisms that were previously substrate-limited or suppress them through desiccation and heat. Early oxic conditions evolve toward reducing conditions. Corrosion produces hydrogen. Radiation changes chemistry. Bentonite swells. Groundwater slowly re-enters excavated spaces.

The microbiology is transient over decades and centuries before it becomes long-term geology.

This is a rare place where human design and deep-time ecology meet in a controlled argument.

The safety case must distinguish verified microbial capabilities from speculative worst cases. It must bound uncertainty rather than narrate it.

That mindset helps with every exotic subsurface claim.

Can microbes corrode metal? Yes.

Will they corrode *this* canister, at *this* rate, through *this* barrier, under *this* hydrology? Different question.

Can radiolysis generate hydrogen? Yes.

Does radiolysis supply enough accessible hydrogen to sustain *this* observed population? Different question.

Can Mars rock generate metabolic energy? In some models, yes.

Does Mars contain life? Different question.

The repository has residents because rock has always had residents.

The unusual thing is that, for once, we are forced to include them in a calculation spanning longer than recorded history.
