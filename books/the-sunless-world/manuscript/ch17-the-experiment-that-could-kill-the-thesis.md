# 17. The Experiment That Could Kill the Thesis

A good strange idea should arrive with instructions for how to disappoint it.

The thesis of this book has several parts, and each deserves a separate failure condition.

First: significant portions of Earth's subsurface host indigenous microbial life rather than contamination. This is already strongly established in many environments through converging microscopy, cultivation, molecular data, geochemistry, tracer-controlled sampling and repeated observation. Killing the global claim would require showing that major classes of supposed deep communities systematically collapse under contamination-aware resampling.

Second: some deep ecosystems can be powered primarily by geologic energy sources with little dependence on contemporary photosynthesis. This is supported strongly in radiolytic and serpentinizing systems, but “primarily” is a budget word. The clean test is to close the electron and carbon balances.

Third: these systems materially expand the range of plausible extraterrestrial habitats. This is an analogy, not an observation. It can be killed locally by showing that a target world's chemistry, water availability, temperature or transport cannot reproduce the needed free-energy flux.

The best next terrestrial experiment would combine all three burdens in one place.

Choose multiple deep crystalline-rock sites with old fracture waters and contrasting radionuclide abundances. Drill with redundant contamination tracers: fluorescent particles, perfluorocarbon compounds and DNA barcodes absent from nature. Use equipment sterilized and sampled at every stage. Preserve cores without exposing interiors to ordinary laboratory air.

Then split the experiment across independent laboratories before anyone sees the biological result.

One team measures geochemistry: noble gases, H2, methane, sulfate, sulfide, iron species, dissolved inorganic carbon, organic carbon, redox potential, pH, temperature and mineralogy.

Another maps cells spatially by microscopy and isotope imaging.

A third performs metagenomics and metatranscriptomics with extraction blanks, reagent controls and synthetic standards.

A fourth measures metabolic flux using isotopically labeled substrates at in situ pressure and temperature without nutrient enrichment.

No team gets to rescue another team's hypothesis after seeing the outcome.

Pre-register the causal model.

If radiolysis is sustaining the community, sites with higher modeled radiolytic production—holding transport and electron acceptors appropriately constrained—should show predictable differences in H2 flux and in the energetics of metabolisms capable of consuming it. Isotopic incorporation should localize activity to uncontaminated interior fractures. Genomes should encode pathways consistent with the observed chemistry. Metabolic rates should fit within the measured energy budget rather than exceed it by orders of magnitude.

Now specify failure.

If cells track contamination tracer penetration rather than native fractures, the biological interpretation fails.

If supposed radiolytic communities consume labeled organic carbon at rates requiring surface-derived flux far larger than measured, the independence claim fails.

If H2 concentrations are best explained by drilling-induced steel corrosion, the radiolysis interpretation fails for that site.

If geochemical energy models predict orders of magnitude less power than measured maintenance requirements, the proposed ecosystem budget fails.

If independent laboratories cannot reproduce the biological signal from blinded subsamples, the signal fails.

The experiment is expensive because the interesting part of the deep biosphere is not collecting another sequence.

It is closing the causal chain.

A parallel Mars test can be stated before we have the hardware to run it.

The radiolytic-habitability hypothesis predicts that subsurface regions combining water, radionuclides, suitable sulfur or carbon chemistry and thermal stability should offer more redox energy than compositionally unfavorable regions. A future drilling mission could target paired sites: one high-energy prediction and one low-energy control.

Do not merely ask whether organics occur in the favorable site. Ask whether any putative biosignature covaries with the predicted energy system while surviving contamination controls.

That is a much harder test than landing somewhere wet-looking.

The book's broadest synthesis—that subsurface life may be closer to the modal planetary biosphere than surface life—is the most speculative and the hardest to kill from Earth alone. It would weaken if surveys of nearby worlds show subsurface liquid water and geochemical energy are rare, or if origins-of-life research demonstrates that viable deep habitats almost never permit genesis without surface-like cycling.

It would strengthen if independent worlds show subsurface metabolic signatures despite sterile surfaces.

The important word is independent.

A claim that cannot lose is not a frontier thesis. It is décor.

The deep biosphere has enough genuine evidence that we can afford to make it vulnerable.
