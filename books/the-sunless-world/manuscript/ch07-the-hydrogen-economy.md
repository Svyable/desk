# 7. The Hydrogen Economy

Hydrogen is the small change of the underground.

It is easy to make badly and hard to keep. It leaks. It reacts. It can be generated biologically, radiolytically, mechanically and geochemically. It is a favorite electron donor for microorganisms because transferring its electrons to the right acceptor can pay for metabolism.

That makes hydrogen both useful and treacherous as evidence.

Find hydrogen in a deep borehole and you have not found life. You may have found water-rock chemistry. You may have found radiolysis. You may have found corrosion of steel. You may even have created part of the signal while drilling.

The Oman Drilling Project gives a clean example of why mechanism matters. The Samail Ophiolite exposes mantle-derived ultramafic rock that has undergone serpentinization: reactions between water and reduced iron-bearing minerals that alter the rock and can generate hydrogen. In boreholes hundreds of meters deep, researchers have measured strongly reducing, hyperalkaline fluids and active microbial communities inhabiting steep redox gradients.

In 2021, Alexis Templeton and colleagues reported cell abundances varying by at least six orders of magnitude across serpentinite cores, from near detection limits to tens of millions of cells per gram in some intervals. They detected low but measurable sulfate reduction in rock, particularly where reduced groundwaters and suitable pH conditions intersected.

This is not a uniform underground Eden.

It is patchwork ecology written by fractures.

A centimeter of mineralization can matter. Porosity can change by orders of magnitude. A vein can connect one chemical reservoir to another and create a place where organisms can harvest a gradient that does not exist on either side alone.

Life often lives at boundaries because boundaries are where disequilibrium survives.

Hydrogen-rich fluid by itself may be too reducing. An oxidized fluid by itself may lack electron donors. Mix them slowly in a fracture and metabolism appears in the margin.

This is the opposite of the popular image of extremophiles as heroic creatures tolerating a uniformly awful environment. The organism may instead be exploiting a narrow interface where the chemistry is unusually favorable.

The distinction matters for planetary exploration. A rover looking for the “most reducing” or “most organic-rich” rock may miss the ecologically useful boundary. The best habitat can be a contact zone, fracture intersection or redox front too small to resolve from orbit.

Hydrogen also links the deep biosphere to prebiotic chemistry and industrial geology, which can confuse discussions. Natural hydrogen has become a resource topic in its own right. Geological systems produce H2 through serpentinization, radiolysis, iron oxidation and other mechanisms. Some accumulations may be economically interesting.

None of this requires biology.

Likewise, methane in deep systems can be biological or abiotic. Isotopes help, but isotopic fields can overlap. Reaction pathways fractionate differently under different temperatures and substrate limitations. A methane measurement is an invitation to a source model, not a verdict.

The hydrogen economy is therefore a training ground for astrobiological false positives.

Mars has methane controversies. Enceladus has plume hydrogen and methane. Europa may host water-rock interactions beneath ice. Each observation opens a family of mechanisms before it opens a biological conclusion.

The correct order is brutally boring:

What molecules are present?

At what concentrations?

What are their isotopic compositions?

What mineral phases are available?

What temperatures and pressures constrain reactions?

What abiotic pathways can reproduce the signal?

What biological pathways would predict something measurably different?

Can the difference be tested?

Hydrogen is especially valuable because it makes energy accounting possible. A concentration can be combined with an electron acceptor activity to estimate Gibbs free energy under in situ conditions. But concentration alone does not tell you flux. A bottle can contain lots of hydrogen because nobody is using it or because it is being produced quickly. A habitat may contain almost none because microbes consume it as fast as geology supplies it.

Flux beats inventory.

The same principle appears in economics, which is why “hydrogen economy” is not only a pun. Wealth is not cash in a vault; it is production, exchange and flows. An ecosystem can survive on a tiny standing stock if the stock is replenished steadily.

Deep life may therefore be missed by chemical surveys that look only for abundance.

A nearly invisible concentration can support a community if turnover is fast relative to supply. Conversely, an enormous concentration can coexist with little biology if a kinetic or thermodynamic barrier prevents use.

The Oman work also helps correct another simplification: deep life is not synonymous with ancient isolation. Some subsurface systems are dynamic and connected. Groundwater moves. Oxidized shallow fluids can intersect reduced deep fluids. Rainfall can ultimately matter. The geological subsurface contains both long-isolated pockets and actively circulating networks.

Calling all of it “independent of the surface” would be wrong.

Independence is a spectrum of material and energetic coupling.

A community can be physically deep yet dependent on surface-derived sulfate. Another can use radiolytic hydrogen but carbon delivered through fractures. Another can fix inorganic carbon but rely on oxidants generated elsewhere. The strongest claim—complete energetic and material independence from the photosphere—requires a complete budget, not a dramatic depth.

This is why the book keeps returning to accounting.

A living system is a ledger of electrons.

If you cannot say where the electrons came from and where they went, you may have discovered something interesting. You have not yet explained it.
