# 13. Corrosion Is Ecology

A steel surface buried underground is not just a material.

It is a habitat boundary with electrons to steal.

Microbiologically influenced corrosion is a useful phrase because it refuses a false choice. Corrosion can be electrochemical and biological at the same time. Microbes alter local pH, consume hydrogen, produce sulfide, create differential aeration, form biofilms and catalyze reactions that change the kinetics of metal attack. The metal still obeys chemistry. The biology changes the chemistry.

This is what makes repository research a powerful control case for claims about deep life changing geology.

We do not have to infer the possibility from billion-year-old minerals. We can place metal, clay, groundwater and microbes together and watch what happens.

In 2026, laboratory work on bentonite-associated microbial communities reported substantially increased corrosion rates for carbon steel under some dynamic, nutrient-replenished conditions relative to sterile controls. The authors identified sulfate-reducing bacteria, fermenters and nitrate reducers among taxa associated with corrosion. The result strengthens the mechanism: subsurface microbial consortia can materially alter corrosion under selected repository-like conditions.

It does not establish a field corrosion rate for a real repository.

The distinction matters so much it is worth making twice.

A flow-through experiment that continuously supplies nutrients is designed to reveal capability. A compacted repository buffer is designed to remove that capability by restricting transport, water activity and microbial mobility. If you report the laboratory acceleration factor without the experimental boundary conditions, you create a false safety claim.

The same mistake appears everywhere in frontier science.

A mechanism demonstrated under optimized conditions becomes a claimed weapon. A physical effect in a laboratory becomes a fielded technology. A metabolic pathway in a genome becomes an ecosystem function. A material with an unusual isotope ratio becomes extraterrestrial hardware.

Capability is not prevalence.

Repository microbiology trains us to ask the missing middle questions.

How much substrate exists? How fast can it arrive? Is the microbial population located at the relevant interface? Does temperature permit activity? Is sulfide transported toward or away from the canister? Do corrosion products passivate the surface? Does bentonite density suppress cells? What happens after the early oxygen pulse is gone?

These are not caveats stapled to a conclusion. They are the conclusion.

Corrosion also shows why the deep biosphere can matter even when biomass is small.

A biofilm occupying a tiny fraction of the rock volume can create a chemically extreme micron-scale environment at a metal surface. Bulk groundwater measurements may miss it. Localized corrosion is local by definition.

This is a recurrent lesson in subsurface ecology: average conditions can be biologically irrelevant.

A fracture wall can host chemistry unlike the center of the fracture. A mineral grain can support a redox reaction not represented in the bulk fluid. A microbial aggregate can consume oxygen faster than diffusion replaces it, creating an anaerobic pocket inside an otherwise oxic system.

The habitat is often the interface.

That matters for sampling. A liter of groundwater can look sterile while the adjacent rock surface carries a biofilm. A crushed core homogenizes spatial structure and can make a boundary process look volumetric. Sequencing tells you who might be there but not where they are physically acting.

The best subsurface science therefore preserves geometry whenever possible.

Microscopy, mineral maps, microelectrodes, isotope imaging and spatially resolved sequencing become more informative than another bulk sample.

Mars again waits at the end of this logic.

If life exists in a martian fracture, it may be attached to a mineral boundary where hydrogen-producing rock meets sulfate-bearing fluid. A drill that powders the sample gains chemistry and loses architecture.

That tradeoff should be explicit in mission design.

Corrosion is ecology because both are stories about reactions made local.

A planet can have the right global ingredients and no useful local gradient. A repository can contain sulfate reducers in the host rock and still suppress sulfide production at the canister. A deep aquifer can contain hydrogen and still starve if no acceptor reaches it.

Life is not the inventory.

Life is the reaction path.
