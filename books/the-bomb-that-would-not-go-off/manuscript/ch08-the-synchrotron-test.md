# The Synchrotron Test

In March 2001, three small hafnium samples went into beamline 1-ID at the Advanced Photon Source. Each contained on the order of 10^15 atoms of the 31-year isomer. The beam was chopped into eleven seconds on, twenty-two seconds off. Germanium detectors watched the gamma rays while the cycle repeated for roughly eight hours per sample.

This is the experiment on which the hafnium-bomb story should turn.

The earlier Texas result had come from bremsstrahlung produced by an ordinary x-ray machine. The APS collaboration—scientists from Argonne, Los Alamos, Lawrence Livermore and the synchrotron itself—could use a much more intense source and knew what quantitative claim it was trying to test. Los Alamos prepared the isomeric material. The white beam peaked near 16 keV at roughly 2 × 10^15 photons per keV per second and extended beyond 100 keV. The detectors could compare irradiation with the dark intervals built into the same run.

Nothing accelerated.

That sentence is less informative than the limit they put on nothing.

Between 20 and 60 keV, Ahmad and colleagues reported an upper limit on the energy-integrated triggering cross section below 2 × 10^-27 cm² keV. At 8 keV, the limit was 5 × 10^-26 cm² keV. The 20–60 keV result sat more than five orders of magnitude below the previously reported values.

Five orders of magnitude is not the usual scientific disagreement in which two laboratories obtain slightly different slopes and spend a conference arguing about calibration. It is the kind of gap that forces a choice among explanations.

The APS experiment could be wrong. The Texas experiment could be wrong. Or the two experiments could be probing materially different physics.

The third possibility deserved serious attention because nuclear triggering need not be simple. A useful pathway might pass through a narrow intermediate state. Target preparation could matter. A cascade might bypass the four-second 8− state used as a signature in the APS analysis; the APS authors explicitly noted that their limits would then be about a factor of ten weaker. Later positive papers did not vanish merely because the national laboratories had obtained a null.

But those qualifications have arithmetic attached to them. A factor-of-ten loophole does not erase a discrepancy exceeding five orders of magnitude. A narrow-resonance explanation has to name a resonance narrow enough, and placed appropriately enough, to explain why one source found a large effect while a far more intense search did not. “Different conditions” is the beginning of that explanation, not the end.

The beam also makes the history harder to narrate as a morality play about brave outsiders and conservative laboratories. The APS team did not test whether nuclear isomers were interesting. They tested whether the particular extraordinary triggering magnitude already in print would survive a source designed to make the effect easier to see. The null was therefore not a vote against exotic physics. It was a measurement against a number.

That distinction matters because the number was doing almost all the work in the weapon concept.

Hafnium-178m2 really does store an extraordinary amount of excitation energy. That fact survives every argument in this book. What turns stored nuclear energy into a controllable device is the switch: how much incident energy is required, with what probability, to open a useful decay path on demand. A reservoir without a practical trigger is not a bomb waiting for an engineer. It is a long-lived nuclear state.

The 2001 result therefore changed the application before it settled the science. If a proposed device required something like the early reported triggering strength, then an upper limit more than 100,000 times lower over a broad relevant energy range had to enter every subsequent engineering calculation. One could still investigate triggering. One could not responsibly carry the old switch into a weapons slide as though the replication dispute were a footnote.

The next experiment widened the search rather than closing the file. The collaboration returned to the question at lower energies, where claimed resonant structure made a more targeted scan worthwhile, and again reported no statistically significant induced decay. By 2003, the dispute had become peculiar in the productive scientific sense: published positive claims, published criticisms and national-laboratory nulls all existed at once.

This is where the word *replication* can hide more than it reveals. The APS work was not a photocopy of the dental-machine experiment. That was part of its strength and part of the strongest objection to it. A literal reproduction asks whether the original apparatus can produce the original observation again. A mechanistic test asks whether the inferred physical effect survives under conditions that should expose it more cleanly. Hafnium needed both. What it got was a much stronger mechanistic challenge and continued disagreement about whether differences between sources and pathways rescued the original observation.

The institutional decision should have followed the structure of that uncertainty.

First reproduce the original effect closely enough that proponents cannot plausibly blame a changed apparatus. Then hand common samples to independent teams. Blind the sample identities where possible. Predeclare which gamma lines count, how backgrounds are estimated and what cross section would justify moving downstream. Deliberately vary beam spectra only after the baseline effect survives. If a new resonance is proposed to explain a null, predict its energy and width before scanning for it.

That is not bureaucratic caution. It is how a spectacular application earns the right to become an engineering problem.

The APS beam could not prove that no conceivable photon-triggered pathway exists in hafnium-178m2. No experiment can search every energy, intermediate state, sample environment and decay branch. It did something narrower and, for the bomb, more damaging: it made a large published triggering claim conspicuous by its absence.

For eleven seconds at a time, the x-rays were on. For twenty-two, they were off. The detector did not know which result would be more interesting.

Nature had no DARPA briefing to attend.
