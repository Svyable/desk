# 16. Why It Lasted

Twenty years is too long to explain with a single gullible colonel.

The program lasted because several weak reasons reinforced one another.

First, the cost was low. Remote viewing did not require a new satellite constellation. A small number of personnel and contractors could preserve the option.

Second, the upside was absurdly high. If minds could obtain target information across distance without ordinary transmission, the capability would bypass physical access and many forms of counterintelligence.

Third, the adversary-threat story reduced the comfort of unilateral skepticism. Reports of Soviet and East European research meant American officials could tell themselves that investigating the field was defensive prudence.

Fourth, the data refused to become uniformly boring. Some sessions were obvious misses. Some laboratory series produced statistical claims that qualified experts took seriously. Some operational cases looked specific enough to resist casual dismissal. The evidence had exactly the intermittent reinforcement pattern that keeps humans engaged.

Fifth, intelligence work already tolerates unreliable sources. The organization was culturally prepared to ask whether a weak channel might still be useful when corroborated.

Sixth, secrecy protected the program from some kinds of correction while also protecting it from some kinds of ridicule. External scientists could not audit what they could not see. Internal participants could interpret criticism as ignorance of classified successes. Later, declassification reversed the asymmetry but only after the program was gone.

Seventh, evaluation criteria kept moving between science and operations. When operational reliability looked poor, laboratory significance could justify research. When laboratory mechanism remained obscure, customer anecdotes could justify operations. The program could migrate to whichever burden of proof was currently easier to satisfy.

This is the central institutional lesson.

A controversial capability survives when nobody forces all of its claims through the same gate at the same time.

Does the effect replicate under blinded controls?

Can the channel be calibrated per trial?

Does it improve decisions prospectively?

Can independent teams reproduce it?

Does it survive adversarial testing designed by people who expect it to fail?

A program that cannot answer all five may still be worth a small research budget.

It should not be confused with an operational intelligence capability.
