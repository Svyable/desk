# 5. The Coordinate Trick

Coordinates were supposed to make the impossible clean.

Give a remote viewer latitude and longitude. Do not tell the viewer what sits there. Ask for impressions. If the description fits the site, perhaps something happened that ordinary sensory channels cannot explain.

The coordinate method also produced one of the archive's best epistemological accidents.

A released Army training report, session XCI, describes an intended target of Offutt Air Force Base, Nebraska. But the coordinates supplied were wrong. When checked, they pointed much farther north, near Pisgah, Iowa, around the Soldier River. The session summary says the viewer described a narrow meandering body of water on a flat plain with cliffs on either side—features the report judged to fit the actual coordinate location rather than the intended air base.

For believers, this is delicious. The human intention said air base. The numbers said river. The viewer, according to the report, followed the numbers.

For skeptics, it is also delicious, because it forces every soft part of the scoring problem into view.

How exact was the coordinate location? How large a region was considered a match? How were "cliffs" and "flat plain" verified? Was the summary written before or after the location was inspected? How much transcript material did not fit? What alternative landscapes within a comparable radius could also have matched portions of the description? Was the target selection process reconstructable from the surviving record?

A strange case becomes scientifically valuable only when it can survive those questions.

The episode also exposes an underappreciated feature of remote viewing: the cue itself has no known physical semantics. Latitude and longitude are human conventions. If an anomalous information channel exists, why should decimal or sexagesimal symbols point consciousness to a place? Perhaps the numbers merely focus intention. Perhaps the tasker's intention is the true cue. Perhaps feedback selects the association after the fact. Each explanation predicts different behavior when tasker intention and coordinates disagree.

The Offutt/Pisgah error should therefore have become an experimental design.

Construct trials in which three things are deliberately separated: the location the tasker intends, the location encoded by coordinates, and the location eventually used for feedback. Keep all parties blinded appropriately. Pre-register scoring. Repeat enough trials to distinguish which association, if any, governs the effect.

This is what frontier research should do with anomalies: turn anecdotes into forks.

Instead, anomalous fields often collect anomalies as trophies. The weird case becomes more valuable for its resistance to explanation than for the experiment it suggests.

Intelligence programs have an additional excuse. Their purpose is not to solve consciousness. Their purpose is to obtain useful information. If a weird cueing system seems to work, operators may prefer exploitation over mechanism.

That preference is common in technology. Engineers used electricity before electrodynamics was complete. Physicians used anesthetics before understanding all molecular mechanisms. A fieldable effect does not require a final theory.

It does, however, require repeatability.

The coordinate accident is memorable because it seems almost too neat.

That is exactly why it deserved to be repeated until it became boring.
