# The Psychic Brief

## Remote Viewing, STARGATE, and the Intelligence Value of Unreliable Information

### Sven Hardy Benson

There is a photograph of the American intelligence community that does not exist but should.

Not satellites. Not men in dark suits walking into Langley. Not the National Security Agency's antennas. A file cabinet.

Inside it are nearly ninety thousand pages about trying to collect intelligence with the human mind.

That number matters because it ruins the easy joke. A joke can survive a memo. It has a harder time surviving procurement paperwork, protocols, evaluation forms, personnel-selection schemes, contractor reports, operational requests, failed sessions, successful-looking sessions, Congressional attention, multiple code names, and a termination review. Bureaucracies are excellent at generating paper around bad ideas, of course. That is one of their central talents. But the paper changes the question. The interesting question is no longer whether somebody once believed something strange. It is why an institution trained to price uncertainty kept returning to the strangeness.

The programs gathered under the later label STARGATE were never one stable thing. CIA research at Stanford Research Institute in the 1970s became one strand. Defense Intelligence Agency threat assessment became another. Army Intelligence and Security Command tried to turn remote viewing into a collection method using assigned personnel. Names changed: SCANATE, GRILL FLAME, CENTER LANE, SUN STREAK, STUNT PILOT, STAR GATE. The spelling itself wandered. The bureaucracy did too.

This book does not ask the reader to believe in remote viewing. It asks the reader to become more exact about what disbelief would have to explain.

A method can fail operationally even if a laboratory effect is real. A laboratory effect can look real because the scoring system leaks judgment. A customer can find value in a vague report because he already knows too much. A striking description can occur by chance, cueing, sensory leakage, selective memory, or something not yet understood. A hostile-state program can be exaggerated, misunderstood, or deliberately advertised to provoke exactly the research response it later receives. An intelligence agency can rationally spend a little money on a low-probability possibility if the downside of ignoring a real adversary capability is large enough.

Those possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

The archive is useful because it preserves the failures. One released training report says the target images showed "absolutely no target correlation." Another report records an odd coordinate error: the intended target was Offutt Air Force Base, but the coordinates actually pointed near Pisgah, Iowa; the session was subsequently judged against the location the coordinates specified, and the viewer's description was considered a good fit. A famous Soviet R&D-site case records a crane and the assembly of large metallic spheres. The Iran-hostage files contain session after session written in the dry grammar of an intelligence requirement while the human material inside them drifts between landscape, architecture, guess, impression, and hope.

The point is not to average those into a verdict before reading them.

The point is to learn what kind of information a system produces when nobody agrees what the channel is.
