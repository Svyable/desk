# 9. The Nuclear Site and the Crane

Every controversial field needs one case that behaves like a splinter.

For American remote viewing, the Semipalatinsk case is one of them.

A released CIA document summarizes work against URDF-3, an unidentified Soviet research-and-development facility at Semipalatinsk, known in the intelligence community by the nickname PNUTS. According to the document, the site featured a large rail-mounted crane and large metallic gores used to assemble a sphere or spheres, apparently underground. The remote viewer was given coordinates and told that the site was an R&D facility in the Soviet Union. The document says the viewer described a large crane and the assembly of large spheres from metallic gores.

This is the kind of result that kept the program alive.

It is also the kind of result that should make a careful skeptic slow down rather than speed up.

The wrong skeptical move is to say, immediately, coincidence. The wrong believing move is to say, immediately, proof. The correct move is to reconstruct the entire information boundary.

What imagery or reporting about Semipalatinsk existed before the session? Who selected the target? What did the interviewer know? Exactly what cue did the viewer receive, and when? Which drawings were produced before feedback? What target features were predefined as diagnostic? What nonmatching details were present? How many comparable sessions failed? Were descriptions scored prospectively or selected retrospectively because they resembled the site?

The released summary answers only some of those questions.

This is a recurring problem with historical anomalies. A document can be authentic while the inference people draw from it remains underdetermined. Declassification authenticates provenance; it does not retroactively supply missing experimental controls.

Still, the case matters because specificity changes the arithmetic. "Industrial place" is cheap. "Large crane" costs more. "Metallic gores assembling a sphere" costs more again. The more distinctive the conjunction, the less satisfying generic-match arguments become—provided the conjunction was genuinely produced before target knowledge and not assembled after the fact from flexible transcript material.

That proviso is the entire scientific problem.

The Semipalatinsk case should be treated as an archival reconstruction challenge. One would want the original session transcript, drawings, dates, all cueing records, the state of U.S. intelligence knowledge at the time, the evaluation procedure, and every session in the relevant series. Only then could we ask whether the famous correspondence is exceptional relative to the program's own base rate.

The broader lesson is uncomfortable for both camps.

Some anomalous cases are too specific to dismiss casually.

Specificity is not the same thing as proof.
