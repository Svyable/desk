# 8. When the Viewer Is Wrong

The most important sentence in the STARGATE archive may be an admission of failure.

Session 876, dated March 1982, was a training exercise. Its summary says post-session analysis indicated the viewer had "very little correlation." Another training report from the earlier period says the images showed "absolutely no target correlation."

These are healthy sentences.

An anomalous research program without explicit misses is not a research program. It is a legend factory.

But admitting misses does not solve the deeper denominator problem. We need to know how often viewers were wrong, how wrong, under what conditions, against what scoring rules, and whether the same rules were used when they were right.

Consider confidence. In ordinary intelligence work, source confidence matters because it can be calibrated. A weather model that says 70 percent rain should produce rain on roughly seven of ten comparable forecasts. A viewer can also report confidence. But unless confidence bins are tracked prospectively across many blinded sessions, "high confidence" is theater rather than probability.

Consider specificity. A wrong claim that a target is a hydroelectric dam may be more informative about the method than ten correct claims that the target has walls. Generic correspondences inflate hit rates. Highly specific misses reveal how easily imagination can mimic signal.

Consider stopping. If a session continues until something resonates, the number of opportunities for accidental correspondence rises. If multiple viewers are tasked and the best account is highlighted afterward, the effective number of attempts is larger than the story suggests.

These are mundane statistical problems. They are also exactly the problems that make paranormal research culturally combustible. Believers experience the criticism as an attempt to explain away whatever survives. Skeptics experience every protocol change as another opportunity for motivated rescue.

The archive offers a way out: count everything.

Every session. Every task. Every viewer. Every descriptor. Every pre-registered scoring rule. Every customer rating. Every feedback event. Every protocol deviation. Every case excluded and the reason for exclusion.

Modern machine-readable methods make this easier than it was in 1982. Qualitative session transcripts could be frozen cryptographically before target reveal. Independent judging teams could score descriptors against large, standardized target databases. Natural-language models could be used as additional blinded scorers, not as arbiters but as a way to expose how much results depend on human interpretive flexibility. Replications could be run across laboratories that disagree philosophically about the phenomenon.

That experiment would not begin by hunting spectacular subjects.

It would begin by making wrongness expensive to hide.

The government program never had the infrastructure to do this at modern scale. That is not an excuse for its conclusions. It is a reminder that some questions can become more testable after the institution that first asked them has died.

The dead program left us the right raw material: successes, misses, arguments and enough bureaucracy to reconstruct where confidence exceeded calibration.
