# 2. The Threat That Came From Moscow

The Cold War did not require American officials to believe in psychic powers. It only required them to believe the Soviets might.

That distinction was enough to fund a surprising amount of work.

A 1970s intelligence bureaucracy looking east encountered Soviet and Eastern European literature on telepathy, psychokinesis, "bioinformation," distant influence and what Western reports often called psychotronics. CIA and DIA documents catalogued the research. Some assessments treated it as a possible military threat. One released reference list points to a 1972 DIA report titled *Controlled Offensive Behavior*, a 1975 report on Soviet and Czechoslovakian parapsychology, a 1977 CIA study of Soviet and East European work, and a 1978 DIA document on Warsaw Pact "paraphysics R&D."

The titles are evidence that the U.S. government investigated the claims. They are not evidence the claims were true.

That sounds obvious. It was not always operationally obvious.

Intelligence organizations are punished asymmetrically. If an analyst dismisses an absurd adversary claim and the claim really is absurd, almost nothing happens. If the analyst dismisses it and the adversary has discovered a capability that bypasses ordinary security, the failure can become historic. The rational threshold for investigation is therefore lower than the threshold for belief.

This is especially true when the alleged capability attacks the institution's deepest assumptions. A spy satellite can be hidden from. A microphone can be swept. A code can be changed. What is the countermeasure against a person who can acquire information without electromagnetic transmission, physical access, or a recruited source? The premise sounds like science fiction because it is almost perfectly designed to terrify a security bureaucracy.

Soviet-interest reporting amplified the effect. American documents repeated claims involving psychokinesis, remote hypnosis, biological influence and laboratory demonstrations whose evidentiary foundations were often inaccessible or weak. Later press accounts noted an obvious counterhypothesis: perhaps exaggerated Soviet work functioned as disinformation, provoking the United States to spend money and attention chasing phantoms.

That possibility must stay alive. Intelligence history punishes anyone who assumes the adversary's public or semi-public research literature is a transparent window into capability.

But disinformation does not make the American response irrational by itself. A cheap exploratory program can be insurance against a low-probability, high-consequence possibility. The right question is how long the insurance premium should be paid after the claims fail to harden into operational evidence.

That is where remote viewing became institutionally sticky.

The Soviet threat rationale could survive American ambiguity. If an SRI experiment looked suggestive, it justified caution. If an experiment failed, advocates could argue the phenomenon was weak, intermittent or dependent on rare individuals. If Soviet evidence remained inaccessible, uncertainty itself could be treated as risk. A program did not need to prove a Soviet psychic corps existed. It only needed to keep the possibility above zero.

This is a general mechanism of technological competition. Missile gaps, bomber gaps, anti-satellite fears, biological weapons assessments, cyber capabilities and artificial intelligence all live partly inside estimates of what the other side might achieve. The stranger the alleged capability, the harder it can be to obtain direct evidence, and the more temptation there is to substitute effort for proof: they are spending money, therefore there must be something there.

Remote viewing offers an unusually clean historical warning.

An adversary's interest is evidence of interest.

It is not evidence of effect.
