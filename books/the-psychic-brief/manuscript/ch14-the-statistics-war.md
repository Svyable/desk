# 14. The Statistics War

By the end, remote viewing had a problem that statistics could not solve because the dispute was not only statistical.

Jessica Utts, a statistician, reviewed the laboratory record for the 1995 AIR evaluation and argued that anomalous cognition had been demonstrated to a standard that made chance an inadequate explanation for the aggregate results. Psychologist Ray Hyman disagreed about what the evidence established, emphasizing methodological issues, replication standards and the distance between statistical effects and a demonstrated paranormal process.

Popular retellings flatten this into believer versus skeptic.

That loses the useful part.

Suppose, for argument, that the aggregate laboratory effect is real in the narrow statistical sense: under certain protocols, target-related information appears in responses at a rate above the specified chance model.

Several possibilities remain.

The effect could arise from an anomalous information channel. It could arise from subtle methodological leakage. It could depend on judging artifacts. It could be inflated by selective reporting or analytical flexibility. It could combine a small genuine effect with ordinary artifacts. It could be real but so small and unstable that mechanism is practically inaccessible.

Statistics can reject a model. They do not automatically name the replacement.

Now add the intelligence question.

A tiny repeatable effect can be scientifically revolutionary and operationally worthless. A one-percent edge over chance may transform a theory of mind while failing to locate a hostage. Conversely, an effect that appears only occasionally might still matter operationally if there were a reliable way to identify the rare high-quality trial before feedback.

The program never solved that gating problem.

This is why the argument about whether remote viewing was "proven" is badly formed. Proven for what?

Proven to produce aggregate deviations from a null model under specific experiments?

Proven to require a paranormal explanation?

Proven to deliver actionable intelligence?

Those are three different thresholds.

The 1995 controversy persists because people answer one and pretend they have answered all three.
