# 6. GRILL FLAME

GRILL FLAME sounds like the code name of a parody because Cold War code names often outran parody.

By the late 1970s and early 1980s, the Army Intelligence and Security Command was not merely reading SRI papers. It was trying to determine whether remote viewing could be used by assigned intelligence personnel.

A declassified briefing draws the distinction carefully. DIA, it says, was concerned with assessing the threat, principally from the Soviet Union and China; determining countermeasures; and determining the potential of remote viewing using established psychics, largely through contracts at SRI. INSCOM's role was different: apply remote viewing to operational requirements and determine whether professional intelligence personnel who were not established psychics could learn it.

That is a profound shift.

A rare prodigy is scientifically interesting and operationally annoying. A trainable skill can become doctrine.

INSCOM screened personnel drawn from intelligence disciplines including SIGINT, PHOTINT and HUMINT. A 1984 CENTER LANE personnel-selection memorandum says 251 people in the Baltimore/Washington area were considered in late 1978 and early 1979, with 117 interviewed under the guise of a survey about psychoenergetic phenomena. The language is revealing. The program was looking for operators, not gurus.

This created a new failure mode: institutionalization before mechanism.

If a skill is trainable, one needs selection criteria, instructors, proficiency measures, refresher training, target protocols, security rules, customer interfaces and evaluation. Each layer makes the activity more real bureaucratically even if the underlying signal remains weak. Forms accrete around uncertainty.

But bureaucracy should not be confused with self-deception. Some of the GRILL FLAME records are blunt about poor performance. Training sessions were evaluated as having little or no correlation. Reports describe the impressions as "raw intelligence data" and leave interpretation to the requester. This is not how a mythology writes itself when it has complete control of the pen.

The more troubling possibility is subtler.

The organization may have been honest locally while biased globally. An individual session report can admit failure while the program survives because supporters emphasize the distribution's upper tail: the one uncanny sketch, the one customer who returns, the one Soviet site apparently described, the one mission where nothing else worked.

This is not unique to anomalous cognition. Venture capital lives on tails. Intelligence collection tolerates many useless leads to obtain one decisive source. Medical screening accepts false positives when the missed disease is catastrophic. Search-and-rescue teams follow weak clues because the alternative is doing nothing.

The key variable is cost.

Remote viewing was cheap compared with satellites, aircraft or national technical means. A small unit of people, rooms, paper and contractor support could remain alive below the level at which an uncertain capability would normally be killed by capital intensity.

The program's survival therefore may tell us less about how convincing the evidence was than about how inexpensive ambiguity can be.
