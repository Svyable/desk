# 18. Intelligence From a Channel You Do Not Trust

The psychic spy is the wrong protagonist.

The protagonist is the institution standing in front of a report it cannot price.

That problem is everywhere now.

Intelligence agencies receive synthetic media, probabilistic model outputs, defectors with mixed motives, commercial satellite imagery, social-media rumors, cyber telemetry, anonymous tips, algorithmic alerts and machine-generated analysis. Each channel can be partly right for the wrong reason. Each can produce a spectacular success that encourages overtrust. Each can fail silently.

STARGATE looks less ridiculous from this angle and more modern.

Its deepest problem was not that the claimed channel was strange. Intelligence has always accepted strange channels when they work. Its problem was calibration. The institution could not reliably convert a given remote-viewing product into a probability that justified action.

That is why the best historical material includes the misses.

A file saying "absolutely no target correlation" is not an embarrassment to be hidden from the story. It is the story. So is the Semipalatinsk crane. So is the coordinate error. So is the statistical dispute. So is the Soviet-threat literature. So is the customer's inability to turn occasional correspondence into a dependable collection discipline.

The mature position is not agnosticism as etiquette. It is claim separation.

The U.S. government funded remote-viewing research and operations. Verified.

Some released sessions look striking and some look terrible. Verified.

Some statistical reviewers judged laboratory evidence anomalous. Verified as their judgment.

Other qualified reviewers disputed what those results established. Verified as their judgment.

The method demonstrated reliable operational intelligence value. Not established.

The archive proves extrasensory perception. Not established.

The archive proves nothing anomalous ever occurred. Also not established.

A non-human or unknown mechanism is required. Open conjecture, presently unsupported by the public record.

That last restraint matters because mystery has a tendency to consume its own boundaries.

The story began with a possibility: maybe information can arrive through a channel we do not understand.

The government spent decades trying to turn that possibility into a tool.

The tool never became dependable.

The possibility, irritatingly, did not become simple either.

There are worse endings for a book about intelligence.
