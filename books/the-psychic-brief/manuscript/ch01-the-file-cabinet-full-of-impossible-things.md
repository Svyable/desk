# 1. The File Cabinet Full of Impossible Things

The remote-viewing archive has the texture of a government that cannot decide whether it is embarrassed or worried.

That is a better starting point than Uri Geller bending spoons.

In 2006, responding to a Freedom of Information Act requester, the CIA described what had become the Star Gate Collection: 11,985 documents totaling 89,901 pages. The agency explained that "Star Gate" had become an umbrella term for a series of CIA and military projects, including STAR GATE, GRILL FLAME, CENTER LANE, SUN STREAK, STUNT PILOT, PHOENIX and SCANATE. The projects were defunct. The paper remained.

A paper trail that large does not prove a paranormal phenomenon. It proves institutional attention. Those are radically different things and the distinction will carry most of this book.

Government can investigate nonsense. Government has investigated nonsense. The twentieth century is full of organizations that poured money into false threat estimates, brittle technologies, ideological science and programs protected from disconfirmation by classification. Secrecy is not a quality mark. A SECRET stamp means that somebody decided disclosure should be restricted, not that the proposition underneath the stamp is true.

But classification creates another problem for the historian. It distorts the denominator. The public usually meets anomalous programs through their best stories because best stories travel. A viewer describes a crane. A missing person is allegedly located. A submarine is supposedly found. Mars is visited. What does not travel as well is the session in which nothing happens, the customer who cannot use the output, the protocol modification that makes comparison difficult, the report whose target was too vague to score, or the administrative memo asking why a project is still alive.

The released collection restores some of that denominator.

A 1982 training session numbered 876 states in its summary that the viewer had "very little correlation." Another older session says "absolutely no target correlation." These sentences matter because they demonstrate that at least some internal reporting did not simply promote every impression into success. They also create a harsher question for spectacular cases: how were successes selected, scored and remembered relative to the mass of ordinary misses?

The intelligence setting makes that question unusually difficult. Intelligence is not a laboratory tournament. Customers do not always need a complete description. Sometimes a weak clue is useful because it points an analyst toward a branch of an existing hypothesis tree. Sometimes a clue is useless because it cannot be distinguished from what the customer already knows. Sometimes a report that sounds specific after the answer is revealed would have been operationally inert beforehand.

This is the recurring mistake in arguments about STARGATE: confusing retrospective resemblance with prospective utility.

Imagine receiving a page before an operation. It says water, metal, a large building, perhaps a tower, a sensation of underground space, a person whose name begins with a hard consonant. How many possible targets does that exclude? If the eventual target has water nearby and an underground structure, the page can feel uncanny. If the target does not, the same symbols can be forgotten. A scientific scoring protocol tries to prevent that flexibility. An intelligence customer under time pressure is rarely operating inside a pristine scoring protocol.

The archive therefore contains at least three different questions disguised as one.

First: did controlled experiments produce results above what their methods and statistics would predict by chance?

Second: if they did, what mechanism—ordinary or anomalous—produced the excess?

Third: could the effect be converted into information reliable enough to change real decisions?

A yes to the first is not a yes to the third. The entire twenty-year story can be understood as the government's repeated failure to keep those levels separated.

It can also be understood as the government's repeated attempt to do exactly that.
