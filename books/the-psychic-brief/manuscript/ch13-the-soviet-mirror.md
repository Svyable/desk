# 13. The Soviet Mirror

The psychic arms race may have been partly a hall of mirrors.

American intelligence read Soviet and Eastern European claims. Soviet researchers noticed American interest. American contractors cited foreign programs as evidence that the threat deserved attention. Journalists reported both sides' activity. Each side's investigation became evidence for the other side's seriousness.

This is a classic reflexive intelligence problem.

If state A fears state B is developing capability X, A funds research into X. B observes A's funding and concludes A may have evidence that X works. B increases its own effort. Neither side needs an initial success. Mutual uncertainty can bootstrap a research race.

Psychotronics was unusually vulnerable to this because its claimed mechanisms were poorly bounded and its evidence circulated through a mixture of scientific literature, intelligence assessments, émigré testimony, demonstrations and rumor.

A declassified American reference list shows how threat assessment aggregated this material. Reports on Soviet and Czechoslovakian parapsychology sat beside papers on novel biophysical information transfer and domestic remote-viewing research. Categories that should have been separated—what Soviet scientists published, what intelligence analysts inferred, what alleged psychics demonstrated, what military planners feared—could become a single impression of adversary momentum.

The strongest skeptical hypothesis is that much of the threat was epistemic inflation.

The strongest counterargument is that Soviet and Russian institutions did in fact investigate unconventional human-performance and bioeffects topics over long periods, and adversary research effort can matter even when the underlying phenomenon is weak. A false scientific belief inside a rival military can still generate technologies, doctrines, countermeasures and deception campaigns with real effects.

The correct question is therefore not: Did the Soviets have psychic weapons?

It is: What exactly did U.S. decision-makers know about Soviet programs at each point, how reliable was that knowledge, and how much of U.S. spending was justified by the adversary's alleged work rather than domestic evidence?

That chronology remains underwritten in popular histories.

It matters because the answer changes the moral of STARGATE. If domestic evidence drove continuation, the story is about anomalous science. If threat reporting drove it, the story is about intelligence competition. If each recursively amplified the other, the story is about how states manufacture technological possibility together.
