# 11. CENTER LANE

Programs change names for many reasons: funding lines, organizational transfers, security compartments, management resets, political discomfort. In anomalous research, renaming also creates the illusion of clean eras where the history is actually braided.

CENTER LANE inherited part of the Army effort in the 1980s. Its records are among the clearest evidence that remote viewing was treated as a serious personnel and operations problem inside a small military unit.

A 1984 memorandum on personnel selection looks almost comically normal. Hundreds considered. Interviews. Screening factors. Discussion of prior research. References to a three-year DIA psychoenergetic research effort and recommendations that the INSCOM unit responding to operational requirements continue.

The normality is the unsettling part.

Institutional seriousness does not authenticate the underlying phenomenon. But it changes the sociology. People assigned to a classified unit build careers, friendships, procedures and internal standards. Customers who receive reports develop expectations. Managers defend budgets. Contractors build expertise. A field acquires stakeholders.

Critics often use this as a total explanation: bureaucratic inertia.

That is too easy.

Small classified programs are killed all the time. Personnel are reassigned. Code names vanish. Contractors lose work. To explain persistence, inertia needs fuel.

Remote viewing's fuel came from four sources.

One was occasional apparently striking performance. Another was unresolved laboratory evidence. A third was adversary uncertainty. The fourth was customer demand in hard cases.

None required strong average reliability.

This creates a survival niche for marginal capabilities. A method can be too unreliable to become mainstream and too intriguing to eliminate. It lives at the edge of the institution, protected by cheapness, secrecy and the possibility that the next target will be the one that matters.

CENTER LANE is therefore less interesting as a chapter in occult military history than as a case study in organizational option value.

The Army was holding an option on an extraordinary capability.

The premium was small.

The exercise price was epistemic confusion.
