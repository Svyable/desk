# 10. Training the Untrainable

A capability that belongs only to a few gifted people is a personnel problem. A capability that can be taught is an institution.

This is why the training question mattered so much.

INSCOM's work tried to move remote viewing away from charismatic exceptions and toward trained intelligence personnel. SRI developed procedures. Ingo Swann became associated with what was later called controlled remote viewing. Stages, ideograms, sensory descriptors and rules against premature interpretation attempted to discipline the viewer's internal noise.

The language again became technical. "Analytical overlay" named a recognizable cognitive failure: the moment an impression such as tall, hard, metallic turns into the story "radio tower," after which the imagination begins generating tower-compatible detail. Anyone who has done serious observation recognizes the problem even if remote viewing itself is rejected. Perception is constantly contaminated by interpretation.

The training system tried to interrupt that contamination.

That is intellectually interesting independent of paranormal claims. It resembles protocols in microscopy, field observation, intelligence analysis and debugging: record the low-level observation before attaching a high-level explanation.

But training also creates expectancy.

Once a student learns that certain marks, sensations or spontaneous images are meaningful, the training culture can teach the student how a successful session is supposed to look. Feedback can reinforce stylistic resemblance to prior hits. Instructor judgment can become part of the phenomenon being measured.

A trainable anomaly therefore requires a special control: training must improve blinded prospective performance, not merely produce more fluent remote-viewing paperwork.

The archive suggests the program understood at least part of this. Research contracts examined targeting methods, evaluation systems and personnel selection. A 1987 report reviewed the Personality Assessment System as a way to predict latent psychic ability. The very existence of such work shows the program was trying to solve a practical problem: not simply whether someone somewhere could produce an impressive session, but whether useful performers could be identified before expensive training.

That effort never matured into a validated selection science.

The reason matters. If a field cannot predict who will perform, when they will perform, or how much confidence to assign a given output, scaling magnifies noise faster than capability.

A small program can remember its stars.

An operational service must survive its averages.
