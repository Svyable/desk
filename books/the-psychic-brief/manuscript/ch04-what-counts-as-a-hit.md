# 4. What Counts as a Hit?

Suppose I tell you I see a long horizontal structure, coldness, water nearby, something circular, heavy machinery and a feeling of isolation.

Have I described a submarine base? A dam? A shipyard? A mountain observatory beside a lake? A power plant? A bridge project in winter?

Until the target is disclosed, the language is mostly possibility. After disclosure, the language becomes correspondence.

Remote viewing lives or dies inside that transformation.

The strongest versions of the method try to make the transformation mechanical. The viewer produces a description while blind to the target. Independent judges compare the description against a predefined target pool. Targets are ranked. Repeated performance is tested against chance expectation. The more discretion removed from the chain, the less room there is for a human evaluator to manufacture a hit unconsciously.

This is straightforward in principle and surprisingly hard in practice.

Target pools have structure. A photograph of a waterfall differs perceptually from a photograph of an office building in many dimensions at once. A subject who produces generic natural imagery may do well whenever natural scenes are present. Judges may use stylistic features rather than true target information. Experimenters can accidentally leak expectations through cueing, feedback timing, file handling or conversational behavior. Published series can look cleaner than the messy research ecology that produced them.

Operational intelligence makes all of those problems worse because the target is rarely one item in a balanced photo set.

A customer asks: Where is the hostage? What is inside the building? What will happen to the missing aircraft? What is the purpose of the Soviet facility? The output arrives as prose and drawings. There may be no agreed list of alternative answers against which to score it. The known facts can change while the session is being evaluated. The customer may already possess fragments from conventional sources. A phrase that confirms a favored hypothesis feels more useful than one that does not.

Retrospective evaluation then rewards survivorship.

This does not mean customers were fools. Intelligence analysis always combines uncertain fragments. A source who is right only some of the time can be valuable if the reliability is known and the information is independent. Human sources lie. Sensors fail. Intercepts are deceptive. The profession is built around imperfect channels.

Remote viewing's problem was harsher: its reliability could not be calibrated in a way that gave the customer stable likelihoods.

If a known human source has a history of accurate reporting on a particular military unit, analysts can weight the next report. If a remote viewer says "bridge" today, what is the probability the target actually involves a bridge? Does performance transfer across target classes? Across emotional states? Across cue types? Across viewers? Across years? Does feedback improve learning or contaminate later scoring? Does confidence track accuracy?

Without those answers, a report is not merely noisy. It is unpriced noise.

That phrase explains much of the program's eventual death.

Intelligence organizations can use weak information.

They struggle to use information whose weakness cannot itself be measured.
