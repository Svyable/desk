# 12. The Customer Problem

The customer is where remote viewing either becomes intelligence or remains an experiment.

By the late program, the government tried to measure this more directly. A declassified summary of STAR GATE operational tasking says that from 1986 through the first quarter of fiscal 1995 the program received more than 200 tasks from military organizations. In 1994, DIA created a numerical method for operational customers to evaluate accuracy and value. By May 1995, three viewers had responded to forty tasks from five organizations, yielding ninety-nine accuracy scores and one hundred value scores.

This is exactly the dataset a serious evaluation needs.

It is also a reminder of how late serious operational scoring arrived.

The terms "accuracy" and "value" should never be allowed to merge. A statement can be accurate and useless because the customer already knows it. It can be inaccurate yet accidentally useful by causing an analyst to check something important. It can be vague enough to earn partial accuracy while imposing a large interpretation burden. It can contain one remarkable detail buried among twenty false ones.

Intelligence utility is decision-relative.

A source is valuable when it changes what a competent customer should do.

That standard is brutal for remote viewing. If the output cannot be trusted without confirmation, and confirmation requires the same conventional collection systems the remote viewer is meant to supplement, then the method may add little. If the customer acts without confirmation, the cost of false positives becomes operational.

The 1995 review concluded that the program had not demonstrated adequate utility for intelligence operations. CIA's later public summary states there was no case in which ESP data had been used to guide intelligence operations in a way that established the case for continuation.

Program advocates have challenged versions of that conclusion, often citing satisfied customers or episodes where viewers allegedly provided useful leads. Those claims deserve case-level examination rather than dismissal by institutional fiat.

But the burden is asymmetric.

To justify an intelligence program, it is not enough to show that a customer once liked a report.

The program must show that the channel improves decisions often enough, and predictably enough, to justify contaminating the analytic process with it.
