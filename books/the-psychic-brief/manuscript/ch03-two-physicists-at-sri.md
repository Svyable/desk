# 3. Two Physicists at SRI

The story becomes stranger when it becomes respectable.

Stanford Research Institute was not a séance parlor. It was a contract-research organization in Menlo Park working across engineering, defense and technology. In the early 1970s, physicists Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ began experiments on purported psychic functioning, including work with Ingo Swann and Uri Geller. CIA money entered the picture.

A declassified 30 August 1973 memorandum announced a final presentation of SRI parapsychology studies and explicitly named Geller and Swann. The document is bureaucratically mundane. Conference room. Time. Distribution list. Attached report. That mundanity is important. An intelligence organization had moved the topic from gossip to contract deliverable.

The SRI work eventually emphasized "remote viewing": asking a subject separated from a target to describe a distant location or object. The label was brilliant whether intended as branding or not. It removed ghosts from the vocabulary. "Clairvoyance" sounded Victorian. "Remote viewing" sounded like a sensor mode.

Language changed institutional affordances. A remote viewer could be discussed alongside SIGINT, PHOTINT and HUMINT without requiring anybody to say soul, astral projection or occult. Army documents later did exactly this, screening intelligence personnel and describing the activity as another possible collection method.

The early SRI literature produced the dispute that would shadow the program to the end. Supporters pointed to blind judging, target matching and cases in which descriptions appeared improbably specific. Critics attacked cueing, sensory leakage, target-set construction, judging procedures, optional stopping, experimenter involvement and the flexibility with which qualitative material could be mapped to targets.

The distinction between fraud and artifact matters. A flawed experiment does not require dishonest scientists. Human beings are exceptionally capable of finding structure once they know what structure to seek. Qualitative descriptions make that ability dangerous. "Tall," "metallic," "open," "water," "angular," "movement": each word is weak alone and elastic in combination. If judges know the candidate targets, subtle correspondences can accumulate into conviction.

The most interesting SRI cases are therefore not those with the most theatrical subjects. They are those that survive attempts to remove human interpretive freedom.

This was recognized inside the program. Over time, the research grew scoring systems, judging protocols, randomization schemes, decoys, rank-order methods and attempts to quantify performance. The bureaucracy wanted a number because a number travels better than a sketch of a crane.

But quantification introduced a second trap. A statistically significant laboratory result can coexist with terrible single-trial reliability. If an effect is slightly above chance across many trials, it may satisfy a scientific question while being useless to an intelligence officer who needs to know which warehouse contains the hostage tonight.

This mismatch would become central in 1995, when Jessica Utts and Ray Hyman could disagree sharply about what the laboratory record established while the practical conclusion still converged toward non-usefulness.

SRI did not merely put psychics in a lab.

It created a recurring institutional category error: the possibility that a weak statistical anomaly could be mistaken for a fieldable sensor.
