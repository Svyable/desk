# Notes on an Unclosed Case

The September 22, 1979 Vela event remains an open historical-technical problem in this edition. That is a deliberate conclusion, not an unfinished sentence.

The strongest public case for nuclear origin begins with the optical waveform itself and is strengthened by the fact that multiple U.S. intelligence and weapons-laboratory actors continued to favor that interpretation after the White House scientific review. Later peer-reviewed work by Christopher Wright and Lars-Erik De Geer argues that hydroacoustic records and iodine-131 observations add independent support. Those analyses are serious and are included in the research trail. They are not presented here as universally accepted closure.

The strongest public case against nuclear origin begins with anomalies in the two Vela bhangmeter traces and the failure of other contemporary systems to provide a decisive, broadly accepted corroborating signature. The Ruina panel concluded that a non-nuclear event was more probable and considered a satellite-local meteoroid/debris explanation. That proposed mechanism was not directly observed or reconstructed from recovered material.

The book’s position should change if discriminating evidence changes. The research package records specific defeat conditions rather than preserving ambiguity for atmosphere.

## Research architecture

The accompanying `research/` directory contains the source log, evidence ledger, chronology, contradiction and incentive audit, and defeat conditions. Links point to original papers, NASA and NNSA histories, CTBTO material, IAEA records, and declassified documents assembled by the National Security Archive where available.

The source trail intentionally distinguishes a document that records an official belief from a document that proves the object of that belief. It also distinguishes later peer-reviewed reconstruction from contemporaneous measurement.

## Language

“Vela incident,” “South Atlantic flash,” “double flash,” and “Event 747” refer to the September 22, 1979 optical event and its investigation. None of those terms implies nuclear origin by itself.

“Gamma-ray burst” refers to the astrophysical transient class that emerged from Vela high-energy observations beginning with the July 2, 1967 event and the 1973 publication by Klebesadel, Strong, and Olson. The book uses later astrophysical knowledge where relevant but distinguishes hindsight from what observers knew at the time.

“Bhangmeter” refers to the optical radiometer used to identify the characteristic time history of atmospheric nuclear detonations. The name is historical and informal; the measurement problem is not.
