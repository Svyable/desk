## Thesis

On 22 September 1979 a Vela satellite recorded the kind of double flash it had been built to recognize as an atmospheric nuclear explosion. What followed was not merely a mystery about whether a bomb went off. It was a collision between instruments, institutions, secrecy, politics, aging hardware, missing corroboration, and the uncomfortable fact that a state sometimes has to act before its sensors have earned certainty. *At 00:53* reconstructs the Vela Incident as a case study in how technological societies decide what happened when the evidence is classified, multimodal, contradictory, and consequential.

The book does **not** presume that the flash was a nuclear test or that Israel, South Africa, or any other state conducted one. It asks a narrower and harder question: what would it take to know?

## Evidence rule

Every consequential claim is assigned to one of four classes: documented/verified; credible but incomplete; disputed/unconfirmed; speculative synthesis. Sensor detection, event classification, yield, location, actor attribution, motive, and policy response are separate propositions. No rung inherits certainty from the rung below it.
