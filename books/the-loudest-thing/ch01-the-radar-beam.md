# The Radar Beam

For a few minutes at a time, Earth becomes much easier to discover.

Not because the oceans brighten. Not because eight billion people switch on their phones. Not because a distant astronomer resolves Manhattan, hears television, notices the International Space Station, or finds a blue marble with suspicious straight lines scratched across it.

A radar transmitter points upward.

That is enough.

This is an awkward place to begin a book about extraterrestrial technology because the technology belongs to us. That is exactly why I want to begin here. The search for technological life elsewhere has spent decades with a peculiar freedom: we can imagine transmitters larger than continents, waste heat from star-enclosing structures, industrial atmospheres unlike any atmosphere humans have yet produced, probes waiting in the Solar System, engines bright enough to rewrite spectra. Some of these are excellent ideas. Some are wonderfully economical ways to spend telescope time. None is an observation of an alien civilization.

Earth is.

We have one technological planet available for calibration. One. It is noisy in some directions and almost embarrassingly quiet in others. Its most detectable signs are not necessarily its most important technologies. Its largest cities are not its loudest astronomical objects. Its intelligence does not scale cleanly with the distance at which that intelligence can be inferred. The civilization that built a global computer network can, from far enough away, disappear more completely than a radar pulse intended to measure an asteroid.

In 2025 Sofia Sheikh and colleagues tried an experiment that SETI badly needed: Earth detecting Earth. Instead of asking how far away an arbitrarily advanced extraterrestrial technology might be visible, they asked how far away present-day human technology could be detected with present-day human instruments. They compared radio, atmospheric, optical, infrared, orbital and surface technosignatures in one framework.

The result was not a tidy ladder of civilization.

It was a range spanning thirteen orders of magnitude.

Intermittent planetary radar won.

That sentence should bother us a little. Planetary radar is not the essence of humanity. It is not even the essence of human technology. It is a particular application that happens to concentrate radio power, direction and time in a way astronomy can exploit. If an extraterrestrial observer happened to sit inside the beam geometry, at the right moment, with an appropriate receiver, our planet could announce itself across distances enormously greater than many persistent signatures we casually think of as more representative of civilization.

The loudest thing about us is not necessarily the truest thing about us.

That creates the problem of this book.

SETI is usually described as a search problem. Where should we point? Which frequency? Which star? Which anomaly? But before it is a search problem it is an inverse problem. We observe some radiation, chemistry, heat, timing, geometry or object and try to reconstruct the hidden process that made it. The same problem appears in intelligence analysis, medical imaging, seismology and astronomy. Effects arrive. Causes do not.

Technology makes the inverse problem nastier because technology is contingent. Biology is contingent too, but a technological signature can vanish because a civilization improves a lamp, changes an energy source, stops using a pollutant, moves communications into fiber, narrows a beam, encrypts a carrier, abandons broadcasting, migrates computation, or simply decides not to transmit in our direction. Technological evolution can erase its own observability without erasing technology.

We know this because we have watched ourselves do it.

Consider radio leakage. The old popular picture imagined Earth wrapped in an expanding sphere of I Love Lucy and news broadcasts, a shell of culture washing over nearby stars. There really is electromagnetic radiation leaving Earth. The harder question is whether another Earth could recover it at useful distance. Power, bandwidth, directionality, interference, integration time and receiver capability matter. `We emit radio` and `we are detectable by radio` are not interchangeable propositions.

Planetary radar is different because it cheats in the astronomer's favor. It concentrates enormous effective radiated power toward a celestial target. The beam is narrow. That makes it spectacularly detectable from some locations and nonexistent from most others. A civilization can therefore be both loud and silent at the same time.

Call this the geometry tax.

It will recur throughout the book. Every claimed technosignature has one. Atmospheric pollution requires a planet with an atmosphere, a molecule with useful spectral bands, sufficient abundance, favorable stellar and planetary conditions, and an instrument able to separate the signal from chemistry that nature can make. Artificial illumination requires contrast. Waste heat requires temperature and area. Megastructures require geometry. Radio requires frequency, power and timing. Artifacts require that we look where the artifact is, at the scale on which it differs from rock.

The geometry tax is not an argument against SETI. It is an argument against converting a nondetection into a census.

In 2025 the third confirmed interstellar object, 3I/ATLAS, entered the Solar System. Its observed behavior and composition were comet-like. That did not make a technosignature search foolish. Quite the opposite. An interstellar object was here. Telescopes existed. A transmitter, if present, would be testable.

The Allen Telescope Array observed 3I/ATLAS for more than seven hours across 1–9 GHz. The analysis began with roughly seventy-four million narrowband detections. Filtering reduced those to about two hundred candidates requiring inspection. All were attributable to human technology—terrestrial interference or our own satellites. No extraterrestrial transmitter was found. The observations nevertheless did something scientifically valuable: for the searched frequencies and geometry, they excluded transmitters above roughly 10–110 watts equivalent isotropic radiated power on or near the object.

That is what a good negative result looks like.

It has units.

It has a frequency range.

It has a target.

It has an observing interval.

It has an instrument.

It has a threshold.

It does not say `3I/ATLAS contains no technology anywhere in any form`. It says what this search would have seen and did not see. The difference is the difference between science and mood.

There is another uncomfortable fact hidden in those seventy-four million detections. The overwhelming technological intelligence in the dataset was us.

We contaminate our own search for technology because technology is everywhere near the detector. Satellites cross beams. Ground transmitters leak. Electronics create artifacts. A candidate technosignature can become more exciting precisely as the chain of mundane explanations becomes harder to reconstruct. The search for another civilization therefore begins with an anthropology of our own machines.

This is useful beyond radio.

Take chlorofluorocarbons. CFCs are attractive technosignatures because their terrestrial abundance is a consequence of technology. Modeling work has asked whether CFC-11 and CFC-12 could be detected in the atmosphere of a habitable exoplanet, particularly around favorable M dwarfs. Other work has proposed artificial greenhouse gases such as SF6 and perfluorocarbons as potentially lower-false-positive signatures, even imagining deliberate climate engineering or terraforming.

The temptation is immediate: find the molecule, find the civilization.

No.

Find the molecule first.

Then establish abundance. Then atmospheric context. Then photochemistry. Then plausible abiotic pathways. Then retrieval robustness. Then ask what technological process could maintain the observed inventory. The word `artificial` in a modeling paper is not permission to skip the atmosphere.

This book will keep returning to that ladder because technosignatures have the same epistemic vulnerability as every anomalous field I care about: a measurement acquires a story faster than the story acquires independent evidence.

A narrowband signal becomes a transmitter. A transmitter becomes an intelligence. An unusual atmosphere becomes industry. Excess infrared becomes engineering. A strange trajectory becomes a probe. A vanished star becomes a civilization. Each step may be reasonable. None is free.

There is a reverse error too. Skepticism can become just as lazy when it treats ambiguity as nullity. If a candidate has natural confounders, the conclusion is not that technology is impossible. It is that the experiment has not yet separated the models. If a radio search sees nothing, the conclusion is not that nobody is there. It is that nobody there was doing the detectable thing during the detectable interval above the detectable threshold.

The denominator matters.

This becomes particularly strange when we turn the telescope back toward Earth. An extraterrestrial survey that searched only for our strongest radio behavior might classify us as detectable during rare beam alignments and absent otherwise. A survey looking for certain atmospheric industrial products could infer technology from molecules humans are actively trying to eliminate. A survey optimized for city lights might miss us at distances where radar gives us away. A survey looking for colossal waste heat might conclude—correctly—that Earth is not a Kardashev Type II civilization, then quietly allow that correct statement to become the incorrect statement that Earth is not technological.

Search design contains a theory of civilization whether the observer admits it or not.

That is the collision I want to follow: technosignature astronomy meets the history of our own technological visibility. Instead of beginning with Dyson spheres and working backward toward instruments, begin with Earth and work outward. What parts of us survive distance? Which technologies are astronomical by accident? Which signatures persist after the civilization that made them changes? Which are episodic? Which require intent? Which look technological only because our own history made them familiar?

And, most importantly, what exactly have we excluded when we look and find nothing?

The answer is often smaller than the headline.

That is not disappointing. A precisely bounded absence is one of the most useful things an instrument can produce. Stack enough bounded absences and the possibility space begins to acquire a shape.

We have spent a century becoming harder to summarize even from inside the civilization. There is no reason to expect the universe to summarize us correctly from fifty light-years away.

A radar beam leaves Earth.

For a moment, it makes the problem easier.
