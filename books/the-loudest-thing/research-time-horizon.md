# Research audit — the 12,000-light-year radar horizon is not a present footprint

## Why this matters

Chapter 1 is built around the excellent asymmetry in Sheikh et al. (2025): targeted planetary radar has an enormous maximum detection distance compared with other terrestrial technosignatures. But the opening currently risks collapsing three different quantities into one: modeled maximum range, the transmitter's historical operating interval, and the volume of space that its emissions have actually reached.

The paper itself supplies the correction.

## Primary-source check

Sheikh et al., *The Astronomical Journal* 169, 118 (2025), DOI 10.3847/1538-3881/ada3c7:

https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ada3c7

The paper defines its Earth-detecting-Earth exercise as an Earth-2024-level comparison and uses an SNR=5 threshold for maximum detectability. For its radio cases it reports:

- planetary radar: **12,000 light-years** maximum modeled detection distance;
- Deep Space Network: **65 light-years**;
- LTE: **4.0 light-years**;
- Voyager: **0.97 light-years**.

But the authors immediately qualify the first two distances: they exceed the number of years those systems have been transmitting, so signals from those systems **have not yet reached those distances**. The 12,000-light-year figure is therefore a sensitivity horizon for a compatible transmitter/receiver geometry, not the current radius of an Arecibo-generated technosignature shell.

There is a second temporal wrinkle useful to the book. The paper identifies the 2.4 GHz Arecibo planetary-radar system as the strongest radio transmitter ever built. Arecibo's 305 m telescope collapsed on 1 December 2020. Thus `Earth-2024-level` in this comparison should not be paraphrased too literally as `hardware Earth is operating right now`; the framework deliberately uses terrestrial technological capability/examples, including a historically real transmitter that no longer operates.

Authoritative Arecibo record:

https://www.nsf.gov/news/arecibo-observatorys-305-meter-telescope-collapses

## Editorial consequence

Keep the 12,000-light-year result, but make it do more work. It demonstrates **potential detectability conditional on beam interception and receiver sensitivity**, not current galactic visibility. The opening's present-tense image (`A radar transmitter points upward`) should either be tied to an operating planetary-radar system or explicitly become a historical/model scene using Arecibo.

A useful distinction for later prose is:

**range is not reach**.

`Range` asks how far a signal could be detected once it arrives. `Reach` asks how far the signal has propagated so far. A third quantity, `coverage`, asks what fraction of directions/times the narrow beam actually illuminated. The book already has the geometry tax; adding the time axis prevents maximum-range plots from quietly becoming maps of actual exposure.

This also improves the proposed `civilization silhouette`: an observer's silhouette of Earth depends not only on distance and wavelength but on **when** the observer is located in the expanding causal history of each technology. An alien astronomer 12,000 light-years away cannot currently see twentieth-century Arecibo transmissions because those transmissions have thousands of years left to travel.

## Strongest counterargument

The paper is not making the mistake. It explicitly labels these values maximum detectable distances and explicitly warns that the strongest signals have not propagated to those horizons. The risk lies in popular or manuscript paraphrase. Nor does Arecibo's collapse invalidate the comparison: the study is intentionally asking what Earth-level technology can do while minimizing extrapolation, not producing a live inventory of every transmitter operating on 3 February 2025.

So the manuscript should not attack the 12,000-light-year number. It should use the authors' own qualification to sharpen the book's central claim: even a perfectly valid detectability number can answer the wrong question if its time dimension is dropped.

## Publication holds

- Do not write that present-day Earth is detectable by Arecibo-like radar at 12,000 light-years without distinguishing modeled maximum range from present signal reach.
- Do not describe Arecibo planetary radar as currently operating.
- Do not infer a probability of interception from maximum range. Beam width, pointing history, duty cycle and observer location are separate denominators.
- Before giving an actual present-day propagation radius for a named radar system, reconstruct its relevant transmission history rather than substituting telescope age or a single inauguration date.
