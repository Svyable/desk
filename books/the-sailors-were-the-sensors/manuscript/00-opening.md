# 1. The Gargle Sample

The machine had samplers.

The men had mouths.

That is the detail I cannot get past.

During some of the shipboard tests now grouped under SHAD, sailors were asked for nasal swabs or throat gargles. Decades later, when investigators tried to explain why, the surviving account was almost absurdly revealing. The biological samples appear to have been an informal supplement to the mechanical samplers distributed through the ship. Human throats could help tell the testers whether an aerosol had penetrated where the instruments said it had penetrated. In at least one test, samples from men wearing masks were used to evaluate the masks.

And then the link broke.

The Autumn Gold report, according to the Defense Department's later public account, contains the readings. It does not link them to the crew members who supplied them.

The measurement survived.

The measured person did not.

Not literally. The sailors went on living their lives. What disappeared was the connection between a body and a number.

Forty years later that missing join would become more important than almost anything the original experimenters could have imagined.

Project 112 was a classified American chemical and biological warfare test program conducted during the Cold War. Its shipboard portion became known as SHAD: Shipboard Hazard and Defense. The engineering question was brutal but legible. If an enemy dispersed a biological or chemical agent against a U.S. warship, where would it go? Through ventilation? Across weather decks? Into compartments assumed to be protected? Would masks work? Would decontamination work? Could the ship keep fighting?

Those are instrumentable questions. Release something. Measure something. Put samplers in known locations. Change a condition. Measure again.

A warship is already an experiment waiting to happen. It is a steel geometry full of ducts, pressure boundaries, fans, hatches, ladders, intakes, hot machinery, wet men, electrical cabinets, sleeping compartments and bad places to stand. It has coordinates. It has decks. It has compartments. It has airflow. You can draw it.

A sailor is harder.

A sailor moves.

He goes below. He comes up. He smokes. He removes a mask because it is miserable. He wears it correctly. He wears it incorrectly. He is twenty-one. He is thirty-eight. He has asthma nobody wrote down. He transfers ships. He leaves the Navy. He develops heart disease in 1996. Or does not. He remembers a cloud. Or remembers being told there was no cloud. He remembers a washdown. The roster survives but the station bill does not. The test report survives but the throat swab is anonymous.

The ship was the object under test.

The sailor was part of the test environment.

That distinction is the whole book.

It would be easy to write SHAD as a secret-experiment horror story. There is enough real secrecy to make embellishment unnecessary. It would also be easy to write the opposite book: decades of suspicion followed by epidemiology that did not discover a clear SHAD disease signature. Both versions are too clean.

The government eventually had to reconstruct the test universe because veterans wanted to know what had happened to them. Congress wanted names. The Department of Veterans Affairs needed exposure information. The Department of Defense had to search records that were roughly four decades old, distributed across services and facilities, some still classified, some missing, some never created in the form later investigators needed. Deseret Test Center, the organization that had run the program, had closed in the 1970s.

GAO's 2004 audit is a document about what happens when an experiment changes owners after everyone has gone home.

DOD had identified 5,842 service members as potentially exposed during Project 112 by its June 2003 report to Congress. But GAO concluded that more people probably remained unidentified. For twenty-one of thirty-one land-based tests, DOD had identified no potentially exposed service members at all because the needed records could not be found. GAO itself found additional personnel and additional repositories worth searching. Veterans who had not appeared in the government's reconstruction contacted VA on their own.

This is not proof of a purge. Old military records go missing for banal reasons with depressing regularity. It is also not a trivial clerical defect. If you are trying to estimate a dose forty years after an aerosol test, a missing roster is part of the experiment now.

The archive has become an instrument.

And like any instrument, it has a detection threshold.

That is the first move this book will make repeatedly: stop treating missing information as atmosphere. Ask what measurement it prevents.

If a sailor's name is missing, what inference dies with it?

If his name survives but his location does not, what survives?

If location survives but concentration does not?

If concentration exists at a mechanical sampler but not at his breathing zone?

If a throat sample exists but is not linked to him?

If the test used a simulant regarded at the time as harmless, what toxicological knowledge existed then and what exists now?

If a cohort study forty years later finds no clear specific pattern of long-term illness, what size effect could it actually have detected given the exposure uncertainty?

Those questions are less cinematic than a secret cloud drifting across a destroyer.

They are also where the truth lives.

## The experiment after the experiment

In 2007 the Institute of Medicine published a long-term health study of SHAD participants. It compared participants with nonparticipant controls. The broad result did not deliver the ending either side might have wanted.

There was no significant difference in all-cause mortality. The study did find higher heart-disease mortality among participants, but the authors emphasized missing cardiovascular risk-factor data and weak biological interpretation. Participants generally reported worse health, but the investigators did not find a consistent, specific, clinically significant pattern of illness. Intriguingly, the group with potential exposure to active chemical or biological agents reported the smallest differences.

Then comes the sentence that should be carved into every archive-based health controversy:

No clear evidence of a specific effect is not clear evidence of no effect.

That is not a loophole. It is statistics.

A later National Academies follow-up, using additional years of mortality and health-care data, again found no significant overall difference in health between SHAD veterans and comparable nonparticipants within the limits of the available data. The earlier heart-disease signal did not persist as an elevated risk in the later follow-up.

This matters. Negative evidence is evidence. A responsible book cannot preserve every feared health effect forever by moving it into the unmeasured shadows.

But the inverse mistake is just as serious. A cohort assembled decades after exposure, from incomplete records, cannot become a time machine simply because its conclusion is reassuring.

The proper question is not whether the study said yes or no.

It is: what causal histories were still distinguishable by the time the study began?

Imagine two ships.

On the first, every sailor wears a dosimeter-like biological badge. His location is logged continuously. Mask state is recorded. Air concentration at his breathing zone is known. Acute symptoms are captured prospectively. Blood and urine are banked. Every later diagnosis can be linked to an exposure estimate with uncertainty bars.

On the second, the ship has excellent environmental samplers. Some sailors provide gargles. The readings are preserved. The names are not attached.

Forty years later, these are not the same experiment.

Even if the cloud was identical.

Even if nobody became sick.

Even if everybody became sick.

The ability to know is different.

## Epidemiological debt

I want a term for that difference.

Call it **epidemiological debt**.

An institution incurs epidemiological debt when it creates an exposure today without preserving enough person-level information to evaluate the health consequences tomorrow.

Like financial debt, it can remain invisible while everything is going well. The test ends. The report is written. The tactical question is answered. The unit rotates home. The program closes. The boxes move to a warehouse.

Then interest begins.

People age.

Diseases with long latency appear.

Diagnostic categories change.

Personnel databases change.

Names are misspelled.

Records are destroyed under ordinary schedules. Contractors disappear. Classification prevents participants from discussing what they remember. A scientist who knew why the samples were taken dies. The person who designed the sampler is no longer reachable. A veteran calls the VA and says he was there, but the government's list says he was not.

Now every missing field costs more than it would have cost to record in 1965.

This does not mean the original program secretly intended to experiment on human beings. That question has to be answered test by test, not by moral vocabulary imported backward through time. It means something narrower and stranger: once humans occupy an instrumented weapons environment, the distinction between *testing the ship* and *exposing the crew* can become administratively convenient long before it becomes biologically meaningful.

The aerosol does not care what the protocol calls the sailor.

His lungs do not know whether he is a subject, operator, bystander, control, crewman, sampler, or classified line item.

That is why the throat gargle matters.

For a moment, the program treated a human body as a sensor.

Then it kept the sensor reading and lost the sensor's name.

Everything after that is interest.
