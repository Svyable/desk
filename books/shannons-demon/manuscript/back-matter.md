# Back Matter

*Shannon’s Demon* is a work of narrative nonfiction about Claude E. Shannon and the intellectual world around information theory. The book is written for general readers, but its factual claims are meant to remain traceable to the historical record.

The research trail for this Desk manuscript is kept alongside the book rather than hidden behind the finished prose. The source ledger distinguishes primary papers, archival records, institutional histories, oral history, later biography, technical retrospectives, and explicit research gaps. That distinction matters unusually much with Shannon because his reputation has attracted stories that become cleaner each time they are repeated.

Several of the most memorable anecdotes require exactly that kind of care.

The Ultimate Machine is a good example. The working box became associated with Shannon because he built versions of it and kept one on his desk, where visitors including Arthur C. Clarke encountered it. Later recollections attribute the underlying idea to Marvin Minsky. The book therefore treats the object as a collaboration of idea and implementation rather than turning it into one more solitary-Shannon invention.

The same discipline applies to the famous story that John von Neumann advised Shannon to call his measure “entropy.” The anecdote is widespread and appealing, but its surviving attribution chain is much later than the event it supposedly describes. The manuscript treats that story as a later recollection rather than documentary proof of a conversation.

Shannon’s 1943 contact with Alan Turing is another boundary case. The two men did meet and talk. Shannon’s own later oral history is the controlling evidence used here, including his statement that they did not exchange cryptographic secrets. The book does not reconstruct classified technical conversations for which the record is absent.

The technical chapters follow a similar rule. Shannon’s papers control claims about what Shannon proved or proposed. Later technologies are described as descendants, applications, or extensions only when the lineage can be stated without giving Shannon credit for engineering work performed by other people. Information theory supplied limits and abstractions; coding theorists, computer scientists, communications engineers, chip designers, network architects, and many other researchers built the systems that followed.

The book also resists using information theory as a universal metaphor.

Shannon himself warned about this. His 1956 editorial “The Bandwagon” argued that the successful language of information theory should not be exported carelessly into other fields. That warning is especially important in a twenty-first-century biography written after words such as *entropy*, *signal*, *noise*, *information*, and *channel* have become ordinary metaphors.

In this manuscript, a metaphor remains a metaphor unless the mathematics is actually being used.

That is why the chapter on Shannon’s final illness does not treat Alzheimer’s disease as “entropy” in any technical Shannon sense. It is also why the closing argument about play and optimization is presented as an interpretation of the documented life rather than as a doctrine Shannon himself wrote down.

The title *Shannon’s Demon* refers to the rebalancing phenomenon associated with Shannon in later investment literature, not to a claim that Shannon created a complete theory of investing. The relevant chapter separates the mechanical effect of rebalancing from the Kelly criterion and from later portfolio practice. Edward O. Thorp’s accounts are used heavily for the roulette and gambling material because he was a participant in that work.

Readers who want to inspect the evidence behind this Desk draft can begin with `research/README.md` and `research/source-ledger.csv`. Those files are part of the working publication package and are intended to preserve the difference between what is known, what is inferred, what is retrospective, and what remains uncertain.

That distinction is not a formality.

Claude Shannon’s most important work was powerful because it discarded details deliberately. Biography has the opposite danger. Discard the wrong detail and a real person becomes a parable.

This book tries to keep enough of the mess.
