Claude Shannon and the Invention of the Digital Mind.

The working thesis is that one obscure, playful, almost pathologically curious engineer wrote the paper that the entire rest of this catalog quietly assumes: that information can be measured, that a bit is a real physical unit rather than a metaphor, and that any channel — a telephone wire, a fiber strand, a strand of DNA, a transformer's attention weights — has a hard mathematical ceiling on how much it can carry. Claude Shannon proved that ceiling exists, told engineers exactly where it was, and then went home to build a machine that juggled.

The book follows Shannon from a barbed-wire telegraph line strung between two Michigan farmhouses to a 1948 paper that reorganized telecommunications, computing, cryptography, and eventually machine learning around a single unit he did not even name. It stays close to the record: his 1937 master's thesis wiring Boolean algebra into physical relay circuits, an unpublished doctoral detour into the algebra of genetics, wartime cryptography work, a Las Vegas casino floor where he and a young mathematician named Edward Thorp field-tested one of the first wearable computers, and a lecture-hall demonstration that a portfolio of two money-losing assets can be rebalanced into a winning one — the trick investors still call Shannon's Demon.

The aim is not hagiography. Shannon's genius came bundled with a documented aversion to publishing, to public life, and eventually to almost everything but juggling balls and unicycles in a house his own students nicknamed Entropy House. The book treats that as part of the argument: a man who measured uncertainty for a living seems to have concluded, in his own life, that not everything worth doing needs to be optimized, defended, or even finished. For a 2027 reader living downstream of language models trained by minimizing the very quantity Shannon defined, that is not a footnote. It is close to the whole point.
