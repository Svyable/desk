# THE LISTENING FIELD

*When vegetation becomes intelligence infrastructure*

Sven Hardy Benson

A plant is not a green animal with bad mobility. It does not need a brain to detect light, gravity, touch, injury, water, chemistry, heat, pathogens or the arrival of an insect with lunch in mind. It already lives inside a dense traffic of signals. The stranger development is that humans have begun trying to read that traffic for purposes the plant never evolved to serve.

This book is about that translation. It is not a book arguing that plants are conscious, telepathic, psychic, secretly intelligent in the human sense, or waiting to testify. Those claims have accumulated around plant biology for more than a century, sometimes because real plant capabilities are startling enough that metaphor outruns mechanism. I will not use that ambiguity as fuel.

The evidence is kept in four bins throughout the research trail. **Documented** means an official record, reproducible experimental result, peer-reviewed finding, or other primary evidence supports the narrow statement being made. **Credible but incomplete** means the source or observation deserves investigation but important denominators, controls, replication, provenance or operational details remain missing. **Disputed** means a claim is materially contested or lacks independent confirmation. **Speculative** means I am building a hypothesis, analogy or proposed test rather than reporting a finding.

The central collision is real enough without embellishment. Plant physiology has established long-distance electrical, calcium, chemical and hydraulic signaling. Engineers have turned plants into substrates for sensors. DARPA publicly pursued Advanced Plant Technologies: engineered plants intended to detect chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear and explosive threats and report responses remotely. A newer DARPA effort, eX Virentia, asks whether *unmodified* vegetation can reveal human activity through natural biochemical responses observable at a distance.

That progression changes the question. The first military plant sensor asks: can we build a detector into a living organism? The second asks something more interesting: was the detector already there?

Neither question licenses the phrase “plants are spying on us.” A biochemical response is not an intelligence report. A stress response is not identification. Detection is not attribution. Remote observability is not operational reliability. A correlation acquired after the fact is not a fielded sensor. The distance between those statements is where most of this book lives.
