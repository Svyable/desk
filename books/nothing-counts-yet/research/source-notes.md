# Source Notes — Nothing Counts Yet

These notes are a research map for revision and publication review. The manuscript is an original synthesis and should not treat any single literature as proof of the entire thesis.

## 1. Deliberate practice and expertise

**K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe, Clemens Tesch-Römer — “The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance,” Psychological Review (1993).**

Foundational account of effortful practice organized around improvement rather than mere repetition. Useful for Chapters 1, 3, 7, and 20. Later literature has debated how much variance deliberate practice explains across domains, so revision should avoid turning the concept into a universal monocausal theory of expertise.

DOI: 10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363

## 2. Error management training

**Nina Keith and Michael Frese — “Effectiveness of Error Management Training: A Meta-Analysis,” Journal of Applied Psychology (2008).**

Meta-analysis of 24 studies (N=2,183) reported a positive overall effect for error-management training and larger effects for post-training and adaptive transfer than for performance during training. Especially relevant to Chapters 4, 9, 10, and 19 because it supports the distinction between looking good during practice and learning to handle novel situations later.

DOI: 10.1037/0021-9010.93.1.59

## 3. Simulation plus deliberate practice

**William C. McGaghie, S. Barry Issenberg, Elaine R. Cohen, Jeffrey H. Barsuk, Diane B. Wayne — “Does Simulation-Based Medical Education With Deliberate Practice Yield Better Results Than Traditional Clinical Education?” Academic Medicine (2011).**

Meta-analysis comparing simulation-based medical education with deliberate practice against traditional clinical education. Useful for the book's claim that designed substitutes can let learners acquire difficult skills before full exposure to patients.

DOI: 10.1097/ACM.0b013e318217e119

## 4. Mastery learning and staged progression

**Ryan Brydges et al. — “Mastery Learning for Health Professionals Using Technology-Enhanced Simulation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis,” Academic Medicine (2013).**

Useful for Chapters 10 and 20 because mastery learning makes progression contingent on reaching a defined standard rather than merely completing a fixed amount of practice. It also highlights a recurring tradeoff in the manuscript: better preparation can require more time.

PMID: 23807104

## 5. Rapid-cycle deliberate practice

**Taras et al. — “Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice in Medical Education: A Systematic Review” (2017).**

The “pause, debrief, rewind and try again” pattern is a strong real-world analogue for Chapter 3's claim that interruption is a mechanism of practice and Chapter 15's proposed AI rehearsal mode.

PMID: 28540142

## 6. Cognitive apprenticeship

**Allan Collins, John Seely Brown, Susan Newman — cognitive apprenticeship literature.**

Use in Chapter 7 revision to ground the idea that novices acquire tacit strategies through modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, and gradually reduced support. The manuscript's phrase “responsibility is staged” should be connected carefully to this broader literature rather than presented as a wholly new pedagogical discovery.

## 7. Psychological safety — adjacent, not identical

**Amy Edmondson — research on psychological safety and interpersonal risk-taking in teams.**

Relevant to the social conditions that allow people to ask naive questions, report errors, and reveal uncertainty. The book should preserve its distinction: psychological safety describes an interpersonal climate; **provisional infrastructure** is broader and includes consequence design, reversibility, data status, simulated environments, supervised responsibility, and explicit gates into evaluation.

## 8. Play and exploratory learning

Research traditions around play, constructionism, exploratory learning, and discovery learning are relevant to Chapter 8. Revision should avoid the simplistic claim that unstructured exploration is always superior to guided learning. The book's stronger claim is architectural: when the destination is genuinely unknown, systems need bounded spaces in which the outcome is not fully specified in advance.

## 9. Sandboxes, pilots, and reversibility

Software staging environments, feature flags, regulatory sandboxes, policy pilots, tabletop exercises, red teams, and pre-mortems provide applied analogues for Chapters 16 and 18. These literatures should be used to sharpen the book's three practice-design dimensions:

- **reversibility** — how cheaply can the attempt be undone?
- **exposure** — who bears the cost if it goes wrong?
- **transfer** — does success here predict performance there?

## 10. Recording, context collapse, and persistence

Research on networked publics, context collapse, searchable persistence, surveillance, workplace analytics, and adolescent development should inform Chapters 11 and 14. Revision should keep the central nuance: recording can create accountability and protect vulnerable people; the developmental problem appears when provisional artifacts lose the status and context that explain how they should be interpreted.

## 11. AI as rehearsal partner

The AI chapters should be revised against emerging research on AI tutoring, role-play, simulation, adaptive testing, learning transfer, automation bias, skill atrophy, and privacy of educational/workplace data. The manuscript's key falsifiable question is not whether AI improves output, but whether a given interaction design increases **human transfer** when assistance is reduced or removed.

A useful product-research agenda follows directly:

1. attempt-before-hint interfaces;
2. difficulty that adapts upward as performance improves;
3. simulated disagreement and varied counterpart behavior;
4. explicit practice versus assessment modes;
5. private developmental data with limited retention;
6. withdrawal tests that measure what remains without assistance.

## 12. Novelty / title sanity check

During drafting on 2026-09-03, web searches were run for the exact title **“Nothing Counts Yet”** together with terms including *book*, *practice*, *failure*, and *rehearsal*, and for adjacent phrases around “hidden infrastructure” and practice.

No book using the exact title for this thesis surfaced in those searches. The phrase itself does occur generically—for example in sports/preseason and practice contexts—which is unsurprising and part of its intuitive appeal. Absolute novelty cannot be established by a web search; the defensible claim is that the current search did not surface an existing book organized around this synthesis.

## 13. Automation, out-of-the-loop performance, and skill degradation

**Linda Onnasch, Christopher D. Wickens, Huiyang Li, Dietrich Manzey — “Human Performance Consequences of Stages and Levels of Automation: An Integrated Meta-Analysis,” Human Factors (2014).**

Useful for Chapter 23. Automation can improve routine performance while changing workload, situation awareness, and human performance when intervention becomes necessary. The chapter should therefore avoid claiming that automation automatically causes deskilling; the stronger claim is that task allocation changes what humans repeatedly practice, which can create capability risk if the remaining human role still requires skills no longer exercised in ordinary work.

DOI: 10.1177/0018720813501549

**“Autopilot, Mind Wandering, and the Out of the Loop Performance Problem” (Frontiers in Neuroscience, 2017).**

Review of the out-of-the-loop problem in highly automated systems, including vigilance decrement, reduced comprehension, and difficulty taking back control when automation behaves unexpectedly. This is a direct research analogue for Chapter 23’s argument that institutions cannot rely on rare emergency handoffs to preserve a skill that routine work no longer rehearses.

PMID: 29051723 · DOI: 10.3389/fnins.2017.00541

## 14. Speaking up, error reporting, and the secret curriculum

**“Individual Characteristics That Promote or Prevent Psychological Safety and Error Reporting in Healthcare: A Systematic Review” (2023).**

A systematic review of 28 studies found that fear/self-preservation, perceptions of leadership and organization, confidence, experience, and knowledge all shape decisions to speak up or report errors. Useful for Chapter 24 because it supports a bounded version of the chapter’s claim: people learn the real rules of an institution partly by observing what happens when someone reveals uncertainty, reports a problem, or challenges authority.

PMID: 37091553

**Wawersik & Palaganas — “Organizational Factors That Promote Error Reporting in Healthcare: A Scoping Review” (2022).**

Relevant to the institutional side of Chapter 24. The chapter should not reduce speaking up to individual bravery; organizational design, leadership behavior, response to reports, and psychological safety affect whether concerns surface.

DOI: 10.1097/JHM-D-21-00166

## 15. Data retention, forgetting, and developmental records

**U.S. Department of Education, Student Privacy Policy Office — “Data Retention and Data Destruction” guidance (2025).**

Useful as a concrete adjacent example for Chapter 22: mature data governance distinguishes between information that must be retained and information that should be deleted when its purpose has ended. The manuscript should not generalize this into a universal legal rule. Its claim is architectural: systems should make retention periods, data purpose, and transitions from developmental to evaluative use explicit rather than treating indefinite persistence as the default.

## 16. Expertise, status, and becoming a beginner again

Chapter 21 is primarily a conceptual chapter about the social cost of visible learning after status has accumulated. Its strongest defensible form does not require a universal empirical claim that experts always resist learning. It argues instead that institutions should notice and reduce avoidable status penalties attached to asking basic questions, entering novice roles, and updating publicly. This section should stay connected to psychological-safety and identity literature without pretending that a single study proves the chapter’s full account.

## Revision standard

The book should not claim that practice, rehearsal, simulation, apprenticeship, play, pilots, learning from error, psychological safety, data minimization, or automation-related skill risks are new discoveries. They are not.

The originality claim is the **category and synthesis**:

> Human capability depends on provisional infrastructure that separates becoming from judgment; modern recording, ranking, optimization, and automation are changing that infrastructure; AI can either erode it further or make high-quality rehearsal radically abundant.

That thesis should be tested against counterexamples during revision, not merely illustrated.
