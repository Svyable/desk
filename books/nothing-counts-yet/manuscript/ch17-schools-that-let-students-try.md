# Chapter 17 — Schools That Let Students Try

School has a practice problem.

It contains enormous amounts of practice and often grades too much of it as performance.

A child learns a new idea on Monday.

Attempts it on Tuesday.

Makes the predictable beginner mistakes on Wednesday.

Receives a number that may remain in the record long after the idea has been learned.

This can make the rational student risk-averse.

If early attempts affect the final judgment, why expose confusion honestly?

Why choose the harder problem?

Why write the strange thesis?

Why try the new method when the familiar one protects the grade?

Why answer before being certain?

The score begins before the skill has finished forming.

Education should know better because education exists to change capability over time.

A school that cannot distinguish practice from certification confuses its two major jobs.

One job is developmental:

help the student become able to do something.

The other is evaluative:

establish what the student can currently do.

Both matter.

A society needs trustworthy signals of competence. Students deserve meaningful feedback about where they stand. Professions need to know whether a graduate can perform safely.

But the developmental system and the evaluative system should not be identical at every moment.

The student needs some work whose purpose is to reveal weakness without converting the weakness into permanent penalty.

This is already how many good teachers teach.

Low-stakes quizzes.

Drafts before final papers.

Practice sets.

Office hours.

Peer critique.

Revisions.

Mock exams.

Oral rehearsal.

Demonstrations before assessment.

The logic should become more explicit, especially as AI makes polished output easier to produce.

When artifacts become less reliable evidence of capability, schools need more situations in which students practice visibly and demonstrate selectively.

That does not mean more surveillance.

It means better staging.

A student might use AI extensively during rehearsal: generate practice questions, request explanations, role-play debate, receive feedback, compare approaches, practice in a second language, simulate oral examination.

Then the student might enter a bounded demonstration in which the decisive reasoning must be their own.

The machine helps produce repetitions.

The assessment checks transfer.

This is a stronger design than either blanket prohibition or unlimited substitution.

It makes the role of the tool explicit.

Schools should also reconsider revision.

Revision is sometimes treated as generosity after a student fails to meet the standard the first time.

But revision is not merely mercy.

It is one of the most educationally valuable behaviors a school can teach.

Notice what is wrong.

Interpret feedback.

Change the work.

Decide which criticism to accept.

Try again.

That sequence resembles adulthood far more closely than the one-shot submission culture many students internalize.

Most serious work gets revised.

The strange exception is the school assignment that disappears forever after grading.

There should still be deadlines.

There should still be final attempts.

The point is to make the path from draft to final visible enough that students learn how quality is produced.

This can reduce another problem: the myth of effortless talent.

When students mostly see classmates' finished work, they may conclude that good work appears whole inside other people's minds.

A classroom that makes revision normal shows the machinery.

The strong essay had a weak paragraph.

The elegant proof began with a wrong turn.

The fluent presentation sounded awkward in rehearsal.

The excellent programmer debugged.

The artist changed the composition.

Competence becomes less mystical and more attainable.

Schools also need private rooms.

Not every question should become a participation score.

Not every rough journal should be read by the teacher.

Not every practice conversation should be recorded.

Some work should belong to the learner precisely because self-observation is part of learning.

A notebook can be a backstage.

A scratch file can be a backstage.

A conversation with a tutor can be a backstage.

A rehearsal with an AI system can be a backstage if the data is treated accordingly.

The student needs places where the incentive is truth rather than impression.

What do I actually not understand?

Which part am I pretending to understand because everyone else seems ahead?

Can I do this without the hint?

What happens if I start over?

These are difficult questions to ask inside constant evaluation.

The deepest educational reform may be simple:

Make it obvious when the score is on.

Students should know when an activity exists to teach, when it exists to diagnose, and when it exists to certify.

Those modes can overlap, but they should not be carelessly confused.

Then the school can become more demanding, not less.

Practice can be harder because practice is safer.

Students can attempt more ambitious work because early imperfection is expected.

Feedback can be more candid because it is not identical to judgment.

Assessment can become more trustworthy because it occurs after meaningful opportunities to learn.

A school should not promise that nothing counts.

It should promise something better:

Some things count because they help you improve.

Some things count because they show what you can do.

We will tell you which is which.
