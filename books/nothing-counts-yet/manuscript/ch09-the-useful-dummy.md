# Chapter 9 — The Useful Dummy

A crash-test dummy is valuable because nobody mistakes it for a passenger.

This seems obvious until we look at how often people use real humans as training equipment.

The first difficult employee becomes the new manager's practice case.

The first desperate patient meets the clinician who has never rehearsed the conversation.

The first angry customer becomes the service worker's improvisation exercise.

The first public emergency becomes the institution's discovery process.

The first child in a new classroom policy becomes the experiment without being told they are inside one.

Some learning can only happen in reality.

But reality should not be the default place to discover preventable incompetence.

Civilization advances partly by inventing substitutes that can absorb mistakes before people have to.

The mannequin.

The mock-up.

The dummy account.

The staging server.

The fake patient.

The paper prototype.

The sandbox.

The tabletop exercise.

The synthetic dataset.

The spare part.

The practice negotiation.

These substitutes are ethically important because they relocate the cost of learning.

A novice still needs to make mistakes.

The question is who or what should pay for them.

The best answer is often: a designed object or scenario built to be wrong against.

This principle deserves more attention as technology makes synthetic environments abundant.

We can now create simulated customers, synthetic documents, generated adversaries, fictional scenarios, artificial datasets, digital twins, and conversational agents at low cost.

That should allow more practice to happen before exposure.

It should also make us more demanding about where first attempts occur.

If a team can rehearse an outage, why discover basic role confusion during the outage?

If a manager can practice a layoff conversation, why make the employee absorb every avoidable beginner mistake?

If a student can practice oral defense with a tireless model, why should the first attempt happen on the grade that decides the course?

If a public agency can run a limited simulation of a process, why launch nationwide and call citizens beta testers after the fact?

The useful dummy turns failure into a design variable.

But there is a trap.

The substitute can become too easy to dominate.

Real people resist.

They misunderstand.

They carry history.

They have rights.

They can refuse the premise.

They can respond to tone rather than content.

They can experience harm in ways the simulation cannot represent.

A manager who becomes excellent at controlling a simulated employee may become worse at listening to a real one.

A medical trainee can memorize the mannequin's expected cues and still miss the frightened human being.

A negotiator can learn a game against a model and overestimate how much real counterparts resemble rational agents.

A useful dummy must teach humility as well as skill.

One way to do that is to vary the scenario.

Do not let the learner memorize one response pattern.

Change the personality.

Change the timing.

Remove information.

Introduce an irrelevant detail.

Make the obvious diagnosis wrong.

Let the counterpart refuse.

Require the learner to explain what would make the simulation misleading.

The goal is not to create a perfect fake person.

It is to create enough friction that the learner develops a repertoire instead of a script.

There is also a deeper cultural use for dummies: they make absurdity safe.

A designer can create an exaggerated prototype to reveal a hidden preference.

A strategist can model the ridiculous extreme to discover where a trend would break.

A writer can put terrible dialogue in a character's mouth to learn what the scene cannot tolerate.

A team can role-play the most unreasonable customer, the most skeptical regulator, the most hostile journalist, the most panicked executive.

Nobody has to defend the scenario as likely.

It only needs to expose something.

This is another advantage of provisionality.

It permits representations that would be irresponsible as predictions but useful as probes.

“What if everything goes wrong at once?” may be a bad forecast and a good rehearsal.

“What if the customer interprets this in the least charitable possible way?” may be socially unrealistic and rhetorically useful.

“What if the model is wrong?” should be asked even when the model usually works.

Practice benefits from extremes because extremes reveal structure.

Performance cannot live there continuously.

The dummy can.

We should therefore evaluate practice objects by a different standard from production objects.

A production object should work.

A practice object should teach.

Sometimes teaching requires breaking.

Sometimes it requires provoking the wrong response.

Sometimes it requires being intentionally incomplete.

Sometimes it requires making the learner uncomfortable enough to notice a habit they carry invisibly.

The dummy is useful because it can be sacrificed to the lesson.

People cannot.

That boundary should become more valuable, not less, as simulation gets cheaper.

A humane society does not eliminate beginner mistakes.

It gets better at deciding where beginners are allowed to make them.
