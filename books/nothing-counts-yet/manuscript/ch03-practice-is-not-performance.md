# Chapter 3 — Practice Is Not Performance

The easiest way to ruin practice is to make it look too much like performance.

This sounds backward. Surely realism is the point. If someone is preparing for the real thing, shouldn't the preparation resemble the real thing as closely as possible?

Eventually, yes.

At the beginning, often no.

The first purpose of practice is not faithful imitation. It is learnability.

A violinist slows the passage below concert speed.

An athlete removes defenders from the drill.

A programmer tests one component without running the entire system.

A student solves five versions of the same mathematical structure before meeting it disguised inside a larger problem.

A speaker practices the opening alone before facing an audience.

These exercises are unrealistic in exactly the way they need to be.

They simplify the environment so attention can reach the thing being learned.

Performance compresses many skills into one moment.

Practice decompresses the moment.

That is why experts often train with exercises that look less sophisticated than their actual work. They know that complexity can hide error.

If a golfer takes a hundred full swings while the same flaw appears in each one, repetition may simply automate the flaw. If a team rehearses an entire presentation from beginning to end while avoiding the difficult transition, they become fluent around the weakness rather than through it.

Good practice isolates.

Then it reintegrates.

This has a useful consequence: practice should sometimes look worse than performance.

A person practicing a weakness will appear less capable than a person displaying a strength.

That is a feature.

Yet many modern environments reward visible competence so continuously that people are pushed toward the opposite behavior. They choose tasks on which they can already look good.

The student selects the safer topic.

The employee volunteers for familiar work.

The creator repeats the format that already performs.

The organization pilots only ideas that are already politically defensible.

The professional avoids the question that might reveal uncertainty.

The result is a performance trap: people become increasingly skilled at displaying their current level and increasingly deprived of situations that would change that level.

This is why a culture obsessed with excellence can accidentally become hostile to improvement.

Improvement requires temporary evidence of non-excellence.

It requires a before picture.

The mistake is to confuse standards with constant scoring.

High standards are compatible with long periods in which attempts are not final.

A demanding music teacher may tolerate hundreds of ugly repetitions precisely because the standard for the concert is high.

A rigorous editor may permit a chaotic first draft because rigor belongs to the finished argument, not to the first hour of thinking.

A serious engineering team may welcome an early prototype that fails because early failure is evidence arriving on schedule.

Standards describe the destination.

Practice describes the route.

The route should be allowed to look like travel.

There is another difference between practice and performance that is easy to miss.

In performance, interruption is usually failure.

In practice, interruption is often the mechanism.

Stop.

Again.

Why did you choose that?

What did you notice?

Try it without the slide.

Do it with the other hand.

Explain the step.

Start from the mistake.

Now make the scenario harder.

A good practice environment breaks continuity to increase awareness.

Performance wants flow.

Practice wants diagnosis.

This is why some people who perform beautifully are poor teachers. Their skill has become too integrated. They can do the thing without easily seeing the pieces a novice must assemble.

The expert experiences one motion.

The beginner experiences twelve decisions.

Practice design lives in those twelve decisions.

The same principle applies to organizations.

A company preparing for a crisis should not simply run a smooth theatrical exercise in which everyone follows a script. It should interrupt the script. Remove a dependency. Hide a piece of information. Force a handoff. Ask why a person made a decision. Discover which assumptions remain invisible when the system is running normally.

A government piloting a new service should not only ask whether the pilot can be made to work with extraordinary attention. It should ask what breaks when ordinary staffing, ordinary confusion, ordinary incentives, and ordinary citizens enter the system.

Practice is not a small performance.

It is a different epistemic machine.

Its job is to produce knowledge about capability.

What is automatic?

What is fragile?

What transfers?

What depends on ideal conditions?

What happens under pressure?

Where does the learner improvise intelligently, and where do they merely remember a script?

These questions matter more now because artificial systems can make performance easier to simulate.

A person can produce a polished artifact without possessing the underlying capability. A team can generate plausible language, attractive slides, functioning snippets, and professional-looking plans faster than ever.

That makes it even more important to preserve environments where the artifact is not the objective.

If the goal is to learn public speaking, the rehearsal should sometimes remove the generated script.

If the goal is to learn analysis, the practice should sometimes require the learner to show the chain of decisions.

If the goal is to learn programming, the exercise should sometimes ask what the code will do before it runs.

If the goal is judgment, the practice must surface judgment.

Otherwise we risk building a world in which the performance layer becomes exquisite while the capability layer becomes mysterious.

The solution is not to ban tools from practice.

It is to decide which layer is being practiced.

A calculator can be appropriate when the learner is practicing modeling rather than arithmetic.

A language model can be appropriate when the learner is practicing critique rather than first-draft generation.

A simulator can automate the environment while preserving the human decision.

A coach can provide the answer after the learner has committed to an attempt.

Good practice protects the difficult decision long enough for the learner to make it.

Then it gives feedback.

Then it repeats.

Then it adds complexity.

Then, finally, it looks like performance.

By that point, resemblance is no longer the enemy of learning.

It is the test of transfer.

Practice begins by taking the world apart.

It ends by putting the person back inside it.
