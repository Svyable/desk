# Chronology

## 1960s: the optical anomaly appears

- **1967 — Surveyor 5 and 6:** surface television systems return imagery later discussed in the horizon-glow literature.
- **1968 — Surveyor 7:** post-sunset western-horizon brightness becomes the clearest early surface case later interpreted as sunlight forward-scattered by fine dust.

## Early 1970s: electrostatic transport becomes a quantitative hypothesis

- **1972 — Criswell, “Lunar dust motion”:** models Surveyor 7 horizon glow as near-surface dust scattering and develops an electrostatic production picture.
- **1973 — Rennilson & Criswell:** Surveyor 5/6/7 observations are synthesized as temporary horizon glow near sunlight-shadow boundaries, with electrostatic fields proposed as the grain-lifting mechanism.
- **1972–1976 — Apollo surface dust/meteor instrumentation:** unexpected particle-like events around illumination transitions enter later discussion of charged lunar dust. Full event-level reconstruction remains a research priority.

## 1972: Apollo 17 adds the high-altitude observation

- Apollo 17 crew members record/sketch a lunar horizon-glow structure seen from orbit. The observation later becomes central evidence for a much taller dust population than the Surveyor near-surface interpretation.

## 1991: the tall cloud is modeled

- **Zook & McCoy (1991):** model the Apollo 17 glow as scattering from submicron dust with a vertical scale height of approximately 5–20 km. The result is a physically explicit optical interpretation, not a direct particle measurement.

## 1990s–2000s: better remote sensing tightens the space

- Clementine, Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter and related analyses constrain the abundance of high-altitude dust. The field increasingly distinguishes direct dust detection from optical inference.
- Laboratory and theoretical work expands on charging, adhesion, photoelectron sheaths, terminator fields and patch charging.

## 2013–2014: LADEE goes to the Moon

- **September 6, 2013:** LADEE launches from Wallops Flight Facility.
- **October 2013:** enters lunar orbit.
- **November 2013:** science operations begin.
- **April 17, 2014:** mission ends in a planned lunar impact.

## 2015: two decisive LDEX results

- **Horányi et al., Nature:** report a permanent asymmetric lunar dust cloud. The observed structure is consistent with secondary ejecta continuously produced by interplanetary micrometeoroid impacts.
- **Szalay & Horányi, GRL:** dedicated terminator search reports no evidence for the putative ~0.1 μm electrostatically lofted population in the approximately 3–250 km altitude range within LDEX sensitivity/model assumptions.
- Follow-up work shows the impact cloud’s local-time and temporal structure tracks sporadic meteoroid source geometry.

## 2016: the story splits by size and altitude

- **Champlain et al.:** laboratory/simulation work argues microscopic field amplification can permit near-surface dust ejection despite bulk cohesion objections; explicitly focused below the high-altitude regime constrained by LDEX.
- **Wooden et al., Nature Geoscience:** report evidence from LADEE UVS for a dynamic nanodust cloud, introducing a much smaller particle regime that should not be conflated with either the LDEX impact cloud or the classic Apollo-scale electrostatic population.
- **Poppe et al.:** LADEE/LDEX observations contribute to a broader picture of lunar pickup ions and solar-wind coupling in the exosphere.

## 2018–2021: refinement rather than closure

- Dust-cloud modeling uses LADEE to constrain meteoroid source populations.
- LRO-based horizon-glow searches and LADEE-UVS reanalyses continue to push optical constraints downward and clarify the low-altitude/nanodust parameter space.

## 2025: the near-surface physics remains active

- **Nakazono & Miyake (2025):** particle-in-cell simulations show lunar cavity size changes charging structure, reinforcing the need to treat topography and microgeometry by scale.
- **Richmond et al. (2025):** laboratory experiments report enhanced dust charging/mobilization under combined vacuum-ultraviolet radiation and low-density plasma, relative to either forcing alone; proposed plasma charge pumping provides a new experimentally grounded near-surface mechanism.
- **March 2025:** NASA reports successful operation of an Electrodynamic Dust Shield on Firefly Aerospace’s Blue Ghost Mission 1, removing lunar regolith from selected surfaces with engineered electric fields. This is engineering validation of electric dust removal, not a measurement of natural lofting.

## 2026: operations make the old science practical

- NASA continues Artemis-related lunar dust mitigation and lunar-hardware testing, including electrostatic dust tools and plume-surface interaction instrumentation.
- The unresolved scientific opportunity is increasingly concentrated in the first meters above undisturbed regolith: synchronized measurements of grain trajectories, charge, local electric field, plasma, illumination and impact background through repeated natural and controlled shadow transitions.

## Next decisive date

Unknown.

The next milestone should not be another anniversary review. It should be the first modern lunar surface dataset that measures natural grain trajectories and local charging simultaneously over multiple lunar day-night cycles.
