# Mercury, Asteroids, and Airless Worlds

The lunar dust problem becomes more useful when it stops being only lunar.

The Moon is the best laboratory because we have landed on it, walked on it, returned samples from it, orbited it repeatedly, and watched its surface under changing light for decades. But the underlying ingredients are common across airless bodies: ultraviolet illumination, plasma, weak gravity, impact bombardment, insulating regolith, and moving shadow.

If charged dust can move on the Moon, the same family of processes should appear elsewhere with different relative strengths.

That makes the Moon a calibration body.

Mercury is an obvious comparison.

It sits much closer to the Sun. Ultraviolet flux is stronger. Solar-wind conditions are harsher and more variable. Surface temperatures swing dramatically. Mercury also possesses an intrinsic magnetic field, weak by terrestrial standards but strong enough to create a magnetosphere and alter plasma access to the surface.

A dust grain there lives in a different electromagnetic world.

Impact vaporization contributes to Mercury’s tenuous exosphere, and micrometeoroid bombardment is an important source process. Sodium and other species have been observed above the surface. Fine ejecta and charged particles should therefore be considered alongside the atomic exosphere.

The comparative question is clean: if photoelectric and plasma charging produce meaningful dust mobilization on the Moon, how should the effect scale with solar distance, surface gravity, and magnetic environment?

A model that cannot make comparative predictions is incomplete.

Asteroids push the problem harder because gravity becomes tiny.

On a kilometer-scale or smaller body, a grain needs very little speed to escape. A weak electrostatic kick irrelevant to lunar high-altitude transport can remove material entirely. Micrometeoroid impacts also eject debris efficiently. Rotation complicates effective gravity. Irregular shape creates nonuniform potential and gravitational fields.

The same microscopic process can therefore change category.

On the Moon, a grain hops.

On a small asteroid, it leaves.

This is why electrostatic transport has appeared repeatedly in discussions of asteroid surface evolution, particle sorting, and so-called dust ponds. The detailed evidence varies by body, and the book should not pretend one mechanism has been established everywhere. But small-body observations make an important point: the force ratios are not fixed properties of “dust.” They depend on the body.

A grain-size regime that is glued to the Moon can be mobile on Eros.

A particle that remains bound to Mercury may escape a rubble pile.

This gives lunar measurements predictive value beyond hazard mitigation.

Phobos and Deimos offer another comparison because they sit inside Mars’s plasma environment and have extremely weak gravity. Proposed missions and future human operations around Mars may interact with dust from these moons. Their surfaces experience solar ultraviolet light, charged particles, and impacts, but the surrounding planetary environment differs from the Moon’s.

Again, comparative physics can separate universal mechanisms from lunar accidents.

There is an epistemic advantage to studying several bodies.

On the Moon, old horizon-glow observations can bias interpretation. Researchers know what they are trying to explain. On an asteroid with no historical glow claim, the same charging model can make blind predictions about particle motion, size sorting, or exospheric dust.

If the model succeeds there, confidence rises independently.

If it fails, the lunar rescue space shrinks.

This is one reason comparative planetology is so powerful: another world can function as a control group.

The reverse is also true. A process obvious on a tiny asteroid may be negligible on the Moon. One should not use evidence of electrostatic particle movement under milligravity as proof of lunar lofting without scaling the forces.

The body’s escape speed matters.

The solar flux matters.

The plasma current balance matters.

The grain composition matters.

The surface roughness matters.

Comparative work is therefore a discipline against analogy.

The word “airless” hides enormous diversity.

The Moon has substantial gravity, a mostly global lack of magnetic shielding, ancient regolith, and a 29.5-day solar cycle.

Mercury has stronger gravity than the Moon, a magnetic field, intense solar forcing, and a very different day-night cycle.

Small asteroids can rotate in hours and barely hold loose grains.

The moons of outer planets see weaker sunlight but complex magnetospheric plasmas.

A process driven by photoemission should weaken with distance from the Sun unless compensated by other currents. A process driven by magnetospheric electrons may become stronger in places where sunlight is weaker.

This creates natural experiments across the Solar System.

One of the most interesting cases is Saturn’s and Jupiter’s dusty environments, where spacecraft have directly measured ejecta clouds around small moons produced by impacts. Those observations helped establish the general physics of impact-generated dust exospheres before LADEE mapped the lunar one.

The Moon therefore joined an existing family when LADEE found its permanent ejecta cloud.

That family strengthens the impact interpretation because similar source physics appears around multiple bodies.

Electrostatic lofting needs the same kind of comparative signature.

What would it look like?

Perhaps local-time dependence that scales with illumination and plasma rather than impact direction.

Perhaps a sharp size cutoff predicted by charge-to-mass ratios.

Perhaps enhancement near shadow boundaries across several bodies.

Perhaps dust escape rates on asteroids that correlate with solar-wind disturbances after controlling for impacts.

Any such pattern would move electrostatic dust from a lunar-specific explanation toward a general planetary process.

The comparative program should also include spacecraft surfaces.

A spacecraft is an artificial airless body.

It sits in sunlight and plasma, charges differentially, casts sharp shadows, and accumulates contamination. Dust can adhere to or leave its surfaces. Many spacecraft charging problems are better instrumented than the lunar surface because engineers monitor voltage and current for safety.

Lessons from spacecraft electrostatics can therefore inform lunar dust models, especially around artificial structures.

But there is a warning: spacecraft are conductive and engineered in ways regolith is not. Grounding paths, materials, geometry, and active electronics create charging behavior unlike granular soil.

The analogy is useful only where the boundary conditions match.

There is a larger scientific payoff in getting airless-body dust right.

Surface transport affects how we interpret remote sensing.

If fine particles migrate electrostatically, optical maturity and grain-size distributions can be decoupled from impact gardening alone. Brightness patterns may reflect particle sorting. Thermal inertia can change. Exposed rocks may accumulate or lose fines. Samples collected at one site may not represent purely local geology if dust migrates over distance.

These are subtle effects, but planetary science often infers history from the top millimeters of a surface.

A weak transport mechanism integrated over millions of years can matter.

The scale problem returns.

A process too weak to make a visible cloud can still alter regolith texture.

This means orbital nulls for dust exospheres do not necessarily eliminate surface-evolution effects on other worlds either.

We need to distinguish exosphere production from regolith migration.

A useful comparative metric would be a dimensionless mobility number combining characteristic electrostatic energy with gravitational potential, adhesion, and particle size.

Not a branded law for its own sake, but a way to plot regimes.

Bodies with high electrostatic energy relative to gravity and low adhesion after disturbance would occupy an escape regime.

Bodies with intermediate ratios might show hopping and sorting.

Bodies where adhesion dominates would show almost no natural motion unless impacts free grains first.

Laboratory and in situ data can populate the map.

The Moon should be the anchor point because its environment is measured most densely.

This is where the lunar horizon-glow mystery becomes unexpectedly useful.

A fifty-year dispute forced unusually detailed work on charging, dust detection, optical scattering, and impact ejecta. Even if the original glow turns out not to be electrostatic dust, the research program built around it can improve our understanding of every airless surface.

Failed explanations often leave good instruments behind.

That may be one of science’s underrated efficiencies.

Phlogiston left careful combustion measurements.

The luminiferous ether motivated precise interferometry.

The search for lunar electrostatic fountains produced dust detectors, charging models, and laboratory techniques valuable whether or not the fountain exists in its classic form.

A theory can lose and still finance the map.

The next step is to use that map comparatively rather than defending the original theory forever.

Send dust-electricity packages to multiple bodies.

Standardize particle-size and charge measurements.

Record local plasma and illumination together.

Publish nulls with explicit sensitivity.

Predict before arrival.

If electrostatic transport is a general feature of airless worlds, it should reveal itself as a pattern across them.

If it is mostly a near-surface lunar curiosity amplified by a few ambiguous historical observations, comparison will reveal that too.

The Solar System gives us controls.

We should use them.
