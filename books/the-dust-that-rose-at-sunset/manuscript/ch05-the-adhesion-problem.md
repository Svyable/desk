# The Adhesion Problem

Every elegant model of lunar dust eventually arrives at an ugly question: why does the grain leave the ground at all?

It is easy to make dust move in language. Charge the particles. Apply an electric field. Lower the gravity. Let them rise.

The Moon helps the intuition. Surface gravity is about one-sixth of Earth’s. A grain that feels heavy in a terrestrial laboratory has much less weight there. There is no atmosphere to damp its motion. Once launched, it can travel ballistically without aerodynamic drag. If one starts from gravity alone, lunar dust appears eager to fly.

But weight is not what usually keeps a fine grain attached to another fine grain.

Adhesion is.

For the particles relevant to horizon-glow theories—micron and submicron sizes—the force binding contacts can dwarf lunar weight. Van der Waals attraction, electrostatic contact forces, mechanical interlocking, and the geometry of irregular asperities matter more than the simple mg term. Lunar gravity becomes weaker as the particle gets smaller because mass scales with volume. Contact forces do not fall away at the same rate.

Small grains are therefore simultaneously easier to accelerate once free and harder, relative to their weight, to detach.

This is not a minor correction. It changes the governing problem.

The old electrostatic-fountain picture was often drawn as though grains were isolated beads resting lightly on a plate. The electrical force qE rises until it exceeds lunar gravity, and the bead lifts. Replace the bead with an angular piece of space-weathered regolith nested among other angular grains and the force balance can move by orders of magnitude.

The Moon’s notorious dust behavior during Apollo gives direct qualitative evidence that adhesion is formidable. Dust coated spacesuits, visors, seals, tools, radiators, camera equipment, and cabin interiors. Astronauts did not describe it as obedient powder falling neatly to the floor. It clung. It worked into joints. It became an engineering problem almost immediately.

Some of that adhesion involved triboelectric charging and electrostatics. Some was mechanical. Some reflected the sharp, unworn nature of grains created without weathering by liquid water or wind. Whatever the partition, the historical record is enough to reject a model in which lunar fines are trivially detached by any weak field.

This makes the Surveyor and Apollo glow interpretations more interesting, not less.

If dust really was suspended, there must be a release pathway.

That pathway could be electrostatic, but it has to solve contact mechanics before it gets to solve altitude.

The easiest way around adhesion is not to beat it electrically. It is to let something else break the contact.

Micrometeoroid impacts are obvious candidates. They continually disturb the surface and launch debris. Thermal cracking can rearrange material. Larger nearby grains can shift. Human activity can shake or excavate soil. Once a fine grain is already airborne, even a relatively modest electric field may alter its path. Electrostatics can therefore matter as a transport modifier without being the primary detachment force.

This hybrid regime is underappreciated because people like mechanisms with clean ownership.

Was the grain impact-ejected or electrostatically lofted?

Perhaps both.

An impact frees it. Charging changes its ballistic arc. A shadow boundary changes the field. It lands somewhere different from where pure gravity would have put it. Over many hops, electrostatic sorting can occur without an electric field ever doing the initial mechanical work.

This is a more subtle kind of “electrostatic transport” than the classic fountain, but it could matter geologically.

The Moon has had billions of years to integrate tiny biases.

Suppose a grain is displaced only a few centimeters more often toward or away from certain illumination boundaries. Suppose fine particles are preferentially mobilized relative to coarse ones. The instantaneous flux can be too small to produce a visible cloud and still modify surface texture over geological time.

That possibility is difficult to infer from orbital dust density alone.

The adhesion problem therefore splits the science into two timescales: event-scale lofting and accumulated migration.

Visible horizon glow requires enough scattering cross section in the line of sight at one time. Surface evolution requires only that an asymmetry persist long enough.

A mechanism can fail the first test and pass the second.

This is one of the reasons “no visible glow” is not equivalent to “no electrostatic geology.”

To measure the second, however, we need signatures that cannot be produced easily by impact gardening alone.

Particle-size sorting across shadow boundaries is one candidate. Preferential accumulation on certain slopes is another. Changes correlated with local electrostatic potential rather than impact exposure would be stronger. Laboratory experiments can derive expected sorting behavior, but lunar validation requires samples or imaging from controlled environments.

The permanent shadowed regions near the lunar poles offer an extreme testbed.

There, sunlight geometry is not a simple dawn-to-dusk cycle. Crater interiors can remain dark while nearby peaks experience long illumination. Temperature drops dramatically. Plasma access differs. Volatiles, including water ice, can change surface material properties. The adhesion and charging behavior of dusty ice is not the same as dry equatorial regolith.

A theory tuned to Apollo landing sites may fail at the poles.

That matters because the poles are where human exploration is now concentrated.

The south polar region is attractive because of access to volatiles and favorable illumination on certain ridges. It is also a landscape built from gradients—light-dark, hot-cold, volatile-dry, electrically exposed-shielded. If electrostatic dust motion needs boundaries, polar operations may create and occupy them constantly.

Adhesion itself changes with temperature and contamination.

A monolayer of adsorbed material can alter contact forces. Frost can cement grains. Repeated thermal cycles can loosen them. Human exhaust can deposit volatiles. The pristine force balance is not guaranteed to survive a landing campaign.

This is a practical reason to stop using “lunar dust” as a single engineering material.

Dust at an equatorial Apollo site at local noon is not dust in a permanently shadowed crater. Freshly excavated subsurface grains are not identical to space-weathered surface grains. A grain coated by rocket exhaust is not pristine. Size, shape, mineralogy, charge history, temperature, and contact history all matter.

The adhesion problem is not one number.

Yet mission design often wants a number.

How much force does a brush need? What voltage repels dust? What coating reduces adhesion? How quickly will a radiator lose performance? How much particulate loading reaches a habitat airlock?

Engineers require distributions and margins, not philosophical warnings about complexity.

That demand has driven an entire branch of lunar dust mitigation research: electrodynamic dust shields, surface coatings, mechanical removal, magnetic approaches where iron-bearing components matter, suit-port concepts, landing-pad stabilization, plume modeling, filtration. The very existence of this research program is evidence that dust’s most consequential property may not be its ability to rise kilometers into the sky.

It is its ability to stay exactly where you do not want it.

Electrodynamic dust shields are particularly relevant to the science because they deliberately create traveling electric fields to move particles across surfaces. If engineered fields can transport lunar simulant or actual regolith, the question becomes how those field strengths compare with natural lunar microfields.

This provides an empirical bridge.

Instead of asking whether electrostatics “can” move dust, ask what threshold distribution is measured for representative grains under representative contacts. Then compare that distribution with in situ natural electric-field measurements at the relevant scale. The overlap between the two distributions estimates the fraction of grains susceptible to natural mobilization.

That is better than arguing from possibility.

The threshold experiment should be done with size-resolved particles, multiple mineral phases, realistic surface roughness, and a range of contact histories. The same sample should be tested after exposure to solar-wind analog plasma and ultraviolet light. If possible, actual lunar material should be included under strict conservation and contamination controls.

Then take the experiment to the Moon.

A small platform could carry a tray of characterized grains with known size distribution and contact geometry alongside undisturbed local soil. Controlled electrodes would slowly ramp fields while cameras record release. This would calibrate adhesion and charging under actual lunar gravity and vacuum. The artificial field threshold could then be compared to naturally measured transients nearby.

Such a calibration experiment would resolve an enormous amount of literature because it translates qE arguments into observed release probabilities.

It also reveals a broader epistemic habit.

Frontier theories often focus on the force that attracts attention and neglect the force that resists it.

Propulsion proposals talk about thrust before heat rejection. Exotic energy schemes talk about stored energy before coupling. Biological signaling hypotheses talk about emitted signals before noise and receptor thresholds. Lunar dust theories talk about charge before adhesion.

The neglected term is often boring because it does not create the phenomenon. It prevents it.

But a mechanism is a race between terms, not a list of interesting ones.

For a lunar grain, the race may be won or lost at a contact patch nanometers wide.

This is why microscopic topography became a rescue route for electrostatic models. If adhesion is controlled by a tiny contact and electric field is simultaneously enhanced at a tiny irregularity, the macroscopic averages on both sides are misleading. The event happens in the tails of two distributions.

That is plausible.

It is also hard to scale.

Suppose one in a million grain contacts experiences a geometry favorable enough to launch during a charging transition. There are astronomical numbers of grains in the top layer of the Moon. One in a million could produce a measurable flux. Or it could be negligible depending on area, grain size, recurrence, and flight distance.

The phrase “rare” has no physical meaning until multiplied by population.

A good model therefore needs the surface distribution of contact geometries, not merely a demonstration that a favorable geometry exists.

Modern imaging and computation make this possible in principle. X-ray tomography can reconstruct granular contacts. Electron microscopy can characterize grain roughness. Discrete-element models can simulate force networks. Plasma and photoelectron models can map local potentials. Monte Carlo methods can integrate launch statistics over heterogeneous surfaces.

The missing piece remains validation.

If a model predicts a launch-flux distribution at sunset, put a counter there.

One reason this has not been done cleanly is that the act of placing the counter disturbs the surface. Landing exhaust changes regolith. A robotic arm compacts grains. Wheels create tracks. Electrodes and structures alter local fields. Even the instrument casts a shadow—the very thing being studied.

The measurement apparatus becomes part of the lunar electrical environment.

This is not fatal. It just has to be designed as part of the experiment.

Use remote cameras and lidar from a mast observing undisturbed terrain tens of meters away. Deploy miniature passive probes with minimal disturbance. Characterize the spacecraft’s electrostatic potential and model its field. Compare multiple azimuths to separate platform effects. Use natural terrain boundaries in addition to artificial ones.

The Moon rewards careful geometry.

There is another adhesion-related clue hidden in the returned regolith itself: dust’s sharpness.

On Earth, sand grains are rounded by water, wind, and collisions. Lunar grains can retain angular edges and glassy features because the weathering regime is different. Micrometeoroid impacts continually fracture and weld material, producing agglutinates and jagged fragments. These shapes affect both mechanical interlocking and optical scattering.

A grain population inferred from brightness therefore depends on the same morphology that controls detachment.

Optical and mechanical uncertainties are coupled.

Treating particles as spheres makes equations tractable. It may systematically misestimate both how strongly they stick and how efficiently they scatter light. A horizon-glow inversion using spheres can infer a number density that is wrong for the same reason an adhesion model using spheres predicts the wrong launch threshold.

The obvious solution is to measure realistic grain phase functions and force distributions together.

This is the sort of work that sounds painfully incremental compared with “electric dust fountains on the Moon.”

It is also the work that decides whether the fountains exist.

The most dramatic unresolved version of the problem concerns nanograins.

At nanometer sizes, charge may come in a handful of elementary units. Discrete charging matters. Electromagnetic forces can dominate gravity profoundly. Adhesion remains intense at contact, but once such particles are produced by impacts or other processes, their subsequent dynamics can differ from micron-scale grains. Some later analyses of LADEE ultraviolet data invoke nanodust populations that are not equivalent to the larger grains LDEX searched for individually.

This is where terminology threatens to collapse again.

“Dust” now spans orders of magnitude in radius.

A ten-nanometer particle and a ten-micron grain differ in mass by roughly nine orders of magnitude if density is similar. Their charge-to-mass ratios, optical scattering, detector response, and ballistic behavior are radically different. A mechanism that dominates one can be irrelevant to the other.

The adhesion problem is therefore also a taxonomy problem.

We need to stop asking whether dust sticks.

Which dust?

We need to stop asking whether electricity can lift it.

From which contact state, under which field, to which altitude?

The Moon did not give us one nuisance called dust. It gave us a particulate system spanning sizes where different physics takes turns being important.

The finest grains may be the easiest to accelerate after detachment and the hardest to detect directly. Larger grains are easier to count but harder to move electrostatically. Optical observations weight particles by scattering cross section in ways that depend on wavelength and geometry. Impact detectors weight them by momentum or ionization yield. Adhesion filters them before either instrument sees them.

The observed population is therefore the product of multiple selection functions.

Source.

Detachment.

Acceleration.

Trajectory.

Survival.

Detection.

Remove any one of those from the analysis and the phrase “dust density” becomes ambiguous.

This is why the adhesion problem belongs near the beginning of the book.

It forces humility before altitude.

Before asking why Apollo astronauts may have seen dust tens of kilometers high, ask how the first grain got off the ground.

If the answer is wrong, everything above it is architecture built on a contact point.
