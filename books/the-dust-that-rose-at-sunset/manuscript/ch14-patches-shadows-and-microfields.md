# Patches, Shadows, and Microfields

The lunar terminator is usually drawn at continental scale.

That may be the wrong scale for dust.

From orbit, the terminator is a line thousands of kilometers long separating the illuminated hemisphere from the dark one. But the surface does not experience one clean electrical boundary. Every pebble makes a shadow. Every crater wall divides light from darkness. Every pore in the regolith creates a miniature cavity with restricted access to photons and plasma.

There are terminators everywhere.

This realization changed the electrostatic dust problem because it relocated the strongest fields from the global Moon to the granular surface.

A planar charging model averages too much.

Imagine a smooth patch under sunlight. Ultraviolet photons eject electrons, leaving the surface modestly positive until currents balance. A neighboring shadowed patch lacks photoemission and can acquire a different potential. Over meters, the gradient may be limited. Over micrometers at the edge of a shadowed cavity, the same potential difference can imply a much stronger electric field.

Field is voltage divided by distance.

Small distance matters.

This is the physical heart of patch charging.

It also gives electrostatic dust models a plausible route around the adhesion problem. A grain may sit at the edge of a tiny shadow where local potential gradients far exceed the smooth-surface average. If its charge is favorable, the upward or lateral force can become large enough to detach or reposition it.

The important word is local.

A thousand-volt-per-meter field occupying a tiny fraction of the surface does not make the Moon globally hostile with electricity. It may still launch enough grains to matter if those sites are numerous.

The statistical geometry becomes the question.

How much lunar surface area resides in high-field microstates at any moment?

Granular materials are almost entirely surface and void. A scoop of regolith contains an astronomical number of contacts and shadow pockets. Sunlight at low incidence angle exaggerates this structure. Near dawn and dusk, long shadows create gradients across larger features while microscopic roughness remains embedded inside them.

The result is a hierarchy of patches.

Mountain shadow.

Crater shadow.

Boulder shadow.

Grain shadow.

Pore shadow.

Each scale has its own charging time and field geometry.

The global terminator may therefore matter not because it is a singular electric cliff, but because it changes the statistical population of smaller cliffs.

This is a much richer theory than the old cartoon.

It is also harder to falsify unless turned into measurable predictions.

A microfield model should predict the distribution of surface electric fields as a function of solar elevation, plasma state, and regolith geometry. From that distribution, combined with measured grain adhesion and charge, it should predict a launch-rate distribution.

The entire problem can be written as overlapping probability distributions.

P(E | geometry, plasma, illumination).

P(q | grain, plasma, illumination).

P(F_adhesion | grain contact).

A launch occurs where qE exceeds the combination of adhesion, gravity, and geometric constraints.

This is not one threshold because every term varies.

The field’s historical tendency to discuss “the electric field at the terminator” therefore hides exactly the heterogeneity most likely to matter.

Modern numerical tools can model this heterogeneity far better than early lunar studies could. Particle-in-cell plasma simulations can resolve photoelectron sheaths over structured surfaces. Electrostatic solvers can handle irregular geometries. Discrete-element models can describe granular contacts. Machine vision can extract actual regolith morphology from microscopic images.

The danger is computational seduction.

A beautiful simulation of charge patches is not evidence that nature populates the assumed boundary conditions.

The model needs in situ field measurements at comparable scale.

That measurement is difficult because a probe changes the field it tries to measure. A conductive electrode hovering millimeters above regolith perturbs local potentials. A lander structure casts shadows and collects charge. Wiring provides current paths absent in natural soil.

The instrument is not a neutral observer.

This resembles scanning-probe microscopy on Earth, where tip-sample interaction becomes part of the measurement model. Lunar electrostatics needs the same humility.

One strategy is redundancy across distance. Measure field with multiple miniature sensors at several heights while imaging the surface remotely. If a strong microfield exists only within micrometers, the signal should decay rapidly. Combine this with electrostatic modeling of the probes themselves.

Another strategy is to infer fields from grain trajectories.

A charged grain is a natural test particle. If its charge-to-mass ratio can be estimated, acceleration reveals the field. Track enough grains and they map local electrostatics without inserting a separate probe into every cavity.

This turns dust from problem into instrument.

The Moon repeatedly invites that inversion. Impact ejecta becomes a detector of meteoroids. Nanodust becomes a tracer of plasma. Electrostatic grains could map fields.

The same particles responsible for uncertainty can become the measurement channel.

Patch charging also offers a route to explain why some older observations might be highly local and irreproducible globally.

Surveyor cameras sat in particular terrain under particular illumination. A camera line of sight grazing a rough horizon could integrate scattering through many small launch sites. Another landing site with different texture might show less. Apollo crews saw the Moon from changing orbital geometries, perhaps intersecting local regions with unusual activity.

This does not prove the old observations were dust.

It shows how a genuine electrostatic phenomenon could be heterogeneous rather than global.

Heterogeneity is often mistaken for intermittency.

If a process occurs continuously somewhere but the observer samples only one path, it appears intermittent. The distinction matters for experiment design. A long-duration fixed station can separate temporal changes at one site from spatial variation if paired with multiple stations.

A network would be transformative.

Put identical dust-electricity packages at equatorial mare, highlands, south-polar sun-shadow terrain, and a magnetic anomaly. If launch rates differ systematically, geology and plasma context become explanatory variables.

Lunar swirl regions are particularly intriguing because crustal magnetic anomalies modify solar-wind access and are associated with distinctive high-albedo patterns. The origin of swirls remains an active topic, with magnetic shielding and space-weathering differences among major ideas. Dust transport is not required to explain them, and the book should not launder speculation into fact. But they offer natural laboratories where plasma-surface interaction differs from ordinary terrain.

If electrostatic dust transport is sensitive to local plasma structure, magnetic-anomaly sites should show different statistics under otherwise comparable illumination.

That is a prediction worth making before measurement.

Patch charging also complicates human infrastructure.

A solar array is a giant artificial illuminated surface with conductive paths. Its underside may remain shadowed. A habitat creates sharp boundaries and can float electrically relative to the regolith. Rover wheels repeatedly contact and separate materials, generating triboelectric charge. Excavation opens fresh shadowed pores and surfaces. Landing pads alter roughness and dielectric properties.

Humans will manufacture patches larger and cleaner than nature’s.

These artificial boundaries could dominate local electrostatics.

That means future dust observations near a base may tell us more about architecture than the Moon unless instruments are placed outside the facility’s electrical footprint.

Facility design should therefore include electrostatic zoning the way terrestrial observatories include radio-quiet zones.

Keep some terrain electrically boring.

No exposed high-voltage systems. Minimal moving machinery. Known grounding—or its lunar equivalent, where “ground” is an electrically complex regolith rather than an infinite reservoir. Strict contamination control. Long-baseline monitoring.

The phrase electrically boring is funny until you need a baseline.

The microfield concept has a broader scientific significance because it is an example of how extremes emerge from heterogeneity.

Average conditions can be weak while rare local conditions control outcomes.

Cloud formation on Earth depends on uncommon nuclei. Fracture starts at stress concentrators. Chemical reactions occur at active sites. Electrical breakdown begins where fields concentrate. Biological signaling often depends on microenvironments invisible in bulk measurements.

Lunar dust may be another case where the mean is the wrong statistic.

If so, orbital measurements of mean density and global surface potentials will never be enough.

We need tail distributions.

How often does E exceed the release threshold?

How many grains occupy contacts weak enough to move?

How do those tails change at sunset?

This also clarifies what “electrostatic levitation” might mean geologically.

Suppose only one in a billion grain contacts launches during a given charging transition. That sounds negligible. Across a square kilometer of fine regolith, the absolute number of grains could still be enormous. If most hop only millimeters, no orbiter sees a cloud. Over millions of cycles, the surface can still be sorted.

Geological effect integrates tiny probabilities over huge populations and long times.

Optical glow integrates scattering cross section over a line of sight at one time.

Engineering hazard integrates particle flux onto a component over mission duration.

These are three different integrals of the same microscopic event distribution.

A process can matter to one and not the others.

This is one of the book’s most important separations.

The old literature often asked whether electrostatic transport is “important.”

Important to what?

Visible horizon glow?

Regolith maturation?

Dust contamination?

Nanoparticle escape?

Surface sorting?

The answer can differ.

Patch charging makes this pluralism unavoidable because it naturally generates many weak local events rather than one grand fountain.

It also changes the aesthetics of the subject.

The electric Moon is less like a levitating desert and more like static crackle at the scale of dust contacts.

There may be no visible spectacle.

A grain twitches in a cavity.

Another hops two centimeters.

A nanofragment acquires enough charge to escape the immediate surface.

Billions of tiny events happen below the threshold of human sight.

That is physically plausible and scientifically difficult.

It is also the regime where a modern surface mission can excel.

High-speed macro imaging, digital holography, laser-sheet illumination, charge-sensitive grids, and local plasma probes can watch a cubic meter of lunar space with exquisite sensitivity. The mission does not need to photograph a glow. It needs to count trajectories.

The strongest version of the microfield hypothesis should therefore make a boring prediction:

At specified illumination and plasma conditions, the number of grain trajectories per cubic meter per minute should change by a measurable factor near newly formed shadow boundaries.

No glow required.

No astronaut testimony.

No fifty-year mystery.

Just grains and clocks.

If that prediction fails repeatedly, patch charging may still exist microscopically but its natural flux will become too small to carry much explanatory weight.

If it succeeds, the field finally gets a positive modern in situ result in the regime LADEE left open.

Either outcome is clean.

The global terminator launched the mystery.

The solution may be hiding in a shadow the width of a grain.
