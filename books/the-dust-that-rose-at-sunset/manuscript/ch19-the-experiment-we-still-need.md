# The Experiment We Still Need

The lunar dust argument has lasted long enough that the next experiment should be designed to make at least one side unhappy.

That is not a joke.

A good experiment in a mature controversy should force meaningful loss of hypothesis space. It should not merely produce another image that each side can interpret. It should not add one more upper limit in a regime already well constrained. It should not demonstrate in a laboratory, yet again, that charged grains can move under sufficiently strong fields.

We need the experiment where the surviving natural claim actually lives.

Near the surface.

Through illumination transitions.

With dust trajectories, local electric fields, plasma, impacts, and optical scattering measured at the same time.

The useful news, as of 2026, is that this no longer requires inventing an instrument suite from scratch.

NASA has selected the DUst and plaSma environmenT survEyoR—DUSTER—for Artemis IV lunar-surface science. The planned rover-mounted package includes an Electrostatic Dust Analyzer intended to measure the charge, velocity, size, and flux of lofted particles, paired with the RESOLVE plasma sounder for electron-density measurements. That combination reaches directly into the observational gap this book keeps circling: the first meters above the regolith, where LADEE's strongest high-altitude limits do not decide the problem. NASA's description is here: https://science.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-4/nasa-selects-2-instruments-for-artemis-iv-lunar-surface-science/

That matters because the remaining experiment is now easier to state precisely. DUSTER can measure the natural dust-and-plasma environment. The unresolved causal problem is whether illumination-driven charging actually *launches* grains from an undisturbed surface under lunar conditions, as opposed to charging or redirecting grains already mobilized by impacts, vibration, plume disturbance, thermal fracture, human activity, or some other process.

The technology ladder is also less hypothetical than it was a few years ago. In March 2025 NASA reported that the Electrodynamic Dust Shield flown on Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost Mission 1 successfully removed lunar regolith from test surfaces on the Moon. That is an engineered field, not evidence for a natural lofting rate, but it closes one argument cleanly: real lunar grains can be moved electrostatically on the lunar surface. NASA's 2026 low-gravity Electrostatic Dust Lofting program likewise uses UV illumination, vacuum, high-speed imaging, and reduced-gravity conditions to test lofting models that cannot be validated completely in one-g laboratory work. The remaining leap is not from equations to hardware. It is from controlled hardware to a causal field experiment on undisturbed lunar ground.

The instrument package can therefore be described partly as an extension of systems now selected, flown, or under test.

First: a stereoscopic high-speed imaging system focused on undisturbed regolith from roughly a centimeter to several meters above the surface. Use at least two viewing angles, ideally more, to reconstruct three-dimensional trajectories. Laser-sheet illumination can make small grains visible without depending entirely on sunlight, though the laser must be designed not to charge or thermally perturb the target significantly.

Second: a dust-impact and trajectory detector spanning a wider size range than imaging alone. DUSTER's Electrostatic Dust Analyzer points toward the right measurement space: particle charge, velocity, size, and flux rather than a binary dust/no-dust indicator. Multiple sensing heights would add the one thing a single sampling plane cannot give easily—vertical evolution of the population.

Third: charge-sensitive trajectory analysis. Place weak calibrated deflection fields away from the primary undisturbed patch or use non-invasive induction electrodes to estimate charge-to-mass ratio for grains that pass through a measurement volume.

Fourth: electric-field and surface-potential probes at multiple scales. The probe geometry must be modeled because it perturbs the very field being measured. Several heights and lateral offsets allow extrapolation toward the undisturbed surface.

Fifth: plasma diagnostics. Electron and ion density, temperature, energy distribution, and flow are required to understand charging currents. RESOLVE-like electron-density measurement would cover one important piece; full interpretation still needs enough plasma characterization to know what current system is charging the surface.

Sixth: calibrated ultraviolet and visible photometry, including polarization if possible. The same station should monitor horizon brightness so particle measurements can be connected directly to optical signatures.

Seventh: an impact monitor. A small seismic package, optical flash detector, or dedicated meteoroid/ejecta discriminator is needed so dust bursts from impacts are not misclassified as electrical events.

Eighth: thermal and vibration monitoring. Every actuator, shutter, or instrument cycle should be logged. Mechanical noise must be separated from true grain release.

Ninth: a controlled shadow maker.

This is still the decisive piece, and it is the part the observational program does not automatically supply.

A lightweight blade or mask, positioned far enough above the surface not to touch or mechanically disturb it, should create and remove a sharp shadow boundary on command. Its motion must be isolated from the regolith. The sequence should be randomized and blinded in analysis where practical.

The intervention converts correlation into causation.

Natural sunset changes illumination, temperature, plasma geometry, and instrument conditions together. An artificial shadow changes illumination locally while leaving many other variables nearly constant on short timescales.

The physics is not invented for this experiment. Laboratory work has spent years showing that light-shadow boundaries can generate strong local differential charging. A 2025 vacuum-chamber study explicitly simulated electrostatic activation of lunar-regolith dust at a moving light-shadow boundary, while earlier experiments found much stronger fields near such boundaries than the few-V/m large-scale sheath fields usually invoked for a uniformly illuminated surface. The experiment on the Moon would ask whether those local fields beat adhesion often enough to produce a measurable natural flux.

If dust flux jumps after the shadow arrives, and the effect repeats with timing predicted by charging models, the case for electrostatic mobilization strengthens dramatically.

If nothing happens across hundreds of trials under field conditions expected to favor launch, the surviving near-surface theory loses major territory.

Tenth: controlled ultraviolet illumination.

At night or inside a shadow, illuminate a patch with a tunable UV source while holding mechanical conditions fixed. Change wavelength across the photoemission threshold of relevant minerals. If grain motion follows photoelectron production rather than simple heating, the mechanism becomes much harder to dismiss.

NASA's current Electrostatic Dust Lofting flight program already uses the core laboratory version of this idea: UV illumination of regolith simulant under vacuum, observed with high-speed imaging under reduced-gravity conditions. The lunar version should not merely repeat that demonstration at one-sixth g. It should use the UV source as a perturbation *against a simultaneously measured natural baseline*.

These interventions should be paired with sham trials.

Move the shadow apparatus without actually changing illumination.

Run a visible-light source with similar heating but insufficient photon energy for comparable photoemission.

Cycle electronics without applying fields.

The experiment should be designed as though the most skeptical competent critic wrote the controls.

Site selection matters.

The first station should avoid a heavily trafficked landing zone. A pristine or minimally disturbed patch gives natural baseline. The lander should touch down far enough away that plume alteration at the target is characterized and small. A rover architecture like DUSTER's helps because the sensor package can move away from the landing disturbance, but the rover itself can charge, vibrate, shed dust, cast shadows, and disturb grains. Distance is not innocence. The instrument has to map its own contamination field.

The station should operate through multiple lunar days.

One sunset is anecdote.

Ten are a beginning.

A year of events across solar-wind conditions, magnetotail passages, meteor streams, and thermal cycles gives statistics.

The experiment should be preregistered before the first sunset.

That word is more common in clinical trials and psychology than planetary science, but it belongs here.

Define primary outcomes.

For example: change in upward flux of 0.5–10 micrometer grains within five seconds, one minute, and ten minutes after artificial shadow onset, relative to randomized control intervals.

Define secondary outcomes: launch velocity, charge-to-mass distribution, maximum height, lateral displacement, and optical scattering.

Define event cuts before seeing the data.

Define what constitutes a positive electrostatic response.

Define what null result would reject a model family.

This prevents the theory from moving after every measurement.

Different models can submit their own predictions in advance.

Model A predicts high flux at natural sunset but little response to small artificial shadows because global sheath structure dominates.

Model B predicts strong local response because microfields dominate.

Model C predicts no direct detachment but measurable trajectory deflection for grains freed mechanically.

Model D predicts nanograin motion below optical imaging threshold but visible in charge-sensitive sensors.

All can be tested in the same station.

The experiment becomes a tournament of mechanisms rather than a referendum on one word.

The horizon-glow question should be included explicitly.

If near-surface dust is measured, use its actual size, density, altitude, and scattering properties to calculate expected brightness in Surveyor-like geometry. Does it reproduce the old line after sunset?

If yes, a fifty-year loop closes.

If no, electrostatic motion can be real while the Surveyor interpretation remains wrong.

The same should be done for Apollo orbital geometry.

Take measured launch distributions and propagate them upward under observed fields. Calculate high-altitude density. If the result is orders of magnitude below what is needed for Apollo glow, stop calling near-surface confirmation an Apollo solution.

This distinction should be built into the experiment's public communication plan before results arrive.

Controversial results are distorted fastest when the first press release collapses nested claims.

“Electric dust found on Moon” would instantly be read as “Apollo glow confirmed,” whether or not the measured grains rose five centimeters.

The mission should publish a claim matrix with the result.

Charging confirmed?

Natural detachment confirmed?

Near-surface flux measured?

Kilometer transport supported?

Visible glow reproduced?

Each gets its own answer.

The field no longer has to wait for a hypothetical lunar dust observatory before it can begin. DUSTER is already a selected measurement architecture. Blue Ghost has already provided a lunar-surface electrostatic engineering demonstration. Reduced-gravity UV-lofting hardware exists. The clean next move is to graft a discriminating intervention onto observational capability: a controlled shadow or UV perturbation beside a patch whose natural charge, dust flux, plasma state, vibration, and optical scattering are all being watched.

A second station at a different site can test geology dependence.

A third at the pole can test illumination extremes.

A station near a magnetic anomaly can test plasma effects.

The network becomes a proper environmental observatory.

One result would be especially interesting: robust grain motion with no corresponding visible horizon glow.

That outcome would separate the mechanism from the historical mystery decisively. Electrostatic dust transport would become established science in the near-surface regime, while Apollo's high-altitude glow would remain an optical problem.

Another result would be even cleaner: no measurable electrostatic launch under natural or controlled conditions, despite fields and charging matching model predictions.

Then cohesion wins.

The field would pivot toward impact-generated dust as the dominant natural mobile population, with electrostatics mostly modifying already-free particles or acting only at nanoscales.

A third outcome could surprise everyone: strong dust motion exists but correlates primarily with thermal fracture, micrometeoroid disturbances, or another source, while electric fields merely sort trajectories afterward.

This is why simultaneous sensors matter.

Nature is allowed to answer a question we did not ask elegantly.

The mission should also preserve raw high-cadence data publicly.

Dust events are sparse and weird. Future analysts will have better models. Archiving only processed flux products would repeat the mistake of leaving later generations with summaries instead of event streams.

Include calibration data, housekeeping, actuator logs, and exact geometry.

Make the instrument's mistakes inspectable.

This book began with a line of light whose original measurement context is difficult to reconstruct completely.

The next experiment should be designed so nobody in 2076 has to guess what the camera did.

Science needs examples of long-running anomalies that end well.

Not with ridicule.

Not with belief winning.

With a better instrument placed in the right location.

Lunar dust offers that chance.

The question has survived because every generation measured a neighboring part of the system.

We now know exactly where the gap is.

Put the intervention in the gap.
