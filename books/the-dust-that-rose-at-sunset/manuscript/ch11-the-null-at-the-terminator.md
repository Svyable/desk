# The Null at the Terminator

The most important sentence in a null result is usually the one after “we found no evidence.”

No evidence of what, where, when, and above which threshold?

Lunar dust makes this unusually visible because the disputed phenomenon occupies a parameter space too large for one instrument. Grain radius ranges from nanometers to hundreds of microns and beyond. Altitude ranges from a contact point to hundreds of kilometers. Velocities range from creeping rearrangement to hypervelocity impact debris. Source mechanisms overlap. A detector’s silence slices through that space; it does not erase it.

The 2015 LDEX search by Jamey Szalay and Mihály Horányi is one of the book’s central documents because it states the slice clearly. The authors searched LADEE observations over the lunar terminator for the putative population of small electrostatically lofted grains long associated with the Apollo horizon-glow interpretation. For a characteristic radius around 0.1 micrometers, within the instrument’s detection model, they found no evidence of such grains from roughly three to 250 kilometers altitude.

That is a hard result.

It is not a universal one.

The distinction has been abused in both directions.

Skeptical summaries sometimes turn the paper into “LADEE proved lunar dust levitation does not happen.” That claim extends the null downward through three kilometers of unmeasured space and across particle sizes and fluxes outside the modeled population.

Enthusiastic summaries do the opposite. They emphasize that LDEX could have missed smaller grains or near-surface motion and treat the old high-altitude hypothesis as essentially untouched.

That ignores what the experiment was designed to test.

A good null result should hurt.

If it does not force the target theory to give up parameter space, either the test was weak or the theory has become immunized against evidence.

LDEX hurt the classic tall electrostatic cloud.

The old literature had proposed enough 0.1-micrometer-scale grains at high altitude to generate measurable optical effects. LDEX passed through the terminator repeatedly. The expected enhancement was absent. Other remote observations had also pushed upper limits lower. The straightforward persistent-cloud interpretation lost credibility.

The surviving possibilities became more specific.

One: the grains are smaller than the assumed population and therefore much harder for LDEX to detect individually or collectively under the same calibration.

Two: the grains stay mostly below three kilometers, perhaps far below.

Three: the phenomenon is intermittent and LADEE did not sample the relevant conditions at sufficient strength.

Four: the Apollo optical glow came from another source.

Five: more than one of these is true.

Each branch can be tested.

This is how a null result becomes a research map instead of a tombstone.

The first branch—smaller grains—leads toward nanodust and ultraviolet remote sensing. Smaller particles have radically higher charge-to-mass ratios, but their scattering and impact-ionization behavior changes. A population can become optically relevant at certain wavelengths while remaining difficult for a detector calibrated to larger individual impacts. Later LADEE UVS analysis reporting evidence for a dynamic nanodust cloud lives in this branch.

The second branch—lower altitude—leads to surface experiments. If electrostatic fields are strongest near microtopographic shadow boundaries and grain launches are weak, density may collapse within meters. An orbiter is then simply the wrong tool.

The third branch—intermittency—is the most dangerous intellectually because it can explain any null after the fact. To remain scientific, intermittency must be tied to an independently measurable trigger: solar-wind shocks, magnetotail passage, eclipses, meteoroid events, local surface charging, or another environmental variable. “It happens when you were not looking” is not a mechanism.

The fourth branch—wrong optical interpretation—is the cleanest and often the least emotionally satisfying. It says the old observations can remain real while dust loses the job of explaining them.

This branch should always remain on the table.

There is a useful analogy with particle physics. A collider experiment excluding a particle over a particular mass and interaction range does not prove the particle class impossible. It removes a region from the exclusion plot. Theoretical interest then migrates, sometimes for good physical reasons and sometimes because the unexcluded regions are simply where hope remains.

Lunar dust needs its own exclusion plots.

Horizontal axis: grain radius.

Vertical axis: near-surface source flux or density.

Separate panels for altitude, velocity, local time, and plasma conditions.

Overlay Surveyor optical requirements, Apollo brightness models, Clementine limits, LRO limits, LDEX limits, UVS constraints, laboratory launch rates, and future surface data.

The surviving hypothesis space would become visible.

Right now, much of it lives in prose.

Prose is a terrible place to keep exclusion boundaries.

Terms like “small,” “tenuous,” “transient,” “low-altitude,” and “possible” can drift over decades. A plot forces numbers onto them.

This is particularly important because the evidentiary direction changed after LADEE. Before the mission, models often asked what dust population could explain the glow. After a strong null, the better question is what glow population remains allowed by all later constraints.

Those are not the same optimization.

The first finds a fit.

The second finds an intersection.

Scientific history is full of theories that looked persuasive when fitted to one dataset and evaporated when required to occupy the intersection of several independent datasets.

The intersection is where lunar electrostatic dust now lives.

There is another reason the LDEX null matters: it occurred alongside a successful discovery of impact dust, reducing uncertainty about the instrument’s in-flight behavior. LDEX was not merely functional; it measured a population whose spatial and temporal structure matched a coherent external forcing. That positive control strengthens confidence in the absent electrostatic signature.

This does not eliminate all calibration uncertainty. Impact ejecta and slow electrostatic grains may strike at different relative velocities and charges. Detector response can differ. The collective-signal search for subthreshold grains has its own noise model.

But “maybe the detector just could not see dust” is no longer adequate.

A serious alternative has to engage the instrument physics.

This is an important standard for controversial evidence generally. When an experiment produces a null, the strongest counterargument is not “measurement can be wrong.” Measurement can always be wrong. The strongest counterargument identifies a specific transfer-function mismatch large enough to reconcile the predicted effect with the observed signal.

How much lower would impact ionization yield need to be?

How much smaller must grains be?

How rare must events become?

How steeply must density fall with altitude?

Quantify the escape route.

If the required adjustment is modest and independently plausible, the theory survives comfortably.

If it requires five simultaneous special conditions, confidence should fall.

The Apollo glow problem has not always been discussed with that discipline because the old observation itself is hard to quantify. A hand-drawn streamer does not give a clean error bar. This asymmetry favors theory survival: later nulls are precise while the target observation remains flexible.

One way out is to turn the Apollo record into a formal likelihood range.

Reconstruct minimum and maximum plausible brightness from photographs, crew reports, camera settings, and visual thresholds. Propagate uncertainty in grain phase functions. Then ask what dust populations can generate any member of that range without violating LDEX and other limits.

Perhaps a narrow window remains.

Perhaps none does.

Either outcome would be more informative than another review sentence saying the issue remains controversial.

The terminator itself also deserves more careful definition.

From orbit, the terminator is a global curve dividing day and night.

At the surface, there are millions of terminators: boulder edges, crater rims, grain shadows, equipment shadows, and microcavities. An orbiter crossing the global terminator samples one scale of electrical transition. The strongest local fields may occur around much smaller boundaries everywhere on the dayside.

This means “no terminator enhancement” at orbital altitude does not necessarily constrain “shadow-edge transport” near the ground as strongly as the shared word suggests.

Again, scale saves or kills interpretations.

A compelling future test would deliberately connect scales.

Place a surface dust tracker beneath a low orbiter carrying a particle detector. Observe the same region through local sunset. If the surface instrument records a burst of electrostatic launches but the orbiter sees nothing, we learn the vertical transfer efficiency directly. If both see correlated enhancement, the old tall-cloud picture regains territory. If neither sees anything under favorable charging conditions, near-surface models take a major hit.

The paired experiment would convert a fifty-year argument into a transfer function.

The null result also has an engineering lesson. Hazard models should not treat absence above three kilometers as reassurance about the first meter. A rover, seal, optical window, or solar panel lives where the remaining uncertainty is largest. Conversely, mission planners should not use speculative near-surface transport to imply an orbital hazard contradicted by LADEE.

Different assets occupy different slices of parameter space.

The scientific uncertainty should be mapped to the engineering geometry.

There is no such thing as “the lunar dust hazard” any more than there is one “radiation hazard.” Radiation risk depends on particle type, energy, shielding, exposure time, and biology. Dust risk depends on size, velocity, charge, abundance, material, and interface.

LADEE made this specificity unavoidable.

The best scientific reaction to a null is gratitude mixed with irritation.

Gratitude because the world has removed a possibility.

Irritation because the surviving possibilities usually become harder to measure.

The classic electrostatic cloud was easy to imagine from orbit. The remaining near-surface regime requires delicate instruments next to abrasive regolith under extreme thermal and plasma conditions. Nanodust requires different diagnostics. Rare transient states require long-duration monitoring.

Better evidence often makes the experiment more annoying.

That is a sign of progress.

The Moon’s terminator is no longer a blank stage on which any dust fountain can be projected. Large regions of parameter space have been occupied by impact ejecta or excluded by measurements. The remaining electrostatic problem has moved closer to the surface, toward smaller particles, lower fluxes, and more conditional environments.

If the effect is there, that is where we should go.

A null result does not tell science to stop looking.

It tells science where not to look again in the same way.
