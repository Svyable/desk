# Adoption Becomes a Labor Market

A technology becomes easier to adopt when you can hire someone who already knows how to use it.

That sounds almost tautological.

It is one of the strongest feedback loops in technological change.

The first organization to adopt a system cannot recruit from a mature labor market because the labor market does not exist yet. It has to create capability internally.

Employees learn by doing.

They make mistakes.

They invent job descriptions after the work has already begun. They become the people other companies later want to hire.

The first adopter is therefore training the future labor supply.

This is another contribution that ordinary product metrics barely capture.

A company buys software. Revenue is recorded.

The company then spends thousands of employee hours learning how to integrate, govern and use the system. Those hours create human capital. Some of it stays inside the company. Some of it leaves through turnover, conferences, open-source contributions, consulting, documentation and professional networks.

The knowledge diffuses.

A second company can now hire experience instead of manufacturing all of it from scratch.

Adoption cost falls.

This is how a technology acquires a profession around itself.

At first, there are users.

Then there are specialists.

Then there are trainers, consultants, recruiters, certifications, agencies, vendors and managers whose careers assume the technology will continue to exist.

The labor market becomes a complement.

It also becomes a constituency.

People who have built careers around a system have reasons to support its continuation. They write articles, teach others, request features, attend conferences, create businesses and persuade employers that the capability deserves a budget.

The technology now has human infrastructure.

This can be more durable than a marketing campaign because careers create sunk costs and incentives distributed across many organizations.

A platform with millions of trained practitioners becomes easier to adopt even if a competitor is technically better.

The competitor is not only fighting the product.

It is fighting the labor market.

This is one reason established enterprise software can persist for decades. Companies know how to hire for it. Consultants know how to implement it. Universities teach adjacent skills. Recruiters recognize the keywords. Employees can move between employers without discarding their expertise.

Skill portability creates network effects of its own.

A worker learns a widely used technology because the skill is employable.

Employers adopt the technology because workers exist.

More adoption makes the skill more employable.

The loop can stabilize a technical ecosystem long after the original product advantages have narrowed.

Early adopters help start it.

This gives the first organizations an uncomfortable strategic choice.

Train people and risk losing them.

Do not train people and fail to adopt.

Companies often respond with retention agreements, specialized career paths or attempts to keep knowledge proprietary. These measures can slow leakage. They cannot prevent a labor market from forming if the technology spreads broadly.

In fact, trying to hoard skill can weaken the ecosystem on which the adopter depends.

A company using an obscure technology may enjoy scarce internal expertise while suffering from expensive hiring, weak vendor support and few external consultants. A more open skill market reduces the company’s uniqueness and increases its resilience.

The adopter has to decide which advantage it wants.

Scarcity of expertise can create temporary differentiation.

Abundance of expertise can create durable infrastructure.

The best strategy often converts one into the other.

Use the scarce period to build lead.

Support the abundant period so the technology stops depending on a few heroes.

This mirrors the product’s journey from enthusiast tool to mainstream system.

The first practitioners are heroic because heroics are necessary.

They work around bugs, understand undocumented behavior and know whom to contact when the system fails.

Maturity means reducing the amount of heroism required.

Documentation absorbs tacit knowledge.

Automation absorbs repetitive expertise.

Training standardizes basics.

Monitoring makes failure legible.

Job roles separate responsibilities.

The technology becomes an organization.

This is why labor-market formation can be read as a maturity signal.

When a new capability first appears, job titles are unstable.

Companies advertise for people with impossible combinations of skills because nobody knows where the work belongs. A “growth hacker,” “cloud architect,” “data scientist,” “prompt engineer” or “AI operations” role can emerge before professional boundaries settle.

The titles are awkward because the work is awkward.

Over time, some titles disappear because the capability diffuses into ordinary jobs. Others harden into professions.

The trajectory tells you something about the technology.

If a specialized role disappears because everyone now possesses the skill, the technology may have become more important, not less.

Typing is the obvious historical example. “Typist” was once a distinct occupation because typewriting skill was scarce and machinery specialized. Keyboard use later became embedded across office work. The standalone role lost importance as the capability became ubiquitous.

Spreadsheets followed a similar pattern in softer form. Organizations once had specialists doing calculations that ordinary workers later performed themselves. The skill migrated from department to desk.

AI may produce many transitions of this kind.

Some early roles will become professions.

Some will become features of every profession.

The early adopter cannot know which with certainty.

That is why transferable skill matters more than title speculation.

A person who learns how to evaluate model output, structure a task for automation, design human checkpoints, protect sensitive data and reason about delegation has acquired capabilities that can survive the disappearance of a particular role called “prompt engineer.”

The interface can change.

The underlying work remains.

This is the labor-market version of adopting a problem rather than a product.

Early career advantage comes from learning where work is moving, not memorizing the branding around the movement.

Institutions can use the same logic in workforce planning.

A company deciding whether to adopt a technology should ask not only whether the product works but whether a labor market is forming around it.

Can we hire people?

Can we train people internally?

Are the skills transferable?

Are consultants appearing?

Do universities or vocational programs recognize the capability?

Are experienced workers moving between companies?

Is the vendor the only source of expertise?

These questions reveal dependency.

A product with no external skill market can be powerful and fragile.

The company may become dependent on a tiny internal team. If two people leave, the “strategic platform” becomes an archaeological site.

A healthy adoption plan therefore creates redundancy in knowledge.

This is not merely an HR concern.

It changes the technology’s practical value.

A system that can be operated only by one genius is a prototype no matter how much revenue it processes.

The labor market also changes bargaining power between workers and employers.

Early expertise can command a premium because supply is scarce. Workers who learn a new capability before it becomes widely taught may receive opportunities disproportionate to the difficulty of the skill.

The premium attracts learners.

Training supply expands.

The skill becomes less scarce.

Wages normalize.

This is another adoption curve.

The early practitioner’s advantage decays unless she moves outward with the frontier.

She can become the person who trains others.

The person who integrates the technology into a more complex domain.

The person who understands the next bottleneck after the basic skill commoditizes.

Early adoption therefore creates a career rhythm.

Learn scarce capability.

Use it to reach harder problems.

Do not build identity around scarcity that success is likely to destroy.

This can feel unfair because mastery is supposed to create security.

In fast-moving fields, mastery often creates access to the next learning problem instead.

The first advantage buys the second only if the person keeps moving.

This is one reason early adopters who become evangelists can get stranded.

They become so attached to teaching the current technology that they stop noticing when the market has absorbed it. Their status depended on explaining what is now ordinary.

The expert becomes a historian before realizing the transition occurred.

The remedy is to follow the work, not the applause.

What remains difficult after everyone learns the tool?

That is where the next labor market forms.

With cloud computing, basic server provisioning became easier while distributed systems, security, cost management and platform engineering became important. With smartphones, mobile application development grew, then many mobile patterns became standard while new work moved into payments, location, sensors and platform-specific experiences. With data science, basic model fitting became commoditized while data engineering, deployment, governance and product integration grew in importance.

Capabilities move bottlenecks.

Bottlenecks create jobs.

Early adopters see the movement from inside.

This is why a worker can use early adoption as career research.

You do not need to bet your livelihood on every technology.

You can run small experiments to learn where the work is moving.

Use the tool on a real problem.

Notice what remains hard.

Notice what the tool makes newly possible.

Notice who has to coordinate for it to work.

Notice which failures require judgment rather than more automation.

Notice what customers now expect because the capability exists.

These observations reveal emerging labor demand before job boards do.

Job boards are lagging indicators.

They list work after organizations have decided they need it.

Early adopters experience the need before the title stabilizes.

This is the personal advantage in its clearest form.

You can become competent in a job before the job has a name.

That does not mean inventing a ridiculous title for yourself.

It means accumulating evidence that you can solve a class of problems organizations are about to encounter more often.

The evidence can travel through projects, open-source contributions, case studies, internal results, portfolios or simply reputation among people working nearby.

The worker becomes a lead user of her own career.

The labor-market loop also explains why companies sometimes open their tools, APIs or training materials even when openness appears to give value away.

A technology without trained users is harder to sell.

Documentation, free tiers, educational licenses, developer tools and certifications are forms of labor-market investment. They create people capable of demanding and operating the product.

The company subsidizes learning because learned customers are easier to acquire.

The students become future users.

The users become employees.

The employees become buyers.

The buyers train another cohort.

This is how a product can become embedded in institutions through people rather than contracts.

It also creates risks of capture.

A vendor can design certification so that a worker’s employability depends heavily on one proprietary ecosystem. Employers then face switching costs because their workforce’s credentials are tied to the vendor. Workers face switching costs because their accumulated expertise is less valuable elsewhere.

The labor market becomes another moat.

Early adopters should notice whether the skill they are accumulating belongs to them.

Can it travel?

Does learning this product deepen understanding of a domain, or merely teach the product’s menus?

Does certification represent real capability, or permission granted by a vendor?

Would a competitor value the skill?

Would the skill survive a platform change?

These questions matter because early career investments can be larger than early product purchases.

A worker can replace a bad device.

Replacing five years of identity tied to a dying platform is harder.

The best early-adoption strategy therefore compounds human capital independently of vendor fate.

Learn the architecture.

Learn the problem.

Learn the failure modes.

Learn the organizational work around the technology.

Then use the particular tool as a laboratory.

If it wins, you are early.

If it loses, you still know the territory.

This is how adoption becomes a labor market and how a labor market becomes part of the technology.

The future needs people who can operate it before operating it is ordinary.

The first adopters create those people simply by needing them.

Then the people carry the future somewhere else.
