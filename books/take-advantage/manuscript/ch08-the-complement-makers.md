# The Complement Makers

On July 11, 2008, Apple opened the App Store.

Three days later, the company said users had downloaded more than ten million applications.

The number was large enough to make the launch look like an ordinary consumer success story: a company created a store, customers arrived, downloads followed.

But the App Store was not a store in the usual sense.

Apple did not make most of what was being sold.

More than eight hundred native applications were available at launch, created by outside developers. Two months later Apple said the store had more than three thousand applications and had passed one hundred million downloads. By July 2009, after one year, Apple reported more than sixty-five thousand applications, more than one hundred thousand developers in its iPhone Developer Program and more than 1.5 billion downloads.

Users had not merely consumed Apple’s product.

Their presence gave other people a reason to make products for it.

Those products gave still more people a reason to buy Apple’s product.

This is the complement loop.

A complement is something that becomes more valuable when used with something else. Gasoline complements cars. Games complement consoles. Software complements computers. Charging networks complement electric vehicles. A trained consultant complements a business platform. A good restaurant can complement a theater district because the combined trip becomes more attractive.

Complements are everywhere, which makes them easy to ignore.

We tend to evaluate a product as though its value were contained inside the object. The phone has this camera, this processor, this battery, this price. Yet a large part of the phone’s usefulness may live outside the phone: applications, services, accessories, payment acceptance, repair networks, cloud storage, creator communities and the habits of other people who know how to use the same system.

The early adopter can influence all of those external layers.

A developer deciding whether to build an application needs a market.

The first users become that market.

This creates an unusual relationship between customer demand and product supply. When early users download applications, they are not only rewarding existing developers. They are producing evidence for developers who have not built anything yet.

The observed market recruits the unobserved supply.

That can happen quickly when the complementary product is cheap to create and distribute.

Software is especially powerful because a small team can build a product and reach a global installed base without manufacturing physical inventory for every user. Digital distribution lowers the cost of searching for useful complements.

Thousands of developers can try different ideas at once.

Most will not matter.

That is the point.

A platform does not need to know in advance which applications should exist. It needs enough developers willing to search.

The users finance that search indirectly through attention and purchases.

This is another way early adoption extends invention beyond the original company.

Apple built the phone and the distribution architecture.

Developers explored the space of things a phone could become.

Users rewarded some experiments and ignored others.

Successful categories attracted imitation and specialization.

The platform learned what it was for by watching an ecology form around it.

The ecology then changed the hardware roadmap.

This kind of feedback can be difficult to attribute because causality runs in circles.

Did users adopt the iPhone because applications were good?

Did developers build good applications because users adopted the iPhone?

Yes.

The circularity is the mechanism.

A platform tries to start the loop on both sides. It recruits developers before the user base is large enough to guarantee returns. It recruits users before the complement catalog is mature enough to guarantee variety. Subsidies, developer tools, distribution, marketing, hardware quality and brand reputation all reduce the initial uncertainty.

Then adoption can make the proposition improve itself.

This is why platform companies care so much about developer conferences, software development kits and application marketplaces. The company is building a machine that persuades outsiders to make the company’s product more valuable.

The outsiders participate because they hope to capture some of the value they create.

The arrangement is productive and unstable.

The platform owner wants enough openness to attract complement makers and enough control to capture value, maintain security and shape the experience. Developers want access to users and enough freedom to build businesses. Users want abundant complements, low prices, safety and the ability to move without losing everything they bought.

These interests overlap until they do not.

The early period can disguise the tension because everyone is benefiting from growth.

Developers receive distribution they could not easily create alone.

Users receive a rapidly expanding product universe.

The platform receives a stronger ecosystem.

As the market matures, bargaining over fees, rankings, access, APIs, data and competing services becomes more visible.

The complement makers discover that they helped build a city on land they do not own.

This does not erase the value of the city.

It changes how early adopters should think about ecosystem participation.

If your adoption creates value for a platform, ask what claim you retain on the complements you build around it.

A user may build years of photographs, playlists, documents, automations, relationships and purchases inside a system. A developer may build a company whose distribution depends on one store. An enterprise may build workflows around one vendor’s interfaces.

These are complementary investments made by adopters.

They can become the platform’s moat.

The moat may not have been built by the platform owner alone.

This is one reason the word ecosystem is strategically convenient. It makes commercial dependencies sound like nature.

An ecosystem seems to emerge organically.

A platform economy is designed.

Rules determine who can enter, what can be sold, how payment works, what data can move, which interfaces are stable, who can compete with the platform owner and what happens when the owner changes the rules.

The complement loop therefore contains governance from the beginning.

Early adopters often have more influence over that governance than later users because the platform still needs them badly.

Developers can threaten not to build.

Large customers can insist on interfaces.

Communities can coordinate around open alternatives.

A platform without complements cannot credibly dismiss the people making them.

Success changes the balance.

Once the platform owns scarce distribution to a large audience, complement makers may need it more than it needs any one of them.

The relationship reverses.

This is another reason early adoption is a wasting source of leverage.

The first complementary investment can be strategically important to the platform.

The ten-thousandth may be interchangeable.

The best early adopters understand this and try to convert temporary leverage into durable rights, standards, customer relationships or transferable capabilities.

A developer can build on open protocols where possible.

An enterprise can require exportability.

A creator can preserve direct relationships with an audience instead of relying entirely on an algorithmic feed.

A company can avoid architectures in which every complement is specific to one vendor.

The goal is not to reject platforms.

Platforms are powerful precisely because they coordinate complements efficiently.

The goal is to know what you are helping create.

This applies outside software.

Electric vehicles illustrate the physical version.

A car without convenient charging is a different product from the same car surrounded by reliable charging. Early vehicle buyers create utilization for charging sites. Charging sites reduce anxiety for later vehicle buyers. Greater vehicle adoption improves the business case for more charging. Standards around connectors and payment reduce friction. Repair shops acquire equipment. Insurers accumulate loss data. Used-car markets learn how to price battery health.

The automobile becomes easier to own because complementary institutions learn the automobile.

A manufacturer can build some of these complements itself.

Tesla did, notably with its charging network.

Other complements emerge from separate companies and public policy. The final ownership experience is a bundle assembled across institutions.

The early adopter helps create demand for the bundle.

The same pattern appears in heat pumps, rooftop solar, drones, laboratory equipment, enterprise software and new construction methods.

A technically excellent device can fail because the installer ecosystem is weak.

A new building technology can save material but lose because local contractors do not know it, inspectors distrust it and lenders lack comparable projects.

A diagnostic system can perform well but fail because hospitals cannot integrate it into records and reimbursement.

The missing product is often a complement.

This is why early adopters sometimes spend more time building around a technology than using the technology itself.

They find installers.

They write scripts.

They train staff.

They create policies.

They change contracts.

They teach suppliers.

They solve the complement deficit privately.

If enough adopters encounter the same deficit, a business appears to solve it publicly.

That is a market being pulled into existence from the edges.

A consultant sees companies struggling with the same implementation and packages a service.

A training company sees employers needing the same skill and creates a course.

A hardware maker sees the same accessory request and begins manufacturing.

A financial company sees an unfamiliar asset class becoming common enough to underwrite.

A standards body sees incompatible implementations creating enough pain to justify coordination.

The complement is born because adoption made the missing piece visible.

This leads to a counterintuitive way to look at immature technologies.

Do not ask only whether the core product is improving.

Watch the periphery.

Are unrelated companies beginning to build around it without being paid by the producer?

Are people creating careers around implementing it?

Are suppliers offering specialized components?

Are training materials becoming better?

Are insurers, lenders, regulators and professional associations learning how to classify it?

A healthy complement ecology is evidence that other actors believe the installed base will persist long enough to justify their own investments.

Their commitment is a second-order adoption signal.

They are adopting the adopters.

This can be more informative than consumer excitement.

A product can be fashionable for a season without generating serious complementary investment. Nobody builds a profession around a novelty unless the expected market is larger than the novelty itself.

The complement makers are betting on continuity.

Their bets make continuity more likely.

Again the loop appears.

Expectations produce investment.

Investment improves the user proposition.

The improved proposition validates expectations.

The sociology of technological expectations has long examined how visions of the future can coordinate present action. People build today because they expect a tomorrow in which the investment makes sense.

The complement ecosystem is that idea with invoices.

A developer does not need certainty that a platform will dominate.

She needs enough expected users to justify six months of work.

An installer does not need certainty that heat pumps will replace every furnace.

He needs enough jobs to justify training and equipment.

A lender does not need certainty that an unfamiliar asset is risk-free.

It needs enough performance history to build a model.

Each actor has a threshold.

Early adopters help the system cross thresholds one at a time.

That is how a future gains institutional thickness.

The phrase matters because mature technologies are thick.

They have specialists, routines, laws, vendors, spare parts, courses, insurance, repair, finance, documentation, standards and cultural expectations layered around them.

Immature technologies are thin.

They may have one brilliant product and almost nothing else.

Adoption thickens them.

Not automatically and not always efficiently.

It creates enough repeated need that specialized complements can survive.

Once those complements exist, the core technology can become easier to adopt than a rival that is technically superior but institutionally thin.

This is why late entrants sometimes face a deeper problem than catching up on features.

They have to catch up on everything users built around the incumbent.

The App Store made this visible at digital speed.

Within a year of launch, tens of thousands of applications and a huge developer community had turned the iPhone from a fixed product into a platform whose capabilities could expand after purchase.

A person choosing the phone was increasingly choosing access to what other people had made.

The early users helped create the audience that made that making worthwhile.

They did not write every application.

They did something subtler.

They made writing the next application economically legible.

That is what complement makers need from early adopters.

Not applause.

A reason to build beside them.

There is a practical difference between a real ecosystem and a sponsored one.

Early platforms often pay for complements because the private economics are not yet good enough. They fund developers, guarantee purchases, subsidize hardware, offer cloud credits, waive fees, provide marketing or build reference applications themselves. This can be sensible. Somebody has to populate the empty shelves.

But a subsidized complement does not tell you the same thing as an independent one.

The first tells you the platform owner is committed.

The second tells you an outsider expects to make money or achieve some other durable objective without being paid merely to participate.

That distinction becomes useful when evaluating immature markets.

If every complement disappears when the grants stop, the ecosystem may have been theatrical.

If outside firms continue investing, hiring and competing after the introductory support fades, the platform has begun to create its own economic gravity.

Independent complementary investment is therefore one of the clearest signs that adoption is escaping the producer’s balance sheet.

The future is recruiting its own capital.

That capital can take small forms.

A repair technician buys diagnostic equipment because enough customers now own the device.

A lawyer creates a contract template because enough clients are entering the same category.

A training instructor builds a course because students are willing to pay before employers formally require the skill.

A niche software company integrates with a new platform because existing customers keep asking.

None of these actors needs to believe in the platform’s mission.

They need a credible path to revenue.

Their indifference can be reassuring.

Believers are willing to lose money for a while.

Complement makers who are simply doing business force the ecosystem to answer a harder question: is there enough repeated demand to support strangers who owe the core product nothing?

A thick ecosystem should eventually contain people who do not care about the founding story.

They care about service calls, invoices and repeat customers.

That is maturity.

Complement quality matters as much as complement count.

A platform can advertise a huge catalog while most of the catalog is abandoned, derivative or economically irrelevant. A marketplace full of applications can still leave users dependent on three essential vendors. A hardware ecosystem can contain dozens of accessories while repair remains impossible. A technology can have many consultants and no one willing to provide a warranty.

Counting complements is like counting customers without asking whether they return.

The useful question is what uncertainty the complement removes.

Does the installer make deployment routine?

Does the lender make financing available at ordinary terms?

Does the developer make the missing workflow possible?

Does the standard make competing suppliers interchangeable?

Does the repair network reduce the fear of being stranded?

Does training lower the amount of expertise a new adopter must invent internally?

Different complements thicken different parts of the future.

Some are especially important because they preserve exits.

An exporter, adapter, independent repair shop or multi-platform consultant can make an ecosystem more attractive by making dependence less frightening. This sounds paradoxical. Why would a platform benefit from businesses that help customers leave?

Because credible exit can increase willingness to enter.

A customer may adopt sooner when she knows data can move, hardware can be serviced outside one vendor, and skilled people understand multiple systems. A developer may build more confidently when an open protocol lets the core business survive a change in platform owner.

The healthiest complement can be the one that weakens lock-in.

It turns participation from a permanent bet into a revisable one.

That can enlarge the market even if it reduces the platform owner’s theoretical control.

Closed ecosystems sometimes win anyway because integration quality outweighs exit risk. The point is not to pretend openness always dominates. It is to recognize that complement structure changes the adopter’s risk, not merely the adopter’s feature list.

There is also a darker version of the complement loop.

A large installed base can support parasitic complements as easily as useful ones.

Scams, low-quality consultants, manipulative extensions, predatory financing and businesses built around exploiting confusing defaults can flourish where attention and money concentrate. The existence of an ecosystem proves opportunity, not goodness.

Early adopters help discover this too.

The first fraud cases teach marketplaces what identity checks they need. The first bad installers teach manufacturers why certification matters. The first integrations that leak data teach enterprises to ask different security questions. The ecosystem matures partly by learning which complements should be excluded.

Selection is part of complement formation.

A platform that never removes bad complements can become unsafe.

A platform that controls complements too aggressively can suppress the distributed search that makes ecosystems valuable.

The governance problem has no permanent solution because the system changes as it grows.

Early on, scarcity of complements argues for permissiveness.

Later, abundance can make quality and bargaining power more important than raw variety.

The adopter should notice when that phase changes.

At the beginning, a handful of weird complement makers can make a thin technology usable.

At maturity, the strategic question becomes whether the complement layer remains competitive enough that the core technology continues to improve rather than simply collect rent.

The future is not thick merely because many things have been built around it.

It is thick when enough independent actors can survive around it that the next customer no longer has to solve the surrounding problems alone.

That is the real gift early adopters give to complement makers.

And the real gift complement makers return.
