# When the Customer Is an Agent

For most of commercial history, adoption had a body.

Somebody walked into a store, signed a contract, carried a device into a building, persuaded a manager, learned a new interface, installed a machine, or typed a credit-card number into a checkout form. Even when organizations were the formal customers, the act of adoption could usually be traced to human attention. A buyer had to notice, compare, authorize and act.

That assumption is beginning to loosen.

In 2026, payments companies, merchants and software platforms started building infrastructure for a customer that can search, compare and transact without being a person at the moment of purchase. Visa described agents already paying for things such as travel, inventory, data and compute. Mastercard introduced infrastructure for machine-to-machine payments designed for transactions that may be continuous, high-frequency and in some cases worth only fractions of a cent. Shopify and Google co-developed an open Universal Commerce Protocol intended to let agents discover products, negotiate capabilities, check out and handle post-purchase workflows across merchants.

The announcements are easy to dismiss as another round of platform positioning around artificial intelligence.

Some of them may prove to be exactly that.

But the economic possibility underneath them is more interesting than the branding.

What happens to adoption when the adopter can run all night?

A human customer has bounded attention. She cannot evaluate ten thousand suppliers before buying a $12 part. She cannot renegotiate a cloud workload every forty seconds. She cannot continuously check whether a different data provider offers a better price, latency or license. She cannot maintain a standing market search across every consumable input used by a small business.

Software can.

That does not mean software should be given unrestricted authority to spend. The difficult work of agentic commerce is precisely the construction of boundaries: budgets, permissions, identities, audit trails, merchant verification, fraud controls, recourse, human escalation and rules for what an agent is allowed to decide. The payments industry is not merely trying to make a faster Buy button. It is trying to create a recognizable customer where the customer is partly code.

That shift changes the subject of this book.

Early adopters have always helped produce the future because their commitments become evidence, learning, infrastructure and social permission. Agentic customers could accelerate each loop while making the identity of the adopter less obvious.

Imagine a small software company that gives an internal agent a monthly budget for infrastructure. The agent can purchase compute, storage, database capacity, security scans and specialized APIs as needs arise. It can compare alternatives using current usage rather than last quarter’s procurement assumptions. It can discover a new service because another tool exposes it through a machine-readable description. It can test the service on a small workload and abandon it if performance disappoints.

The agent is now an early adopter on behalf of the company.

But what exactly has adopted?

The organization authorized the budget. A developer defined the objective. A model or program discovered the vendor. A payment network authenticated the transaction. The vendor observed demand. Usage generated telemetry. The system may repeat the purchase without any employee remembering the product’s name.

Commercial agency has become distributed.

This matters because one of the hidden limits on technological diffusion has always been evaluation cost.

A new supplier can be objectively better and still fail because nobody has time to discover it. A new API can save money and remain unused because migration requires a person to read documentation, obtain procurement approval, configure credentials, test the integration and remember to reconsider the incumbent six months later. Search cost, switching cost and organizational inertia protect existing providers even when the technical alternative is superior.

Agents can lower some of those costs.

If they do, early markets may form faster.

A service that once needed a salesperson to recruit its first hundred enterprise customers might instead become callable by software that encounters the relevant need. A specialized model, data source or microservice could earn revenue because another agent discovers it at the moment of use. A machine-readable capability description could matter as much as a homepage. Reliability, latency, unit price and permissions could become adoption signals that software evaluates continuously.

The early adopter would no longer need to enjoy novelty.

It would need a rule that permits experimentation.

That is a profound change.

Human early adoption has always been filtered through psychology. Curiosity, status, fear of missing out, ideology, professional identity and tolerance for frustration shape which technologies receive early attention. A machine buyer can be designed around different preferences. It may be indifferent to brand prestige. It may care intensely about latency. It may test unknown suppliers if the downside is bounded. It may prefer open standards because integration is cheaper. Or it may reproduce the biases of its training, ranking system and default marketplace so faithfully that apparent machine rationality simply hardens yesterday’s distribution.

Automation does not remove preference.

It relocates preference into policy.

The question “What will people adopt?” therefore gains a second form.

“What will agents be permitted and instructed to adopt?”

Consider the architecture of a purchasing agent.

It needs to know what it may buy. It needs a way to identify merchants or services. It needs a representation of the principal’s intent. It needs spending limits. It needs rules for data handling and security. It needs a way to compare offers. It needs payment credentials or another settlement mechanism. It needs receipts, logs and recourse. It may need to ask a human when a purchase exceeds a threshold or enters an unfamiliar category.

Each requirement becomes a new gate into the market.

A vendor can be excellent and invisible if the agent cannot discover it.

It can be discoverable and unusable if its terms cannot be parsed.

It can be parseable and unpurchasable if the payment rails do not recognize the transaction.

It can be purchasable and still excluded because a corporate policy requires a certification the vendor lacks.

The future of agentic commerce will therefore be built partly through standards.

That is why the Universal Commerce Protocol is more consequential than another conversational shopping interface. Its purpose is to create shared primitives for commerce between agents, merchants and payment providers. It is designed to describe capabilities such as discovery, checkout, fulfillment and post-purchase activity in forms different systems can understand.

A standard does not guarantee adoption.

It can still become one of the places where adoption compounds.

The first merchants that expose agent-readable capabilities make the protocol more useful to agent builders. The first agents that use the protocol make implementation more attractive to merchants. Payment providers that support the same interactions reduce integration work. Tool builders can write against a common interface rather than a collection of bilateral adapters.

This is the familiar complement loop wearing machine clothing.

The early adopters are again helping build the conditions for the next adopters.

But machine customers introduce a difference in tempo.

A person can be an early adopter of one product at a time.

An agent can be an early adopter thousands of times in miniature.

It can route five percent of a workload to a new provider. It can buy one dataset. It can purchase a tiny inference job. It can test a shipping option on one order. It can try an unfamiliar merchant for a low-risk commodity while preserving the incumbent for everything else.

This creates a possibility that human commerce rarely supports well: adoption as continuous portfolio rebalancing.

The traditional enterprise migration is often a cliff. A company selects a vendor, signs a long contract, performs an integration, migrates data and then lives with the decision. The size of the switching event makes experimentation expensive. Procurement therefore prefers known suppliers, which makes it harder for new suppliers to accumulate the evidence needed to become known.

A well-designed agent could turn some cliffs into ramps.

It could test before committing.

That favors reversible adoption.

Reversibility has appeared throughout this book as one of the most useful defenses against early-adopter error. The person who can try a tool for a week has a different risk profile from the city replacing physical infrastructure. The company that can route ten percent of queries to a new service can learn without betting the whole product. The developer who builds to an open protocol can switch providers more easily than one who integrates around proprietary assumptions.

Agents could make reversible experimentation cheap enough to become ordinary.

If that happens, the meaning of “early adopter” changes.

It stops being a personality type and becomes an operating mode.

An organization can encode curiosity.

It can reserve a small fraction of spend for exploration. It can let agents test alternatives inside bounded risk budgets. It can require evidence before expanding allocation. It can automatically reduce exposure when reliability falls. It can retain logs explaining which supplier entered the portfolio and why.

The firm becomes early-adopting by design rather than by temperament.

This sounds like a procurement optimization problem. It is also a future-making mechanism.

Every exploratory transaction is a small piece of demand evidence.

A new provider that receives ten thousand tiny machine-originated purchases may acquire a customer base without ever running a conventional marketing campaign. Those transactions can finance capacity, attract developers and reveal failure patterns. The provider becomes more viable because agents were permitted to experiment.

The loop can be extremely fast.

That speed creates new pathologies.

If many agents share the same ranking model, marketplace, payment rails or default protocol, they can converge on the same suppliers simultaneously. A small change in ranking could redirect enormous volume. A bug could generate purchases at machine speed. A bad incentive could create transaction storms. A manipulative vendor could optimize for whatever machine-visible metrics the agents use while degrading qualities that matter to humans but are harder to encode.

Human markets already have versions of these problems.

Algorithmic markets can compress their timescale.

A brand reputation that took years to build could be less valuable if purchasing agents weight current measurable performance heavily. That sounds meritocratic until a vendor learns to game the measurement. A price advantage could recruit demand instantly, then vanish when capacity is exhausted. An outage could trigger synchronized migration and turn resilience software into a mechanism for amplifying a shock.

The adoption feedback loop can become a control-system problem.

The faster the loop, the more important the brakes.

This is one reason trust infrastructure appears so prominently in the early agentic-payment systems. Payment networks are discussing authentication, credentials, consent, transaction controls and the ability to distinguish legitimate agent activity from fraud. Those are not decorative features added after the fun part. They are the architecture that determines whether autonomous adoption can scale without making commerce unusable.

The history of the internet offers a warning.

Protocols can make action cheap before institutions learn how to govern cheap action. Email became extraordinarily useful and extraordinarily cheap to send. The same economics enabled spam. Programmatic advertising made media buying efficient and created incentives for fraud, surveillance and arbitrage. APIs made software composable and created dependency chains that can fail in surprising ways.

A machine customer is another expansion of cheap action.

Cheap action creates abundance.

Abundance moves scarcity somewhere else.

In agentic commerce, one likely scarcity is trustworthy authorization.

Who is this agent acting for?

What did the principal permit?

What evidence can prove that permission later?

What counts as informed consent when an agent makes a sequence of intermediate purchases that no human reviews individually?

How does a merchant distinguish a legitimate autonomous buyer from a bot probing prices, inventory or vulnerabilities?

Who absorbs the loss when an agent buys the wrong thing for a defensible reason?

These questions will shape adoption more than another percentage point of model accuracy.

The first organizations willing to use agents for real purchases will create the precedents.

Their policies will become templates. Their incidents will become warnings. Their contracts will become clauses copied by lawyers who did not witness the original failure. Their accounting departments will decide what evidence is needed to reconcile a machine-originated purchase. Their security teams will define what credentials can be delegated. Their employees will discover which categories can be automated safely and which still need judgment.

The early adopter is again producing institutional knowledge.

Only this time the adopter is producing knowledge about how to delegate adoption itself.

That distinction deserves attention.

For centuries, markets assumed that the customer was the principal or a human agent of the principal. A purchasing manager could exceed authority, but the legal and organizational concepts were familiar. Software acting with persistent delegated purchasing power creates a new layer between intention and transaction.

The layer will be governed somehow.

Defaults will matter.

A personal shopping agent might prefer merchants that support easy returns. A corporate agent might prefer vendors with strong security attestations. A travel agent might optimize a weighted combination of cost, schedule reliability and loyalty status. A household agent might refuse subscriptions automatically. A procurement agent might favor suppliers with open data-export formats because the organization wants to preserve exit.

Each preference rewards a different commercial world.

This is the moral dimension of machine adoption.

An agent does not need moral agency for its purchasing policy to have moral consequences.

If millions of agents automatically prefer repairable products, repairability becomes more valuable to producers. If they reward low carbon intensity where reliable data exists, environmental characteristics enter competition differently. If they value privacy, merchants have a financial reason to expose verifiable privacy properties. If they optimize only for immediate price, they may punish exactly the forms of resilience, labor quality and maintenance that become visible only later.

The principal’s values become market pressure through code.

We have had algorithmic recommendation for years, but recommendation and delegated purchase are different powers.

A recommendation shapes attention.

A purchasing agent can shape revenue directly.

That shortens the distance between ranking and production.

Suppose a travel agent repeatedly favors hotels with free cancellation because its principal’s schedule is uncertain. Hotels observe the booking pattern. Some change pricing. Others redesign inventory rules. Distribution platforms expose cancellation terms more clearly because agents use them. A property-management software vendor adds machine-readable policy fields. The payment provider supports conditional authorization. What began as a preference becomes an ecosystem requirement.

This is adoption manifesting the future at machine speed.

The same mechanism can work badly.

Suppose agents optimize for products with the most reviews because review volume is an easy trust signal. Established sellers gain more traffic, which creates more reviews, which attracts more agents. New entrants become nearly invisible unless they buy distribution. The feedback loop concentrates the market even when new entrants are better.

Or suppose agents optimize heavily for price and delivery speed. Merchants learn that durability barely affects machine ranking. Products become cheaper, faster and more disposable. The optimization succeeds against its stated objective while making the surrounding world worse.

Delegated adoption does not eliminate the need for judgment.

It makes the location of judgment more important.

A human who chooses a product sees the compromise, at least imperfectly. A system that makes a million small choices can bury the compromise inside an objective function, a policy file or a default nobody remembers approving.

That is why agentic commerce should not be treated as a story about replacing shoppers.

It is a story about making purchasing policy executable.

Once policy becomes executable, policy becomes infrastructure.

Infrastructure compounds.

The organizations that learn this first may gain an advantage that resembles the advantage earlier chapters found in factories, software platforms and new professional tools. They will not merely have agents. They will know how to assign authority, constrain risk, evaluate results, recover from failures and decide which kinds of choices should remain human.

That knowledge cannot be downloaded from the vendor.

It comes from use.

The first year of agentic procurement will include stupid purchases.

It should.

A system that never makes a bounded mistake is probably not exploring much. The relevant question is whether the mistake is recoverable and whether the organization converts it into policy. Did the agent buy a duplicate subscription? Add a rule. Did it select a cheaper supplier whose license forbids the intended use? Improve the contract parser and require human review for ambiguous licenses. Did a merchant manipulate machine-readable fields? Add independent verification. Did an automated budget expand through a chain of subtasks beyond what the principal expected? Define aggregate limits.

The bug report economy returns, now pointed at delegation.

Early users will teach the systems how purchasing authority fails in reality.

Some of those lessons will travel into standards. Some will remain proprietary organizational advantage. Some will become regulation after a public incident. Some will become insurance requirements. Some will become ordinary accounting practice so quickly that five years later nobody will remember a time when they were controversial.

That is how infrastructure becomes invisible.

A mature technology hides the arguments that created it.

The current agentic-commerce infrastructure is not mature. That is precisely why it belongs in a book about early adoption. We can see the arguments while they are still being made.

Who gets to authorize an agent?

How should an agent prove who it represents?

What merchant information should be standardized?

How should payments distinguish delegated intent from stolen credentials?

When must a human re-enter the loop?

What rights survive when software performs the transaction?

Which protocols become common enough that builders stop asking whether to support them?

The answers will not arrive from theory alone.

They will be produced through deployments.

A surprising possibility follows.

The next great population of early adopters may consist partly of agents whose principals did not think of themselves as early adopters at all.

A small business owner enables an accounting agent because she wants fewer administrative chores. The agent begins purchasing a new category of machine-readable service. A developer gives a coding agent a budget for testing infrastructure. It becomes one of the first customers of a specialized verification provider. A logistics system buys tiny bursts of route data from unfamiliar suppliers because the cost of testing is low.

No one wears a badge saying innovator.

No one attends a launch event.

Adoption happens as a side effect of delegation.

That could make technological diffusion less theatrical and more pervasive.

It could also make deliberate refusal harder.

A person can decide not to use a product she recognizes. An agent may encounter thousands of vendors and protocols outside her awareness. If the principal wants purchasing behavior to reflect values such as privacy, interoperability, labor standards or environmental impact, those values have to become machine-actionable somehow. Otherwise the agent will optimize what is easy to measure.

The old instruction “vote with your wallet” becomes literal code.

The wallet needs rules.

The rules need evidence.

The evidence needs standards.

The standards need adopters.

We arrive back at the same loop.

The future still needs customers.

It may simply get some of them in software form.

That does not make the human less important. It makes the human’s earlier decision more leveraged. A person may no longer choose every transaction, but she chooses the conditions under which transactions can occur. An institution may no longer approve every vendor manually, but it chooses the boundaries of autonomous exploration.

This is the deeper pattern of technological advantage.

As action becomes cheaper, advantage moves toward the design of permission.

The early adopter of agentic commerce is therefore not the person who lets a machine buy everything.

It is the person or institution that learns where delegated buying creates real learning, where it merely creates noise, and where the consequences are too large to automate casually.

The first generation of agentic customers will be clumsy.

Their mistakes will look obvious in retrospect.

So did many mistakes made by the first users of every important system in this book.

The useful distinction is not between people who were early and people who were right.

It is between people who turned being early into information and people who turned it into identity.

A machine can help with the first.

It has no need for the second.

That may be one of its advantages.
