# Users Build What Producers Cannot Yet See

The producer usually meets a need after the user has already been living with it.

That sounds obvious until you notice how much innovation theory quietly assumes the opposite.

A company studies a market, discovers an unmet need, designs a product, launches it and waits for adoption. The sequence is clean because the company is the active verb in every important sentence.

Users appear at the end.

Real innovation often begins with the order reversed.

Somebody encounters a problem before a market exists to solve it. The need is too unusual, too small, too new or too badly described for a producer to recognize. The user improvises.

A scientist modifies an instrument.

An athlete changes equipment.

A factory engineers a fixture that no supplier sells.

A programmer writes a script around a repetitive task.

A hospital builds a spreadsheet because the official software cannot represent the workflow.

A disabled user creates a workaround that later reveals an accessibility pattern useful to thousands of people.

The invention begins as use refusing to fit the available product.

Eric von Hippel spent much of his career documenting this phenomenon. His work on user innovation challenged the assumption that manufacturers possess a monopoly on product ideas. In many fields, users develop important innovations for themselves because they experience needs before producers see a market large enough to serve them.

He gave special attention to “lead users”: users who face needs that will later become more general and who expect substantial benefit from solving those needs now.

The concept is more interesting than the label.

A lead user is living in a local future.

Not the future in the science-fiction sense. The user has a present problem whose distribution is expected to widen.

That position creates unusual information.

A manufacturer can interview people about what they might want.

A lead user can show what she already had to build.

The difference is enormous.

The homemade solution contains revealed priorities. The user has spent time, money or reputation on it. Tradeoffs have already been made. Features that sounded important in conversation may have disappeared under real constraints. New requirements may have emerged that no survey question anticipated.

The prototype is a theory of the need expressed in matter or code.

This is why sophisticated producers look at what advanced users are doing at the edge.

They are searching for futures that have already begun in inconvenient form.

An organization can do the same thing internally.

Walk through a company and look for unofficial software.

The spreadsheet with macros nobody wants to touch.

The shared document that runs a process supposedly managed by an enterprise platform.

The small Python script one analyst wrote and forty people now depend on.

The form employees fill out twice because two systems cannot talk to each other.

The group chat where actual coordination happens while the approved workflow tool records a ceremonial version afterward.

Managers often see these artifacts as disorder.

Sometimes they are.

They can be insecure, brittle, undocumented and dependent on one employee who understands them. They create operational risk.

They are also evidence.

The organization has a need strong enough that people are solving it against policy.

That is adoption before procurement.

A future product category can hide inside an unauthorized workaround for years.

The producer who notices the workaround is not merely collecting feature requests. The producer is observing a customer that has already crossed the hardest threshold: the problem matters enough to motivate construction.

This changes the role of early adopters.

They do not merely choose among products.

They generate product concepts.

The idea sounds almost democratic. It can be, but user innovation has its own selection effects.

The people who build solutions are unusual.

They have skill, tools, urgency or autonomy. Their solutions may assume expertise that ordinary users do not possess. They may optimize for performance rather than ease. A laboratory’s custom apparatus can be brilliant and commercially impossible. A developer’s personal tool can require a command-line workflow that excludes most of the eventual market.

The producer still has work to do.

The user discovers a need and perhaps a solution.

The producer often turns that solution into a product that strangers can buy.

That transformation requires manufacturing, packaging, support, reliability, documentation, pricing, distribution and reduction of complexity. The user innovator can reveal where to go without building the road everyone else can travel.

This is the division of labor that the heroic inventor story misses.

The future can be co-produced by people who never share a payroll.

A user solves the first version because the need is intense.

A company recognizes the pattern.

The company productizes it.

Early commercial adopters expose the next set of problems.

Suppliers and complementors respond.

The market expands.

By the time the solution appears in a store, the origin may be impossible to attribute cleanly.

That is not a defect in the history.

It is the history.

Innovation is often a relay race in which the baton keeps changing shape.

This has a practical implication for anyone trying to adopt early.

Do not look only for new products.

Look for new workarounds.

A polished launch tells you what a company believes is ready to sell. A recurring workaround tells you what users believe is worth solving even without a product.

The workaround can therefore be an earlier signal.

In artificial intelligence, this became visible whenever people stitched together systems before vendors offered an integrated workflow. A developer connects a model to a code repository, search tool and shell because no single product provides the desired loop. A research team creates evaluation harnesses because model benchmarks do not reflect its actual tasks. A customer builds an approval layer because autonomous action is useful only within defined boundaries.

These improvised systems are often ugly.

That is what makes them informative.

People tolerate ugliness when the underlying capability is valuable.

An adopter who learns to distinguish valuable ugliness from mere bad design can see a market early.

The distinction depends on the direction of effort.

If users are spending effort to escape the product, the product may be failing.

If users are spending effort to extend the product into places the maker has not yet reached, a category may be growing.

The same technical behavior can mean opposite things.

A plugin exists because the base product is missing something. Is that a defect or an ecosystem?

A spreadsheet sits beside the official system. Is that evidence the system is unusable or evidence of a valuable workflow waiting to be productized?

A developer writes a wrapper around an API. Is that patching a bad interface or discovering an abstraction that thousands of other developers will want?

The answer arrives through adoption.

Do other users copy the workaround?

Do they maintain it?

Do they request it formally?

Do companies emerge to sell it?

Does the producer absorb it into the core product?

Replication turns an idiosyncrasy into a signal.

This is one reason communities matter so much in early technology markets.

A lone workaround is difficult to interpret. A forum full of people independently solving the same problem is harder to dismiss.

Community makes latent demand legible.

Open-source software communities can perform this function with extraordinary speed because the modification itself can circulate. One user’s patch becomes another user’s starting point. Forks reveal disagreement about direction. Extensions reveal missing capabilities. Repeated issues reveal where the abstraction leaks.

The community is simultaneously consuming, testing and designing.

Commercial ecosystems do similar work with more explicit boundaries. Developers build applications around platforms. Consultants invent implementation methods. Agencies specialize in new advertising formats. Hardware accessory companies fill gaps left by the primary manufacturer.

The producer may call these businesses partners.

Economically, they are externalized product development.

They are exploring the solution space beyond the boundaries of the firm.

This can make a platform extraordinarily adaptive.

A central company does not have to imagine every use. It provides primitives and distribution. Users and complementors search the adjacent possibilities. Successful experiments attract attention and investment. Weak ones disappear without consuming the platform owner’s full product-development budget.

The early adopter becomes part of a distributed search process.

That search can produce a future no original designer intended.

This is why technologies are often repurposed.

The maker has a theory of use.

The world has more imagination.

Text messaging grew from capabilities that were not originally marketed as the center of mobile communication. Spreadsheets became programming environments for people who did not call themselves programmers. Cameras attached to phones became scanners, mirrors, translators, measurement tools and identity devices. General-purpose computing repeatedly escapes the category in which it was sold because software lets users redefine the machine after purchase.

The more general the technology, the more important adoption becomes to discovering what the technology is for.

A hammer arrives with an obvious category of action.

A programmable system arrives with a space of possible actions.

The users search the space.

This makes early adoption particularly consequential during platform shifts.

When a new computing platform appears, nobody knows which applications will justify it. The first applications are often translations of old applications because those are easy to imagine. Then users and developers encounter affordances that did not exist before.

A touchscreen is not merely a keyboard without keys.

A phone with location is not merely a smaller computer.

A language model that can call tools is not merely a chatbot with buttons.

The new category becomes visible through experiments that exploit the new capability rather than imitate the old interface.

Early adopters participate in this discovery whether they intend to or not.

Their weird uses are market research.

This is where producers can make a serious mistake by over-managing the product.

A company may believe it knows the correct use and suppress deviations. It can restrict APIs, forbid modifications, limit data export or make integrations difficult because every external use looks like a support burden.

The result may be a beautifully controlled product with a narrow future.

Control reduces noise.

Noise is sometimes where the next market is hiding.

The opposite extreme is not automatically better. A product that exposes everything can become insecure and incoherent. Platform openness creates attack surfaces, privacy problems and compatibility obligations. The producer must decide which layers to stabilize and which to leave open for experimentation.

The strategic question is where users are allowed to invent.

That question also belongs to adopters.

A person choosing a technology early should care about optionality.

Can I export my work?

Can I connect other tools?

Can I automate repetitive parts?

Can I build on top of the system?

Can I preserve what I learn if this vendor disappears?

A closed product can deliver immediate capability while giving the adopter little durable learning. An open or interoperable product can let the adopter’s experimentation survive changes in vendors.

This is not a universal argument for open source or open standards. Closed systems can be excellent, secure and stable. Open systems can be chaotic and abandoned.

The point is to recognize that early adoption creates knowledge, and architecture determines who can reuse that knowledge.

If the skill you acquire exists only inside one vendor’s proprietary workflow, your advantage is tied to the vendor.

If the skill is conceptual or transferable across tools, the failed product may still leave you ahead.

This is the difference between adopting a future and renting a feature.

Lead users often understand this instinctively because their need is more durable than the product.

They begin with the problem.

The product is provisional.

That orientation is useful for anyone trying to participate early without becoming captured by novelty.

Start with the frontier you actually inhabit.

What problem reaches you before it reaches most people?

What constraint do you encounter because your work is unusually demanding, your volume unusually high, your environment unusually regulated, your customers unusually impatient or your ambitions unusually strange?

That is where early adoption can become more than consumption.

The best early adopter is often not the person who loves new technology most.

It is the person whose current problem makes tomorrow’s capability valuable today.

This is why “tech enthusiast” is a poor synonym for early adopter.

An enthusiast can adopt novelty because novelty is pleasurable.

A lead user adopts or invents because the need has arrived early.

The two groups can overlap. Their informational value is different.

A company selling an industrial tool should care less that ten thousand gadget lovers think the demonstration is exciting than that twenty factories have independently built crude versions of the same workflow.

The factories are not forecasting.

They are suffering.

Pain is often a stronger market signal than admiration.

The future hides inside that asymmetry.

A producer that learns to watch users at the edge can discover needs before they appear in market-size reports. An adopter who learns to inspect his own workarounds can notice when he is accidentally building expertise in an emerging category.

The question becomes less “What new thing should I try?”

It becomes “What am I already trying to do that the existing world makes unnecessarily hard?”

That question is older than technology marketing.

It is how users become inventors without asking permission.

And once enough of them solve the same future problem in the present, the producer finally has something unusually valuable to see.

Not a forecast.

A prototype made by demand itself.
