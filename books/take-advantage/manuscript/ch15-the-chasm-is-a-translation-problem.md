# The Chasm Is a Translation Problem

The first customer and the thousandth customer may be buying different products even when the box is identical.

The first customer buys possibility.

The thousandth buys dependability.

The first customer asks whether the new capability can be made to work.

The thousandth asks whether the capability can be made to disappear into work already happening.

This is the transition Geoffrey Moore famously described with the metaphor of crossing a chasm between early markets and mainstream adoption. The metaphor survived because companies repeatedly encounter a gap after winning enthusiasts. A product can be loved by technically adventurous customers and then stall before the larger market.

The usual explanation is that mainstream customers are more conservative.

That is partly true and not very useful.

The deeper problem is translation.

The product has been speaking the language of the frontier.

The mainstream speaks the language of continuity.

An early adopter sees missing features and imagines a roadmap.

A mainstream buyer sees missing features and imagines a support ticket.

An early adopter enjoys access to engineers.

A mainstream buyer wonders why access to engineers is necessary.

An early adopter sees a workaround as evidence of flexibility.

A mainstream buyer sees a workaround as unpaid labor.

The product can be exactly as capable as it was yesterday and suddenly feel inadequate because the audience changed.

This is why early success can make crossing harder.

The producer has learned from customers who reward the qualities of an immature product.

Speed of change.

Configurability.

Technical depth.

Direct access.

Novel features.

The next market may reward the opposite.

Stability.

Defaults.

Documentation.

Predictability.

Compatibility.

The company has to preserve what made the technology valuable while removing what made participation feel like participation.

That is translation.

It happens across more than usability.

The economic story must translate.

Early customers may justify a tool through strategic learning. They expect uncertain returns and accept the possibility that the experiment fails. Mainstream finance wants a budget category, a payback period and comparison with incumbent alternatives.

The risk story must translate.

Early users may manage risk through personal expertise. Mainstream organizations want controls that work for ordinary employees and evidence that mistakes can be contained.

The social story must translate.

Early users enjoy being visible. Mainstream users do not want to explain why they are using the tool.

The labor story must translate.

Early deployments depend on a few specialists. Mainstream deployment needs a hiring pool, training and support systems.

The ecosystem story must translate.

Early adopters build their own integrations. Mainstream buyers expect integrations to exist.

The product crosses when these translations become convincing enough at the same time.

This is why price reductions alone often fail to unlock adoption.

A company assumes the market is waiting because the product costs $1,000. It cuts the price to $700 and discovers that the real barrier was setup complexity, trust or lack of interoperability.

The company was solving the early market’s complaint after the market had changed.

The same mistake appears in enterprise AI.

A model becomes dramatically cheaper and more capable. Technologists conclude that broad adoption is inevitable because the unit economics have improved.

The institution encounters different constraints.

Can outputs be audited?

Who owns the data?

What happens when the model changes?

Which decisions require human review?

Can the organization reproduce an answer during an investigation?

What happens if the vendor disappears?

The mainstream barrier moved from capability to governance.

The tool did not become less revolutionary.

The revolution changed departments.

This is what adoption does to bottlenecks.

The first user solves the technical problem.

The next user creates the organizational problem.

The next cohort creates the standards problem.

The next creates the labor problem.

The producer can interpret each new barrier as resistance.

A better interpretation is maturation.

The market is telling the technology what it must become to fit a larger world.

This does not mean the mainstream is always right.

Mainstream requirements can destroy useful novelty.

A company can spend so much effort satisfying procurement that the product becomes slower, more expensive and less elegant. An open-ended tool can be flattened into a checklist. A flexible workflow can acquire approvals until the advantage disappears.

Translation can become surrender.

The producer needs to distinguish friction that protects the product’s unique value from friction that merely reflects immaturity.

That distinction is hard because founders become attached to both.

Direct access to engineers may feel like customer intimacy.

It may actually be a support system that cannot scale.

Frequent product changes may feel like speed.

They may actually prevent users from developing stable workflows.

Complex configuration may feel like power.

It may actually be evidence the product has not learned what defaults should be.

The mainstream forces the company to decide what was essential.

Early adopters can help with this transition if they understand their role is changing.

The first cohort often knows the product better than the company’s later employees. They have history. They remember why decisions were made. Their edge cases exposed the architecture. They can explain what the product does unusually well.

They can also become guardians of unnecessary complexity.

A community that takes pride in mastery may resist simplification because simplification reduces the social value of mastery.

This happens in software, hobbies, professions and organizations.

Difficulty creates status.

If anyone can do the thing, expertise loses some scarcity.

The early adopter therefore has a conflict of interest.

She may benefit personally from the technology remaining hard while benefiting economically from the technology becoming common.

Recognizing the conflict is part of mature participation.

The mainstream customer has a different conflict.

He wants proof before adopting.

Proof often requires adoption.

The company needs reference deployments to demonstrate reliability. The buyer wants reliability demonstrated before becoming a reference deployment. The later buyer wants standards. Standards bodies want enough implementations to know what should be standardized.

Again the circle appears.

Bridge customers are how the circle moves.

These are adopters who are more demanding than enthusiasts but more tolerant than the mainstream.

They need real operational value.

They also have enough expertise or urgency to accept some remaining immaturity.

A mid-sized company with strong technical staff can play this role for enterprise software. A regional utility can play it for energy technology. A specialized clinic can play it for a medical workflow. A professional creator can play it for a new media tool.

The bridge customer translates frontier capability into ordinary requirements.

This customer is strategically valuable because the feedback changes.

The enthusiast says, “Give me more power.”

The bridge customer says, “Make the existing power survivable.”

Reliability becomes a feature.

Migration becomes a feature.

Undo becomes a feature.

Permissioning becomes a feature.

Support becomes a feature.

The product begins accumulating the boring capabilities that make widespread adoption possible.

These features are easy to undervalue because they produce weak demonstrations.

No audience gasps when a data export works correctly.

No keynote pauses for applause because the administrator can revoke a permission cleanly.

No viral video shows a migration succeeding without downtime.

Yet these capabilities can matter more to the next million users than the feature that generated the viral video.

Mainstream adoption is built from boring victories.

This is where early adopters can gain another advantage if they pay attention.

The move from frontier to mainstream creates businesses around translation.

Consultants appear because organizations need implementation help.

Training companies appear because skills must spread beyond enthusiasts.

Integration firms appear because legacy systems must connect.

Compliance products appear because regulators and auditors need evidence.

Insurance products appear because risk must be priced.

The technology’s greatest commercial opportunities can migrate outward from the core capability into the work required to normalize it.

The people closest to early adoption see these jobs first.

They know which part remains hard after the demonstration.

That is the same residual-cost insight from the learning-curve chapter, now applied to institutions.

A technology becoming more capable does not eliminate work.

It can create a new class of work around making the capability dependable.

This is especially important during AI adoption.

As generation becomes abundant, verification becomes scarce.

As agents become more autonomous, authorization becomes valuable.

As models become easier to access, provenance and identity matter more.

As companies use more AI vendors, interoperability and evaluation become harder.

The bottleneck moves from “can a model do this?” to “can we trust a system to do this repeatedly inside our obligations?”

The market crosses when the answer becomes boring enough.

This is why the mainstream should not be mocked for arriving late.

The mainstream often arrives exactly when a different product has finally been built around the original technology.

The underlying engine may be similar.

Everything surrounding it has changed.

A smartphone in 2007 and a smartphone a decade later share a category, but the later buyer receives mature networks, application ecosystems, cloud services, repair, accessories, payment systems, social norms and accumulated interface conventions. The product is no longer a device. It is access to an environment.

The first adopters helped construct that environment.

The mainstream waited until the environment existed.

Both behaviors can be rational.

This gives early adopters a clearer measure of their own advantage.

Do not compare yourself with someone who waits and later buys a better product.

Of course they get a better product.

Ask what you accumulated while waiting was avoided.

Did you acquire transferable skill?

Did you build relationships with producers?

Did you influence standards?

Did you discover a business around the missing complements?

Did you learn which use cases create value?

Did you build internal adoption capability?

If not, you may simply have paid more for inconvenience.

If yes, the later customer’s better product does not erase your head start.

This is the real crossing strategy for individuals and institutions.

Use the frontier period to acquire assets that survive mainstreaming.

Do not build your identity around the rough edges that mainstreaming must remove.

Then help translate.

The person who can speak both languages becomes valuable during every technological transition.

She understands why the enthusiasts care and why the mainstream does not yet.

She can explain the new capability without demanding that ordinary users become enthusiasts.

She can tell the producer which complexity is essential and which is merely familiar.

She can tell the cautious institution which risks are real and which are artifacts of inexperience.

Translation is a form of leverage because new technology always produces a temporary gap between what is technically possible and what institutions know how to absorb.

That gap is the chasm.

It is not empty space.

It is full of missing work.

Support.

Standards.

Training.

Governance.

Integration.

Finance.

Trust.

One way to see the chasm more clearly is to look at who pays for those missing functions.

In the earliest market, the customer often does.

The customer configures the software, trains colleagues, writes integration code, builds a risk policy, explains the product to finance and searches community forums when something fails. The producer can report a compelling gross margin partly because the buyer is performing work that a mature category would assign to vendors, consultants, support organizations, standards or the product itself.

This creates an adoption subsidy hidden inside the customer.

The technology looks cheaper than it is because enthusiast labor is not priced.

Mainstream buyers withdraw that subsidy.

They do not want to become unpaid systems integrators merely to receive the advertised benefit. Their refusal can appear like conservatism, but economically it is a demand for the category to internalize more of its own operating cost.

The chasm therefore opens when volunteer labor stops scaling.

This is why total cost of ownership often rises in importance precisely as headline unit cost falls. The core product can become cheaper while the market begins counting training, integration, review, compliance, downtime and switching more carefully.

A producer that celebrates cheapness without seeing this accounting transition can be surprised by stalled adoption.

The later market is not rejecting the cheaper product.

It is finally pricing the whole system.

This perspective changes how we interpret “services ecosystems” around young technologies.

Consultants, implementation partners and managed-service providers are not evidence that the core product failed to become simple enough. Sometimes they are the bridge that makes complexity purchasable. They convert expert labor from an accidental burden on each customer into a specialized market.

That is a form of productization outside the product.

A technology can cross the chasm without every hard task disappearing if the hard tasks become standardized enough to buy.

This is how institutional thickness forms.

The first customer solves a problem privately.

The tenth customer hires somebody who has seen it before.

The hundredth customer chooses among firms that specialize in solving it.

The thousandth customer receives the solution as part of a contract and never learns the problem existed.

The burden migrated from user ingenuity into market infrastructure.

That migration is one of the clearest signs that a category is growing up.

Another is the quality of failure.

Frontier failures are mysterious.

Nobody knows whether the product, integration, user, vendor or whole category is at fault. Each incident becomes an investigation into first principles.

Mature failures are classified.

There is a known error code, standard remediation, warranty path, rollback procedure or specialist to call. The technology has accumulated enough history that surprise becomes taxonomy.

This matters to mainstream adoption because institutions are often more frightened by unclassifiable risk than by frequent but understood risk. A known failure can be budgeted, insured and assigned. An unknown failure threatens governance itself.

Translation therefore includes turning mystery into categories.

Early adopters contribute by naming what broke.

Bridge customers contribute by forcing the category to make those names operational.

Mainstream customers arrive when the names are stable enough to become procedures.

The same process occurs socially. The frontier argues about whether a new behavior is acceptable at all. The bridge period develops context-specific norms. The mainstream receives etiquette that no longer feels like policy.

The future crosses when enough uncertainty has been compressed into defaults.

That does not mean all uncertainty is gone.

It means ordinary participation no longer requires personal theories about every unresolved question.

This gives us a practical definition of mainstream readiness.

A technology is becoming mainstream-ready when a competent ordinary user can receive most of the value without becoming a member of the technology’s subculture.

The user can remain a nurse, accountant, teacher, driver, designer or warehouse manager rather than acquiring a second identity as an enthusiast.

The tool fits the profession instead of demanding that the profession fit the tool.

This is why the best translation often looks like subtraction.

Fewer concepts to understand.

Fewer decisions to make.

Fewer exceptions to manage.

Fewer reasons to contact the vendor.

Fewer moments when a person has to remember that the technology is new.

The capability survives the subtraction.

The ceremony does not.

For an early adopter, watching this process can be emotionally strange. The tool that once required expertise becomes accessible to people who know less. The adopter’s hard-won shortcuts become default behavior. The social prestige of mastery declines.

This is exactly what successful diffusion should do.

The question is whether the adopter converted the temporary scarcity into something durable before the scarcity disappeared.

Knowledge of the user problem can remain valuable.

Relationships can remain valuable.

Judgment about where the system fails can remain valuable.

A collection of obscure menu tricks will not.

The chasm is therefore a warning about what kind of expertise to accumulate.

Learn the transition, not merely the interface.

Learn why the mainstream is waiting.

Learn which missing complement will become a business.

Learn which risk is going to be standardized and which must remain case specific.

Learn what the product can remove from the user without removing the reason anyone cared.

That is translation work.

The future crosses when somebody builds it.

Early adopters can be those somebodies.
