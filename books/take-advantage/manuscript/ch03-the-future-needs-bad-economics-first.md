# The Future Needs Bad Economics First

In August 2006, Elon Musk published a short description of Tesla’s strategy.

The logic was not mysterious.

Build an expensive sports car. Use the proceeds and what the company learned to build a less expensive car. Then use that stage to move toward a still more affordable car.

The remarkable part was how openly the plan acknowledged a fact that technology companies often hide behind the word innovation: the first version could not be cheap.

Tesla did not begin with a mass-market family car because the company did not yet possess the manufacturing scale, supply chain, battery economics, service network, production experience or capital base to build that car competitively.

So the first customers were asked to buy under bad economics.

The Roadster was expensive, limited and strange by the standards of the automobile market. That was not an incidental flaw waiting to be removed by a better marketing campaign. It was part of the path.

The early buyer occupied the narrow region where the product’s value could exceed its immature cost.

This region exists in many technological transitions.

A new technology can be superior on one important dimension and still be commercially inferior overall. It may deliver a capability nobody else can provide while costing too much, failing too often, requiring specialized knowledge or lacking the infrastructure that makes the old technology easy to use.

The first market forms around people for whom the unusual capability matters enough to forgive the rest.

They are not irrational simply because later customers get a better deal.

The relevant comparison is not between the first product and the future product. The future product is unavailable.

The relevant comparison is between the first product and the alternatives that exist now.

A wealthy driver in 2008 could rationally value instant electric torque, novelty, environmental symbolism and participation in a technological experiment enough to pay for a Roadster. A satellite operator in an earlier era could rationally pay an extraordinary price for solar cells because carrying fuel into space was not a competing option. A data center can rationally buy an expensive accelerator years before the same compute becomes cheap enough for a teenager’s laptop because the data center can monetize the capability immediately.

A high-value niche is where bad economics can survive long enough to become good economics.

That sentence describes one of the most important paths by which early adopters manifest the future.

They pay for the expensive years.

The payment does not automatically make the technology cheaper. That is the mistake in the simple version of the story. Some products remain expensive no matter how many enthusiasts purchase them. A hand-built object may resist automation. A technology may depend on scarce inputs. A complex system may accumulate costs as it scales. A service may require expert labor that does not become dramatically more productive with experience.

The mechanism matters.

For certain technologies, however, cumulative production changes the economics.

Workers improve processes. Engineers redesign components. Suppliers specialize. Scrap declines. factories get larger. Equipment becomes faster. Quality control becomes more precise. Logistics become routine. Designs use less material. The product is decomposed into parts that can be manufactured independently. Knowledge migrates from a few experts into procedures, machines and supplier relationships.

Economists have described versions of this process as learning by doing.

Kenneth Arrow’s 1962 paper on the economic implications of learning by doing gave formal attention to a fact manufacturers had known in practical form for a long time: production itself can create knowledge.

The factory learns because it produces.

That makes demand more than a reason to produce.

Demand becomes one of the causes of improvement.

This is where the first customers enter the cost curve.

If a technology gets cheaper partly because cumulative production rises, then somebody must purchase the expensive early units that move cumulative production upward.

The later buyer receives a product whose price reflects not only better engineering in the abstract but experience purchased by earlier demand.

Solar photovoltaics provide one of the clearest examples.

Solar cells began as expensive devices suitable for unusual applications. Space was one of them. A satellite has a peculiar willingness to pay for a compact source of electricity because conventional terrestrial fuel logistics are not available once the object is in orbit. Early demand could therefore support photovoltaic technology at costs that made no sense for ordinary electricity production on Earth.

Then a long process began.

Markets widened. Production expanded. Governments funded research and subsidized deployment. Manufacturers improved processes. Supply chains deepened. Firms entered and failed. Module designs changed. Silicon purification, wafer production, cell efficiency, factory automation, installation practice and financing all evolved.

Over decades, the cost of solar power fell dramatically.

Our World in Data’s synthesis of the historical learning curve notes a rough empirical relationship: solar module prices declined by about one fifth for each doubling of cumulative installed capacity over a long period.

The number is not a law of nature.

It is a historical pattern.

That distinction is crucial because people love turning learning curves into prophecies. Once a line looks straight on logarithmic axes, it becomes easy to speak as though another doubling mechanically causes a fixed cost decline.

Reality is messier.

Research matters. Policy matters. Input prices matter. trade matters. Factory scale matters. Interest rates matter. Supply gluts matter. Firms learn unevenly. Technological changes can interrupt the relationship. Demand and innovation affect each other simultaneously, which makes clean causal attribution difficult.

Still, the broad feedback loop is real enough to shape industries.

More deployment creates more opportunities to learn.

Learning can lower cost.

Lower cost expands the set of viable applications.

Expanded applications create more deployment.

The early customer is one turn of that flywheel.

Notice how different this is from the romantic story of invention.

A scientist does not discover a $25 solar panel in a laboratory and then wait for society to notice. The cheap panel emerges from a system that has manufactured, installed, financed and operated earlier expensive panels.

The product is partly an accumulation of production history.

This is why technological abundance often has an awkward ancestry.

The cheap thing had expensive grandparents.

The reliable thing had unreliable grandparents.

The convenient thing had grandparents that required expert installation.

The mainstream product is easy to admire because the ugly transitional forms have been forgotten.

Consumers forget them for good reason. Nobody buying electricity cares how much a solar module cost thirty years earlier. Nobody choosing a laptop wants to honor the people who purchased workstation-class computing at extraordinary prices. The mature market should be allowed to become ordinary.

But strategy requires remembering the path.

If you want to understand which technologies can plausibly become cheap, you have to ask what gets better with repetition.

Manufacturing is one obvious answer.

Software provides another kind of learning, though the economics differ. The millionth copy of a software product does not require the same manufacturing effort as the millionth physical unit, but more usage can reveal defects, edge cases, performance bottlenecks and desired features. Distribution can become nearly free while quality improves through iteration.

AI systems introduce yet another variation. More usage can provide evaluation data, failure examples and commercial evidence that justifies greater investment, while the underlying cost structure may also change through better hardware, model optimization and serving infrastructure. But more usage can increase costs too, especially when each interaction consumes significant compute.

The feedback loop must be traced, not assumed.

“Scale” is not an explanation.

What exactly does scale teach?

What exactly becomes cheaper?

Which cost moves with volume and which does not?

Who captures the savings?

Who pays for the learning period?

That last question is political as well as commercial.

Some learning curves have been paid down by wealthy early consumers. Some by governments. Some by militaries. Some by regulated utilities. Some by employers buying expensive equipment for specialized work. Some by venture capital subsidizing prices below cost. Some by countries willing to support domestic industries for strategic reasons.

The future rarely has one patron.

Solar power did not become cheap because one heroic class of gadget enthusiasts purchased rooftop systems. Public research, feed-in tariffs, manufacturing policy, tax credits, global trade, corporate investment, low-cost finance and large utility deployments all mattered. The early adopter is a participant in that system, not a substitute for it.

This matters because “early adopters manifest the future” can become a way of laundering institutional history into a consumer morality tale.

Buy the right thing and civilization improves.

That is too easy.

Individuals influence markets, but some technologies require coordination at scales individuals cannot supply. A grid needs transmission. A semiconductor industry needs fabrication plants. A vaccine platform needs trials, regulation and manufacturing. A new aircraft needs certification. The customer can create demand; demand does not abolish infrastructure.

The more accurate claim is that early demand can make complementary investment rational.

That is enormous enough.

Consider a supplier deciding whether to build a component specifically for a new class of product.

The supplier does not need the final mass market to exist today. It needs sufficient evidence that investing now could pay. Early orders can provide that evidence. Once the component exists, the final product may improve. The improved product recruits more buyers. The supplier increases capacity. New competitors enter. Costs fall.

The early customer has indirectly changed the supplier landscape.

This is one reason premium markets often sit at the beginning of mass-market technologies.

Premium pricing creates room.

Room for inefficient production.

Room for expensive components.

Room for hand assembly.

Room for high support costs.

Room for mistakes.

A product that must achieve mature mass-market economics on its first production run may never get built.

The premium customer purchases tolerance.

Tesla’s early strategy used this logic explicitly. The Roadster was not a failed attempt to begin with a cheap car. It was a product for a segment that could sustain the economics available at the time.

By March 2008, when Tesla announced regular production of the Roadster, the company said more than 900 vehicles had been reserved. Those reservations mattered because Tesla was not General Motors launching another model through an established manufacturing system. It was a young company attempting to prove that an electric performance car could be produced and sold at all.

By the time Tesla filed for its public offering in 2010, the company reported thousands of reservations for the planned Model S and millions of dollars in refundable reservation payments.

A reservation did not finance an automobile factory by itself.

It did something strategically useful: it turned a future customer into present evidence and some present cash.

The customer’s willingness to wait became an asset the producer could point to.

This is common in capital-intensive transitions because the order of operations is hostile.

You need a factory to produce the product cheaply.

You need confidence in demand to finance the factory.

Customers want the cheap product before providing confidence.

Early adopters break the deadlock by accepting the expensive product or committing before delivery.

Sometimes governments perform the same function with procurement guarantees. Sometimes utilities sign long-term contracts. Sometimes a company signs take-or-pay agreements with a supplier. The structure is more formal, but the underlying act is similar: someone makes future demand credible enough to support present investment.

This mechanism also explains why hype can be economically useful without being epistemically reliable.

A compelling narrative can recruit the first customers.

Those customers can finance real learning.

Real learning can make the product better enough to justify the narrative after the fact.

The initial story may therefore participate in making itself true.

That is dangerous territory.

It is how genuine technology transitions and bubbles can resemble each other in their early stages.

Both contain stories about future scale.

Both can attract capital.

Both can show rapidly rising demand.

Both can temporarily lower costs through volume.

The difference only becomes clear when the system confronts broader users and harder economics.

Does the product continue improving after the enthusiasts arrive?

Do customers remain when subsidies fall?

Do costs decline because of durable learning or because investors are absorbing losses?

Do complements emerge organically or only through producer spending?

Does the technology solve a problem outside the community that wants the technology to win?

An early adopter cannot know all of this.

That is why early adoption is an experiment.

The mistake is to confuse willingness to pay with proof of inevitability.

Willingness to pay proves willingness to pay.

Then the future gets another chance to earn the next customer.

The best early markets create a sequence of improving bargains.

The first customer pays a lot for a rare capability.

That purchase supports learning.

The next customer gets more capability or lower cost.

The enlarged market supports more specialized suppliers and infrastructure.

The next customer gets an easier product.

Eventually the purchase no longer feels like participation in a technological movement. It feels like shopping.

That is success.

The movement disappears into the shelf.

There is a social irony in this sequence. Early adopters often enjoy being visibly early. They display the object precisely because it is not yet common. Their status can help finance the process that destroys the status value.

If the technology succeeds, everyone gets one.

The early adopter has paid a premium to make her own distinctiveness obsolete.

The economic contribution remains even after the social reward vanishes.

That contribution should change how we think about expensive beginnings.

An ugly first price is not always evidence that a technology can never become ordinary.

Sometimes it is the admission ticket to the learning process that makes ordinariness possible.

The question is not whether version one has bad economics.

The question is whether using, producing and supporting version one changes the economics of version two.

When the answer is yes, an early customer is not simply overpaying.

The customer is purchasing the experience from which the market may learn to charge less.
