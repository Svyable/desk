# When Early Adopters Are Wrong

Google Glass worked well enough to become a social problem before it worked well enough to become a mass-market product.

That sequence is more important than the jokes.

In 2013, Google began placing Glass in the hands of “Explorers,” people invited to buy and use an early version of the wearable computer. The company described the program as a way to learn from first-hand use. The device put a small display near the wearer’s eye and included a camera, microphone and wireless connection.

From the wearer’s perspective, the product offered a new interface to information.

From everyone else’s perspective, it placed a sensor on somebody’s face.

The difference became the product.

Restaurants, bars, workplaces and ordinary conversations encountered questions the demonstration stage had not settled. Was the camera recording? Could it be? Would people know? Was asking the wearer to remove the device rude, prudent or necessary? Did the wearer have the right to bring the technology into every room simply because the device was legal to own?

The Explorer program produced learning.

Some of the learning was that the future Google had placed in public was not socially ready in that form.

Google eventually ended consumer sales of the Explorer edition. The technology did not disappear entirely. Versions of Glass found a second life in enterprise settings where hands-free information had a clearer operational role and where the surrounding environment could establish explicit rules.

The failed consumer vision discovered another market.

That is what wrong early adoption can look like at its most productive.

The adopters were wrong about where the future belonged.

Their failure helped reveal where it might belong instead.

This chapter exists because every argument about early adoption becomes dangerous without it.

If buying, using and supporting a technology can help manifest a future, then early adopters can also create momentum around weak products, bad norms, poor standards and overestimated markets.

They can be causally powerful and epistemically wrong at the same time.

There is no contradiction.

A person can help make something more viable without making it good.

A group can increase a technology’s probability of survival while decreasing society’s eventual welfare.

A community can produce a temporary market large enough to fool producers into scaling before mainstream demand exists.

The feedback loop amplifies whatever enters it.

That is why early adoption should not be treated as a moral category.

Innovators are not the brave people on the correct side of history while later adopters are timid. Sometimes the cautious person is waiting for evidence that the early adopter is busy generating at personal expense.

The cautious person may be rationally outsourcing experimentation.

This makes late adoption an economic strategy, not a character flaw.

A late adopter can receive lower prices, better reliability, mature standards, a larger complement ecosystem and a rich archive of other people’s mistakes.

Waiting has option value too.

The question is which option is more valuable in a particular domain: learning early or avoiding early risk.

The answer changes with stakes.

Trying an unfinished note-taking application is cheap.

Trying an unfinished medical therapy is not.

Buying the first generation of a home robot is recoverable.

Standardizing a city’s infrastructure around an immature proprietary system can create decades of lock-in.

The same personality should not govern every decision.

Early-adopter mythology obscures this because it rewards consistency.

People like to say “I’m an early adopter” as though timing were an identity. The statement feels similar to “I’m adventurous.” It compresses many decisions into a flattering trait.

A better adopter changes timing with reversibility.

Early where failure teaches cheaply.

Late where failure compounds catastrophically.

The distinction becomes especially important when enthusiasm itself changes the evidence.

Suppose a small group of unusually tolerant users adopts a difficult product.

They create tutorials, accessories and workarounds. They make the product more usable. Their visible enthusiasm attracts press. Investors interpret attention as demand. The producer raises capital and expands. New users arrive because the system now appears credible.

The feedback loop is real.

But the original preference may still be narrow.

The early users have improved the product enough to enlarge a niche without proving a mass market.

This is where early adopters can manifest a local maximum.

The product becomes excellent for people who enjoy the product’s unusual demands.

The company mistakes the niche’s intensity for universal appetite.

Then the mainstream refuses.

The failure can feel sudden because every earlier signal was genuine.

Customers paid.

Users engaged.

Communities formed.

Reviews were enthusiastic.

The category mattered deeply to the people inside it.

What failed was extrapolation.

This is one reason market size estimates are so fragile in emerging technology.

The early segment is selected by willingness to tolerate immaturity. Its behavior is not a random sample of the population.

The producer cannot multiply enthusiasm by population and call the result a forecast.

The same problem affects pricing.

If the first customers are wealthy, urgent or technically sophisticated, they may be willing to pay prices that say little about mainstream demand. Lowering the price later may expand the market, but only if cost was the binding constraint.

Sometimes the constraint is inconvenience.

Sometimes trust.

Sometimes aesthetics.

Sometimes social permission.

Sometimes the product solves a problem most people do not have.

Google Glass revealed several of these at once.

The price was high, but the problem was not merely price. The product made sensing visible in a way that imposed uncertainty on other people. The wearer’s benefit could not be separated from the bystander’s experience.

A cheaper version would not automatically solve that.

This is why failure analysis has to identify the constraint that actually stopped diffusion.

Early adopters are valuable because they expose constraints.

They are dangerous when producers assume every constraint can be engineered away.

Some are social boundaries.

Some are legal.

Some are economic.

Some are preferences.

A feature can be fixed.

A public unwillingness to be recorded may require a different product form.

This is where wrong futures become useful.

A failed early market can discover a negative requirement.

Do not put the sensor there.

Do not require the user to behave that way.

Do not make the private benefit depend on a public cost.

Do not assume the expert workflow can become the mainstream workflow.

Do not interpret admiration as willingness to integrate.

The failed future narrows the search space.

That is valuable if the producer survives and learns.

It is also valuable to competitors.

They can observe what failed without paying the same tuition.

This creates another unfairness in innovation.

The pioneer can spend millions discovering a social boundary.

The second entrant designs around the boundary and receives credit for better judgment.

Markets reward the solution, not the cost of discovering what not to do.

The same can happen to individual early adopters.

You can spend years learning a platform that disappears.

The direct asset vanishes.

What remains depends on what you learned.

If you learned only the platform’s interface, the investment is mostly stranded.

If you learned the underlying problem, architecture, customer need or failure modes, the dead platform can still have taught you the future.

This is why the best early adopters are promiscuous about products and loyal to problems.

They do not need the first vendor to win in order for their learning to retain value.

They are willing to say, “This capability matters, and this implementation is wrong.”

That sentence is harder than it sounds.

Humans protect sunk cost.

The more effort we spend mastering something, the more reasons we can find to keep using it. Expertise itself becomes evidence in our own minds. The tool must matter, we think, because otherwise why did we become so good at it?

Communities intensify the effect.

Everyone around us shares the vocabulary. We see success stories every day. Critics look uninformed because they do not understand the details. The community becomes better at explaining why the technology will win than at noticing why outsiders still do not care.

The early-adopter bubble is then cultural before it is financial.

The cure is contact with non-adopters.

Not contempt for them.

Contact.

Ask why they are waiting.

The answer may be ignorance.

It may be conservatism.

It may also reveal the requirement the early community has normalized away.

“I don’t want to configure that.”

“I cannot move our data there.”

“My employees will not wear that.”

“Our insurer will not cover it.”

“Our customers do not want a camera in the room.”

“The savings are smaller than the disruption.”

These objections are market research.

An early adopter who dismisses them as resistance to change throws away the most valuable information the mainstream can provide.

The mainstream is not merely the destination of diffusion.

It is another sensor.

Late adopters tell the market what the product must stop asking from users.

This is a different kind of manifestation.

The early user says what can be tolerated.

The later user says what must disappear.

Mature products are often defined by the disappearance of burdens their enthusiasts learned to love.

Automatic configuration replaces setup rituals.

Cloud services replace local maintenance.

Contactless payments replace mechanical steps.

Consumer cameras hide photographic expertise inside computation.

Mainstream adoption strips away the ceremony.

The enthusiast can experience this as loss.

The market experiences it as usability.

This is why early communities sometimes become hostile to the very changes required for growth.

They fear simplification will remove power.

They fear new users will dilute culture.

They fear commercial priorities will replace craft.

Sometimes they are right. The product can become worse for advanced users.

Sometimes the trade is necessary. A capability becomes socially important only after it stops requiring devotion.

The producer has to decide which future it wants.

Niche excellence and ubiquity are both legitimate.

Confusing them is expensive.

The early adopter faces the same choice personally.

Do you want to live at the frontier because the frontier itself is rewarding?

Or are you investing early because you expect the capability to become ordinary?

If the answer is the first, mass adoption may not matter.

If the answer is the second, you need evidence that the technology can translate beyond people like you.

Look for signs of translation.

Support becomes simpler.

Prices fall without service collapsing.

New users succeed without expert coaching.

Standards stabilize.

Non-enthusiast organizations renew contracts.

The product creates value in settings where novelty carries no status.

The complement ecosystem serves ordinary needs.

People who dislike the technology category still choose the product because it solves a problem.

Those are stronger signals than a passionate launch community.

There is another failure mode that looks almost like success: the future can arrive and still strand the early adopter.

A person can be correct about the capability, correct about the timing, and wrong about where value will settle.

The web became enormous, but many early web businesses disappeared. Smartphones became central, while early handset leaders lost position. Cloud computing grew while individual infrastructure products were absorbed, copied or commoditized. Artificial intelligence can become ubiquitous without rewarding every company, worker or investor who adopted one early implementation.

Category truth and capture are different questions.

This matters because early-adopter stories often collapse them.

“I knew this was the future” is presented as evidence that the bet was good. But a future can be obvious at the capability level and destructive at the asset level. Competition can drive prices toward zero. Standards can make vendors interchangeable. Open-source alternatives can diffuse a capability broadly while eroding one company’s moat. The technology wins and the investment loses.

An adopter therefore has to ask what kind of advantage early participation can actually preserve.

Is it access?

Skill?

Customer relationships?

A proprietary dataset?

A physical location?

A regulatory license?

A reputation for competent implementation?

Or merely familiarity with a product everybody will soon receive for free?

The answer determines whether being right early produces any private return.

This is one reason transferable learning matters so much in the portfolio later in the book. You want an experiment to leave an asset after the prediction has been resolved. Otherwise you have converted uncertainty into trivia.

The same principle applies to firms.

A company can adopt a frontier technology, spend heavily integrating it, teach the vendor what the market needs and still find that competitors receive the improved version at lower cost a year later. The company has subsidized the market unless it transformed the early learning into something harder to copy.

That something might be a redesigned process, a data advantage, a new customer experience, organizational speed or an accumulated body of judgment. It cannot simply be “we had the tool first.”

Tools diffuse.

The advantage must migrate before the tool does.

There is also a deeper warning in wrong early adoption: positive feedback can delay correction.

A technology attracts users. Users attract capital. Capital funds marketing and subsidies. Subsidies attract more users. The larger user count attracts complements. The complements create apparent validation. Every layer makes the system look more inevitable even if the underlying economics remain dependent on continuous subsidy.

At that point the feedback loop is not lying.

It is just amplifying a condition that may not survive when support is removed.

This is why the book keeps asking what happens when the next subsidy, novelty premium or special accommodation disappears.

Can the system carry its own weight?

Can users still justify it when the price is honest?

Can complements survive without grants from the platform?

Can the product retain people after the social status of being early vanishes?

Can the organization operate it without heroic staff?

These are de-subsidization tests.

A future that survives them has moved from advocacy toward institution.

A future that fails them may still contain a valuable capability. It simply has not earned the architecture built around it.

This is how to stay early without becoming gullible.

Treat enthusiasm as fuel, not proof.

Treat adoption as causal, not clairvoyant.

Treat failure as evidence about the shape of the future rather than evidence that the future itself has been canceled.

Google Glass did not prove that wearable computing was foolish. Watches, earbuds, industrial headsets and other forms continued to grow. What failed was a particular combination of form, social signaling, utility, price and expectation in a particular consumer moment.

The future moved sideways.

That happens constantly.

A technology arrives first in the wrong object.

A business model arrives before the enabling infrastructure.

A product solves the right problem for the wrong customer.

A standard wins an early battle and loses the war after a new layer makes the old compatibility question irrelevant.

The early adopter’s advantage is not the ability to avoid these mistakes.

It is the ability to learn from them before everyone else is forced to.

That requires one habit above all.

Do not defend the bet.

Inspect what the bet taught.

If the future you helped manifest turns out to be wrong, your next advantage is becoming willing to leave it before the crowd that copied you arrives.
