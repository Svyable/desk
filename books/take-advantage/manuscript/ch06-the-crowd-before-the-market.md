# The Crowd Before the Market

A market is easiest to recognize after it exists.

There are prices, competitors, suppliers, comparison sites, analysts, job titles and enough history for people to argue about market share. A customer can enter without feeling responsible for whether the category survives.

Before that, there is often a crowd.

The crowd is less orderly.

It may gather around a mailing list, a developer conference, a preorder page, a hobbyist forum, a research community, a Kickstarter campaign or a product that is technically on sale but still behaves like a public experiment. Its members do not necessarily agree on what the technology is for. They may not even agree on what to call it.

What they share is earlier commitment.

That commitment can create a market before the institutions we normally associate with markets have arrived.

Crowdfunding made this visible because it compressed several steps of market formation into one page.

A project described a possibility. People pledged money. The total rose in public. Comments accumulated. Stretch goals appeared. Journalists wrote about the campaign because the number itself became news. Suppliers, competitors and investors could watch demand form in real time.

The crowd was not merely purchasing a product.

It was publishing evidence of itself.

That second function is powerful.

Traditional purchasing is mostly private. A person buys a refrigerator and the neighbors do not receive a live dashboard showing that another household has joined the refrigerator market. Retailers and manufacturers eventually aggregate sales data, but the purchase does not usually recruit the next purchaser through its visibility alone.

Crowdfunding turned commitment into spectacle.

The spectacle can coordinate belief.

A person who would hesitate to back an obscure project with seventeen supporters may feel differently after fifty thousand people have pledged. The additional backers do not necessarily make manufacturing easier. They make social uncertainty smaller.

Someone else believes this enough to pay.

That statement can change behavior even when the observer has no private information about the product.

The result resembles a bank run in reverse.

Instead of people rushing to withdraw because others are withdrawing, people can rush to participate because others are participating. Success produces evidence of success. The evidence recruits more success.

This is one reason early-adopter markets can move discontinuously.

The underlying technology may improve gradually while social confidence jumps.

A project that looked eccentric at breakfast can look inevitable by dinner because a visible threshold was crossed. The crowd gives permission to people who were interested but unwilling to be alone.

That permission is part of adoption.

Social proof is often discussed as a psychological bias, which it can be. People infer quality from popularity even when popularity contains little independent information. But in networked or ecosystem technologies, the inference can be partly rational.

Other adopters may genuinely improve the proposition.

If I am considering a new communication tool, the arrival of people I need to communicate with makes the tool more useful. If I am considering a hardware platform, a large installed base can attract developers and accessory makers. If I am considering a new professional software system, a growing user community can mean better documentation, more trained employees and more consultants.

Popularity can create some of the quality it appears to measure.

That is a dangerous sentence because it explains both virtuous cycles and bubbles.

The crowd can manifest infrastructure.

It can also manifest confidence without infrastructure.

A product can attract attention faster than a factory can learn to manufacture it. A token can attract buyers without creating productive use. A social platform can accumulate users before discovering a durable business model. A startup can book customers faster than it can support them.

Visible demand may pull a future into existence or merely pull expectations away from reality.

The difference is what the commitment causes next.

Does money become capacity?

Does usage become learning?

Does the installed base attract complements?

Do repeated purchases follow the launch cohort?

Do costs improve?

Does reliability improve?

Do users continue to care after the novelty signal fades?

The crowd is evidence of demand.

It is not evidence that every bottleneck downstream has been solved.

This distinction became painfully clear in the history of crowdfunding. Campaigns sometimes raised far more money than expected and then discovered that success had increased the difficulty of delivery. A production plan designed for a few thousand units is not automatically better when asked to produce fifty thousand. Suppliers change. Quality control changes. packaging changes. shipping changes. Support changes. Cash-flow timing changes.

The crowd can create the scale problem before the producer has earned the scale capability.

That does not make crowdfunding a failure. It makes it a more honest representation of markets.

Demand and production have always had to find each other.

Crowdfunding simply lets us watch the collision.

This is useful for understanding early adoption because the first crowd performs functions that a mature market later distributes across specialized institutions.

The crowd provides customer discovery.

It provides some financing.

It provides word of mouth.

It provides early technical feedback.

It provides reputation through visible association.

It may provide volunteer support to other users.

It may create the first third-party products.

As the category matures, venture investors, banks, retailers, distributors, analysts, training companies and professional service firms take over parts of this work. The spontaneous crowd becomes an ecosystem.

The transition is one of the places where futures are made or lost.

Enthusiast energy does not automatically professionalize.

A forum can answer setup questions for a few thousand hobbyists. A hospital cannot depend on a forum answer during a critical incident. A community-built integration can be excellent for developers and unacceptable to a regulated bank. A founder can personally reassure the first hundred customers. The ten-thousandth customer expects a service-level agreement.

The future has to graduate from belonging to a crowd to belonging to institutions.

That graduation requires boring things.

Contracts.

Documentation.

Insurance.

Support hours.

Version policies.

Compliance certifications.

Replacement inventories.

Training.

Accounting treatment.

Procurement categories.

These are not the opposite of innovation.

They are what allow innovation to stop requiring believers.

The crowd’s role is to carry the technology through the period before those institutions exist.

This is why the first community around a technology can look almost religious from the outside. Members develop shared language. They explain the technology to skeptics. They forgive defects. They celebrate milestones that ordinary customers would consider irrelevant. They know founders by name. They interpret setbacks as part of a larger mission.

The emotional intensity can be useful.

It can sustain work that would otherwise fail the first comparison with a mature incumbent.

It can also corrupt judgment.

A community invested in a future has incentives to reinterpret evidence in favor of that future. Delays become proof of ambition. Missing features become signs of a disciplined roadmap. Criticism becomes ignorance. The product stops being evaluated as a tool and starts being defended as an identity.

At that point the early adopter loses part of the advantage that made early adoption valuable.

Learning requires the possibility of changing your mind.

A crowd can accelerate learning only if bad news is allowed to remain bad news.

This is harder than it sounds because early technologies create sunk costs of more than money.

People build status around being early. They make public predictions. They advise colleagues. They spend months learning systems. They create businesses on top of platforms. Their social identity becomes attached to the proposition that the technology matters.

Leaving now means admitting that some of the investment may not return.

The crowd can become a prison made from yesterday’s enthusiasm.

This is why healthy early communities are strangely skeptical places.

They want the technology to work and complain in detail when it does not.

They distinguish the capability from the company.

They allow forks, alternatives and competing implementations.

They preserve memory of failures instead of editing history after every pivot.

They treat adoption as participation in a search, not enrollment in a prophecy.

The producer benefits from this skepticism even when it is uncomfortable.

A community that applauds everything is weak instrumentation.

A community that can say “this is not good enough yet” gives the producer information about the frontier between enthusiasm and broader adoption.

That frontier is where markets form.

The first crowd also creates language.

This is easy to miss.

New technologies are difficult to buy because they are difficult to describe. Is a smartphone a phone, computer, camera or internet terminal? Is cloud computing rented servers, utility computing or something else? Is a large language model a search engine, writing tool, interface, colleague, runtime or model?

Categories become clearer through use.

Early adopters invent metaphors, labels and comparisons. Reviewers establish criteria. Communities decide which differences matter. Producers learn which words recruit the right expectations and which create disappointment.

Language is infrastructure because procurement depends on categories.

A budget needs a line item.

A job description needs a noun.

A regulator needs a classification.

An insurer needs a risk category.

A customer needs to know what old thing this new thing can reasonably be compared with.

The first crowd helps produce that vocabulary.

Sometimes the vocabulary outlives the products that created it.

This is another way adopters can be right about a category and wrong about a company.

The first widely discussed version teaches society how to talk about a capability. Competitors then enter with better execution. The pioneer’s commercial failure becomes part of the market’s education.

That can feel unfair to the early company.

Markets do not pay royalties for teaching a category what to want.

The early adopters participate in that teaching too.

Their public demonstrations, reviews, complaints and use cases show later customers what questions to ask.

A mature buyer rarely begins from zero.

She inherits a comparison framework built by people who already made expensive mistakes.

This is why the phrase “market education” should be taken literally.

Somebody pays tuition.

The first crowd pays in money, time and uncertainty.

The later market receives a syllabus.

There is strategic value in recognizing when a crowd is becoming a market.

The transition often appears in the kinds of questions people ask.

Early enthusiasts ask what is possible.

A broader market asks whether it is dependable.

Enthusiasts ask how to hack it.

Enterprises ask who supports it.

Enthusiasts ask what the most advanced users can make it do.

Mainstream customers ask what an ordinary employee can reliably do on Tuesday afternoon.

The technology has not necessarily become less interesting.

The risk has moved.

A market begins to form when people no longer need to participate in the invention in order to benefit from it.

That is the paradox of successful early adoption.

The crowd works to make itself unnecessary.

Documentation replaces tribal knowledge.

Support replaces forum archaeology.

Standards replace improvised compatibility.

Reliable supply replaces group-buy logistics.

Training replaces self-teaching.

The customer becomes less important to the product’s survival precisely because the first customers were important enough.

This suggests a practical test for people deciding whether to enter early.

Ask what role the crowd is currently performing.

If the community is supplying basic support, assume support risk.

If users are building essential integrations, assume integration risk.

If every success story depends on unusual expertise, assume usability risk.

If financing depends on continuous enthusiasm, assume capital risk.

If the category has no stable vocabulary, assume procurement and social risk.

None of those risks means do not adopt.

They tell you what you are adopting in addition to the product.

You may be joining a support organization, standards committee, financing experiment and language workshop without being told.

That can be an advantage if you want influence and learning.

It is a burden if you only wanted a tool.

The distinction separates productive early adoption from accidental unpaid institution-building.

A mature market sells products.

An early market often sells the chance to help create the conditions under which products can someday be sold normally.

The crowd is what holds the possibility together in the meantime.

Then, if things go well, the crowd disappears into a market and everybody forgets how provisional the future once looked.
