# A Purchase Is a Message With Money Attached

In the spring of 2012, a small company asked the internet for $100,000.

The product was a watch.

Not a luxury watch and not quite the kind of smartwatch that would become familiar later. Pebble used an e-paper display, connected to iPhone and Android devices, and promised the kind of glanceable information that phones were already making abundant but still required a person to pull from a pocket. The company took the project to Kickstarter.

By the end of the campaign, 68,929 backers had pledged more than $10 million.

Those people did not merely tell Pebble that the idea sounded good.

They moved money.

That difference is easy to underestimate in an economy saturated with low-cost expressions of preference. A person can like a product announcement, share a rendering, join a mailing list, answer a survey, watch a demonstration twice, bookmark a preorder page and tell three friends that the thing is “definitely the future” without ever becoming a customer.

Companies collect these signals because they are useful. They are also weak.

A payment is stronger because it introduces sacrifice.

The customer gives up liquidity, accepts delay, tolerates uncertainty and exposes herself to disappointment. The amount may be small relative to her income, but the decision still crosses a boundary that a click does not. The producer receives something more valuable than applause: evidence that at least one person prefers the product to the other things that money could buy.

This is why a purchase is not merely the end of a marketing funnel.

It is a message with money attached.

The message says: under the conditions I understand, at this price, with this level of uncertainty, I will act.

Early markets are built from messages like that.

Pebble’s campaign became famous partly because the scale was startling. The company had asked for six figures and received eight. Yet the more important fact is not the theatrical ratio between goal and pledges. It is the conversion of uncertainty into an observable order book.

Before the campaign, Pebble’s founders could claim that people wanted a connected watch.

After the campaign, they could point to tens of thousands of people who had committed money to receive one.

That changes conversations.

Suppliers hear demand differently. Employees hear it differently. Journalists hear it differently. Retailers, competitors and investors hear it differently. The number does not prove that a mass market exists, but it narrows the set of stories that skeptics can tell.

A reservation can do the same thing.

An advance order says something about demand before the producer has built enough inventory to discover demand through ordinary retail. A refundable deposit says less than a nonrefundable purchase, but more than a poll. A letter of intent says less than a signed contract, but more than a compliment after a demonstration.

Markets contain a ladder of commitments.

The further a person or institution climbs, the more useful the signal can become.

This is not because money makes people truthful. People regret purchases. They speculate. They buy for status. They return products. Organizations sign contracts for political reasons, then fail to deploy what they bought. Crowdfunding backers can behave more like patrons than ordinary consumers.

The signal is stronger, not pure.

That distinction matters because early-adopter markets are vulnerable to theater.

A startup wants evidence of momentum. A platform wants developers. A developer wants users. Users want reassurance that they are not entering a dead ecosystem. Each participant therefore has an incentive to make the crowd look larger, more committed and more inevitable than it is.

Waitlists become headlines.

Reservation counts become valuation stories.

Downloads become users.

Registered users become active users.

Pilots become customers.

Customers become deployments.

A disciplined observer asks where the money and behavior actually changed hands.

The answer is often less glamorous and more informative.

In August 2012, only months after Pebble’s campaign, Oculus launched a Kickstarter for a virtual-reality development kit. It asked for $250,000 and received more than $2.4 million from 9,522 backers. The campaign was explicitly oriented toward developers and enthusiasts. The product was not pretending to be a polished mainstream consumer appliance.

That made the early adopters unusually important.

A development kit has almost no meaning without developers.

The buyers were not simply financing units. They were building capability around the units. They would write software, test interaction patterns, discover sources of motion sickness, experiment with games, share techniques and produce demonstrations that could recruit more interest.

The purchase created a user and a potential complement maker at the same time.

Two years later, Facebook announced an agreement to acquire Oculus for approximately $2 billion. In its announcement, Facebook noted that Oculus had received more than 75,000 orders for development kits.

It is tempting to retell that sequence as a fairy tale about crowdfunding discovering a billion-dollar future.

The real lesson is more useful.

Early demand changed the credibility of the future.

The Kickstarter campaign did not prove that virtual reality would become a mass medium. It did not prove that Oculus would dominate it. It did not solve comfort, cost, social acceptance, content, distribution or the peculiar problem of persuading people to wear a computer over their eyes.

It did something narrower and consequential.

It demonstrated that thousands of people were willing to spend money and time on an immature version now.

That evidence justified more work.

More work produced more evidence.

This is the first feedback loop of adoption: commitment can finance the search for the thing that justified the commitment.

A skeptical economist might object that this sounds circular.

It is.

Many growing systems are.

A company needs customers to improve the product, but customers prefer an improved product. A platform needs developers to attract users, but developers prefer platforms with users. A new manufacturing technology needs volume to lower cost, but customers prefer lower cost. A communications network needs subscribers to become useful, but people prefer useful networks.

The early adopter is one way the circle starts moving.

The adopter accepts a temporarily bad bargain because some other source of value compensates for immaturity.

Maybe the problem is unusually severe.

Maybe the new capability is worth more to this user than to an average user.

Maybe curiosity itself has value.

Maybe status has value.

Maybe the customer expects to learn.

Maybe the organization wants strategic influence over the vendor.

Maybe the developer sees an empty market around a new platform.

The reasons differ. The economic function is similar.

Somebody buys before the product has earned ordinary trust.

That first commitment can be valuable even when the customer is not representative of the eventual market. In fact, early adopters are often useful precisely because they are unrepresentative. They have stronger needs, more technical ability, more tolerance for failure or more willingness to pay.

The danger arrives when the producer forgets the difference between a beachhead and a country.

Pebble’s success on Kickstarter proved that Pebble had a remarkable early market. It did not prove that the company would control the smartwatch category after much larger technology companies entered. The company eventually ceased independent operations, and Fitbit acquired key assets in 2016.

Early demand can manifest a category without guaranteeing the early company owns the category.

This is one of the recurring asymmetries in technological change.

The adopters can be right about the future and wrong about the vendor.

They can be right that wearable computing matters and wrong that this particular watch becomes the durable platform. They can be right that virtual reality matters and wrong about the timing. They can be right about electric propulsion while choosing a manufacturer that disappears. They can be right about a software architecture and still watch the commercial value migrate to a different layer.

The future is not a single security you can buy.

Adoption creates signals about capabilities, needs and categories. Those signals leak.

Competitors can see them.

Capital can move toward them.

Talented employees can change jobs because of them.

Suppliers can build capacity around them.

A successful crowdfunding campaign is therefore public information as well as private finance. The producer receives the cash, but the market receives a discovery.

People will pay for this.

That sentence can be worth more than the money that proved it.

It can also be misleading.

Crowdfunding makes the problem especially visible because backers occupy an ambiguous position. They can be customers, supporters, hobbyists, speculators in a cultural sense, or members of a community that wants the project to exist even if the economics would not survive ordinary retail.

A successful campaign can therefore answer the wrong question.

It may prove that a compelling story can recruit patrons.

It may not prove that a product can be manufactured at the promised cost, shipped on schedule, supported at scale or sold repeatedly after the initial enthusiasts have been exhausted.

The distinction between demand and a durable business is where many early-adopter stories break.

Demand is necessary for most commercial futures.

It is not sufficient.

The producer still has to convert demand into capability.

That conversion is difficult because early money often arrives with obligations attached. A company that receives a million dollars from backers has not received a million dollars of free capital. It has received a million dollars and a queue of people expecting products. If manufacturing costs were underestimated, the successful campaign can accelerate the company toward a crisis. More demand can make a bad unit economy fail faster.

This is why the phrase “customers finance the future” needs qualification.

Customers can finance iteration only when the producer can deliver enough of the present to survive.

The same principle appears in more formal markets.

Enterprise customers may prepay for capacity. Airlines place deposits on aircraft that will not be delivered for years. Buyers sign long-term agreements that help a new factory secure debt. Software customers agree to multiyear contracts that let a vendor hire ahead of revenue recognition. Utilities sign power-purchase agreements that support project financing.

The structures differ, but they all convert future demand into present confidence.

Confidence can then become capital.

Capital becomes capacity.

Capacity makes delivery more credible.

Credible delivery recruits more demand.

The loop is not guaranteed to close. But the buyer is inside it.

This is one reason markets sometimes move suddenly after long periods of apparent stasis.

For years, a technology can exist in laboratories and presentations while every participant waits for somebody else to commit. The manufacturer wants orders before building capacity. The buyer wants capacity before placing orders. The financier wants contracts. The supplier wants volume. The regulator wants operational evidence.

Nothing is impossible. Nothing is bankable.

Then one participant moves.

An anchor customer signs. A government offers procurement. A community backs the first run. A major platform adopts the standard. The move does not solve every problem. It changes the beliefs of participants who were waiting on evidence from one another.

The first commitment has coordination value.

That is different from forecasting value.

A forecaster can be perfectly correct that a technology will matter and still have little influence over whether it matters. An adopter can be uncertain and still increase the probability that the technology receives another iteration.

Prediction asks what will happen.

Commitment changes what other people can rationally do next.

This is the material sense in which adoption manifests a future.

Suppose ten thousand people say they would buy a product for $500.

A producer hears optimism.

Suppose ten thousand people each place $50 deposits.

The producer now has half a million dollars and a measurable pool of people who crossed a financial threshold.

Suppose ten thousand people pay $500 in full.

The producer now has $5 million, obligations, demand evidence and a reputational disaster waiting if delivery fails.

The statements are not equivalent.

The increasing weight comes from consequences.

A useful signal is one that is expensive enough to fake that participants cannot produce it casually.

Money is one such expense. Time can be another. Integration effort can be stronger than payment. A company that spends six months connecting a new system to its operations has revealed more commitment than a company that purchased twenty unused licenses. A developer who ships a real application for a new platform has revealed more than a developer who joined the forum.

This suggests a better way to read early markets.

Do not ask only how many people are interested.

Ask what they have made costly to reverse.

Have they paid?

Have they learned?

Have they integrated?

Have they moved data?

Have they trained staff?

Have they built complements?

Have they reorganized a workflow?

Have they signed contracts whose value depends on the technology continuing to exist?

Each action ties another piece of the present to the proposed future.

That tie can become momentum.

It can also become lock-in.

The same sunk costs that make an ecosystem durable can make it difficult to leave after better alternatives appear. An organization that has spent years training employees around a platform will not evaluate a competitor on feature quality alone. A developer with customers on one operating system does not switch merely because another system is elegant. A city with installed infrastructure has physical history embedded in concrete and procurement rules.

Early commitments therefore have an ethical and strategic weight that “vote with your wallet” does not capture.

A vote usually ends when counted.

A purchase can start a chain of complementary investments.

That is why companies fight so hard for the first important customers.

They are not only buying revenue. They are buying referenceability, learning, credibility and a chance to induce other people to invest around the same system.

The customer can bargain with that power.

An early enterprise buyer may be able to demand roadmap influence, interoperability, contractual protections, data portability, service commitments or access to engineers that would be impossible after the vendor has ten thousand customers. A public buyer can insist on standards that shape a market beyond the individual contract.

The period of highest product risk can also be the period of highest customer leverage.

Most consumers will never negotiate terms with a platform, but the principle still matters. Early communities can coordinate. They can request open interfaces. They can publicize failures. They can make certain behaviors socially unacceptable before those behaviors become embedded in the business model.

Their adoption is a message.

So is the condition they attach to it.

This is where early adopters begin to look less like trend hunters and more like market makers.

They are not market makers in the financial sense. They do not necessarily quote prices or provide liquidity. They make a market in the older sense: they make continued exchange plausible.

The first purchases establish that exchange can happen at all.

The next chapter asks why those first exchanges so often look economically ridiculous.

The answer is that many important technologies need somebody willing to buy before the economics become good enough for everyone else.
