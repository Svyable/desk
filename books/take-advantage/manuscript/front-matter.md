# The Future Has Customers

Most people meet the future after somebody else has already made it ordinary.

The card arrives in the mail after the payment network exists. The phone appears in the store after factories have learned how to make millions of radios, processors, batteries, cameras and displays fit in a pocket. The electric car becomes a reasonable family purchase after charging networks, battery suppliers, repair procedures, insurance models, used-car prices and software updates have all had years to become less strange. By the time a technology feels inevitable, much of the work that made it feel inevitable has disappeared from view.

We remember the inventor. We remember the founder. We remember the laboratory, the patent, the launch event and sometimes the investor. We rarely remember the customer who bought version one.

That omission makes technological change look more unilateral than it is.

A producer introduces a possibility. Somebody has to accept it.

Acceptance sounds passive. It often is not.

The first buyers of a new product can be unusually expensive customers. They need support. They discover defects. They use the thing incorrectly and then, inconveniently, discover that their incorrect use is better than the intended one. They request features the producer did not plan to build. They complain about incompatibilities that only become obvious when the invention leaves the controlled environment in which it was created. They teach one another workarounds. They write documentation the manufacturer neglected. They build accessories. They form clubs, forums, mailing lists, meetups and eventually professions.

They also pay.

That part matters because a prediction does not finance a factory. Interest does not clear inventory. A favorable article does not necessarily justify another production run. A purchase is a peculiar kind of belief because it creates a cost for the believer and a resource for the thing believed in.

The money can be trivial in any one transaction. The information can be more valuable than the money. Ten thousand people paying for an awkward product are telling the producer, its suppliers, competitors, lenders and employees something that ten million people saying they like the idea are not.

They are demonstrating that a market exists at this price, with these defects, under these conditions, now.

Then the product changes because it was adopted.

The factory learns. The support queue teaches. Components are reordered in greater volume. Suppliers negotiate against a larger forecast. Developers decide whether a platform has enough users to justify building for it. Employers notice a skill appearing in résumés. Training courses appear. Reviewers establish a vocabulary for comparing products that barely had a category a year earlier. Insurers receive claims. Regulators receive complaints. Competitors reverse-engineer what customers seem to value. Standards committees acquire a reason to settle disagreements that could remain academic when nobody was shipping anything.

The adopters do not control this process. They are inside it.

That distinction is the subject of this book.

The word *manifest* has been damaged by wishful thinking. It can imply that wanting something intensely enough causes reality to cooperate. That is not what I mean. The future does not materialize because a group of enthusiasts visualizes it.

It can, however, become more likely because people spend money, tolerate inconvenience, report failure, build complements, acquire skills, demand interoperability, accept one standard, reject another, or place a large institutional order before the economics make sense for everybody else.

Those are causal acts.

A technology is not only an object. It is an arrangement of capabilities around an object. A telephone is more useful when other people have telephones. A software platform becomes more useful when developers build applications for it. A solar panel becomes cheaper when enough panels are manufactured for producers to learn and scale. An electric vehicle becomes easier to own when enough owners justify charging sites and service capacity. A new programming tool becomes less risky for an employer after enough programmers have learned it that hiring no longer depends on finding three unusually committed enthusiasts.

In each case adoption alters the environment faced by the next adopter.

That is the overlooked advantage.

Early adopters are usually described by their distance from the average buyer. Everett Rogers’s work on diffusion gave us a vocabulary that has become so familiar that people use it without remembering where it came from: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards. The categories are useful because populations do not adopt at once. Some people accept uncertainty, cost or inconvenience earlier than others.

But a diffusion curve can produce an optical illusion. Draw the familiar S-shape on a whiteboard and the technology appears fixed while people move toward it. The line describes who arrives when. It says less about what happens to the thing while they are arriving.

In reality the object at the left side of the curve is often not the object at the right.

Its price changes. Its reliability changes. Its social meaning changes. Its network changes. Its complement ecosystem changes. Its legal treatment changes. Its supply chain changes. Its installation process changes. Its interface changes. The people using it change what counts as normal performance. Sometimes they change the category itself.

The first buyers therefore do something more interesting than move earlier along a curve.

They bend the curve they are on.

This book follows the mechanisms by which that happens.

Some are obvious. Revenue pays bills. A premium-paying early customer can support a business while the producer works toward lower costs. A reservation can provide evidence that helps attract financing. A large launch customer can buy enough units to justify production equipment that no speculative forecast would support.

Some mechanisms are informational. A product used by real people produces evidence that a prototype cannot. Failure becomes specific. Reliability stops being a laboratory estimate and becomes a distribution of ugly events happening at inconvenient times. Customers reveal what they are willing to learn, what they refuse to learn and which missing capability turns a novelty into a tool.

Some are social. Early adopters become translators. They explain the strange thing to colleagues who would never read the technical documentation. Their presence makes use visible. Their visible use can create status, embarrassment, aspiration, suspicion or reassurance. In some fields a respected practitioner adopting a tool carries more information than a thousand advertisements because everyone else knows what that practitioner has to lose.

Some mechanisms are structural. Network effects make a system more valuable as participation grows. Standards make complementary investments safer. A growing installed base can justify repair shops, training programs, accessories, data formats and dedicated suppliers. These complements then make adoption easier, creating another turn of the loop.

And some mechanisms are negative.

People can manifest a future by refusing one.

The rejection of a product can reveal a social boundary that engineers failed to model. Privacy resistance can force a change in sensing, storage or consent. Customers who insist on an open format can make lock-in less attractive. Accessibility complaints can change an interface. A failed consumer product can survive inside factories, hospitals or warehouses because the failed launch discovered where its awkwardness was acceptable and where its capability was unusually valuable.

Early adopters are not always heroes. Sometimes they are rich enough to buy expensive toys, insulated enough to tolerate consequences, or socially rewarded for novelty regardless of usefulness. They can amplify bubbles. They can make weak products look important for a season. They can reward harmful design. They can mistake personal fascination for broad demand. Their preferences can send a producer toward a local optimum that the mainstream market does not share.

The possibility of failure is not an exception to the argument. It is part of it.

If adoption can change what gets built, then bad adoption can change what gets built too.

This is why *Take Advantage* is not an instruction to buy everything early. It is a book about participation under uncertainty.

The practical advantage of early adoption is often misdescribed as access. You get the new capability before other people do. Sometimes that matters. A new tool can make you faster, more capable or better informed while competitors are still waiting.

But access is the shallow advantage.

The deeper one is learning while the field is still plastic.

When a technology is young, conventions have not hardened. Job titles are unsettled. Best practices are unfinished. Standards remain negotiable. The distance between user and maker can be unusually short. A competent customer can still influence a roadmap because there are not yet a million other customers asking for different things. A programmer can become expert before the certification industry knows what to certify. A small company can establish a workflow before procurement departments turn that workflow into a requirement. A creator can learn the new medium while the audience is still learning what counts as good.

This is not the advantage of knowing what will win.

Nobody knows reliably enough.

It is the advantage of being present while winning is still being defined.

That requires a different temperament from hype. Hype wants certainty because certainty sells. Useful early adoption requires curiosity, bounded exposure and the willingness to abandon an experiment without rewriting history to make the experiment look wise.

The person who adopts early needs two contradictory abilities: enough imagination to see what an immature system could become, and enough skepticism to notice what it actually is.

The institution needs the same pair.

A company that waits for perfect proof can arrive after the important skills, supplier relationships and standards have been claimed. A company that treats every demonstration as destiny can burn capital and attention faster than any competitor. The problem is not choosing optimism or caution. It is designing experiments whose downside is survivable and whose learning remains useful even when the particular product fails.

That is how an adoption becomes an option rather than a bet-the-company prophecy.

The chapters ahead move from first buyers to demand signals, learning curves, user innovation, network effects, complement ecosystems, launch customers, social normalization, failed futures and the emerging case of software agents that can themselves become customers. The examples span more than a century because the mechanisms are older than the technologies currently making them visible.

What changes now is the speed.

Software can reach millions of users before institutions have agreed on vocabulary for what the software does. AI systems can acquire new capabilities between annual planning cycles. Open protocols can recruit complementors across the world before a proprietary product team could negotiate individual integrations. A developer can become an early adopter on Monday and publish a tool on Tuesday that turns thousands of other people into adopters by Friday.

The distance between trying a future and producing evidence for it is collapsing.

That makes the customer more consequential.

It also makes the customer easier to manipulate. Companies understand the value of early adoption. They manufacture scarcity, invitation lists, founder programs, preview tiers and launch communities because a visible crowd can become part of the product’s credibility. The language of participation can disguise the transfer of risk from producer to customer. Paying to test unfinished software is still paying to test unfinished software, even when the badge is flattering.

So this book will keep two ledgers.

One asks what early adoption does to the future.

The other asks what the future does to the early adopter.

Both matter.

The first buyer can be part of the invention and still get a bad deal.

The first organization to master a tool can gain a durable advantage and still discover that the vendor capturing the value is somebody else. A community can improve a platform that later encloses the ecosystem it helped create. A public institution can accelerate a valuable technology and accidentally lock an industry into a brittle standard.

Manifesting a future is not the same as owning it.

That may be the most important caution in the pages ahead.

The future has customers before it has consensus.

Those customers are not merely waiting at the end of the production line. They are part of the machinery that determines whether there will be another production line, whether the next unit will cost less, whether somebody will build the missing complement, whether a standard will settle, whether a profession will form and whether the unfamiliar thing will eventually become boring enough for everyone else.

Once you see that machinery, early adoption stops looking like a personality type.

It becomes a form of participation in technological causality.

And participation can be designed.
