# Proposal — Exit Conditions

## Working title

**Exit Conditions: Why the World Knows How to Start Things—and Not How to Stop**

## Author

Sven Hardy Benson

## One-sentence promise

A narrative nonfiction book about the neglected science and governance of endings: how airplanes, software, medicine, companies, public programs, infrastructure, wars, and institutions decide when continuation has become more dangerous than stopping—and what a well-designed ending requires after the decision is made.

## Why this book

Modern civilization is rich in launch architecture and poor in termination architecture. Projects have kickoff meetings, products have launch dates, companies have founding stories, laws have enactment dates, missions have objectives, and software has deployment pipelines. The end state is frequently underspecified until the system is already expensive, politically defended, socially depended upon, technically entangled, or emotionally difficult to abandon.

That asymmetry creates a recurring failure mode. Continuation becomes the default not because somebody has freshly proved that continuation is still best, but because the system lacks a credible trigger, authorized closer, safe procedure, or funded cleanup path.

The book gives readers a compact vocabulary for seeing that problem:

- **stop trigger** — the observable condition that reopens the continuation decision;
- **decision authority** — the person or institution empowered to act on the trigger;
- **abort window** — the interval in which stopping remains feasible at acceptable cost;
- **point of no ordinary return** — the boundary after which a normal stop may become riskier than managed continuation;
- **graceful shutdown** — a controlled transition that preserves necessary state and protects dependents;
- **residual obligation** — responsibility that survives the end of the original mission, revenue stream, or organization;
- **steward of the remains** — whoever owns the system after its glamour and cash flow are gone;
- **exit debt** — future shutdown work accumulated by choices made while a system is growing.

The argument is not "quit sooner." It is that serious systems should decide in advance how evidence can change the answer.

## Reader promise

The reader should finish the book able to look at a project, policy, product, institution, treatment, technology, or personal commitment and ask better questions than "Is it working?" or "Should we quit?"

The stronger questions are:

1. What evidence would make continuation irrational or unsafe?
2. Who is empowered to recognize that evidence?
3. Is there a deadline after which the cost of stopping rises sharply?
4. What dependencies make shutdown non-binary?
5. What must be preserved, transferred, unwound, remediated, paid, or remembered?
6. Who is harmed by a premature stop?
7. Who is harmed by indefinite continuation?
8. What would falsify the current plan?
9. Are we continuing because the objective remains valid, or because no ending architecture exists?
10. What would a graceful shutdown look like if it had to begin tomorrow?

## Opening

Chapter 1 begins on the takeoff roll.

A transport airplane is one of the rare systems in ordinary life with a carefully engineered decision boundary between rejecting a takeoff and continuing it. FAA guidance describes V1 as a speed that sits between accelerate-stop and accelerate-go performance. The key insight is counterintuitive: the safe action can change while the same emergency is unfolding. At one point, stopping is the planned response. Later, continuing may be the safer response because the runway remaining is no longer enough for an ordinary rejected takeoff.

The 2008 Learjet 60 accident at Columbia, South Carolina provides the first hard case. The NTSB found that multiple tire failures followed inadequate tire maintenance and that the captain executed a rejected takeoff after V1, contrary to training and standard operating procedures. Four people died.

The 2017 Ameristar MD-83 overrun at Willow Run complicates the rule. The airplane had an elevator jam that prevented rotation. The captain rejected after V1. The airplane overran, but all 116 people evacuated and the NTSB said the captain's timely decision to reject contributed to survivability. A precomputed boundary is not a substitute for judgment when the system enters a state the boundary did not successfully resolve.

That pair gives the book its governing tension: good exit conditions reduce improvisation under pressure, but no exit condition should become a ritual that prevents people from responding to a changed reality.

## Structure

### Part I — The Moment of No Return

The first four chapters define the problem. V1 supplies the decision-boundary model. Chapter 2 explains the political economy of beginnings and endings: starts attract owners; endings inherit orphans. Chapter 3 follows sunk cost from cognitive bias into organizational machinery. Chapter 4 asks how real systems define kill criteria before results arrive.

### Part II — Machines That Know How to Stop

Engineering becomes the laboratory. Emergency stops, graceful shutdowns, trip systems, interlocks, and abort windows show that "stop" is not a single action. A high-quality ending controls energy, preserves state, isolates hazards, communicates status, and leaves the system in a condition somebody can safely inherit.

### Part III — Institutions That Refuse to Die

The book moves from machinery to organizations and public power. Sunset clauses, permanent programs, bankruptcy, wars, and companies that outlive their economic reason expose how incentives change when an ending requires somebody to impose visible losses.

### Part IV — What Remains

This is the book's most distinctive section. Ending does not erase dependency. Nuclear and industrial decommissioning, final users of discontinued products, cessation or transition of medical treatment, data retained after institutions disappear, and environmental cleanup all show that the costliest phase can begin after the productive phase ends.

### Part V — Designing the End

The final section turns the case material into a practical design philosophy. Every consequential system should identify a steward of the remains, distinguish the right to stop from the ability to stop, test stop conditions before crisis, fund residual obligations while benefits are still being collected, and treat completion as a designed state rather than a euphemism for abandonment.

## Adjacent Desk books and the boundary

This book must remain visibly separate from several existing Sven Hardy Benson projects.

### The Reliability Tax

*The Reliability Tax* argues that economies undervalue redundancy, slack, reversibility, and alternative pathways because resilience costs are visible before failure and benefits are visible only during failure.

*Exit Conditions* is not about resilience capacity. It is about termination decisions and end states. A system can be highly redundant and still have no safe way to end. A system can be intentionally nonredundant and have excellent shutdown architecture.

### Gatekeepers

*Gatekeepers* studies control over transitions into valued systems: permission, denial, access, appeal, and exit.

*Exit Conditions* begins after the system is already operating. Its unit of analysis is not whether an actor may leave a gatekeeper's domain but how a consequential system decides to terminate itself or a mission, and how obligations are handled after termination.

### Single Points of Failure

*Single Points of Failure* asks where concentrated dependencies make large systems fragile.

*Exit Conditions* asks what happens when continued operation is itself the risk. The book is about stopping conditions, not dependency concentration.

### The Delegation Machine

*The Delegation Machine* studies authority passed from one actor to another. Authority matters here only as one component of termination architecture: somebody must actually be empowered to stop the thing.

## Tone

Narrative, concrete, skeptical of slogans, and willing to carry counterevidence into the center of the argument. Avoid "everything is a system" abstraction. Each chapter should earn its mechanism through a real case with authoritative sources, then generalize only as far as the evidence supports.

The book should repeatedly resist the seductive simplification that endings are synonymous with failure. Some shutdowns are catastrophic. Some continuations are heroic. Some sunset clauses destroy valuable continuity. Some bankruptcies preserve more value than liquidation. Some treatments deserve another cycle. Some missions need patience. The useful question is not whether ending is good. It is whether the decision architecture is capable of learning.

## Novelty claim discipline

The project should not claim that nobody has ever written about endings, shutdowns, sunk cost, decommissioning, bankruptcy, sunset clauses, or graceful shutdown. Those literatures exist.

The novelty is the synthesis: treating **termination architecture** as one cross-domain design problem and following the complete chain from stop trigger to authority, timing, shutdown mechanics, residual obligations, and stewardship of what remains.

A September 2026 title/topic scan found no obvious general-audience trade nonfiction book matching this exact scope and architecture. That is a collision check, not proof that the subject has never been discussed.
