# Research and falsification spine — Exit Conditions

Research initiated September 3, 2026.

This document defines the evidence lanes and the claims the manuscript is not allowed to smuggle in. It is a research plan, not evidence that every planned chapter has already been verified.

## Central hypothesis

Consequential systems become harder to govern when they lack explicit termination architecture: observable stop triggers, authorized decision makers, time-sensitive stop windows, controlled transition procedures, and assigned responsibility for what remains after active operation ends.

The hypothesis is stronger than "people dislike quitting" and narrower than "everything should have a sunset date."

The book should survive the following counterclaims:

1. **Persistence is often rational.** Many valuable projects look bad before they look good. A rigid exit rule can destroy long-horizon discovery, infrastructure, institutions, and relationships.
2. **Stopping can be more dangerous than continuing.** Aviation's V1 logic makes this literal. The same pattern can exist in finance, medicine, industrial processes, software migrations, and public systems.
3. **Some systems have no clean terminal state.** Maintenance, stewardship, archives, pensions, environmental residue, long-lived waste, and dependent users can make "ended" a misleading description.
4. **Sunsets can create instability.** Automatic expiration can encourage brinkmanship, repeated bargaining, underinvestment, or strategic delay.
5. **Kill criteria can be gamed.** Actors can redefine metrics, move deadlines, narrow measurements, or suppress evidence rather than honor a stop rule.
6. **Authority to stop can itself be dangerous.** A unilateral kill power can be abused, captured, or exercised on bad information.
7. **Post hoc exit narratives can masquerade as design.** The existence of an eventual ending does not prove the system had a credible ending architecture beforehand.

If the book cannot carry these objections, the thesis is too simple.

## Chapter 1 — V1

### Claim to earn

A mature stop rule is a time-dependent decision boundary, not a general preference for stopping. Aviation takeoff performance makes the trade visible because the same abnormal event can call for a rejected takeoff before a decision boundary and continued takeoff after it, while exceptional failures can still make an after-V1 reject the least-bad option.

### Authoritative lane

- FAA AC 120-62 and Pilot Guide to Takeoff Safety.
- FAA Airplane Flying Handbook, Chapter 16.
- FAA lessons-learned material on rejected-takeoff certification and performance.
- NTSB Learjet 60, Columbia, South Carolina, September 19, 2008.
- NTSB Ameristar MD-83, Willow Run, Michigan, March 8, 2017.

### Boundary

Do not reduce V1 to a universal command that a pilot must never reject after that speed. The 2017 Ameristar accident is central counterevidence: the airplane could not rotate, the captain rejected after V1, the airplane overran, and the NTSB credited the decision as contributing to survivability.

## Chapters 2–4 — Organizational continuation

### Questions

- Who owns the closing phase when the builder or sponsor is gone?
- What makes prior spending become institutional commitment rather than merely a cognitive bias?
- Which research programs, projects, or capital-allocation systems use predeclared kill criteria?
- When have such rules prevented waste, and when have they killed valuable options?

### Evidence lanes

- classic and modern sunk-cost / escalation-of-commitment research;
- megaproject governance and stage-gate literature;
- DARPA / R&D program-manager practice where documented;
- venture and pharmaceutical portfolio termination criteria, with primary company or regulatory evidence where possible;
- public audit institutions for canceled or continued major projects.

### Falsifier

If strong organizations routinely terminate programs at the economically optimal moment without formal stop architecture, the book is overstating the need for precommitment. Look for examples.

## Chapters 5–8 — Engineered shutdown

### Questions

- What is the difference between emergency stop, protective trip, controlled shutdown, isolation, and restart inhibition?
- What must a shutdown preserve?
- When does an automatic trip reduce human hesitation, and when does it create cascading or nuisance failures?
- How are abort windows modeled in systems whose state changes rapidly?

### Evidence lanes

- OSHA / NIOSH / process-safety guidance where applicable;
- NIST and major open technical standards for software/service shutdown;
- nuclear and grid protection guidance from NRC, NERC, FERC, or national laboratories;
- FAA and NASA abort logic;
- post-incident reports from NTSB, CSB, NRC, NERC, NASA, and equivalent investigative bodies.

### Falsifier

If the most reliable high-consequence systems depend mainly on expert improvisation rather than explicit shutdown states and procedures, the engineering analogy weakens. Investigate real operating doctrine rather than assuming formality.

## Chapters 9–13 — Institutional endings

### Sunset clauses

Use statutory text and legislative research rather than advocacy summaries. Test both directions: sunsets can force review, but they can also create policy cliffs or merely trigger ritual reauthorization.

### Programs

Choose cases with a documented original time horizon or objective and a later continuation decision. Do not label longevity as failure merely because a program survived.

### Bankruptcy

Primary lane: U.S. Courts, Bankruptcy Code, major court records, FDIC receivership materials where relevant. Distinguish liquidation, reorganization, receivership, and ordinary dissolution.

### War termination

Use official histories, congressional records, treaty/armistice texts, high-quality military histories, and declassified policy records. Avoid claiming a single counterfactual "correct" exit date. The chapter is about objective definition and termination authority under uncertainty.

### Companies

Use SEC filings, court records, restructuring documents, pension/warranty obligations, and wind-down disclosures. Avoid using market-cap decline as evidence that an organization "should" have died.

## Chapters 14–18 — Residual obligation

### Decommissioning

Primary lanes: U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission decommissioning requirements and trust funds; BOEM offshore decommissioning; EPA remediation programs; mining-reclamation regulators. Compare sectors because end-of-life funding mechanisms differ.

Key question: when is cleanup financed—during productive operation, at closure, by successor owners, by insurance/bonds/trusts, or ultimately by the public?

### The last user

Use vendor end-of-life policies, long-term support commitments, interoperability/export requirements, and documented service shutdowns. Preserve cases where indefinite support would itself block security or innovation.

### The last patient

Use FDA, NIH, peer-reviewed trial methodology, data-monitoring committee guidance, and major medical-ethics bodies. Distinguish stopping a clinical trial, stopping an ineffective treatment, tapering a drug, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, and transition to palliative care. Do not turn the chapter into medical advice.

### Data after death

Use privacy statutes/regulations, bankruptcy cases involving data assets, archival rules, and vendor shutdown policies. Test the assumption that deletion is always the correct end state; records can also carry accountability, continuity, and legal value.

### Cleanup

Use CERCLA/Superfund, producer-responsibility regimes, reclamation bonds, decommissioning funds, and documented orphaned liabilities. The chapter should ask whether end-of-life cost was priced while the productive activity was earning revenue.

## Chapters 19–21 — Design synthesis

The final framework is allowed to generalize only mechanisms supported repeatedly across prior chapters.

Candidate synthesis:

1. State the purpose in falsifiable terms.
2. Define evidence that should reopen the continuation decision.
3. Name who has authority to act.
4. Identify the abort window and points where stopping changes character.
5. Predefine safe intermediate and terminal states.
6. Preserve the evidence and state required for handoff, audit, or restart.
7. Protect dependent users through transition.
8. Fund residual obligations before productive cash flow disappears.
9. Assign a steward of the remains.
10. Revisit the stop rule when reality enters a state the original rule did not contemplate.

### Final falsifier

If the framework cannot distinguish a wise continuation from escalation of commitment, or a wise stop from premature abandonment, it has failed. The final chapter must demonstrate the distinction on a case where both options were plausibly defensible before the outcome was known.

## Novelty / collision discipline

A web and Desk-catalog collision scan on September 3, 2026 found extensive literatures on sunk costs, graceful shutdown, decommissioning, bankruptcy, sunset clauses, and endings. It also found technical uses of the phrase "exit conditions." The manuscript therefore must not claim invention of those concepts.

The book's proposed contribution is the cross-domain synthesis of the **whole termination chain**: trigger, authority, timing, transition, residue. That synthesis should remain the organizing structure rather than drift into a miscellaneous anthology of things that ended.
