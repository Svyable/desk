# Exit Conditions

*Why the World Knows How to Start Things—and Not How to Stop*

Sven Hardy Benson

There is a ceremony for beginning almost everything.

A ribbon is cut.

A bell rings.

A contract is signed.

A ship is christened.

A founder walks onto a stage.

A president puts a pen to paper.

A countdown reaches zero.

A new system comes alive in public, which makes the beginning easy to see and easy to remember. Beginnings attract cameras because they contain possibility before consequence has had time to accumulate.

Endings usually look different.

The last customer is transferred quietly. The old server stays online because one forgotten process still calls it. The program that was supposed to last three years acquires a fourth appropriation and then a fifth. The factory stops taking new orders but still has chemicals in the ground, machines in the building, pensions on the books, data on old drives, and a town that grew around its payroll. A treatment stops helping, yet stopping it can feel like giving up. A war outlives the objective that was used to begin it. A company loses the reason it existed before it loses the legal ability to continue existing.

The beginning had a plan.

The ending has leftovers.

This book is about the leftovers, but it is not a book about failure.

That distinction matters.

Some things should continue for a very long time. A bridge should not be abandoned because it requires maintenance. A scientific program should not be killed because its first experiment disappoints. A company can survive years of skepticism before proving a market exists. A democracy would be intolerable if every temporary frustration triggered institutional demolition. Persistence is one of civilization's great capabilities.

So is stopping.

The problem is that we often treat these capabilities asymmetrically. We design elaborate mechanisms for commitment and crude mechanisms for termination. We ask how a project gets funded before asking what evidence should make the funding stop. We ask how software will be deployed before asking how the last dependency will be removed. We ask who is responsible for operating an asset before asking who inherits responsibility when the asset is no longer productive. We ask what success looks like more often than what completion looks like.

The result is not simply waste.

It is a category of risk that hides in continuation.

A system without exit conditions does not have to be ordered to continue. Continuation is what happens when nobody has enough authority, evidence, incentive, timing, or courage to end it.

That is the central idea of this book.

An exit condition is not an off switch.

An off switch is a mechanism.

An exit condition is a decision architecture.

It answers questions that become harder once a system is moving.

What would count as enough evidence to stop?

Who is allowed to make the call?

At what point does stopping become more dangerous than continuing?

What must be preserved before shutdown?

Who carries obligations after the revenue, mission, administration, or attention disappears?

What happens to people who depended on the system but did not choose its ending?

How do we tell the difference between resilience and denial, patience and sunk cost, stewardship and inertia?

These questions appear in places that rarely sit on the same bookshelf.

A flight crew accelerating down a runway has to know when a rejected takeoff is still the safer option and when the remaining runway has become too short for an ordinary stop.

Software engineers distinguish between killing a process and shutting a service down gracefully.

Clinical trials can use stopping rules because continuing to expose people to an inferior or harmful arm can itself become unethical.

Industrial systems use trips, interlocks, emergency stops, relief systems, and shutdown procedures because detecting danger is not enough; the system has to convert detection into a controlled change of state.

Bankruptcy law exists partly because an economy needs a procedure for entities that cannot simply continue honoring every promise in full.

Public law sometimes uses sunset provisions because a power granted for one moment can become permanent through inertia.

Infrastructure reaches the end of service before it reaches the end of responsibility.

Products disappear while customers remain.

Data survives organizations.

Wars can be easier to authorize than to conclude.

None of these systems is identical. The point of putting them together is not to pretend that shutting down a web server and ending a war are the same problem.

It is to notice a recurring structure.

Beginnings concentrate benefits in imagination. Endings concentrate costs in reality.

The people who begin a thing are often celebrated. The people who close it are asked why it failed.

The budget for construction is visible. The budget for decommissioning feels like paying for nothing new.

The first user proves momentum. The last user creates obligation.

The launch team works against a date. The shutdown team works against dependency.

The incentives are different because the emotional meaning is different.

To stop can look like an admission.

To continue can look like hope.

That bias is useful sometimes. Human beings accomplish difficult things partly because they refuse to quit at the first sign of trouble. But a civilization cannot rely on motivational language to decide when a reactor should close, when a clinical trial should stop, when a failing institution should be wound down, or when an automated system should lose authority.

High-consequence systems need something better than vibes.

They need conditions.

The opening chapter begins with one of the cleanest examples I know: a speed on a runway called V1.

V1 is often described as a decision speed, but the important thing is what the concept refuses to do. It does not say stopping is always safe. It does not say continuing is always brave. It forces the system to recognize that the relative safety of those choices changes with time, speed, runway, weight, configuration, and circumstance.

There is a point at which the meaning of "stop" changes.

That is a profound design idea.

Most of the world does not have a V1.

This book asks what happens when it should.
