# V1

On September 19, 2008, a Learjet 60 accelerated down runway 11 at Columbia Metropolitan Airport in South Carolina.

The flight was supposed to go to Van Nuys, California.

It did not leave the ground.

During the takeoff roll, tires failed. The captain rejected the takeoff. The airplane ran out of runway, crossed the airport boundary, struck obstacles, and came apart in fire.

The captain died.

The first officer died.

Two passengers died.

Two other passengers were seriously injured.

The National Transportation Safety Board eventually identified more than one failure behind the accident. The operator had inadequately maintained the airplane's tires, leaving them severely underinflated. Multiple tires failed during the takeoff roll.

Then came the decision that makes the accident belong at the beginning of this book.

The captain rejected the takeoff after V1.

V1 is one of those pieces of technical language that is easy to flatten into a slogan. It is often called the takeoff decision speed. That sounds like a line painted invisibly across the runway.

Before the line, stop.

After the line, go.

Reality is more careful than the slogan.

For a transport airplane, takeoff performance is planned around two different futures that have to remain survivable under specified conditions. In one future, something goes wrong and the crew rejects the takeoff. The airplane must have enough usable runway and stopping performance to accelerate and then stop. In the other future, something goes wrong and the crew continues. The airplane must be able to keep accelerating, leave the runway, and climb.

The two futures compete for the same pavement.

A decision to stop that is made early consumes relatively little runway in acceleration and leaves a great deal of runway for deceleration.

A decision to stop later consumes more runway getting fast and leaves less runway for getting slow.

Nothing about the emergency itself has to become worse for the stopping problem to become worse.

Time does that.

Speed does that.

Distance already spent does that.

The airplane can be in the same general category of trouble and yet require a different response because the state of the system has changed around the trouble.

This is the first idea in *Exit Conditions*.

The meaning of stop changes while a system is moving.

The Federal Aviation Administration's guidance makes the timing explicit. Flight crews are trained to recognize conditions requiring a rejected takeoff and to take the first action to reject by V1. FAA training material also explains why the decision itself has to lead the mechanical action: a crew needs time to recognize a problem, close thrust, apply braking, deploy the available deceleration devices, and convert a mental decision into a physical stop.

At high speed, a second is distance.

The airplane does not care that the crew has decided to stop if the brakes have not yet begun slowing it.

That sounds obvious.

It is also a remarkably complete model of a problem that organizations fail to recognize every day.

A decision is not an outcome.

A vote to close a facility is not a closed facility.

A board resolution to retire a product is not the migration of its last customer.

A law that expires is not the disappearance of the systems built around it.

A chief executive saying a project is over does not unwind contracts, preserve records, redeploy people, sell equipment, settle claims, or remove dependencies.

Stopping has latency.

If the system is moving quickly enough, the decision to stop can arrive while the practical ability to stop has already disappeared.

A runway makes that visible because the remaining distance can be measured in feet.

Most institutions measure the equivalent badly.

They know how much money has been spent but not how much commitment is still reversible.

They know how many users a product has but not how many of those users depend on some feature that cannot be migrated cleanly.

They know when a program is scheduled to end but not what obligations survive the appropriation.

They know the date a contract can be terminated but not the operational work required to make termination real.

They know that a project is late and expensive without knowing whether another six months will preserve an option or merely make the eventual exit harder.

The missing quantity is not always runway.

The structure is familiar anyway.

There is an interval in which stopping is relatively cheap.

There is an interval in which stopping is still possible but increasingly disruptive.

There may be a point after which an ordinary stop becomes more dangerous than a managed continuation.

And there may be a later point at which continuation itself becomes impossible and the system stops catastrophically because nobody stopped it deliberately.

A good exit condition is partly an attempt to keep the decision away from that last point.

That is not the same thing as saying good systems stop early.

The aviation example is powerful precisely because it refuses that simplification.

A pilot who rejects every takeoff at the first sign of ambiguity would not be practicing a superior form of caution. High-speed rejected takeoffs carry their own risks. Brakes absorb enormous energy. Tires and stopping systems have limits. Runway conditions matter. Wind matters. Weight matters. Configuration matters. Reaction time matters. A late stop can create a problem the original malfunction did not.

Safety is not always on the side of the brake pedal.

Sometimes the safer act is to continue.

This is difficult for human storytelling because continuation and stopping come with moral costumes.

Persistence looks courageous.

Stopping looks cautious.

Or persistence looks stubborn and stopping looks wise.

We choose the costume after we know what happened.

The airplane has no interest in the costume.

It has a mass, a speed, a runway, a configuration, a set of working and nonworking components, a surface, an atmosphere, and an amount of energy that has to go somewhere.

The point of V1 is not to moralize the crew.

It is to organize a decision before the decision becomes an argument with physics.

The 2008 Learjet accident gives that organization terrible weight. The NTSB found that the captain's decision to reject after V1 was inconsistent with training and standard operating procedures. Once the airplane was moving that fast, the ordinary stopping branch no longer carried the same safety margin it had earlier in the roll.

But if that were the only case in this chapter, this would become a book about obedience.

It is not.

Nine years later, another airplane reached V1 with a problem its crew could not yet see.

On March 8, 2017, an Ameristar Charters MD-83 accelerated down runway 23L at Willow Run Airport in Michigan. There were 110 passengers and six crewmembers aboard.

The airplane reached V1.

It kept accelerating.

The captain pulled back on the control column to rotate.

The airplane did not respond normally.

The problem was not a hesitant pilot or a weak engine. The right elevator was jammed. The control surface the airplane needed in order to pitch up and leave the runway was not doing what the crew commanded.

The failure revealed itself at an awful time.

The captain rejected the takeoff twelve seconds after the airplane had achieved V1.

The MD-83 overran the runway, crossed the runway safety area, went through the airport perimeter, and was substantially damaged.

Everyone got out.

One passenger suffered a minor injury.

The NTSB's finding contains the complication every serious stopping rule needs. The investigation said the captain's timely and appropriate decision to reject the takeoff contributed to the survivability of the accident.

After V1.

The threshold mattered.

The threshold was not magic.

The ordinary design assumed that after the decision point, the airplane still possessed a viable path into the air. The jammed elevator removed that branch from reality. The crew was no longer choosing cleanly between a planned stop and a planned go. The physical system had changed the decision tree.

This is the second idea in *Exit Conditions*.

A stopping rule is only as good as the state assumptions underneath it.

That sounds like a loophole.

It can become one.

Anyone who dislikes a precommitted rule can always claim that the situation is exceptional. A manager can say the missed milestone is different this time. A government can say a temporary power is still needed because the emergency has evolved. A founder can say the company's cash burn should not trigger the agreed retrenchment because the next product cycle is unlike the last one. A project team can redefine success just before a kill criterion would be reached.

Rules are often invented because humans are talented at exceptions when an exception protects something they already want.

But the opposite failure is real too.

A rule written before the world changed can become dangerous if following it requires everyone to pretend the world did not change.

The problem is not solved by choosing rules or judgment.

A mature exit architecture needs both.

It needs a predeclared boundary strong enough to resist ordinary rationalization and a disciplined way to recognize extraordinary states that invalidate the assumptions behind the boundary.

That combination is harder than a kill switch.

A kill switch is a device.

An exit condition is an argument made in advance about evidence, authority, timing, and consequence.

Who decides that the condition has been met?

What information counts?

How quickly must the decision become action?

What happens if the designated decision maker is the person whose reputation depends on continuing?

What if the metric used as the trigger can be manipulated?

What if stopping one subsystem creates a larger hazard elsewhere?

What if the safe shutdown itself takes months?

What if there are customers, patients, employees, creditors, citizens, or communities who cannot simply be switched off when the system is?

What if the end state was never designed because everyone was busy building the beginning?

These are not abstract questions once the system has acquired momentum.

Momentum changes governance.

At the beginning of a project, almost everything is optional. The office can remain unleased. The factory can remain unbuilt. The code can remain undeployed. The contract can remain unsigned. The public can remain unpromised. The employee can remain unhired. The customer can remain unconverted.

Then decisions accumulate.

A dependency attaches.

Someone builds against the interface.

Someone moves near the plant.

Someone trains for the job.

Someone borrows against the revenue.

Someone promises a voter, a customer, a patient, a supplier, a lender, or a family that the thing will exist.

The system becomes less like an idea and more like an airplane moving down a runway.

It acquires an abort window.

The phrase is useful because it separates two questions that are routinely confused.

Should we stop?

Can we still stop cleanly?

A team can answer yes to the first question after it has already made the second answer painfully expensive.

That is exit debt.

Exit debt accumulates when a growing system makes choices that increase the future cost of ending without pricing that cost in the present.

A piece of software acquires exit debt when customers are encouraged to depend on a feature that has no migration path.

An industrial project acquires exit debt when productive revenue is enjoyed while closure and remediation obligations remain underfunded.

An institution acquires exit debt when temporary work is converted into permanent dependency without a plan for transfer.

A company acquires exit debt when shutting a product means abandoning data, warranties, integrations, or users nobody wants to own.

A public program can acquire exit debt when beneficiaries rationally organize their lives around continuity that the original authorization treated as temporary.

The debt is not necessarily evidence that the original choice was wrong.

Dependency can be the point.

A hospital is useful because people come to depend on it. A power grid is valuable because entire cities assume it will be there. A standard becomes useful because other systems build on it. A pension promise matters because a worker plans around it decades before payment.

The problem begins when dependency is treated as if it appeared for free.

The more useful a system becomes, the more care its ending can require.

That is why the final user matters more than the first user in this book.

The first user proves that someone wanted the thing.

The last user reveals what the builder owes when almost nobody wants it anymore.

Growth economics are usually kind to averages. If ninety-nine percent of customers migrate to the new product, the launch can be called a success.

Endings are governed by tails.

The one percent may include the hospital whose device still depends on the old protocol, the municipality with a procurement cycle measured in years, the customer whose data export does not work, the employee whose pension obligation does not disappear because the product line did, or the community left with the contaminated site after production moved away.

The median user leaves early.

Responsibility can remain with the last one.

Aviation understands the tail in a different way. Takeoff performance is not designed around the average day on which every engine works and nothing requires a rejected takeoff. The safety architecture exists for the abnormal branch.

The abnormal branch may almost never be used.

It still shapes the runway calculation.

This is one reason exit design can look wasteful before it is needed.

A shutdown procedure consumes engineering time that could have gone into features.

A decommissioning fund ties up money while the asset is still productive.

A migration path supports customers who have not yet asked to leave.

A sunset review creates administrative work around a program everyone currently supports.

A clinical stopping rule can force an uncomfortable conversation in the middle of a trial everyone wants to succeed.

A board's predeclared kill criteria can become inconvenient precisely when the team most wants another chance.

The cost is visible now.

The avoided catastrophe, trapped obligation, or chaotic closure remains hypothetical.

This asymmetry makes endings easy to postpone.

A launch can be sold as growth.

A shutdown plan is sold as the cost of someday having less.

The incentives are not mysterious.

The consequences can be.

Consider how many different verbs we use when a system stops.

Abort.

Reject.

Trip.

Shut down.

Wind down.

Sunset.

Cancel.

Terminate.

Retire.

Decommission.

Liquidate.

Dissolve.

Withdraw.

Taper.

Archive.

Delete.

Abandon.

Complete.

Fail.

The vocabulary is telling us something.

There is no single thing called stopping.

A reactor does not end the way a trial ends. A company does not end the way a software process ends. A military mission does not end the way a subscription ends. A treatment does not end the way a factory ends.

Even inside one system, stopping can mean different states.

Pause new work.

Finish work already accepted.

Prevent restart.

Transfer users.

Preserve records.

Drain inventory.

Isolate energy.

Cool equipment.

Settle claims.

Remediate land.

Maintain monitoring.

Keep enough of the old system alive to support whatever could not move.

A graceful shutdown is often not an instant of death.

It is a period of controlled decline.

That phrase sounds negative because modern organizations are trained to narrate themselves through growth.

Controlled decline can be an extraordinary accomplishment.

A system that serves ten million people and then safely serves five million, one million, ten thousand, one hundred, and finally zero may require as much design discipline at the end as it did at the beginning.

The final hundred can be harder than the first million because scale no longer pays for the obligation.

That is another form of exit debt.

The revenue curve can fall faster than the duty curve.

Who pays the difference?

That question will recur through the rest of this book.

It will appear in bankruptcy, where promises have to be reordered because they cannot all be honored as written.

It will appear in infrastructure, where a facility can stop producing value while still requiring monitoring and cleanup.

It will appear in medicine, where stopping treatment can require its own protocol and where continuing treatment can become a harm rather than a kindness.

It will appear in public power, where temporary authority can become administratively permanent and where automatic expiration can create its own instability.

It will appear in software, where a clean shutdown has to stop accepting new work, finish or preserve existing work, release resources, and leave enough state for somebody to understand what happened.

It will appear in war, where the language of victory can make changing an objective look like surrender even after the objective itself has shifted.

It will appear in products, where the last customer is an accounting rounding error and a real person at the same time.

But the runway comes first because it strips the question down to motion.

An airplane begins stationary.

Then it commits energy.

Every second changes the available options.

Before V1, a class of failures can lead to a planned reject.

After the boundary, a planned continuation may be safer.

Then reality can still produce a failure so severe that the ordinary continuation branch disappears and the crew must choose a late stop with too little runway because no clean option remains.

That is not a defect in the idea of a decision boundary.

It is the reason the idea deserves respect rather than worship.

The boundary compresses knowledge gathered before the emergency.

It is a way for engineers, regulators, operators, test programs, performance data, procedures, and training to put a conclusion inside the cockpit before two pilots have time to derive the conclusion from first principles at high speed.

The rule carries institutional memory.

It says, in effect, we have thought about this class of moment already.

That is what a good exit condition does.

It moves part of the hardest decision out of the crisis.

A board that decides in advance what level of cash, safety performance, technical progress, or customer response will force a review is trying to do the same thing.

A clinical protocol that defines stopping boundaries before outcomes arrive is trying to do the same thing.

A law with an expiration date is trying, imperfectly, to force a future decision rather than allow inertia to make it silently.

A decommissioning fund is trying to move the cost of ending backward in time, into the years when the system still has money.

A graceful shutdown routine is trying to turn an abrupt loss of execution into an orderly transition.

The details differ.

The design instinct is the same.

Do not wait until the moment of maximum pressure to invent the meaning of stop.

There is one more reason to start here.

V1 exposes the lie inside the phrase "keep our options open."

Options can disappear while nobody closes them.

The crew does not have to vote to surrender the earlier stopping distance. The airplane consumes it by accelerating.

Organizations do this constantly.

A negotiation window closes because another contract is signed.

A migration becomes harder because another year of customers is added to the old system.

A cleanup bill grows because another season of production occurs without funding the end state.

A policy becomes harder to reverse because people make rational long-term plans around it.

A military deployment creates new commitments that change the political cost of withdrawal.

A project hires another team, builds another dependency, announces another date, and interprets every added commitment as evidence that quitting would now waste too much.

The option did not remain open.

It was spent.

Sometimes spending it was correct.

That is what commitment means.

No ambitious project can preserve every exit forever. A bridge that can be unbuilt at negligible cost was never built. A marriage that preserves every condition of single life has not fully committed. A research program that can be canceled without losing anything probably had not accumulated much unique capability. A nation cannot make every long-term promise while reserving the right to walk away costlessly.

Commitment creates value partly by closing options.

This book is not an argument against commitment.

It is an argument for knowing which options are being closed, when they close, and what obligations survive after they do.

The mistake is not passing V1.

The mistake is pretending you are still at the beginning of the runway.

The Learjet crew in Columbia did not have the stopping margin they would have had seconds earlier.

The Ameristar crew at Willow Run discovered that the continuation branch they had planned around was no longer physically available.

Between those cases is the entire problem.

A good system prepares a default.

A good system also knows the default has assumptions.

A good system gives someone authority to act.

A good system recognizes that action takes time.

A good system understands that the end state has to be survivable, not merely labeled "stopped."

And a good system does not confuse the end of operation with the end of responsibility.

The rest of this book will leave the runway and follow that structure into places where the boundary is less visible.

There will be no single speed to calculate.

There will be no universal red line.

There will be projects whose correct ending becomes obvious only in hindsight and institutions whose continued existence is itself evidence that somebody still finds them useful. There will be shutdowns that arrive too late and shutdowns that should never have happened. There will be people who preserve valuable systems by refusing fashionable calls to quit, and people who prevent catastrophe by ending what everybody around them wanted to continue.

That uncertainty is not a reason to avoid exit conditions.

It is the reason to design them well.

A system without an exit condition still has an ending.

It has simply delegated the ending to exhaustion, insolvency, accident, abandonment, irrelevance, or collapse.

Those are exit conditions too.

They are just badly designed ones.
