## Premise

Civilization is unusually good at beginnings. We have launch plans, founding teams, ribbon cuttings, deployment checklists, appropriations, product road maps, opening nights, commissioning ceremonies, and growth targets. Endings are usually treated as what happens after the important work is over.

That is backwards.

A consequential system needs an ending architecture as much as it needs a starting architecture. It needs to know what evidence should make it stop, who has authority to stop it, when stopping becomes more dangerous than continuing, what must be preserved, who owns the cleanup, and what happens to the people still depending on the system after its builders have moved on.

*Exit Conditions* follows that neglected half of system design across aviation, software, industrial safety, medicine, public programs, bankruptcy, infrastructure, war, products, data, and institutions. The claim is not that persistence is bad or that quitting is virtuous. The claim is narrower and more useful: **if a system has no legible conditions for ending, continuation becomes the default even after the original reason for continuing has changed.**

The book asks what mature endings look like—and why stopping well may be one of the hardest forms of competence.
