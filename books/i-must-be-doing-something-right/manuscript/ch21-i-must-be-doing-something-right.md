# I Must Be Doing Something Right

There is no final audit.

No one arrives with a folder at the end of a decade and tells you which decisions counted, how much credit belonged to luck, which relationships you handled well, whether the work was meaningful enough, whether you rested the correct amount, whether you should have taken the other job, whether your children became themselves because of you, despite you, or mostly beside you.

A life does not close its books cleanly.

That may be why people reach for crude verdicts.

Successful.

Unsuccessful.

Good parent.

Bad parent.

Great career.

Wasted potential.

Disciplined.

Lazy.

Lucky.

Self-made.

Behind.

Ahead.

The labels create relief because they compress an unruly body of evidence into one word.

Then the word begins distorting the evidence.

A successful person becomes unable to admit the part that is failing.

An unsuccessful person becomes unable to see the part that works.

A good parent cannot tolerate evidence of harm.

A bad parent cannot accept evidence of love.

A lucky person disappears from causality.

A self-made person deletes everyone else.

The verdict becomes more important than the life.

This book has argued for a less efficient form of self-understanding.

Look at what happened.

Look again.

Preserve the sequence.

Ask what repeated.

Ask what changed.

Ask what became easier.

Ask what became harder because the problem became more advanced.

Ask who returns.

Ask what people entrust to you.

Ask what you keep choosing.

Ask what survives when nobody is watching.

Ask which metrics are measuring the life and which have quietly replaced it.

Ask where luck entered.

Ask what you did after it entered.

Ask which pain is evidence of damage and which pain belongs to something you still choose.

Ask what no longer requires panic.

Ask what you have maintained.

Ask what the people who knew you before can see that you cannot.

Ask what success has made harder to hear.

Ask what is enough for the purpose.

Then leave room for the answer to be mixed.

This is not as emotionally satisfying as a verdict.

It is more useful.

The title phrase, *I must be doing something right*, is valuable because it refuses to finish the sentence too quickly.

Something.

Not everything.

Something.

The word preserves uncertainty.

It also preserves agency.

The person does not claim total control, and does not pretend to be absent from their own outcomes.

A friend keeps calling.

Something in the relationship may be working.

The work keeps returning.

Something in the work or process may be valuable.

The same crisis no longer consumes three days.

Something in the internal system changed.

The family tells bad news earlier.

Something about safety may have improved.

The business survived a period that would once have killed it.

Something became more robust.

You can take a day off and nothing collapses.

Something in the system matured.

You no longer need the room to agree before making a decision.

Something in self-trust strengthened.

You apologize faster.

Something became less defended.

None of these statements needs to become a personality brand.

That is part of the relief.

You do not need to become “a resilient person” because you recovered.

You recovered.

You do not need to become “a leader” because people trusted you.

They trusted you in that context.

You do not need to become “successful” because the business worked.

The business worked in these ways and failed in others.

Behavior can remain behavior.

This keeps identity light enough to change.

It also makes self-recognition less embarrassing.

Many people reject praise because accepting it seems to require making a grand claim.

It does not.

You can accept the smallest accurate statement.

That went well.

I handled that better.

I learned this.

They came back.

I kept going.

I stopped in time.

I built something that lasted.

I helped.

These statements do not require a motivational soundtrack.

They are descriptions.

One of the reasons the descriptions matter is that life can become difficult again.

Good evidence is not only for good days.

It becomes especially useful when current conditions are noisy.

A bad week can make a person forget the decade.

A rejection can make the career look fraudulent.

A fight can make a relationship’s whole history feel suspect.

A financial loss can make years of discipline feel irrelevant.

A health problem can make the capable body disappear in memory.

A child’s struggle can make a parent reinterpret every earlier decision through the current fear.

This is what intense present experience does. It expands to fill the frame.

Longer evidence restores scale.

Not to deny the current problem.

To keep the problem from becoming the only available fact.

The same discipline is necessary during success.

A good month can make risk look imaginary.

A large audience can make criticism easier to dismiss.

A run of winning decisions can make judgment feel general when it was conditional.

New money can make old constraints feel permanently solved.

Praise can turn a useful strength into an identity that must be protected.

Success expands to fill the frame too.

Longer evidence restores scale there as well.

You have been wrong before.

The environment helped.

Other people carried part of the result.

Some things that worked then no longer work now.

The same evidence system should protect you from despair and intoxication.

That is what calibration means.

It is tempting at the end of a book like this to produce a method.

Five signs you are doing something right.

Seven questions for a Sunday review.

A scorecard.

A framework with a satisfying acronym.

The irony would be difficult to miss.

You do not need another scoreboard unless a scoreboard solves a specific problem.

The deeper habit is attentional.

Notice causality with more patience.

Notice repeated trust.

Notice changed difficulty.

Notice invisible maintenance.

Notice the cost of what looks like success.

Notice when your standards moved.

Notice when they should move.

Notice which compliments contain information and which are merely kind.

Notice which criticisms contain information and which are merely loud.

Notice what your private behavior says after the public story is removed.

Notice the parts of the life your most visible metrics cannot see.

Then make decisions.

This is not passive reflection. Better evidence should change action.

If people repeatedly trust you with a certain kind of problem and you enjoy solving it, perhaps that is a direction.

If they repeatedly trust you with a problem that is consuming your life, perhaps it is a boundary.

If a system keeps producing the same preventable emergency, your competence at rescue is no longer enough.

If the work keeps returning but you have stopped wanting it, the evidence is not telling you to stay. It is telling you what you built before deciding whether to leave.

If the scoreboard improves while the life deteriorates, change the scoreboard or change the game.

If praise does not land, inspect the evidence rules.

If every positive outcome is disqualified, make the model falsifiable.

If every positive outcome confirms your brilliance, make the model falsifiable there too.

If a good life hurts, classify the pain before blowing up the life.

If pain keeps repeating from the same source and nothing improves, stop using endurance as proof of virtue.

If maintenance feels like stagnation, ask what would deteriorate without your continued attention.

If enough sounds like quitting, ask what more is for.

Evidence becomes wisdom only after it affects choice.

There is a quieter implication.

Accurate self-recognition changes how we see other people.

When you understand how much your own outcomes depended on timing, help, context, preparation, luck, mistakes, repetition, and other people’s patience, you become less interested in simple explanations of someone else’s position.

The person behind may not be lazy.

The person ahead may not be brilliant.

The person struggling may be carrying conditions you cannot see.

The person succeeding may have worked extremely hard even if they also had advantages.

The person who appears confident may be operating with less evidence than the uncertain person.

The person with the impressive title may be winning the wrong game.

The person with the ordinary-looking life may have built exactly the life they intended.

Calibration creates humility not because it teaches that nobody earns anything, but because it reveals how crowded causality is.

You become less likely to worship outcomes.

Including your own.

This can make success feel warmer rather than colder.

Gratitude becomes easier when credit is shareable.

You do not need to defend the achievement as entirely yours in order to enjoy it.

Someone taught you.

Someone opened a door.

Someone stayed.

Someone told you the truth.

Someone bought the first thing.

Someone forgave the mistake.

Someone handled the part you could not.

Someone believed before the evidence was strong.

You were lucky.

You also did things.

The causal room can hold everyone.

The same is true of failure.

You can own your part without claiming the whole disaster.

This is one of the most humane consequences of accurate evidence. Shame becomes more specific.

I did this wrong.

That is different from: I am wrong.

Specific shame can lead to repair.

Global shame mainly produces hiding, defense, or collapse.

The book began with the mind’s complaint department because the department is not the enemy. It is part of how we learn. We need discrepancy. We need error. We need warnings. We need the ability to see where reality failed to match intention.

The goal was never to close the department.

The goal was to stop letting it issue the annual report by itself.

There are other departments.

Maintenance.

Trust.

Recovery.

Learning.

Joy.

Restraint.

Curiosity.

Friendship.

Work.

Rest.

Enough.

They have data too.

If you listen only for complaints, you will hear complaints because complaints know how to raise their voices.

Much of what is working sounds like machinery in the next room.

You stop hearing it until it stops.

One reason to notice good evidence before loss is simply that loss should not be the first moment value becomes visible.

Do not wait until the friend moves to understand the friendship.

Do not wait until the body is injured to understand what ordinary movement provided.

Do not wait until the business is threatened to understand the boring systems that kept it alive.

Do not wait until the child leaves home to understand the repetitive evenings.

Do not wait until the relationship is gone to understand the person who knew the history.

Recognition is not insurance against loss.

It only prevents loss from having to do all the teaching.

This is not easy because ordinary goodness is low contrast.

It blends into the background.

A good day often contains no event you would tell someone about.

You did the work.

A person you care about was reachable.

You ate something.

You solved a problem.

You laughed once.

You were useful and not consumed by usefulness.

You went home, or stayed home, and the home felt roughly like yours.

You slept.

No one would make a film.

A life contains many days like this if it is fortunate.

The cultural imagination can make them feel insufficient because nothing was maximized. They are not the days people announce.

But a life is mostly made of days nobody announces.

The ability to inhabit them may be one of the final forms of success.

Not because striving ends.

Tomorrow you may want more.

More skill.

More courage.

More money.

More reach.

More time.

More truth.

More capacity to help.

Desire keeps moving. That is part of being alive.

The question is whether tomorrow’s desire is allowed to erase today’s evidence.

It does not have to.

You can be unfinished and accomplished.

Fortunate and skillful.

Grateful and dissatisfied.

Needed and boundaried.

Confident and teachable.

In pain and on the right path.

Proud of what you built and ready to build something else.

Enough and still moving.

These are not contradictions to solve.

They are adult states to carry.

There will always be missing evidence.

You do not know the counterfactual life. You do not know what would have happened if you took the other job, married the other person, moved to the other city, started five years earlier, waited five years longer, invested differently, spoke up, stayed quiet, had children, did not have children, stayed, left, said yes, said no.

The unlived lives remain excellent at marketing.

They never had to encounter weather.

Your actual life did.

It has repairs, awkward parts, bills, compromises, unfinished rooms, people who disappointed you, people you disappointed, luck you did not deserve, effort no one saw, and choices you still cannot classify cleanly.

It also has evidence.

You are allowed to use it.

One evening, or one morning, or in the middle of a completely ordinary day, you may look at something that has become familiar.

The work.

The person.

The room.

The calmer reaction.

The problem you now know how to solve.

The people who still call.

The thing that lasted longer than expected.

You do not need to turn it into a lesson.

You do not need to post it.

You do not need to decide what it says about your ultimate worth.

The smaller sentence will do.

I must be doing something right.
