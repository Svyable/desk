# The Wrong Scoreboard

A scoreboard can be wrong in two ways.

It can report the number incorrectly.

Or it can report the correct number for a game you should not be playing.

The second error is harder to detect because precision creates authority.

Revenue is up 18 percent.

Followers crossed one hundred thousand.

The house is worth more.

The title changed.

The ranking improved.

The streak is at 412 days.

The child got into the school.

The resting heart rate dropped.

The portfolio reached the number.

Nothing about these measures is inherently empty. Many matter. Revenue can keep people employed. Money can create safety. Fitness metrics can reveal real changes. Grades can open doors. Reach can distribute useful work.

The problem begins when the measure quietly replaces the reason it was chosen.

You wanted money because you wanted freedom.

Then the money required a life with less freedom.

You wanted an audience because you wanted the work to reach people.

Then the audience began determining the work.

You wanted fitness because you wanted to feel capable in your body.

Then the body became a project whose data generated anxiety all day.

You wanted a promotion because you wanted more interesting decisions.

Then you discovered the role contained fewer interesting decisions and more organizational maintenance.

The scoreboard can improve while the game deteriorates.

This is not a new problem. Social scientists have long warned that measures can distort systems when people begin optimizing for the indicator itself. The idea appears in different forms in Goodhart’s law, Campbell’s law, and a large family of arguments about targets, incentives, and proxies. Once a measure becomes a target, behavior can change in ways that weaken the relationship between the measure and what it was intended to represent.

Organizations provide obvious examples.

If a school is judged mainly by test scores, teaching may narrow toward the test.

If police performance is judged by a particular count, reporting and enforcement can shift around the count.

If a company rewards call-center workers for shorter calls, customers may receive faster endings rather than better help.

If a publication rewards clicks, headlines may become more efficient at producing curiosity than articles are at producing understanding.

The measure did not become inaccurate.

The system became responsive to it.

Personal life is full of smaller versions.

A person wants to read more, so they set a target for books completed. Soon short books feel attractive and difficult books feel inefficient.

A person wants to write consistently, so they track words. They learn to produce words and become reluctant to delete them.

A person wants to walk daily, so they track steps. A restorative hour outside becomes a number that must close a ring.

A person wants financial security, so they track net worth. At some point spending money on an experience they genuinely value feels like moving backward.

Every metric creates a shadow behavior.

The shadow is not always harmful. Targets can focus attention. Progress monitoring can support goal attainment. Visible feedback can make vague intentions operational.

The question is whether the metric remains subordinate to the purpose.

This is difficult because numbers are psychologically clean.

Values are not.

A number resolves an argument.

Did we grow? Yes, 18 percent.

Did the work become better? Depends what you mean.

Are we healthier? The lab markers improved.

Is this way of living healthier? Harder question.

Are we succeeding as a family? There is no dashboard that can answer without becoming absurd.

People often choose measurable goals partly because measurement protects them from ambiguity.

Ambiguity is expensive.

If you decide that success means earning a certain amount, you know when you succeeded. If success means having enough money, meaningful work, physical health, time with people you love, room for surprise, and the ability to sleep, the variables can conflict. There may be no maximization point.

That is more like actual life.

It is also why people retreat to one number.

One number gives direction.

One number gives comparison.

One number gives status.

One number gives the comforting possibility that someone else can look at your life and know how well it is going.

The world is happy to supply the number.

Income.

Property.

Followers.

Citations.

Downloads.

Weight.

Rank.

Title.

Hours.

Children’s outcomes.

There is always a leaderboard if you are willing to accept its definition of winning.

Social comparison makes the leaderboard more powerful because we often do not know how to evaluate ourselves in isolation. A classic line of research beginning with Leon Festinger argued that people compare themselves with others partly when objective means of evaluation are unavailable. The details of social comparison theory have evolved, but the everyday observation remains obvious: other people become reference points.

Reference points are not neutral.

A person earning an amount they once considered extraordinary can feel poor in a richer neighborhood.

A strong recreational runner can feel slow after joining a competitive club.

A writer with thousands of readers can feel invisible after spending time with writers who have millions.

A parent can feel satisfied with a child’s path until a school conversation turns childhood into comparative admissions strategy.

Nothing material changed during the conversation.

The scoreboard did.

This creates one of the strangest features of success: it often grants entry into reference groups capable of making the success feel smaller.

The person improves and the comparison set improves faster.

This can be useful. Better peers can teach, stretch, and correct you. A room full of skilled people can reveal how much more is possible.

But if belonging to the room automatically resets your self-assessment to the room’s median, a life can contain perpetual upward mobility and perpetual felt inadequacy.

The person is always the beginner in a better club.

There is honor in being the beginner.

There is distortion in forgetting every club already entered.

A wrong scoreboard is not necessarily imposed by other people. Sometimes it is self-created and maintained long after it serves any purpose.

A founder decides in an early year that annual growth proves the company has momentum. Growth becomes the number. Years later the company is stable, profitable, valuable to customers, and capable of supporting the people inside it. But growth has slowed.

The scoreboard says trouble.

Maybe trouble is real. Markets change. Stagnation can hide decay.

Or maybe the original metric belonged to a different stage.

A seedling and an oak should not be evaluated by the same growth rate.

The same is true of personal metrics.

The hours worked that helped you establish a career may become a bad measure once judgment matters more than volume.

The social activity that helped you build a community may become exhausting once the community exists.

The savings rate that helped escape fragility may become overly restrictive after basic security is achieved.

The scale weight that helped a clinician and patient monitor a particular intervention may become irrelevant or harmful as a general measure of health.

Metrics have lifecycles.

People often keep them because the number has become identity.

“I am a person who never misses a day.”

“I always grow.”

“I am the top performer.”

“I earn more every year.”

“I publish constantly.”

The metric stops being information and becomes a promise about who you are.

Now any deviation threatens the self.

This is where optimization becomes brittle.

A streak that was meant to support consistency makes rest feel like failure.

A revenue target that was meant to encourage selling causes bad-fit customers to be accepted.

A productivity target causes work to be generated for the sake of counting work.

A family that values high achievement becomes unable to recognize a child whose good life takes a different shape.

The number begins consuming the purpose.

There is a reliable test for this.

Would you still want the metric to increase if the underlying thing you care about stayed the same or became worse?

Would you want more income if it required less freedom, worse health, and less time with the people you love?

Maybe yes for a period. Circumstances matter. But the trade should be conscious.

Would you want a larger audience if the only way to hold it was to make work you respected less?

Would you want a higher title if the work moved away from your strengths?

Would you want a lower weight if energy, strength, mood, or health markers deteriorated?

Would you want your child to rank higher if the process made the child afraid of learning?

Would you want the company to grow if every additional unit of growth made the product worse?

When the answer becomes no, the scoreboard has lost jurisdiction.

This is more difficult than replacing one metric with a better metric because some of the most important things resist singular measurement.

Trust has indicators, not one number.

A good marriage has indicators, not one number.

Meaningful work has indicators.

Freedom has indicators.

Health has many measures that vary by context.

Good parenting can be partially observed and never fully controlled.

A life is a portfolio of partly measurable goods.

This means judgment cannot be outsourced completely.

People who love data can find this irritating.

Judgment creates room for bias, rationalization, and self-serving stories. Numbers discipline stories.

Yes.

Use them.

Then remember that numbers also create room for gaming, tunnel vision, false precision, and the accidental optimization of what was easiest to count.

The answer is not anti-measurement.

It is measurement with hierarchy.

What is the purpose?

Which indicators give information about the purpose?

What behaviors might the indicators distort?

What important dimensions are missing?

When should the metric be retired?

These questions belong in companies and in kitchens.

They also belong in self-recognition because people often refuse to acknowledge that they are doing well if the most visible number is unimpressive.

A person can earn less and live better.

A company can grow slower and become stronger.

A writer can reach fewer people and write a better book.

A parent can have a child whose résumé looks ordinary and whose life is deeply alive.

A person can exercise less intensely and become healthier because the routine becomes sustainable.

A worker can produce fewer visible outputs and make better decisions.

These possibilities sound suspicious because they are easy to use as excuses.

The underperformer can always claim to be optimizing an invisible value.

That is why evidence remains necessary.

If you say you chose less income for more freedom, is there actually more freedom?

If you say slower growth improved the company, did customer retention, product quality, cash resilience, employee stability, or some other relevant evidence improve?

If you say you are publishing less to make better work, can trusted readers detect the improvement?

If you say you stopped chasing status to invest in relationships, did the relationships receive the time?

A new scoreboard should not merely be harder to audit.

It should be closer to the life you are trying to build.

External aspirations deserve special scrutiny here. Research in self-determination theory and related work has distinguished goals organized around things like wealth, image, and fame from goals more closely connected to autonomy, competence, relationships, and growth. The literature is broader and more nuanced than a simple claim that money is bad and intrinsic goals are good. Money can support autonomy. Recognition can widen opportunity. External rewards can coexist with meaningful work.

The useful warning is about centrality.

If the visible reward becomes the organizing purpose, it can crowd out the reason the reward once mattered.

The person does not need to reject achievement.

They need to know what achievement is purchasing.

This is perhaps the most practical question in the chapter.

What is the number for?

The savings are for what?

The career is for what?

The audience is for what?

The strength is for what?

The schedule is for what?

The house is for what?

The company is for what?

The answers can change.

That is allowed.

A scoreboard should serve the game, not preserve an old answer.

There is one more danger. Sometimes people discover that they are winning the wrong game and respond by despising the skills that helped them win it.

That is unnecessary.

If you became excellent at selling and no longer want a life centered on sales, the skill remains real.

If you built wealth and now want to work less, the discipline that built the wealth did not become morally suspect.

If you pursued status and learned that status does not satisfy what you hoped, the achievement still contains information about your ability to operate within a system.

You can move games without rewriting the old score as meaningless.

The point is not to be embarrassed that the scoreboard mattered.

It may have been exactly the right scoreboard for that season.

The mature move is to notice when it is no longer measuring the thing you came for.

This matters to the sentence at the center of the book because doing something right cannot mean winning every visible comparison.

Some of the strongest evidence will never appear on a public board.

People tell you the truth.

You can sleep.

You have enough margin to help.

Your children do not have to manage your emotions.

The work is better.

The business can survive your day off.

Your body can do ordinary things without becoming the central project of the day.

You can change your mind without experiencing it as humiliation.

You have people to call.

You are becoming easier to live with.

These are not consolation prizes for people who failed to win.

They are parts of the game.

If the scoreboard does not display them, that may be a problem with the scoreboard.
