# When Praise Does Not Land

Praise has a delivery problem.

Someone says the work is excellent.

The words arrive.

They do not always enter the building.

Maybe they are stopped at reception.

They are being nice.

They do not know enough to judge.

They only saw the finished version.

They are comparing me with weaker people.

They want something.

They have to say that.

If they knew how long it took, they would think differently.

The praise is logged as an event and rejected as evidence.

Criticism takes the service elevator straight upstairs.

This asymmetry is not simply low confidence. It can be a learned method of quality control.

People in demanding fields often become good by distrusting easy approval. The first person who says “looks great” may not have inspected the thing. Family praise can be kind and technically useless. Audiences reward familiarity. Managers sometimes praise to maintain morale. Customers can be satisfied with work that does not meet your own standard.

A person who accepted every compliment as proof would learn badly.

The problem begins when skepticism becomes one-directional.

Praise must prove its legitimacy.

Criticism is admitted on sight.

A stranger’s dismissive comment can outweigh a colleague’s detailed positive assessment even though the colleague has more information. One rejection can outweigh years of repeat work. A person will question whether admirers understand the field but rarely question whether the critic was tired, confused, poorly informed, or simply wrong.

This is not rigor.

It is selective epistemology.

The first task is therefore not to “learn to take a compliment.” That phrase makes the problem sound like etiquette.

The task is to improve evidence weighting.

Who is speaking?

What do they know?

How specific is the feedback?

What incentive do they have?

Does the observation repeat across independent sources?

Does behavior align with the words?

These questions apply equally to praise and criticism.

A vague compliment from a stranger may deserve little weight.

A vague insult from a stranger deserves little weight too.

A detailed positive observation from a person with expertise may deserve serious weight.

A detailed criticism from the same kind of person does too.

The standard should not change because one message feels emotionally safer to believe.

Impostor feelings make this difficult because they often contain an asymmetry of attribution. Success is assigned outward; failure is assigned inward.

The original work on what was called the impostor phenomenon described high-achieving women who struggled to internalize evidence of competence despite significant accomplishment. Success might be explained through luck, effort, charm, or other people’s mistaken judgment rather than ability. The concept has since become culturally enormous, sometimes so broad that ordinary uncertainty is given a clinical-sounding label.

It is worth restoring a distinction.

Feeling uncertain does not mean your self-assessment is distorted.

Sometimes you are new.

Sometimes the room knows more.

Sometimes the promotion did arrive before the skill was fully built.

Sometimes the work succeeded because someone else quietly saved it.

Sometimes praise is inflated.

The relevant question is not whether you feel like an impostor.

It is whether your model continues refusing evidence that should update it.

This is where praise becomes diagnostic.

Imagine two people receive the same positive feedback.

The first hears, “You explained that unusually clearly,” and thinks, Good. I have been working on that.

The second hears the same sentence and immediately searches for the loophole.

They did not know how confused I was before the meeting.

The loophole is irrelevant to the claim.

The feedback was not “you felt calm.”

It was “you explained that clearly.”

The person rejects evidence by changing the question.

This happens constantly.

“You handled that conflict well.”

“But I was furious afterward.”

The claim concerned behavior, not internal serenity.

“You’re good at this.”

“But it takes me longer than people think.”

Time may matter, but it does not automatically negate quality.

“People trust you.”

“They shouldn’t.”

That may be a joke. It may also be a refusal to ask why they do.

Praise fails to land when it targets a model the recipient has made unfalsifiable.

No amount of evidence can prove competence because the definition of competence moves to exclude whatever evidence arrived.

If the work is good but difficult, real competence would make it easy.

If the work becomes easy, it must not be difficult work.

If experts approve, they are generous.

If nonexperts approve, they do not know enough.

If people pay, the market is irrational.

If the market does not pay, the work must be bad.

The rules guarantee insufficiency.

This is psychologically expensive because external validation can never complete its assigned task.

More is required.

Another award.

Another client.

Another number.

Another proof.

The person is not hungry for praise in the ordinary narcissistic sense. They may be embarrassed by praise. Yet because praise never updates the model, achievement must keep generating new trials.

The court never adjourns.

Research on regulating positive emotion offers another useful clue. People do not simply experience positive events; they respond to them internally. Some savor them, extending or deepening positive affect. Others dampen them by minimizing, distracting, finding the downside, or telling themselves the good feeling will not last. Studies have linked these patterns to differences in self-esteem and well-being, though the mechanisms and causal directions deserve care.

The everyday behavior is recognizable.

Good news arrives and is immediately converted into risk.

The book did well. Now expectations are higher.

The relationship is good. That makes loss more frightening.

The company has cash. It could disappear.

The child is thriving. Something could still happen.

The test was clean. What about next year?

Every joy contains a future in which the joy is gone.

This is true.

It is also true of every sandwich.

Impermanence is not a reason food cannot be lunch.

The mind that refuses positive experience because it will end does not become safer. It simply experiences the ending in advance and often again when the ending comes.

This is not an argument for forced positivity. If good news creates complicated feelings, those feelings are real. A promotion can bring grief for an old team. A healthy test can bring survivor guilt. A child’s milestone can contain loss of an earlier stage. Success can increase responsibility.

Multiple emotions can occupy the same event.

The useful skill is allowing the positive evidence to remain true beside the complication.

You do not have to amputate the joy to prove you understand reality.

The way other people respond to good news can help or hinder this. Research on sharing positive events has found that active, constructive responses from close others are associated with greater positive affect and relationship quality. Again, the finding should not become a rigid rule for every conversation. Context matters.

But most people know the difference between someone receiving their good news and someone processing it into a warning.

“That’s fantastic. Tell me what happened.”

versus

“That’s great. Don’t get ahead of yourself.”

One lets the event exist before adding complexity.

The other begins the audit immediately.

Many ambitious people have internalized the second voice even if nobody around them speaks it anymore.

They are their own risk department.

Good event detected. Reduce exposure.

Praise received. Check for manipulation.

Hope rising. Prepare disappointment.

The logic is understandable. If you never let success become real, perhaps failure will hurt less.

This is emotionally similar to refusing to unpack in a hotel because you eventually have to leave.

The cost is that temporary things can no longer be inhabited.

Praise is temporary.

So is criticism.

The question is what information each contains now.

One useful practice is to ask for specificity instead of reassurance.

If someone says, “That was great,” ask, when appropriate, “What worked for you?”

Not as a fishing expedition.

As diagnosis.

Specific praise is easier to integrate because it points toward behavior.

The opening made the technical part easier to follow.

You did not become defensive when the client changed direction.

The way you framed the options helped us decide.

You remembered what I said last time.

The team seemed calmer after you clarified ownership.

Now the feedback is less about being “good” and more about what produced value.

This is useful for improvement too.

What works can be repeated deliberately.

There is a symmetrical question for criticism.

What specifically did not work?

Generic criticism is often more emotionally powerful and less operationally useful than specific criticism.

“This is weak” creates shame.

“The middle section assumes the reader understands a term you did not define” creates a task.

The goal is to make both praise and criticism more behavioral.

This keeps identity out of places where identity creates noise.

Feedback research has repeatedly shown that feedback is not universally beneficial. A large meta-analysis by Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi found that feedback interventions improved performance on average but reduced it in a substantial portion of cases. Their theory emphasized, among other things, the way feedback can shift attention toward the self rather than the task.

This is relevant far beyond management.

Praise can make you worse when it turns a task into identity preservation.

Now you are the talented one.

The smart one.

The calm one.

The person who never misses.

The next performance carries the burden of maintaining the adjective.

Criticism can create the same problem from the other side.

Now you must prove the critic wrong.

Attention leaves the work and moves to the self.

This is why the best praise often identifies an effective choice without turning the choice into a permanent trait.

The structure is not “you are brilliant.”

It is “that decision worked because…”

The recipient can learn without becoming trapped by the compliment.

Parents, teachers, coaches, and managers have all encountered versions of this distinction even though popular summaries often oversimplify the research. The point is not to ban trait language from human affection. Tell people they are wonderful when you feel it.

The point is that development requires information more specific than worth.

A person who cannot accept praise may also have learned that praise comes with obligations.

In some families and workplaces, approval is not free.

“You’re the responsible one” means you will carry more.

“You’re so good with people” means you handle the difficult client.

“You always know what to do” means you do not get to be uncertain.

“You’re strong” means your pain receives less attention.

Praise becomes a job assignment.

No wonder it does not land as pleasure.

The recipient hears the invoice.

This is another reason to study your reaction rather than scold yourself for it.

What do you think the compliment is going to cost?

Sometimes the answer reveals an old bargain.

If I am seen as capable, I will not be helped.

If I am successful, people will expect more.

If I am attractive, attention will become unsafe.

If I am wealthy, everyone will want something.

If I admit the work is good, I will have to do it again.

These are not absurd fears. Some contain real social consequences.

The goal is not to make them disappear.

It is to stop requiring praise to remain false in order for you to remain safe.

You can accept a compliment and set a boundary.

You can be strong and ask for help.

You can be good at something and decline the next assignment.

You can succeed and refuse the larger version.

You can say thank you without signing a contract with your future self.

This is perhaps the simplest form of the skill.

Someone offers praise.

You say thank you.

Then, privately, you decide whether the information deserves to update your model.

No performance of modesty is necessary.

No self-congratulation is necessary either.

If a credible person notices a repeatable strength, record the observation.

If the observation repeats, increase its weight.

If later evidence contradicts it, revise.

This is what you would do with any other uncertain claim.

You do not need to feel the praise intensely for it to be information.

This matters for people whose emotional systems remain unconvinced long after the evidence changes. Feelings can lag.

You may still feel like the beginner while performing like someone experienced.

You may still feel financially precarious after the balance sheet changes.

You may still feel socially uncertain in a room that already experiences you as belonging.

The lag is not proof that the evidence is false.

It is proof that models update at different speeds.

Give the slower model time.

Do not feed it false slogans.

Give it receipts.
