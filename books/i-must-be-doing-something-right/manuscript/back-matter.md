# Keep the Receipts

The argument of this book can be reduced until it almost sounds too simple to need a book.

Do not throw away good evidence.

That is not the same as thinking positively. Positive thinking can ask a person to improve the story while leaving the evidence untouched. The better task is to improve the evidence system itself.

A working instrument has to move in both directions.

When you make the same mistake repeatedly, the model should change.

When people stop trusting you, the model should change.

When the body keeps producing the same warning, the model should change.

When the business loses the same kind of customer, the model should change.

When someone you love tells you that the way you behave under pressure hurts them, the model should be capable of receiving that information.

And when people return, when the same skill works under new conditions, when old fears lose authority, when the system survives your absence, when the hard conversation becomes less destructive, when the work gets better, when you maintain something through a difficult season, when a person who knew you before can name the change, the model should change then too.

No special ceremony is required.

The phrase *keep the receipts* is not an invitation to turn a life into a spreadsheet. Some people would enjoy that. Some would make the spreadsheet beautiful enough to become a new avoidance strategy. The receipts can be ordinary.

An old draft.

A message from someone who came back.

A photograph of a room before it became normal.

A note from the year when the current problem was still the dream.

A memory another person can verify.

A bank statement that restores scale.

A calendar that shows how the life was actually being spent rather than how you remember spending it.

A version of yourself who no longer needs to be argued into existence because the behavior is there.

The evidence should not be kept so that you can win a case against yourself.

That would preserve the courtroom.

Keep enough evidence to remember sequence.

What was difficult?

What became easier?

What remained difficult?

Which strength transferred?

Which strength became a trap?

What did luck provide?

What did you do with it?

Who helped?

Who returned?

Who stopped returning, and why?

What did you maintain?

What did you finally stop maintaining because it no longer deserved the work?

What metric once helped and later became the wrong game?

What problem did you have to solve in order to earn the current problem?

What no longer owns the whole day?

These questions are useful precisely because they can produce answers you do not like.

If every year’s retrospective concludes that you were secretly excellent, the instrument has become flattering. If every year’s retrospective concludes that you are still not enough, the instrument is broken in the other direction.

There should be mixed years.

Mixed decades.

Mixed days.

Most lives are mixed because causality is crowded and values compete. A person can become better at work while worse at rest. More financially secure while less socially alive. More confident while less teachable. More generous while increasingly resentful. More capable while carrying too much because everyone has learned the capability is available.

Recognition is not a verdict.

It is a way of steering.

The best evidence should affect what you protect next.

If deep work consistently produces your best thinking, protect conditions for deep work.

If a particular friend helps you become more honest, protect the friendship.

If people repeatedly tell you difficult things because you respond without punishment, protect that behavior when authority increases.

If sleep changes the quality of your decisions, stop treating sleep as leftover time.

If a business model produces money and a life you do not want, do not let the money answer the whole question.

If the ordinary family meal turns out to be one of the places everyone still wants to be, do not dismiss it because nothing impressive happens there.

The evidence of what is working is partly a maintenance manual.

This is perhaps the least glamorous conclusion available.

A person learns what to keep.

We live in a culture unusually good at presenting the next thing. The next role, market, city, device, program, upgrade, project, audience, investment, transformation, identity. Novelty is commercially legible. Maintenance is harder to sell because the thing already exists.

But a life is not built only by acquisition.

It is built by not casually destroying what took years to become ordinary.

There is a second reason to keep receipts.

Memory becomes less trustworthy under pressure.

During a bad period, you may need evidence from a calmer one.

Not to prove that everything will be fine.

No receipt can do that.

To remember that the current state is not the entire sample.

There were other states.

Other problems.

Other recoveries.

Other people.

Other versions of you.

Some failed.

Some adapted.

Some built things you now complain about maintaining.

The evidence cannot promise an ending. It can prevent the present from pretending it has always been the ending.

This is particularly useful in creative work. A bad chapter can make the whole book feel bad because the bad chapter is the part currently occupying attention. An old version reveals that other difficult sections changed. A trusted reader can distinguish between a weak passage and a weak premise. The history of the work creates evidence that revision is a process rather than a moral event.

Relationships need the same longer memory, used carefully. One bad month should not automatically erase years of trust. Years of trust should not automatically excuse a current harmful pattern. Historical evidence gives weight; current evidence gives direction.

The two need to speak.

There is no formula for the weighting.

That omission is deliberate.

A formula would imply that the same quantities mean the same thing in every life. They do not. Ten years in a relationship can represent ten years of chosen repair or ten years of fear. A high income can represent freedom or captivity. A large audience can represent connection or dependence on attention. A quiet life can be peaceful or numb. A hard season can be meaningful effort or accumulated evidence that a system should end.

The evidence needs context and judgment.

The research behind this book is useful here mostly because it makes simplistic advice harder to defend. Negative information can receive disproportionate weight, but that does not mean negative information should be ignored. Mastery experiences can shape efficacy beliefs, but past success does not guarantee future success. Monitoring progress can help goal attainment on average, but tracking can also shift attention in unhelpful ways. Feedback can improve performance and can also make it worse. Social connection is associated with consequential health and well-being outcomes, but relationships differ in quality and causality is not reducible to “more friends equals better life.” Goals aligned with values can support well-being, but values do not remove material constraints. Positive events can be savored, and pain should not be argued out of existence by gratitude.

The interesting pattern is not that psychology has discovered the recipe.

It is that the recipe keeps refusing to exist.

Humans are contextual systems.

The same behavior can help in one stage and hurt in another. The same feedback can instruct one person and derail another. The same ambition can rescue someone from constraint and later become the mechanism recreating constraint. The same strength can solve the original problem and create the next one.

This is why calibration is more useful than confidence.

Confidence asks how sure you feel.

Calibration asks whether the sure feeling has earned its size.

You can be uncertain and calibrated.

You can be confident and badly calibrated.

You can know that the evidence currently favors a conclusion without requiring the conclusion to become permanent.

That is the spirit of the title.

I must be doing something right.

The sentence remains open to revision.

It leaves room for luck.

It leaves room for other people.

It leaves room for the thing you are doing wrong.

It leaves room for tomorrow’s evidence.

It also leaves room for a little pleasure in what happened.

That part matters.

People sometimes treat pleasure in accomplishment as morally suspicious, as if the only safe response to success is immediate awareness of privilege, impermanence, or the next obligation. Awareness matters. So does the fact that something went well.

You are allowed to know the difference.

A difficult thing became possible.

A person stayed.

You stayed.

The work was useful.

The system held.

The fear did not make the decision.

Someone trusted you.

You changed your mind when the facts changed.

You became less dramatic about a problem that once consumed you.

You made the room safer for bad news.

You kept something alive.

You stopped keeping something alive that should have been allowed to end.

You did not need anyone to see the best thing you did that day.

These facts can be held without a speech.

There is no need to become your own fan club.

Just stop being an unreliable witness for the defense.

## A note on evidence

The research trail for this book is kept separately from the narrative because the narrative is not meant to feel like a literature review. The studies support narrower claims than popular psychology often assigns them. Where the evidence is correlational, contested, domain-specific, or dependent on context, the research notes say so.

This matters because the book’s premise would be self-defeating if evidence were used decoratively. A book about calibration should not turn a meta-analysis into a commandment or one classic experiment into a universal law.

The research notes also include claims that would weaken the book’s argument if better evidence emerged. Falsifiability belongs here too.
