# The Problems You Get to Have

There are problems that announce deterioration.

The roof is leaking. The account is overdrawn. The relationship has become unsafe. The customer base is disappearing. The pain is getting worse. A promise was broken and nobody wants to discuss it. These problems should not be romanticized. They are not hidden gifts waiting for the correct podcast host to explain them.

There are also problems that exist because something worked.

You wanted customers. Now you have to support them.

You wanted people to read your work. Now strangers misunderstand it in public.

You wanted responsibility. Now decisions cannot always be delegated upward.

You wanted a home. Now the water heater has opinions.

You wanted children. Now someone has to be collected from a place you did not know existed at a time that conflicts perfectly with everything else.

You wanted savings. Now you have to decide how to invest money you once would have been relieved merely to possess.

You wanted a stronger body. Now you are arguing with yourself about the final five percent of a performance goal that would have been unimaginable at the beginning.

The presence of a problem does not tell you whether the system is failing.

Sometimes the problem is evidence that the system has advanced far enough to generate a new class of difficulty.

This distinction sounds obvious until you listen to how people describe their lives.

A founder says the company is a disaster because hiring is difficult. Two years earlier the company’s main problem was that nobody wanted the product.

A writer says the launch is stressful because there are too many interviews. Five years earlier the problem was that nobody returned the emails.

A manager says she cannot get any uninterrupted work done because people constantly ask for judgment. Earlier in her career she was desperate to be included in decisions.

A parent says the teenager never stops negotiating. Years earlier they worried because the child did not talk enough.

None of these new problems are imaginary. Some can be severe. Growth does not turn pain into gratitude homework.

But problem type contains information.

If you do not preserve that information, progress can become indistinguishable from failure because every stage has friction.

This is one reason adulthood can feel strangely continuous. The content changes; the sensation of having a problem remains. At twenty-five you worry about getting the job. At thirty-five you worry about becoming trapped by the job. At forty-five you worry about whether the job has displaced too much else. The emotional texture can be similar enough that you conclude nothing improved.

Something did improve.

Something else became visible.

Better problems are not necessarily smaller problems. This is where the idea is often cheapened.

A hospital administrator dealing with the consequences of rapid growth may face enormous stress. A family deciding how to care for aging parents can be overwhelmed even if financial stability gives them options. A company deciding whether to lay off employees after an expansion can face a morally painful situation that would not exist if the company had never hired anyone.

The phrase “good problem to have” can become obnoxious when used by someone who is not carrying the problem.

The person carrying it is allowed to dislike it.

The analytical point is different: what had to become true for this problem to exist?

That question can reveal change without demanding cheerfulness.

Consider an ordinary example. Someone spends years trying to become employable in a field. They study, take bad assignments, learn the tools, meet people, get rejected, improve. Eventually they reach a point where more opportunities are available than they can accept.

Now the problem is choosing.

Choice can be agonizing. Which project leads somewhere? Which pays enough? Which person do you disappoint? Which opportunity is real and which only looks flattering? Saying yes to one thing closes time for another. There is no clean answer.

Still, the problem of choosing among credible opportunities is categorically different from the problem of having none.

If the person treats both states as equivalent because both produced anxiety, their self-assessment will be badly distorted.

Emotion is not enough to classify the system.

This is true in relationships too.

Early in a relationship, the problem may be uncertainty: does this person want the same thing? Later the problems can involve coordination, compromise, family, money, sex, childcare, geography, aging, grief, and the accumulation of different needs inside a shared life.

These later problems are not evidence that the early hope failed. Some exist precisely because the relationship endured long enough to become infrastructure.

A twenty-year relationship can contain arguments that would have been impossible in month two because the couple had not yet accumulated anything complicated enough to fight about.

Time creates assets and maintenance.

The same is true of competence.

Beginners have beginner problems. They do not know where to start. They cannot distinguish important errors from cosmetic ones. They need instructions for the ordinary case.

Intermediate people have different problems. They can produce acceptable work but cannot do it reliably under pressure. They see more options and struggle to choose among them. They are fast enough to create mistakes quickly.

Experienced people have still different problems. They deal with edge cases. Their work affects other people. They must make tradeoffs that do not have a correct answer. They know enough to see risks that were invisible before. They may feel less certain precisely because the questions are harder.

This creates a dangerous illusion: increasing competence can increase experienced difficulty.

The work feels harder because you have graduated to harder work.

If you forget that the task changed, you can interpret the feeling as decline.

A person who once struggled to write a coherent page may later feel incompetent because they cannot make a complicated argument both accurate and beautiful. The difficulty is real. It is also evidence of a more advanced problem.

A programmer who once fought syntax may later worry about architecture, security, reliability, team comprehension, and the consequences of a design five years from now. More anxiety is not necessarily less skill. The field of concern expanded.

A parent who once worried about keeping an infant alive may later be tormented by questions about freedom, character, identity, technology, friendship, and how much to intervene. The problems became less physically immediate and more morally ambiguous.

Growth often trades simple problems for ambiguous ones.

This is why it can feel less satisfying than expected.

At the beginning, progress is visible. You can do the thing or you cannot. The first sale. The first mile. The first day without help. Later, progress becomes judgment. You are not trying to cross a threshold; you are trying to handle complexity without damaging what matters.

There may be no applause because the accomplishment is preventative.

You noticed the risk before it became a crisis.

You declined the deal that would have looked impressive and caused months of trouble.

You had the conversation before resentment hardened.

You kept the company smaller than you could have because the larger version would require a life you did not want.

You did not say the clever thing because the relationship mattered more than winning the moment.

Prevented problems are almost invisible evidence.

Nothing happened.

That can be the success.

A great deal of mature competence consists of things not happening.

The invoice is paid before the late fee.

The backup exists before the drive fails.

The child is picked up.

The medicine is renewed.

The team knows who owns the decision.

The difficult customer is not accepted in the first place.

The boundary is stated before resentment requires a speech.

The car is serviced before the warning light becomes an engine.

These acts do not produce the narrative satisfaction of rescue. They make rescue less necessary.

This creates a cultural bias toward visible recovery over quiet prevention. The person who fixes the emergency can look heroic. The person whose systems prevented the emergency may look like nothing happened because, in fact, nothing happened.

If you are evaluating whether you are doing something right, look at the emergencies that stopped recurring.

This is a better signal than excitement.

There is another category worth noticing: problems caused by increased options.

Options sound purely positive until you have them.

A person with one path has the pain of constraint. A person with five paths has the pain of opportunity cost. The second person can be more fortunate and more indecisive at the same time.

Financial security creates allocation problems. Career success creates prioritization problems. Visibility creates boundary problems. A strong network creates obligation problems. A large house creates maintenance problems. More freedom creates responsibility for how the freedom is used.

This is not an argument that abundance is secretly suffering. That is too neat and often insulting.

Constraint and choice carry different burdens. It is useful to know which burden you have.

If you have a choice problem and keep treating it like a scarcity problem, you may continue accumulating options instead of learning to choose.

This happens constantly.

Someone feels uncertain about which project to pursue and concludes they need more opportunities.

Someone feels exposed by financial decisions and concludes they need more money.

Someone dislikes the ambiguity of leading and concludes they need more authority.

Someone feels anxious about an audience and concludes they need a larger audience.

More of the input does not necessarily solve a downstream problem.

At some point success requires a new skill.

The skill may be selection.

Or delegation.

Or maintenance.

Or saying no.

Or tolerating disappointment.

Or staying with a decision after alternatives remain visible.

This is one reason the phrase “next level” is often less useful than it sounds. It implies the same game with larger numbers. In reality, the next stage may punish the behavior that created the current one.

The founder who solved everything personally must stop solving everything personally.

The expert who became valuable by being the best individual contributor must learn to create conditions in which other people can be good.

The person who escaped scarcity by saying yes to every opportunity must learn that continued yeses can recreate scarcity as time.

The person who built a reputation by being constantly available must learn that availability can become a tax on depth.

The skill that created the new problem may be the skill that prevents its solution.

This is useful evidence too.

Doing something right does not mean continuing to do exactly that thing forever.

Success changes the environment in which your habits operate.

This is where people can become trapped by identity. They are proud of being responsive, hardworking, self-reliant, frugal, ambitious, generous, agreeable, decisive, or needed. Each trait had a season in which it solved real problems. Later the same trait can produce new ones.

Frugality can become inability to spend on things that save time.

Self-reliance can become refusal to ask for help.

Responsiveness can become permanent interruption.

Decisiveness can become premature closure.

Generosity can become resentment when boundaries never arrive.

Ambition can become an inability to inhabit any achieved condition.

A mature self-assessment therefore asks not only, “What am I doing right?” but “What did this strength make possible, and what problem has it now created?”

The answer does not invalidate the strength.

It updates its use.

There is a temptation to rank problems morally. The person with the more fortunate problem feels ashamed of complaining because someone else has it worse.

Perspective can be valuable. Shame usually is not.

If your business problem is too much demand, you are still allowed to be exhausted by too much demand. If your family problem is coordinating an abundance of activities, the stress is still real. If your problem is deciding how to invest money, the decision can still frighten you.

You do not need to pretend your pain is the largest pain available in order to address it.

You only need to classify it accurately.

Gratitude and frustration can coexist without canceling each other.

This is a recurring theme because adult life rarely offers pure emotional states. The same thing can be a privilege and a burden. A child can be loved beyond language and make the week impossible. A company can be thriving and make its founder miserable. Aging parents can be difficult to care for and their continued presence can be a gift. A successful career can create choices that hurt.

The insistence that one interpretation must win makes self-understanding worse.

The more useful question is causal.

What produced this problem?

If the answer is deterioration, repair the deterioration.

If the answer is growth, learn the skill the growth now requires.

If the answer is a conflict between values, stop pretending optimization can remove the conflict.

If the answer is too much of a previously useful behavior, reduce it.

If the answer is a system you no longer want to maintain, consider leaving the system.

A good problem is not automatically a problem you should keep.

This matters because people sometimes become attached to sophisticated problems as status markers. They talk about the difficulty of scaling, hiring, travel, visibility, or capital partly because the difficulty signals that they have something worth scaling, hiring for, traveling about, being visible through, or capitalizing.

Problems can become luxury goods.

That is another reason to stay specific.

Does this problem indicate that something valuable exists?

Or are you keeping the problem because it tells a flattering story about what exists?

A person can be too busy because people trust them.

They can also be too busy because they like being the person everyone must wait for.

The surface looks similar.

The second system is much harder to fix because the problem is doing identity work.

So when you notice that your problems have changed, do not congratulate yourself too quickly.

Study the change.

Some problems are evidence of progress.

Some are evidence of success without adaptation.

Some are evidence that a strength is now overused.

Some are evidence that the scoreboard is wrong.

Some are just problems.

But there is one question worth asking when today’s difficulty makes you feel as if nothing has improved:

What problem did I have to solve in order to earn this one?

Sometimes the answer will make you smile.

Sometimes it will make you tired.

Either way, it restores the sequence.

You are not standing where you started simply because the ground is still uneven.
