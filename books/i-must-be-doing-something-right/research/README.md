# Research trail — I Must Be Doing Something Right

This research file supports *I Must Be Doing Something Right: How to Notice the Evidence of a Life That Is Working*.

The manuscript is narrative nonfiction, not a clinical guide, diagnostic instrument, or claim that one psychological framework explains a good life. The research is used narrowly: to test claims about self-evaluation, motivation, feedback, social comparison, relationships, positive and negative information, habit, progress, and affective forecasting. Concrete claims in the manuscript should remain no stronger than the cited evidence.

## Evidence rules

- Prefer original peer-reviewed papers, meta-analyses, and established reviews over popular summaries.
- Treat correlational findings as correlational.
- Do not turn an average effect into a universal prescription.
- Do not use a psychological label to diagnose ordinary uncertainty, sadness, ambition, self-doubt, or conflict.
- Preserve counterevidence and boundary conditions when a finding is mixed or context-dependent.
- Distinguish outcome quality from decision quality. A good result can follow a bad decision and a bad result can follow a reasonable decision under uncertainty.
- Treat this book's central claim as falsifiable: positive evidence should update self-assessment only when the evidence is relevant, credible, and proportionate.

## Core sources

### Self-efficacy and mastery evidence

Albert Bandura, “Self-efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change,” *Psychological Review* 84(2), 1977. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.2.191

Use: Chapters 3, 14, 16. Supports the narrower claim that efficacy expectations are shaped by sources including performance accomplishments/mastery experiences. The manuscript does not treat self-efficacy as global self-worth or as a guarantee of future success.

### Impostor phenomenon

Pauline Rose Clance and Suzanne Ament Imes, “The Impostor Phenomenon in High Achieving Women: Dynamics and Therapeutic Intervention,” *Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice* 15(3), 1978. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/h0086006

Use: Chapters 3 and 11. Historical source for the construct and for the pattern of discounting success. Boundary: the original work concerned a specific population and does not justify diagnosing ordinary professional uncertainty as an impostor condition.

### Negativity bias

Roy F. Baumeister, Ellen Bratslavsky, Catrin Finkenauer, and Kathleen D. Vohs, “Bad Is Stronger than Good,” *Review of General Psychology* 5(4), 2001. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.5.4.323

Paul Rozin and Edward B. Royzman, “Negativity Bias, Negativity Dominance, and Contagion,” *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 5(4), 2001. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327957PSPR0504_2

Use: Chapters 1 and 19. Supports the claim that negative information can receive disproportionate weight or processing in multiple domains. Boundary: these papers do not imply that every negative thought is an evolutionary adaptation, that negative evidence should be discounted, or that people are incapable of learning from positive evidence.

### Progress monitoring and goal attainment

Benjamin Harkin et al., “Does Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence,” *Psychological Bulletin* 142(2), 2016. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025

Use: Chapter 16. The meta-analysis covered 138 studies with 19,951 participants and found that interventions increasing progress monitoring improved goal attainment on average. Effects varied, and some features such as physically recording or publicly reporting progress were associated with stronger effects. Boundary: this is not evidence that every person should track every goal or that more measurement is always better.

### Feedback can help or harm

Avraham N. Kluger and Angelo DeNisi, “The Effects of Feedback Interventions on Performance: A Historical Review, a Meta-Analysis, and a Preliminary Feedback Intervention Theory,” *Psychological Bulletin* 119(2), 1996. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254

Use: Chapter 11. Feedback interventions improved performance on average, but more than one third of effects reduced performance. The manuscript uses this to resist the claim that feedback is automatically beneficial and to emphasize attention shifting toward task versus self.

### Social connection

Julianne Holt-Lunstad, Timothy B. Smith, and J. Bradley Layton, “Social Relationships and Mortality Risk: A Meta-analytic Review,” *PLOS Medicine* 7(7), 2010. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316

Use: Chapter 6. Meta-analysis of 148 studies involving 308,849 participants found stronger social relationships associated with increased likelihood of survival. Boundary: observational association across heterogeneous studies should not be translated into a simple causal slogan such as “friendship is medicine,” nor does relationship quantity capture relationship quality.

### Sharing positive events and capitalization

Shelly L. Gable, Harry T. Reis, Emily A. Impett, and Evan R. Asher, “What Do You Do When Things Go Right? The Intrapersonal and Interpersonal Benefits of Sharing Positive Events,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 87(2), 2004. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.228

Shelly L. Gable, Gian C. Gonzaga, and Amy Strachman, “Will You Be There for Me When Things Go Right? Supportive Responses to Positive Event Disclosures,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 91(5), 2006. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.5.904

Use: Chapters 6 and 11. Supports the idea that the response to another person's positive event can matter for positive affect and relationship quality. Boundary: not every good-news disclosure requires one prescribed response style; cultural, relational, and situational context matters.

### Savoring and dampening positive emotion

Joanne V. Wood, Ladd Wheeler Heimpel, and John L. Michela, “Savoring Versus Dampening: Self-Esteem Differences in Regulating Positive Affect,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 85(3), 2003. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.3.566

Use: Chapter 11. Supports the narrower discussion of how people can respond to positive events in ways that extend or reduce positive affect. Boundary: the book does not claim that dampening explains every failure to enjoy good news.

### Gratitude interventions

Robert A. Emmons and Michael E. McCullough, “Counting Blessings Versus Burdens: An Experimental Investigation of Gratitude and Subjective Well-Being in Daily Life,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 84(2), 2003. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377

Use: Chapter 19 and back matter. Boundary: gratitude practices are not presented as treatment for pain, evidence that fortunate people should not be unhappy, or a method for invalidating legitimate dissatisfaction.

### Adaptation and sustainable changes in well-being

Sonja Lyubomirsky, Kennon M. Sheldon, and David Schkade, “Pursuing Happiness: The Architecture of Sustainable Change,” *Review of General Psychology* 9(2), 2005. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.9.2.111

Use: Chapters 2 and 19 as background for adaptation and the fading salience of changed circumstances. Boundary: the manuscript avoids claiming a fixed percentage of happiness is determined by circumstances, intentional activity, or genetics; such popular decompositions are too strong for the evidence.

### Affective forecasting and impact bias

Timothy D. Wilson and Daniel T. Gilbert, “Affective Forecasting: Knowing What to Want,” *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 14(3), 2005. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x

Use: Chapter 14. Supports discussion of systematic errors in predicting the intensity and duration of future emotional reactions. Boundary: this work does not imply that people adapt fully to every loss, illness, trauma, disability, or chronic condition.

### Social comparison

Leon Festinger, “A Theory of Social Comparison Processes,” *Human Relations* 7(2), 1954. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/001872675400700202

Use: Chapters 2 and 9. Historical theoretical foundation for comparison as a way people evaluate opinions and abilities, particularly when objective standards are unavailable. The manuscript treats later social-comparison research as broader than this original formulation.

### Self-concordant goals and self-determination

Kennon M. Sheldon and Andrew J. Elliot, “Goal Striving, Need Satisfaction, and Longitudinal Well-Being: The Self-Concordance Model,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 76(3), 1999. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.76.3.482

Richard M. Ryan and Edward L. Deci, “Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations: Classic Definitions and New Directions,” *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 25(1), 2000. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1999.1020

Richard M. Ryan and Edward L. Deci, “Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being,” *American Psychologist* 55(1), 2000. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68

Use: Chapters 9, 18, and 20. Supports distinctions among reasons for pursuing goals and the importance of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Boundary: this research does not prescribe a single lifestyle, imply that external rewards are inherently harmful, or make wealth and meaningful motivation mutually exclusive.

### Extrinsic aspirations

Tim Kasser and Richard M. Ryan, “A Dark Side of the American Dream: Correlates of Financial Success as a Central Life Aspiration,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 65(2), 1993. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.65.2.410

Use: Chapter 9. Supports the narrower discussion of what happens when financial success becomes a central aspiration. Boundary: the book does not use this paper to argue that money is unimportant; material insecurity is consequential and money can support autonomy, safety, care, and choice.

### Goal progress and attention to what remains

Minjung Koo and Ayelet Fishbach, “Dynamics of Self-Regulation: How (Un)accomplished Goal Actions Affect Motivation,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 94(2), 2008. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.94.2.183

Use: Chapter 2. Supports the claim that focusing on accomplished versus remaining goal progress can have different motivational effects depending on commitment and framing. Boundary: it does not establish one universally best way to think about progress.

### Small wins and meaningful work

Teresa M. Amabile and Steven J. Kramer, “The Power of Small Wins,” *Harvard Business Review*, May 2011. https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins

The article reports findings from a multiyear diary study of knowledge workers developed more fully in Amabile and Kramer's work on the progress principle.

Use: Chapter 16. Supports the narrower claim that perceived progress in meaningful work can be important to inner work life and motivation. Boundary: a small completed task is not automatically meaningful progress toward the central goal.

### Habit formation and automaticity

Phillippa Lally, Cornelia H. M. van Jaarsveld, Henry W. W. Potts, and Jane Wardle, “How Are Habits Formed: Modelling Habit Formation in the Real World,” *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6), 2010. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674

Use: Chapter 13. In the study, 96 volunteers selected a daily behavior and repeated it in a consistent context; estimated time to reach 95% of the asymptotic automaticity level varied widely, from 18 to 254 days among participants with sufficient data. Missing a single opportunity did not materially affect the habit-formation process. Boundary: reject universal claims that habits form in 21 days, 66 days, or any other fixed period.

### Measurement distortion / Campbell's law

Donald T. Campbell, “Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change,” *Evaluation and Program Planning* / related social-indicator literature; DOI record: https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00286305

Use: Chapter 9 as background for the general problem that high-stakes quantitative indicators can become vulnerable to distortion or corruption when used as decision targets. Boundary: the chapter does not argue that measurement is bad; it argues that metrics remain proxies requiring interpretation.

## Chapter map

### Front Matter — The Evidence You Keep Disqualifying

Research role: introduces self-efficacy, negativity weighting, progress monitoring, feedback, social comparison, positive-event sharing, and adaptation as pieces of the evidence problem. The chapter deliberately does not imply these literatures combine into a validated unified theory of a good life.

### Chapter 1 — The Complaint Department

Primary sources: Baumeister et al. (2001); Rozin & Royzman (2001).

Claim boundary: negative events can receive disproportionate weight without making negative interpretations automatically true or evolutionarily necessary in every case.

### Chapter 2 — The Moving Finish Line

Primary sources: Koo & Fishbach (2008); Festinger (1954); Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade (2005) as adaptation background.

Claim boundary: changing standards can reflect healthy development, social comparison, adaptation, changed responsibilities, or capture by an escalating metric. No single mechanism is assumed.

### Chapter 3 — Evidence, Not Affirmations

Primary sources: Bandura (1977); Clance & Imes (1978).

Claim boundary: mastery evidence is domain-specific and probabilistic; the chapter does not claim affirmations never help anyone or that self-efficacy is equivalent to self-esteem.

### Chapter 4 — The Problems You Get to Have

Research role: primarily conceptual. Uses the broader self-regulation and expertise logic rather than claiming a validated taxonomy of “better problems.”

Falsification note: a new class of problems should not be interpreted as progress when the underlying system is deteriorating or when the problem is maintained mainly for status or identity.

### Chapter 5 — Boring Competence

Research role: conceptual synthesis around automaticity, expertise, recoverability, and infrastructure. Habit literature is used more directly in Chapter 13.

Boundary: subjective ease does not by itself prove competence. Some tasks are easy because standards are low or conditions are favorable.

### Chapter 6 — The People Who Keep Coming Back

Primary sources: Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010); Gable et al. (2004, 2006).

Boundary: repeated return can reflect trust, but also inertia, coercion, switching costs, economic dependence, family obligation, or unhealthy attachment. “Voluntary in the presence of credible alternatives” is the cleaner signal.

### Chapter 7 — The Work That Finds You Twice

Research role: conceptual application of repeated behavioral choice and reputation. No claim that repeat business proves general ability.

Boundary: recurring demand can be evidence that the market values a capability while simultaneously trapping the person in work they no longer want.

### Chapter 8 — Luck, Skill, and the Argument Afterward

Research role: decision-quality reasoning and probabilistic causality rather than one named psychological literature.

Boundary: repeated success across changing contexts strengthens evidence for transferable capability but never eliminates luck, selection effects, structural advantage, or support from other people.

### Chapter 9 — The Wrong Scoreboard

Primary sources: Campbell's measurement-distortion work; Festinger (1954); Sheldon & Elliot (1999); Ryan & Deci (2000); Kasser & Ryan (1993).

Boundary: measurement is not the enemy. The problem is allowing a proxy to become the purpose without examining incentive effects and omitted dimensions.

### Chapter 10 — The Cost of Being Needed

Research role: systems reasoning around dependence, bottlenecks, role identity, and load distribution.

Boundary: being needed can reflect genuine trust and care. The chapter does not treat dependency as automatically unhealthy or recommend eliminating legitimate obligations.

### Chapter 11 — When Praise Does Not Land

Primary sources: Clance & Imes (1978); Wood et al. (2003); Gable et al. (2004, 2006); Kluger & DeNisi (1996).

Boundary: failure to enjoy praise has many possible causes. The chapter does not diagnose an impostor phenomenon, depression, trauma, or any other condition.

### Chapter 12 — A Life Can Be Working and Still Hurt

Research role: conceptual distinction among pain, damage, effort, loss, transition, value conflict, and uncertainty.

Boundary: the chapter explicitly rejects the inference that pain is evidence of growth. Persistent harm can be evidence to leave or change a system.

### Chapter 13 — Maintenance Is an Achievement

Primary source: Lally et al. (2010), used for automaticity and variability in habit formation.

Boundary: maintenance is not always success. Some systems should end, shrink, or be replaced. Duration alone is weak evidence of quality.

### Chapter 14 — The Things You No Longer Panic About

Primary source: Wilson & Gilbert (2005); Bandura (1977) as background for mastery and future expectations.

Boundary: affective-forecasting errors do not imply all distress fades or that fear should be ignored. The chapter focuses on changed authority and recovery time rather than emotional eradication.

### Chapter 15 — The Witnesses Who Knew You Before

Research role: longitudinal perspective and multiple-observer evidence, used conceptually.

Boundary: old friends and family are biased witnesses too. Historical testimony is evidence, not truth, and can freeze a person into an outdated role.

### Chapter 16 — Small Wins Are Not Small

Primary sources: Harkin et al. (2016); Amabile & Kramer (2011); Bandura (1977).

Boundary: a checked box is not necessarily progress. The small win must connect to a meaningful bottleneck or mechanism rather than reward motion that leaves the central problem unchanged.

### Chapter 17 — The Danger of Believing Your Own Press

Research role: causal reasoning, selection effects, feedback asymmetry, and strength-overuse logic.

Boundary: success contains evidence; skepticism should not become ritual self-denial. The chapter argues for preserving disconfirming evidence, not for refusing accomplishment.

### Chapter 18 — Enough Without Stopping

Primary sources: Sheldon & Elliot (1999); Ryan & Deci (2000); Kasser & Ryan (1993).

Boundary: “enough” is relative to purpose and material conditions. The book does not use sufficiency language to minimize scarcity, economic insecurity, caregiving burdens, or legitimate desire for growth.

### Chapter 19 — The Good You Are Bad at Remembering

Primary sources: Baumeister et al. (2001); Rozin & Royzman (2001); Emmons & McCullough (2003); positive-affect regulation literature.

Boundary: deliberately attending to positive evidence is not a demand to feel grateful, deny current harm, or reinterpret every setback positively.

### Chapter 20 — When Nobody Is Watching

Primary sources: Ryan & Deci (2000); Sheldon & Elliot (1999).

Boundary: public recognition, money, audience, and external incentives are not treated as corrupting by definition. Many meaningful activities are relational and require recipients. The diagnostic question is whether the underlying action still has a reason the person endorses.

### Chapter 21 — I Must Be Doing Something Right

Research role: synthesis. No new empirical claims beyond those mapped above.

Boundary: the title sentence is framed as a provisional hypothesis, not a global self-assessment.

## Falsification and counterevidence map

The manuscript's main thesis would need revision if strong evidence showed that, across relevant real-world contexts, accepting credible positive behavioral evidence generally reduced calibration or performance more than refusing it. The current argument assumes that the problem is weighting and interpretation, not that positive evidence should always increase confidence.

The repeated-trust argument would weaken if repeated voluntary choice under credible alternatives proved to carry little information about experienced value or reliability. The manuscript already limits the inference because switching costs, obligation, coercion, and path dependence can mimic loyalty.

The progress-monitoring discussion would need revision if better experimental synthesis showed that increasing awareness of relevant progress reliably harmed goal attainment across domains. Current meta-analytic evidence instead shows an average benefit with substantial contextual variation.

The feedback chapter would be wrong if feedback were reliably beneficial regardless of content, target, framing, or attentional focus. Kluger and DeNisi's meta-analysis is important precisely because it documents substantial negative effects as well as benefits.

The social-connection discussion would need revision if stronger causal evidence substantially overturned the observed association between social relationships and consequential health/well-being outcomes. The book does not depend on a specific causal magnitude.

The “enough” argument would be weakened by evidence that externally rewarded, comparative goals reliably produce equivalent or better well-being and sustained motivation than goals experienced as self-endorsed, after controlling for material conditions and opportunity. Current self-determination and self-concordance work provides reasons to retain the distinction while avoiding moral absolutism.

The habit discussion would need revision if high-quality replications established a narrow universal habit-formation time. Existing evidence instead supports large individual and behavioral variability.

The affective-forecasting discussion would need revision if impact-bias findings failed to generalize beyond narrow laboratory or survey settings relevant to the chapter. The manuscript already avoids applying adaptation claims to severe chronic harm.

The book as a whole would also need revision if its evidence practice became unfalsifiable. Any claim that “this is evidence you are doing something right” should be answerable with: what observation would make us stop believing that interpretation?

## Claims intentionally not made

- There is no validated “I Must Be Doing Something Right” psychological scale proposed here.
- The book does not diagnose impostor syndrome or any mental-health condition.
- The book does not claim successful people deserve their outcomes.
- The book does not claim gratitude cures distress.
- The book does not claim confidence causes success in general.
- The book does not claim every hard problem is a privileged “better problem.”
- The book does not claim persistence is always virtuous.
- The book does not claim more social connection is always better.
- The book does not claim money, status, metrics, or external motivation are inherently bad.
- The book does not claim old friends know a person's “true self.”
- The book does not claim pain means growth or that suffering should be tolerated because a life is otherwise working.
- The book does not claim a favorable outcome proves a good decision.

## Research maintenance

If the manuscript is revised for publication, re-check classic sources against current systematic reviews and replications, especially for self-esteem, impostor-phenomenon prevalence, gratitude interventions, affective forecasting, self-determination applications, and social-connection causality. Classic papers are included here because they anchor concepts used in the narrative, not because age confers immunity from later evidence.
