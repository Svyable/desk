# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Paradise Found* asks whether a modern society could cross a practical post-scarcity threshold by 2030 without requiring infinite goods, the end of markets, or a technological singularity.

The thesis is deliberately narrower:

> A society becomes practically post-scarcity when involuntary deprivation of a decent material baseline — food, shelter, energy, connectivity, basic mobility, education, essential health care, and useful intelligence — becomes technically and institutionally avoidable, so access to that baseline no longer depends on continuous labor-market success.

The mechanism is convergence. Artificial intelligence reduces the marginal cost of cognitive services and coordination. Cheap low-emissions electricity lowers costs throughout the physical economy. Robotics removes some human labor from repetitive physical production. Flexible manufacturing lowers minimum economic batch sizes and improves substitution. Food systems become more resilient and less wasteful. Housing reform allows productive capacity to respond to demand. Medicine shifts routine information, monitoring, triage, and administration upstream. Logistics raises utilization and reliability. Public capital ownership and universal-service systems turn part of the resulting surplus into portable access.

The book is a **future history**. Observed conditions through September 2026 are factual and should be sourced. Events after that date are scenario. The manuscript uses past tense after 2026 to make the hypothetical system concrete, not to imply that future events have already occurred.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Cognitive cost:** Are capable AI systems becoming cheap, available, and reliable enough to behave like a utility for common low-risk cognitive tasks?
2. **Energy cost:** Are renewable generation, storage, flexible demand, grid investment, and other firming resources reducing the delivered cost of reliable electricity rather than only the cost of generation?
3. **Physical automation:** Are robots expanding beyond tightly structured factory repetition enough to remove meaningful labor hours from essential production?
4. **Manufacturing flexibility:** Are software-defined production, off-site construction, automated inspection, digital twins, and standardization reducing setup, rework, delay, and minimum batch size?
5. **Food security:** Can resilience, storage, trade, diversified production, automation, and public purchasing make a healthy diet reliably affordable despite weather and price shocks?
6. **Housing:** Can supply reform and construction productivity lower the service cost of shelter, or will land, permitting, financing, and local political constraints absorb the gains?
7. **Health:** Can AI-supported administration, triage, monitoring, translation, prevention, and task-shifting reduce the amount of scarce clinician time required per person while improving access and safety?
8. **Logistics:** How much cost is coordination failure — empty miles, misallocated inventory, documentation delay, poor matching, weak visibility, and single-route dependence — rather than physical shortage?
9. **Ownership:** If automated capital captures a larger share of production, can public funds, pensions, land ownership, resource rents, worker ownership, and taxation distribute enough of the return to sustain universal access without suppressing productive investment?
10. **Work:** Does a stronger material floor materially reduce labor supply, or mainly change bargaining power, hours, job composition, entrepreneurship, and the wage required for unpleasant work?
11. **Market design:** Which remaining scarcities are real and should still be priced, and which prices are maintained primarily by monopoly, lock-in, regulation, or constrained supply?
12. **Freedom:** Can universal intelligent administration be built with privacy, portability, appeal, plural providers, and democratic contestability rather than becoming a surveillance or paternalistic system?
13. **Global diffusion:** Can countries with weaker infrastructure, state capacity, connectivity, and access to cheap capital share the transition, or will practical post-scarcity remain a rich-country enclave?
14. **Resilience:** Can the abundance stack continue delivering essential services under cyber incidents, climate shocks, conflict, provider failure, grid stress, strategic-material shortages, and financial crises?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

The manuscript should change materially if the evidence moves against any of the following assumptions.

- Frontier and near-frontier AI may remain too expensive, unreliable, insecure, proprietary, or energy-intensive to support universal low-cost cognitive access.
- Strong benchmark performance may fail to translate into robust real-world agents, medicine, robotics, or public administration.
- Cheap solar and wind generation may not produce cheap reliable electricity if transmission, storage, capacity, minerals, permitting, and integration costs dominate.
- Battery, grid, transformer, copper, and other critical-material constraints may raise system costs faster than deployment and substitution lower them.
- Robotics may remain too brittle in variable physical environments to alter construction, agriculture, care, maintenance, and logistics costs by 2030.
- Flexible and off-site manufacturing may remain limited by certification, financing, fragmented demand, proprietary systems, and capital intensity.
- Food systems may remain vulnerable to conflict, climate shocks, water scarcity, fertilizer prices, disease, and governance even when aggregate production is adequate.
- Housing supply reform may prove politically too difficult, allowing land and scarcity rents to absorb construction productivity gains.
- Medical AI may increase utilization and spending without expanding effective coverage, or may create safety and accountability failures that limit deployment.
- Universal services may prove more expensive to administer than targeted systems in important domains, especially where marginal costs are not actually low.
- Larger unconditional transfers may reduce labor supply more than Alaska-scale evidence suggests; effects can differ sharply by transfer size, population, household composition, and labor market.
- Public capital funds may be captured, raided, politically directed, or governed badly.
- Wealth concentration may translate into enough political concentration to block broad ownership, antitrust, supply reform, or universal access.
- AI-enabled administration may increase surveillance and control faster than rights and appeals can restrain it.
- Climate change, war, pandemics, or geopolitical fragmentation may destroy productive capacity faster than the abundance stack can rebuild it.
- Poorer countries may face capital costs, debt burdens, skills gaps, infrastructure deficits, and state-capacity constraints large enough that global post-scarcity remains remote.
- Voters may simply reject the institutional bargain even when it becomes technically affordable.

## Source discipline

- Prefer government statistics, multilateral institutions, original academic research, first-party technical reports, and primary company or market disclosures for concrete claims.
- Record the publication date and access date for sources likely to change.
- Distinguish marginal cost from total system cost. Cheap generation is not cheap reliable electricity; cheap inference is not a free AI system; cheap construction is not cheap housing.
- Distinguish aggregate productive capacity from household access.
- Separate observed evidence through September 2026 from scenario after that date.
- Never cite the scenario as evidence for itself.
- Preserve counter-evidence about labor supply, rebound effects, monopoly, ecological constraints, capital concentration, safety, surveillance, and global inequality.
- Treat case studies such as Alaska and Norway as institutional precedents, not universal causal estimates.
- Avoid invented quotations, forecasts presented as realized facts, and unsupported precise dates inside the future scenario.
- For every major mechanism, keep an explicit answer to: **What would make this false?**
