# The New Scarce Things

The first mistake of post-scarcity was believing scarcity had lost.

It had moved.

This happened so quickly in some markets that people experienced the transition as contradiction. One kind of good became dramatically easier to obtain while another good, often adjacent to it, became harder to obtain.

Music became abundant. Attention to any particular song became scarce.

Images became abundant. Confidence that an image documented a real event became scarce.

Writing became abundant. Readers became scarce.

Code became abundant. Maintainers willing to own the code became scarcer.

Travel became cheaper. Quiet places became more crowded.

Intelligence became abundant. Judgment remained scarce.

Production became cheaper. Taste became more valuable.

The migration was not new. Economies had always moved value toward the bottleneck.

When books were expensive to print, printing capacity mattered. When printing became cheap, distribution and attention mattered more. When music required physical media and retail shelves, manufacturing and shelf space constrained supply. Streaming made the catalog effectively infinite to the listener and shifted scarcity toward audience attention, recommendation, live performance, and identity.

Artificial intelligence accelerated this process across categories at once.

The result was not the end of scarcity economics.

It was the promotion of scarcity into less tangible goods.

The most important was time.

Every abundance system in the book saved time somewhere and then created new claims on the saved time.

A person no longer spent an evening filling out forms, so she used the evening to answer more messages. A model produced a first draft in seconds, so the organization requested more drafts. Faster transport made farther destinations practical. Better communication expanded the number of people who could reach you. Cheap media created more media than any life could consume.

Productivity did not automatically become leisure.

It became optionality.

Optionality consumes attention.

This is why the attention economy intensified rather than faded after 2030. When the cost of producing a message, advertisement, video, song, game, pitch, product variation, political appeal, or personalized recommendation falls, the number of attempts to capture a person's attention can rise faster than the person's capacity to receive them.

The recipient becomes the bottleneck.

Early digital markets had already built enormous businesses around this fact. A service could be free because advertisers paid for access to users. Recommendation systems competed to maximize engagement. Notifications converted other people's priorities into interruptions. The arrival of generative systems reduced the cost of creating persuasive variations for narrower audiences.

The post-scarcity settlement therefore discovered that attention required property-like protection without becoming literal property.

A person needed the right to remain unavailable.

This sounds trivial compared with food or housing.

It is not.

A society can provide every material necessity and still construct an environment in which people cannot protect their own cognition from continuous commercial and political solicitation.

The right to enough became incomplete without the right to quiet.

The first regulations were clumsy. Limits on automated calls. Consent requirements. restrictions on certain targeted advertising. Rules around children's interfaces. Notification controls. Transparency for synthetic agents. Stronger defaults against third-party tracking.

Then personal agents changed the architecture more radically.

Instead of every merchant, government, platform, employer, acquaintance, and stranger reaching a person directly, the agent became a gatekeeper acting for the recipient.

Urgent message from your daughter: pass.

Appointment reminder: pass.

Credit-card offer number forty-seven: summarize with the others or discard.

A political campaign attempting to contact you through ten generated personas: one disclosure, not ten interruptions.

A subscription trying to raise its price: negotiate or cancel.

The recipient's intelligence system fought the sender's intelligence system.

This was the spam war generalized to life.

It produced a strange equilibrium. Abundant persuasion met abundant defense.

The scarce resource remained human awareness.

Another scarce good was trust.

The more cheaply plausible representations could be produced, the less the representation itself proved.

A professional-looking report no longer demonstrated that someone had spent weeks preparing it. A personalized message no longer demonstrated personal effort. A realistic recording no longer demonstrated that a camera had witnessed the event. A sophisticated software prototype no longer demonstrated a large team.

The signal moved.

Provenance mattered more.

Reputation mattered more.

Institutional responsibility mattered more.

The identity of the person willing to stand behind the output mattered more.

This did not produce a clean technical solution. Cryptographic provenance could show that a file came from a particular device or publisher, but it could not prove the publisher was honest. A verified identity could prove who spoke without proving the statement. A human-made label could become a status signal and a marketing gimmick. Anonymous speech remained valuable and sometimes necessary.

Trust could not be manufactured into abundance.

It had to be accumulated.

That made it expensive in time.

A third scarce good was place.

The abundance economy increased the value of geography in ways early digital futurists had not expected.

Remote work and machine intelligence allowed some activities to happen anywhere. This did not make all locations equivalent. It made some location choices more voluntary.

People used the freedom to seek beauty, climate, community, culture, safety, schools, walkability, wilderness, and other characteristics that could not scale digitally.

The best places attracted demand.

A mountain trail did not become wider because more people could afford the trip. A historic town did not acquire another medieval center. A coral reef could be damaged by the tourism made possible by cheaper mobility. A quiet beach became less quiet when every visitor's agent discovered it.

Discovery destroys some forms of scarcity by revealing supply.

It creates other scarcity by concentrating demand.

This forced post-scarcity societies to become more comfortable with limits.

Reservations.

Visitor caps.

Congestion pricing.

Conservation fees.

Protected zones.

Seasonal closures.

The politics were difficult because abundance had taught consumers to expect access.

Nature did not sign the social contract.

Ecological systems remained bounded by regeneration rates and physical thresholds.

This became the fourth major scarcity: sinks.

Industrial economics had often focused on scarce inputs. How much oil? How much copper? How much land? Climate change taught the world that the environment's ability to absorb waste can be scarce before an input physically runs out.

The atmosphere can contain more carbon dioxide. The relevant question is the damage associated with doing so. A river can receive more pollution. The relevant question is what the pollution destroys. A city can accommodate more vehicles. The relevant question is what congestion, noise, injury, and space consumption do to everyone else.

Post-scarcity therefore required stronger prices around some externalities at the same moment prices around basic consumption became weaker.

This seemed ideologically inconsistent only if price was treated as punishment.

The abundance principle was more coherent.

Do not price people away from essentials when the system can cheaply provide them.

Do price genuine shared constraints when unpriced consumption destroys the ability to provide them.

The baseline electricity allowance could be inexpensive while extreme peak consumption faced high prices.

Local mobility could be guaranteed while congested road space was priced.

Healthy food could be guaranteed while depleted groundwater was constrained.

Housing could be abundant while protected ecosystems remained protected.

The goal was not zero prices.

It was honest prices.

Critical minerals became another reminder that matter never disappeared.

The International Energy Agency's 2026 Critical Minerals Outlook described strong demand growth for copper, battery materials, rare earths, and other inputs used across energy, digital systems, high-tech manufacturing, aerospace, and defense. It also warned that supply remained geographically concentrated and that export restrictions were turning concentration risks into active economic-security problems. Its base case still showed meaningful projected supply gaps for copper and some other minerals into the 2030s.

An abundant energy system could therefore contain a copper scarcity.

This did not invalidate the abundance story.

It disciplined it.

The response was substitution, recycling, new mines, material efficiency, different technologies, inventories, diversified supply chains, and better use of existing assets. None could be summoned instantly.

Mines are slow because geology is slow, permitting is slow, infrastructure is slow, and communities reasonably care about what extraction does to land and water.

Post-scarcity did not mean bulldozing every local objection in the name of global abundance.

It meant confronting tradeoffs without pretending they were someone else's problem.

This became harder because rich societies could outsource material scarcity geographically.

A country might celebrate a clean electric system while the mining impacts occurred elsewhere. A digital service might appear weightless while data centers consumed power, water, chips, concrete, steel, and land. Cheap goods could depend on difficult labor and extraction hidden from consumers.

The moral accounting of abundance had to follow the supply chain.

Otherwise paradise was only local.

This is where the World Inequality Report 2026 sharpened the political problem. Its global estimates showed wealth far more concentrated than income: the top tenth of the world held roughly three-quarters of wealth while the bottom half held only a tiny share. Extreme fortunes had grown rapidly over decades.

A civilization can make material basics cheap and still leave positional power enormously unequal.

Wealth buys more than consumption.

It buys optionality.

It buys influence.

It buys patience during crises.

It buys the ability to finance political speech, litigation, research, media, lobbying, philanthropy, and new enterprises. It buys access to scarce places and scarce people.

Post-scarcity therefore reduced one moral argument for inequality without eliminating the political consequences of inequality.

If nobody is starving, does it matter that one person owns ten billion times more productive capital than another?

Yes, if ownership determines power over institutions both must live under.

No, if the only concern is whether the poorer person can eat.

Those answers coexist.

This is why the post-scarcity coalition eventually split between material egalitarians and political egalitarians.

The first group argued that the historic achievement was enough: nobody needed to be poor in the old material sense. Wealth above that threshold could remain highly unequal as long as markets were competitive and rights strong.

The second argued that concentrated wealth could buy the rules preserving concentrated wealth, eventually threatening the floor itself.

The argument was not resolved.

It became the politics of abundance.

Another scarce good was human presence.

A machine can provide endless conversation without becoming your mother at the hospital.

It can tutor a child without being the adult who notices that the child has stopped laughing.

It can produce music without being the friend who plays badly beside you in a garage.

It can simulate empathy without having a life that can be interrupted on your behalf.

As machine services became more capable, the distinction between attention and commitment became sharper.

Human care acquired a premium because care is partly costly evidence.

Someone could have been somewhere else.

They were here.

This did not make machine companionship worthless. For isolated people, language systems could provide real comfort, stimulation, memory support, and practical assistance. A person can be helped by an interaction even if the system does not experience the interaction as a human would.

But abundant simulated presence did not make human relationships abundant.

A friendship cannot be scaled to a billion close friends without changing what the word means.

A parent has finite attention among children. A teacher can know only so many students deeply. A community depends on repeated mutual obligations among people who cannot all remain anonymous to one another.

This made social capital one of the stubborn scarcities.

Trusting communities take time to build and can be destroyed quickly.

Post-scarcity could give people more time for community.

It could also give them more attractive substitutes for community.

This became a central cultural argument after 2030. If every evening could be filled with infinitely personalized media, games, companions, tutors, and entertainment, why endure the friction of other people?

Because friction is where mutual obligation lives.

A machine optimized to keep you engaged does not need you to reciprocate. A friend does.

The abundance system had removed many forms of necessary dependence.

Society had to decide how much chosen dependence it still wanted.

This is a problem no cost curve solves.

Status remained scarce too.

A society can make everyone materially secure. It cannot make everyone top ten percent.

Positional goods derive value partly from relative ranking. An elite university can expand seats, but if its prestige depends on selectivity, expansion changes the product. A neighborhood can become fashionable partly because not everyone lives there. A title can be distributed to everyone and thereby cease distinguishing anyone.

The post-scarcity society did not abolish status competition.

It liberated it from necessity.

That sounds better than it always felt.

People who no longer needed to compete for food could compete more intensely for admiration. Luxury markets became creative at producing new distinctions. Human-made goods, verified effort, physical difficulty, exclusive gatherings, scarce land, old objects, private attention, and bespoke experiences became status goods because machine abundance could not copy their social meaning.

A handcrafted imperfection could cost more than machine perfection.

A slow service could become more prestigious than an instant one.

A phone-free dinner could become a luxury.

The economy learned to sell scarcity back to people who had escaped it.

There was humor in this and danger.

Voluntary scarcity can train capability. It can create ritual, meaning, craft, and beauty. It can also become an elaborate system for proving distance from people who still lack access.

The material floor did not make hierarchy disappear.

It made hierarchy easier to identify as hierarchy.

The final scarce good in this chapter is one the post-scarcity movement had underrated almost completely.

Legitimacy.

A government can provide services efficiently and still be distrusted.

A model can make correct decisions and still lack authority to make them.

A public fund can earn excellent returns and still lose legitimacy if people believe it serves insiders.

A housing program can increase supply and still generate backlash if residents feel decisions are imposed without voice.

Legitimacy cannot be automated into existence.

It emerges from procedure, history, participation, fairness, competence, identity, and the belief that institutions can be challenged.

Abundance increased the stakes because systems became more integrated. A failure in one infrastructure layer could affect many parts of life. People needed more reason to trust the architecture precisely when the architecture could do more.

This is why the later chapters of this book become political.

The production problem had been difficult.

The legitimacy problem was harder.

Humanity had discovered how to make many essentials cheap enough to guarantee.

It had not discovered how to make attention, trust, place, ecology, power, human presence, status, time, and legitimacy infinite.

It never would.

That was not a reason to abandon post-scarcity.

It was the reason to stop calling it utopia.

The old scarcities had organized survival.

The new ones organized meaning and power.

The argument was about to get more interesting.
