# The Word We Got Wrong

The argument against post-scarcity used to begin with beachfront property.

It was a useful trick because it was obviously correct. There can only be so much beachfront. A concert venue has a fixed number of seats. A particular surgeon can perform only so many operations. A painting by Vermeer cannot be mass-produced into more Vermeers. A desirable neighborhood cannot give every applicant the same corner apartment. A person has twenty-four hours in a day, however advanced the civilization.

Therefore, the argument went, scarcity is permanent.

True.

It also missed the point.

A society can eliminate malaria without eliminating disease. It can make literacy nearly universal without making everyone a novelist. It can make clean drinking water ordinary without making water literally unlimited. The disappearance of one class of deprivation does not require the disappearance of every constraint in the universe.

Post-scarcity became useful only when we stopped treating it as a metaphysical claim.

The older imagination came from science fiction and from a particular reading of economics. In the science-fiction version, machines become so productive that objects can be summoned at will. Food appears. Homes build themselves. Energy is effectively free. People spend their lives making art, exploring space, or inventing interpersonal problems elaborate enough to fill the time. In the economic caricature, post-scarcity is impossible by definition because human wants are unlimited and resources are not.

Both versions set the bar in the wrong place.

The relevant question was never whether human beings could run out of things to want.

The question was whether anyone still needed to experience avoidable material deprivation because the economy could not afford the cost of preventing it.

Those are different questions.

People in rich societies already lived inside islands of practical post-scarcity long before 2030. Most did not ration email. They did not calculate whether they could afford to use one more search query. They rarely worried about the cost of making another digital photograph. A person with a modern word processor did not need to decide whether the next paragraph was worth the paper. In many places, clean tap water arrived so reliably that a person could use it to wash a sidewalk.

None of these goods was literally free.

Servers cost money. Networks cost money. Cameras cost money. Water systems cost money. Electricity cost money. The experience of abundance emerged because the marginal cost became low relative to income and the infrastructure bundled access in a way that made constant rationing unnecessary.

That is the practical meaning of abundance.

You stop thinking about the unit.

The unit may still have a price in a wholesale market. Someone may still pay a bill. Scarce materials may still sit underneath the service. But ordinary behavior changes because the next use is no longer worth economizing.

The distinction had been visible for generations in household utilities. Consider indoor lighting. For most of human history, illumination after sunset was expensive enough to organize behavior. Candles, whale oil, gas, kerosene, and early electric systems carried real costs. Better lamps did not simply make old lighting cheaper; people used more light. They illuminated factories, streets, signs, homes, offices, warehouses, stadiums, parking lots, and spaces nobody would have bothered lighting under the old economics.

Efficiency did not end demand. It changed what counted as normal.

The same thing happened with computing. A calculation once justified scarce machine time. Later, processors waited idly inside toys and thermostats. Storage once demanded careful deletion. Later, people carried years of photographs in their pockets and forgot which server held the copy.

Abundance tends to arrive as carelessness.

That is not always admirable. Cheap plastic created waste. Cheap calories created new health problems. Cheap information created misinformation and attention overload. Cheap transport produced congestion and emissions. A resource becoming abundant does not mean its consequences become benign.

It means the old scarcity stops disciplining behavior.

This became the crucial distinction in the post-scarcity debate because critics were right about nearly every example they raised. Land stayed scarce. Time stayed scarce. Attention stayed scarce. Ecological sinks stayed scarce. Rare materials could remain scarce. Human trust remained scarce. Some forms of beauty were scarce precisely because they could not be copied without changing what they were.

The 2030 settlement did not deny any of that.

It drew a boundary around a different class of goods.

Food sufficient for health.

Shelter sufficient for safety and participation.

Energy sufficient for ordinary household life.

Connectivity sufficient to enter the digital public sphere.

Mobility sufficient to reach work, care, education, and community.

Essential health care.

Education.

Useful cognitive assistance.

The bundle was not chosen because those goods were morally pure. It was chosen because exclusion from them created compounding damage.

A person without food becomes less able to work, learn, heal, parent, or plan. A person without stable shelter spends attention on survival and becomes harder to serve through every other institution. A person without connectivity loses access to employment, government services, education, finance, and increasingly medicine. A child without education does not merely consume less schooling; she enters adulthood with a narrower set of choices. Untreated illness can destroy earning capacity, which then makes treatment less affordable, which produces more illness.

Essential scarcity compounds downward.

That made it different from positional scarcity.

If I cannot get the best table at a famous restaurant, my disappointment does not usually make me less able to participate in society next year. If I cannot buy an original painting, I do not become less employable. If somebody else has a larger yacht, the physical fact of their yacht does not remove calories from my dinner.

Status competition can hurt. Relative inequality can shape health, power, politics, opportunity, and dignity. But the economics of preventing starvation is not the same as the economics of making everyone equally admired.

The old post-scarcity debate confused them.

Once separated, the problem became tractable.

The baseline bundle had another important property: most of it was not consumed as a singular luxury object. It was produced through systems.

Electricity comes from a grid. Connectivity comes from networks. Education can be delivered through institutions and software. Health care is a system of prevention, diagnosis, treatment, logistics, facilities, data, professionals, and financing. Food is an agricultural and distribution system. Housing is produced through land, rules, materials, finance, labor, infrastructure, and construction. Mobility depends on vehicles, roads, rail, scheduling, energy, insurance, and rights of way.

Systems can improve through coordination as well as invention.

This matters because the popular imagination of abundance is too obsessed with miraculous machines.

Sometimes the great reduction in scarcity comes from removing waste.

A truck already driving north with unused space does not require a scientific breakthrough to carry another package. A medical appointment canceled at the last minute does not require more doctors to be reassigned. A building that sits empty while another person sleeps outside represents a coordination failure before it represents a materials shortage. A solar project waiting years for interconnection can be cheaper in hardware than in bureaucracy. A classroom can have a capable teacher and still fail a student because the material is not adapted to the student's language or pace.

There is a reason the word *productivity* understates these transitions.

Productivity usually asks how much output we produce from a set of inputs. Post-scarcity asks something closer to how reliably a person can obtain the outcome.

The distinction is subtle until you need the thing.

A health system can become more productive while leaving remote patients untreated. A housing industry can build more expensive units while low-income families remain rent-burdened. An agricultural system can produce record crops while food insecurity persists. An AI industry can produce astonishing models while billions remain offline.

Aggregate abundance is not experienced abundance.

That lesson was already clear in 2026. The world could celebrate dramatic technical progress while the World Bank still counted hundreds of millions of people in extreme poverty. The International Telecommunication Union could report roughly six billion internet users while more than two billion people remained offline. The Food and Agriculture Organization could describe global food systems capable of enormous production while healthy diets remained unaffordable for many households. The World Health Organization could document medical progress while billions lacked adequate essential service coverage or faced financial hardship from out-of-pocket spending.

The contradiction was only apparent.

Capacity and access are different variables.

Post-scarcity required both.

This is why the 2030 transition cannot be explained as the triumph of technology over politics. It was the point when politics finally caught up to a new cost structure.

For years, policymakers reacted to poverty primarily through income because income was the universal adapter. Give a person money and she can buy food, housing, electricity, transport, or medicine according to need. Cash is flexible, dignified, and often administratively simpler than programs that attempt to micromanage consumption.

That logic remains powerful.

But cash has a weakness in markets where supply is constrained.

Give every household more money for rent without allowing more homes to be built and much of the benefit may become higher land values or rents. Increase medical purchasing power without expanding effective clinical capacity and queues can lengthen. Subsidize electricity without fixing grid bottlenecks and the physical constraint remains. Finance child care without increasing providers and families compete for the same slots.

Money cannot purchase a unit that the system refuses to create.

The post-scarcity project therefore moved from redistribution alone toward supply plus access.

This sounds technical. It was politically explosive.

A policy that expands supply threatens scarcity rents.

Owners of scarce assets benefit when supply cannot respond. Homeowners in constrained markets may enjoy appreciation caused partly by the inability to build nearby. Licensed professionals may benefit when entry remains narrow. Incumbent utilities may benefit from slow interconnection. Firms with proprietary standards may benefit from incompatibility. Platforms may benefit when users cannot easily move their data or relationships.

Scarcity can be a business model.

That fact complicates every cheerful story about abundance. A technology can make an old scarcity technically unnecessary while powerful institutions continue to profit from preserving it.

The end of scarcity is rarely a gift from the owner of the bottleneck.

This became one of the hard lessons of the late 2020s. Post-scarcity was not achieved because everybody agreed abundance was good. It advanced where coalitions formed around lower prices, greater access, interoperability, competition, public capacity, and new ownership structures strong enough to defeat those who earned money, power, or status from artificial restriction.

There was no single ideology behind those coalitions.

A libertarian might support housing deregulation because property owners should be allowed to build. A progressive might support it because exclusionary zoning raises rents and entrenches inequality. A climate advocate might support dense housing near transit. A business group might support it because workers need somewhere to live. A city might support it because a larger tax base funds services.

Different reasons could attack the same bottleneck.

The abundance politics that survived were often like this: less pure than their slogans, more effective than their theories.

The same happened with energy. Some people wanted cheap clean power for climate reasons. Others wanted energy independence. Manufacturers wanted lower electricity prices. Data-center operators wanted capacity. Consumers wanted lower bills. Grid operators wanted storage and flexible demand because they made the system easier to balance. These interests did not agree about the moral story of the energy transition. They could still build many of the same assets.

Post-scarcity became politically plausible when abundance stopped requiring consensus about why abundance was desirable.

That may sound cynical. It is actually one of the hopeful parts of the story.

Civilizations rarely coordinate around a single philosophy. They coordinate around arrangements that let people with different philosophies participate for different reasons.

The word *post-scarcity* had made that harder because it sounded like an ideology. Once translated into concrete questions, the argument became less mystical.

Can we make essential electricity cheap and reliable enough that disconnection for inability to pay becomes irrational?

Can we create enough modest housing in enough places that shelter stops consuming a destructive share of household income?

Can machine assistance make high-quality educational explanation available at negligible marginal cost while human institutions provide care, standards, community, and accountability?

Can medical systems use automation and better diagnostics to reserve scarce clinicians for the parts of care that genuinely require them?

Can food systems reduce waste, volatility, and distribution costs enough that a nutritious baseline is cheap to guarantee?

Can local mobility become cheap enough that access to a job or doctor does not depend on owning an expensive private machine?

Can societies finance these guarantees without destroying the productive systems that make them affordable?

Those are empirical questions.

They can fail.

That is important.

A serious book about post-scarcity has to preserve the possibility that the thesis is wrong. Energy costs can rise. Supply chains can fracture. AI can remain unreliable or concentrated. Robotics can improve more slowly than expected outside controlled environments. Construction can remain institutionally frozen. Climate damage can destroy infrastructure faster than societies build it. War can turn abundance into strategic scarcity. States can become too weak or corrupt to deliver universal systems. Ecological constraints can tighten. Automated wealth can concentrate so completely that political systems cannot translate productive capacity into public access.

The future history in this book is not inevitable.

It is a test of sufficiency.

What would have to go right?

Which failures would prevent the transition?

Which scarcities remain even in success?

The answer to the last question is especially important because societies make dangerous mistakes when they interpret the end of one constraint as the end of constraint itself.

Cheap energy can encourage wasteful land use or overconsumption. Cheap intelligence can flood institutions with plausible nonsense. Cheap manufacturing can accelerate extraction. Cheap mobility can create congestion. Cheap media can consume attention. Cheap biological design can create safety risks. A post-scarcity society can still damage its climate, surveil its citizens, monopolize its platforms, and concentrate political power.

Paradise can be badly governed.

That is why the title of this book is not *Utopia Found*.

Paradise is an old human word for a place of enoughness, but our myths have always put walls around it. A garden can be abundant and still bounded. It can contain beauty and prohibition, provision and danger, freedom and consequence.

The useful claim is not that 2030 abolished economics.

It is that economics changed categories.

The central political problem of industrial society had been how to distribute access to necessities in a world where producing them required enormous quantities of human labor, capital, land, energy, and coordination. As those costs fell, a new problem emerged: why continue exposing people to deprivation when the deprivation costs more to administer than the baseline costs to provide?

This is the moment the word finally became accurate enough to keep.

Post-scarcity meant we were after mandatory scarcity.

Everything else remained to be fought over.
