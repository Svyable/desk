# The Empty Truck Was the Enemy

The truck was already going there.

That was the insult.

It had burned fuel, occupied road space, paid a driver, depreciated tires, crossed a bridge, passed through a gate, and arrived in the right city. Half its capacity was empty.

Industrial civilization contained enormous quantities of this kind of waste.

A vehicle returned without a load. A shipping container waited in the wrong place. A warehouse held months of one part and none of another. A factory stopped because a component worth fifty dollars had not cleared customs. A restaurant threw away food while another buyer ordered the same ingredient from farther away. A hospital stocked an item in one building while another building placed an emergency order. A repair technician drove across town because nobody knew the nearest compatible spare part was three blocks away.

The scarcity was not always the absence of goods.

Sometimes it was the absence of matching.

This became obvious during the supply-chain shocks of the early 2020s. Ports backed up. Shipping schedules broke. Semiconductor shortages idled factories full of every component except the one chip they needed. Companies that had spent years optimizing inventories discovered that a beautifully lean system can be starved by a cheap part on the other side of the planet.

The pandemic did not invent logistics fragility.

It made the hidden buffers visible.

By 2023, the World Bank's Logistics Performance Index was explicitly emphasizing reliability alongside speed. Its survey of 139 countries treated infrastructure, logistics-service quality, border processes, tracking, timeliness, and the ability to establish dependable international connections as separate elements of performance. The distinction between fastest and most reliable had become impossible to ignore.

Post-scarcity logistics inherited that lesson.

The cheapest supply chain in an average month is not necessarily the cheapest way to guarantee access.

That sentence sounds like a retreat from efficiency.

It was a better definition of efficiency.

A grocery network that minimizes inventory until every disruption empties the shelf is not efficient from the household's perspective. A medical supply chain that saves carrying cost but cannot provide a common drug during a shock has shifted cost into the patient. A factory that saves money by depending on one supplier and then closes for six weeks has mispriced dependence.

The abundance economy started measuring idle capacity and redundancy against failure cost rather than treating them as waste by definition.

But it also discovered enormous amounts of genuine waste.

Empty miles were one example.

Freight networks have always faced an imbalance problem. Goods flow unevenly. A truck may carry furniture from one region and find no equivalent load for the return. Containers accumulate where imports exceed exports. Seasonal agricultural demand moves equipment toward a crop and away again. Drivers have hours-of-service limits. Customers have delivery windows. Trailers have different capabilities. Hazardous goods require restrictions. Weight, volume, temperature, insurance, and route all matter.

Matching freight is combinatorially messy.

Humans did it for decades through dispatchers, brokers, phone calls, contracts, relationships, spreadsheets, and increasingly specialized software. Better data and machine intelligence did not eliminate the imbalance.

They found more of the compatible pairs.

A shipment that could leave Tuesday instead of Monday might share capacity. A return route could be offered dynamically. A warehouse could consolidate two partial loads. A vehicle could stop at a regional node instead of returning empty. Small shippers could access capacity that had been economically visible only to large networks.

The truck did not become cheaper because its engine changed.

Its useful fraction increased.

This is one of the least glamorous forms of abundance and one of the most scalable.

You do not need to manufacture another asset when the existing asset is idle.

The same logic applied inside warehouses. The problem was not merely storing inventory. It was deciding where each item belonged, how much to carry, what to replenish, which order to pick first, how to route a worker or robot through the building, and how to avoid holding expensive safety stock everywhere just because the system could not see what was happening elsewhere.

Inventory is a physical hedge against ignorance.

That is not an insult to inventory.

Sometimes the hedge is wise. A hospital should not rely on a same-day delivery for every critical item. A grid should not assume the transformer factory can ship tomorrow. A food system needs reserves. A country may rationally carry strategic materials with terrible annual utilization because the cost of running out is catastrophic.

The post-scarcity system did not seek zero inventory.

It sought intentional inventory.

The cheapest warehouse is not no warehouse.

It is a warehouse holding the things whose absence is more expensive than their presence.

Artificial intelligence helped because demand forecasting became more granular and because agents could observe more of the network. A retailer knew what was selling. A distributor knew what was in transit. A manufacturer knew which machines were down. A port knew which vessels were delayed. Weather services knew storms were moving. Public systems knew where emergency demand was rising.

The technical challenge was not that any one organization lacked software.

It was that organizations had no reason to expose enough information to coordinate without exposing commercially sensitive data.

Interoperability therefore became one of the quiet institutions of abundance.

A shipment did not need to publish every customer detail to announce that compatible capacity existed. A factory did not need to expose its full production schedule to declare a component shortage. A hospital did not need to share patient data to indicate that a category of supply was running low.

Standards made partial cooperation possible.

This was another case where abundant intelligence without institutional design would have stopped at the firm boundary.

A brilliant routing model cannot route a truck whose capacity it cannot see.

The same problem appeared at borders.

International trade had long combined physical movement with documentary movement. A container can cross an ocean faster than the paperwork moves between customs brokers, carriers, terminals, banks, insurers, regulators, importers, exporters, and inspection agencies. A missing code can hold a physical good in place.

Digital systems attacked this slowly because border processes are legal processes. The paperwork is not arbitrary in the sense that countries need to know what crosses their borders, collect duties, enforce safety rules, detect prohibited goods, and manage risk. But much of the administrative cost came from repeatedly representing the same shipment in different systems.

Machine-readable documents, interoperable customs data, better risk scoring, pre-clearance, and automated translation reduced delay without requiring states to abandon control.

The best inspection is the one focused on the shipment that actually deserves inspection.

Risk-based systems had always aimed at this. Better data improved the targeting.

The danger was obvious here too.

An automated border can become an opaque border.

A system that falsely flags a company, country, person, or product can create delay at scale. Post-scarcity logistics therefore inherited the same appeal requirement as intelligence services. Automated routing of ordinary cases made trade cheaper only if unusual cases had a credible path to human review.

Cheap first decision.

Credible second decision.

The physical network changed at the same time.

Autonomous driving developed unevenly. The public had been encouraged to imagine a single threshold where cars everywhere suddenly no longer needed drivers. Logistics found value in narrower domains first.

Ports. Mines. Warehouses. Distribution yards. Dedicated lanes. Predictable highway segments. Repeated local routes. Night delivery. Low-speed vehicles on controlled campuses.

A truck that can drive autonomously for one part of a route still changes economics if the human driver's time is reserved for the complicated part. Remote assistance can supervise fleets. Vehicles can operate longer while still obeying maintenance and safety constraints. Delivery windows can shift away from congestion.

Again, partial autonomy mattered more than rhetorical autonomy.

The cost of moving a box fell without requiring a machine to solve every street in every city.

This helped the baseline bundle because logistics sits underneath every essential good.

Food is a logistics product after harvest.

Medicine is a logistics product after manufacture.

Housing construction is a logistics product before assembly.

Energy infrastructure is a logistics product before connection.

Manufacturing is a logistics product between every production step.

A cheap object stranded in the wrong place is expensive.

The late 2020s also revived a technology older than the internet: the local depot.

For years, e-commerce had pushed toward home delivery for everything. Convenience was real, but the economics of driving a separate vehicle to every door could be terrible for low-value goods, especially where addresses were dispersed or labor expensive. Shared pickup points, neighborhood lockers, micro-depots, and scheduled delivery windows returned as complements to autonomous and electric fleets.

Abundance does not always mean more individualized service.

Sometimes it means a shared interface good enough that individual service is unnecessary.

This became important in lower-density regions. The post-scarcity mobility promise could not assume a subway station on every corner. Rural households needed access to groceries, medicine, services, schools, and work across long distances. Autonomous shuttles, scheduled shared vehicles, delivery consolidation, remote services, and regional hubs reduced the amount of private vehicle ownership required without pretending geography had disappeared.

Private cars remained popular.

The system did not abolish them.

It removed ownership as a precondition for participation.

That distinction mattered because the full cost of a car is not fuel. It is purchase price, financing, depreciation, insurance, maintenance, parking, taxes, repairs, and the risk that one breakdown interrupts work. For many households, mobility scarcity was capital scarcity.

Shared autonomous fleets could convert some of that capital cost into a service cost.

The economics depended on utilization.

A privately owned vehicle spends much of its life parked. A shared vehicle working many hours per day can spread fixed costs across more passenger-kilometers. Electrification lowers operating and maintenance costs in many uses. Automation can reduce the labor cost where it is safe and legal. Routing reduces deadhead miles. Small vehicles can match small trips.

But streets remained scarce.

This became the great correction to autonomous-vehicle utopianism.

A road can carry only so many vehicles. If cheap autonomous rides cause more empty repositioning and longer travel, congestion can worsen even while each ride gets cheaper. A city full of individually summoned vehicles can become an abundant service trapped inside scarce pavement.

The post-scarcity cities therefore priced the bottleneck.

Curb space. Peak road capacity. Congested zones. Parking in high-demand locations.

This was politically difficult because people had grown accustomed to treating some of these scarce assets as free. But the principle was consistent with the rest of the transition.

Guarantee mobility.

Price congestion.

A universal baseline might include a monthly amount of local transit and shared mobility, or guarantee that essential destinations could be reached within a reasonable time. It did not promise unlimited peak-hour road space in the center of a city.

The pricing signal improved the service because agents could respond to it automatically. A traveler could leave fifteen minutes earlier, share a ride, take rail, use a bike, walk, or pay the premium for urgency. The system could route freight at night and reserve expensive peak capacity for loads whose time genuinely mattered.

Time became a logistics commodity.

This is another subtle form of abundance. Not every unit needs to arrive instantly.

The expectation of instant delivery can make a supply chain more expensive by removing batching, consolidation, and scheduling options. When agents knew which requests were urgent and which were merely convenient, networks could spend scarce speed where it created value.

A medication required today is different from detergent required this week.

Industrial logistics had always known this through service levels. Consumer logistics learned it later because one-click interfaces hid the system cost.

The 2030 baseline exposed the trade gently. Faster delivery remained available. Flexible delivery cost less. Essential urgent goods received priority. The system stopped pretending every package had the same temporal value.

This reduced emissions and congestion alongside cost.

Not every efficiency gain required sacrifice.

Some required information.

The deepest logistics innovation was therefore not autonomous trucking, drones, warehouse robots, or digital customs, although all contributed.

It was the collapse of uncertainty between organizations.

When a system knows where the good is, where demand is forming, what capacity is moving, what alternatives exist, and which deadline is real, it can operate with less desperate slack in the wrong places and more deliberate slack in the right ones.

This is not perfect foresight.

A port still closes. A war still blocks a route. A storm still destroys a bridge. A cyberattack can blind a network. A model can be wrong. A factory can burn. A government can impose export controls overnight.

Resilience therefore required the information system to know what it did not control.

Routes had alternatives. Critical goods had reserve inventories. Essential data could be accessed offline. Local nodes could operate when central services failed. Fleets maintained manual or degraded modes. Public agencies rehearsed disruptions instead of assuming optimization software was a substitute for contingency planning.

This made the logistics system slightly more expensive on calm days.

It made abundance believable on bad days.

Post-scarcity is easy in an average.

The real test is interruption.

A wealthy society can appear abundant until the storm, strike, epidemic, war, software outage, bridge collapse, or bank failure reveals how many essentials depended on one narrow path.

The 2030 systems were not unbreakable.

They were harder to strand.

For the household, the result arrived as an absence.

The grocery store was rarely missing the ordinary staple. The pharmacy could route a prescription to another location before the customer made the trip. A replacement part could be drawn from a regional pool. Public transit, shared mobility, and delivery networks could substitute for a broken private car. Emergency supplies moved through channels already used every day.

The box appeared.

The medicine arrived.

The bus came.

Nobody wrote a poem about load matching.

Post-scarcity depended on it anyway.

For a long time, humanity had responded to shortage by making more things.

The logistics revolution revealed how often the thing already existed.

It was simply empty, idle, delayed, duplicated, misrouted, trapped behind paperwork, or waiting where nobody needed it.

The enemy had been the empty truck all along.
