# The Dividend

The machines could produce more.

That did not answer the oldest political question in the book.

Who owned the machines?

For a few years, the post-scarcity debate tried to avoid this question. It was easier to talk about productivity, cost curves, automation, and universal services than ownership. If electricity became cheap, intelligence became cheap, factories became more productive, and logistics wasted less capacity, perhaps the gains would simply spread through lower prices.

Some did.

Competition can distribute technological progress ruthlessly. When many firms can produce the same thing, productivity gains often become lower prices. A company invents a cheaper process. Competitors imitate it. Margins compress. Consumers capture part of the surplus.

This mechanism is powerful.

It is not universal.

A scarce bottleneck can capture the savings produced everywhere around it. Cheap construction does not guarantee cheap housing if land is constrained. Cheap generation does not guarantee cheap delivered electricity if the grid connection is scarce. Cheap intelligence does not guarantee cheap access if one provider owns the only effective interface. Automated factories can make goods cheaply while the ownership of those factories remains concentrated.

Post-scarcity production and post-scarcity distribution are different achievements.

The late 2020s learned this through artificial intelligence first. The economic logic of AI contained a tension that became impossible to ignore. Some services had low marginal cost for users while the frontier systems behind them demanded enormous fixed investment in chips, data centers, power, research, networking, and engineering. The output could become abundant while the capital required to create the output became more concentrated.

Cheap cognition did not imply distributed ownership of cognitive infrastructure.

The same pattern appeared in robotics and manufacturing. A robot could lower the cost of labor embedded in a product, but the return on the robot flowed initially to whoever owned it. An automated factory could produce more output with fewer labor hours and still increase the gap between those who owned productive capital and those who depended on wages.

This was the political fork.

One path led to an economy where essentials became cheaper but wealth and power concentrated around the owners of automation. People might be materially better off and politically weaker at the same time.

The other path added ownership to the abundance stack.

The precedent was old.

In 1976, Alaska voters amended the state constitution to establish a permanent fund from a portion of oil revenues. The fund received its first deposit the next year. The state later created a corporation to manage the investments, and in 1982 Alaska distributed its first equal dividend checks to eligible residents. The program survived for decades in varying amounts. In 2025, the dividend was one thousand dollars per eligible recipient.

Alaska did not become post-scarcity.

It demonstrated a durable idea: a community can treat some returns from a scarce productive asset as belonging partly to the community over time.

The design also offered useful evidence against one common fear. Research by Damon Jones and Ioana Marinescu on the Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend found no significant decline in aggregate employment after the annual transfer, while part-time work increased. The study did not prove that any size of universal transfer anywhere would have the same effect. Alaska's dividend was modest, and the institutional context was particular. It did show that an unconditional, permanent, broad cash payment does not mechanically cause a population to stop working.

Norway offered a different precedent.

Its Government Pension Fund Global converted petroleum revenues into a diversified portfolio of global assets rather than distributing the principal directly. By the end of June 2026, the fund was worth more than twenty-two trillion Norwegian kroner and held ownership interests in roughly seven thousand companies. More than half of the fund's value had come from investment returns rather than net government inflows.

Again, Norway was not a blueprint for automated abundance.

It demonstrated another principle.

A society can convert a temporary natural-resource rent into long-duration financial ownership.

The late 2020s adapted these ideas to a new kind of rent.

Automation did not create one obvious royalty stream like oil pulled from public land. The productive assets were dispersed across private companies, software systems, grids, robots, factories, data centers, land, intellectual property, and financial markets. Trying to declare all automated capital a public resource would have required an ownership revolution far larger than the post-scarcity coalition could sustain and might have damaged the investment that made abundance possible.

The durable approach was more plural.

Public capital funds owned ordinary diversified assets.

Pension systems held productive capital on behalf of workers.

Cities retained land in some development projects rather than selling it permanently.

Governments auctioned or charged for genuinely scarce public assets such as spectrum, extraction rights, congestion capacity, and some forms of land access.

Public investments in infrastructure sometimes received equity, warrants, revenue shares, or repayment rights rather than acting only as grants.

Natural-resource revenues flowed partly into long-lived funds.

Taxes collected part of extraordinary rents without trying to tax every increment of ordinary productive investment away.

The point was not to nationalize the economy.

It was to make sure the public owned something that compounded.

This became more important as labor's share of some production processes fell.

The twentieth-century welfare state was financed heavily from labor income because labor income was where a large share of economic value reached households. Workers earned wages. Governments taxed wages and consumption. Employers paid payroll taxes. Social insurance linked benefits to employment history.

An economy with more output produced by capital complicates that architecture.

If a factory doubles production while reducing direct labor, payroll tax may become a worse proxy for the economy's capacity to fund social guarantees. If software creates large consumer surplus while charging low prices, consumption tax may understate the value created. If capital gains accumulate without realization, annual income measures may miss part of the growth in economic power.

The tax system can adapt.

It can tax profits, wealth transfers, land, consumption, resource rents, property, financial income, carbon, and other bases. But taxation alone preserves a one-way relationship: the private economy earns; the state claims a portion afterward.

Shared capital ownership creates another relationship.

The public earns because it owns.

This distinction was psychologically important.

People understood a dividend differently from a benefit.

A benefit can imply need. A dividend implies ownership.

The difference is partly language, but language shapes political durability. Programs for the poor can be politically isolated because people who do not receive them may view themselves only as payers. A broadly shared asset creates a constituency across income groups, even when taxation or service use remains progressive.

Alaska had shown this in a crude form. The check arrived because the recipient was a resident, not because the recipient had proved poverty.

The post-scarcity systems used the ownership idea more often to finance services than to mail large checks.

This was another break with the universal-basic-income imagination.

If the purpose of the social floor is to make essential access reliable, handing out cash and leaving every underlying market unchanged can be expensive. Housing supply can absorb the cash through rent. Health-care prices can absorb it. Energy shocks can absorb it. Insurance can absorb it.

A dividend spent partly through universal service infrastructure attacks the cost and the ownership problem together.

Imagine a public capital fund earning returns from a diversified portfolio. Some of those returns finance universal intelligence capacity. Cheap intelligence lowers administrative costs in other services. Some returns finance housing land trusts or energy infrastructure. Those assets lower household expenses directly. Lower household expenses mean the cash component of the floor can remain smaller.

The return becomes a cost reducer before it becomes spending money.

This is not inherently better than cash.

Cash preserves choice. Services can become paternalistic. Public procurement can be captured. Governments can choose badly. A household may value an extra dollar more than a service a bureaucracy selected on its behalf.

The 2030 settlement therefore kept both.

Guarantee high-leverage essentials where universal provision or capacity purchasing has strong economics.

Use portable cash or accounts where preferences vary and markets work.

Let people buy above the floor privately.

The exact mix remained political.

What changed was the source of entitlement.

The baseline was not charity funded by the productive.

It was a claim held by every citizen or resident, depending on the jurisdiction, on part of a productive system the society had helped make possible.

That claim was not based on the idea that government invented every technology. It did not.

Private entrepreneurs, investors, workers, engineers, scientists, managers, and customers created enormous amounts of the value. But private production depended on a substrate no firm created alone: law, currency, education, basic research, infrastructure, public order, property rights, standards, courts, communications networks, generations of accumulated knowledge, and markets full of people whose capabilities were developed socially.

The abundance dividend treated that substrate as economically real.

This argument can be abused.

If everything depends partly on society, a government can use the claim to justify ownership of everything. That would destroy the distinction between a public foundation and a private enterprise.

The durable settlement therefore looked for specific public claims rather than metaphysical ones.

Public land creates a public land rent.

A mineral extracted under public ownership creates a resource rent.

Spectrum licenses create value from a publicly allocated scarce band.

A public investment with upside terms creates a contractual claim.

A sovereign wealth fund purchasing shares creates ordinary ownership.

A tax enacted through democratic law creates a defined fiscal claim.

The public did not need to declare itself the hidden owner of every invention.

It needed to stop giving away every compounding asset.

This became particularly important in infrastructure.

Governments had historically spent large sums building roads, grids, ports, research capacity, broadband, and transit, then allowed the increase in adjacent land or company value to accrue mostly elsewhere. Sometimes that was exactly the purpose: public infrastructure creates spillovers. But if the public never captures any return from the value it creates, every new generation must finance abundance almost entirely from current taxes.

Land-value capture, public equity, long-term leases, revenue-sharing, and revolving funds became ways to recycle some gains into the next layer of infrastructure.

The post-scarcity state learned to compound.

It also learned why public ownership can fail.

Political capital is tempting capital.

A large public fund can become a target for every favored project, regional demand, campaign promise, ideological investment, or emergency unrelated to the fund's long-term purpose. A government can force investments into politically connected companies. It can raid principal to solve a temporary budget problem. It can underprice risk because losses arrive after the election.

The institutions that survived separated ownership from day-to-day politics.

Independent fiduciary governance.

Transparent mandates.

Public reporting.

Diversified portfolios.

Rules limiting withdrawals.

Competitive external benchmarks.

Audits.

Conflict-of-interest restrictions.

The details were not decoration.

They were the difference between a public asset and a political bank account.

Norway's fund had spent decades building this kind of institutional distance. Alaska's permanent-fund structure also reflected a desire to preserve resource wealth beyond current political consumption. Neither system was immune to political argument. That argument was part of their legitimacy.

The post-scarcity funds copied the principle, not the exact governance.

The dividend chapter also forced a confrontation with inheritance.

If productive capital becomes more important relative to labor, being born into ownership becomes more important unless society creates other routes into ownership. A person can work hard and still fall behind a compounding asset base inherited by someone else.

Universal services protect the floor.

They do not by themselves broaden the upside.

Some jurisdictions therefore created automatic capital accounts alongside the service floor. Children received small diversified endowments. Workers accumulated portable ownership through retirement and profit-sharing systems. Public funds distributed periodic dividends. Cooperative and employee ownership gained financing support where it made business sense.

None of these mechanisms equalized wealth.

They did something more modest.

They made ownership less binary.

A person did not have to choose between being a wage earner with no productive assets and being a capitalist in the cartoon sense. She could be both, as pension savers already were.

This softened one of the ideological problems of automation.

When a machine replaces a task, a worker who owns no part of the machine sees only competition. A society with broad capital ownership lets some of the return come back through a different channel.

The channel matters because automation's gains are not synchronized with its losses.

A company can automate today. A displaced worker may need months or years to retrain. Consumer prices may fall gradually. New jobs may emerge in another region. Capital gains can arrive immediately to shareholders. Telling the worker that society will benefit in aggregate eventually is not an adequate transition policy.

The baseline protected the downside.

The dividend widened the upside.

Neither made disruption pleasant.

They made it survivable without pretending that every old job should be preserved.

This is where the abundance coalition separated from older politics of employment.

A job is a bundle.

It provides income, sometimes health coverage, status, routine, social connection, skill development, identity, and a claim on future advancement. When automation removes the job, replacing the income alone does not replace the bundle.

The dividend was never meant to.

Its job was narrower: ensure that productive abundance did not become a private river flowing past a public drought.

The next chapter deals with everything the dividend could not solve.

What people did with their time.

What happened to identity.

Why many continued working even after survival stopped demanding it.

Why some stopped.

Why the worst jobs became more expensive.

And why the end of material threat did not produce the end of ambition.

But ownership had to come first.

Without it, post-scarcity could have become an extraordinary historical irony.

Humanity finally learned to make enough for everyone.

Then a small number of people owned the enough.
