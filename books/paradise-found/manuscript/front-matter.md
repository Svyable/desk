# Paradise Found

*How Humanity Achieved Post-Scarcity in 2030*

**Sven Hardy Benson**

This is a book about an event that has not happened yet.

That sentence matters more than the subtitle.

I am writing in September 2026. The year 2030 is still ahead of us. The world described in these pages is therefore not reportage from the future and not a claim that history has already selected one path. It is a future history: an argument written in the past tense so that we can inspect, with unusual discipline, what would have to become true for humanity to say four years from now that material scarcity had ceased to be the organizing fact of ordinary life.

The evidence up to the date of publication is real and sourced in the research trail. The later events are scenario. When the book says that a government changed a rule in 2028, that a new kind of factory became commonplace in 2029, or that households began behaving differently in 2030, the reader should hear an implied phrase: *in the world this book is testing*.

Why write it this way?

Because most arguments about post-scarcity are ruined before they begin by the word *post*.

It sounds absolute. No shortage. No prices. No queues. No unequal access to beautiful land, talented surgeons, playoff tickets, old paintings, human attention, romantic love, or a table at the restaurant everyone suddenly wants to visit. It invites either science fiction or ridicule.

That is not the claim here.

Humanity has always lived with many scarcities at once. Some are physical. Some are institutional. Some are positional. Some exist because there is not enough of a thing. Others exist because we have arranged access badly, because coordination is expensive, because information is missing, because rules prevent supply from responding, because a service requires scarce expert time, or because status depends on not everyone being able to have the same thing.

A civilization can become post-scarcity in the part that matters most without abolishing all of them.

The threshold in this book is narrower and, I think, more interesting: involuntary material scarcity becomes optional when a society can reliably provide every person with a decent baseline of food, shelter, energy, connectivity, basic mobility, education, essential health care, and useful intelligence without requiring that person to win a labor-market contest first.

That is not infinite wealth. It is the end of survival as a bargaining chip.

The surprising possibility is that the decisive event may not look like a technological singularity. It may look like a price event.

For two centuries, industrial civilization got better at making things while preserving a deep dependence on human labor income. Machines raised output, but most people still needed wages to purchase access to the output. The arrangement worked well enough to become morally invisible. We came to treat employment as the natural bridge between a human being and food, rent, medicine, electricity, and participation in society.

Artificial intelligence now puts pressure on that bridge from both sides. It can reduce the amount of human labor required to produce cognitive services while also accelerating the design, operation, and coordination of physical systems. Solar power and storage are changing the economics of energy. Robotics is improving the economics of physical work. Flexible manufacturing is shortening the distance between an idea and a finished object. Digital logistics is attacking empty miles, idle inventory, and administrative delay. Biology is becoming more programmable. Medicine is gaining tools that can move expertise upstream, toward earlier detection and continuous guidance. None of these systems is sufficient by itself.

Together, they suggest a stranger possibility.

The cost of maintaining a decent human life may fall faster than the value-producing capacity of automated capital rises.

If those curves cross, post-scarcity does not require that every person own a robot factory. It requires an institution capable of turning a small share of society's automated surplus into universal access to the baseline bundle.

The hard part may therefore be neither invention nor redistribution in the twentieth-century sense. It may be design: making abundance legible, financeable, contestable, resilient, and boring enough to trust.

That is the story this book tells.

It begins on the morning scarcity stopped being mandatory.
