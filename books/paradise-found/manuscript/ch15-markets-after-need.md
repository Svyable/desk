# Markets After Need

Markets did not disappear after post-scarcity.

They became easier to see.

Before 2030, a supermarket and an emergency room were both markets in some countries, yet the meaning of price in each place was very different. A person could choose strawberries instead of peaches. She could not meaningfully choose appendicitis tomorrow instead of appendicitis today. A household could delay buying a television. It could not delay shelter indefinitely without leaving ordinary social life.

Industrial economies had used prices across categories with radically different degrees of choice.

Post-scarcity drew a boundary.

Below the boundary, the baseline guaranteed access.

Above it, markets became more important, not less.

This surprised people who had imagined abundance as the end of capitalism. If everyone could obtain enough food, enough energy, a decent home, useful intelligence, essential health care, education, connectivity, and mobility, why would markets still matter?

Because enough does not tell you what people want.

A universal food floor cannot predict whether you want Korean noodles or tomatoes from the market down the street. A housing guarantee cannot know whether you prefer a small apartment downtown, a larger house farther out, a cooperative building, or a quiet room over a noisy one. Universal intelligence access cannot know which premium tool is worth paying for. Essential medicine does not decide how much you value an elective procedure, concierge service, a specific physician's time, or an unproven therapy.

Preference remained infinite enough to keep markets busy.

The baseline actually made many markets cleaner because it removed some transactions made under duress.

A person buying bread after three days without food is not expressing the same kind of preference as a person deciding between bakeries. A tenant accepting dangerous housing because the alternative is homelessness does not validate the quality of the housing. A worker taking a job because losing health insurance would endanger a child is not participating in a frictionless labor market.

Economists had always known that markets depend on initial endowments, bargaining power, information, competition, and alternatives.

Post-scarcity strengthened the alternative.

This mattered especially for consumer choice.

When the baseline covers the essential version, the premium market must explain what the premium is for.

Sometimes the answer was obvious.

Location.

Speed.

Beauty.

Craftsmanship.

Privacy.

Personal attention.

Customization.

Novelty.

Prestige.

Convenience.

Risk.

Exclusivity.

A luxury economy remained because human beings remained capable of wanting things that were not necessary.

The abundance transition did not shame this.

It made luxury more honest.

A large home in an unusually desirable place could be expensive without implying that ordinary shelter must be. A reservation at a famous restaurant could be difficult to obtain without implying that healthy food must be. A human tutor with extraordinary expertise could charge a premium even while competent machine tutoring remained universally available.

The market moved upward into difference.

This created a peculiar form of deflation in the ordinary and inflation in the singular.

Mass-produced cognitive output became cheaper. A live hour with a particular person became more expensive.

Manufactured furniture became cheaper. Furniture made by a known craftsperson could become more expensive.

Routine travel became cheaper. Access to fragile, beautiful, uncrowded places became more expensive because more people could afford to visit them.

Basic entertainment became effectively unlimited. A front-row seat did not.

The economy began separating reproducible goods from rivalrous experiences.

The distinction had always existed.

Digital technology made it harder to ignore.

A song file can be copied. A concert seat cannot. A photograph of a painting can be copied. The painting cannot. A model can generate a thousand plausible designs. The architect's personal responsibility for one building is finite. A person can watch a famous athlete from anywhere. Only a limited number of people can sit courtside.

Post-scarcity drove prices toward the part that would not copy.

This made markets less about units and more about claims.

Who gets this location?

Who gets this time slot?

Who receives this person's attention?

Who can use this scarce ecological resource?

Who may consume road space at 8:30 a.m.?

Who gets the original?

Who gets the low-risk seat on the rocket?

These are allocation problems.

Markets solve some of them well.

Not all.

A price can allocate a scarce restaurant table without creating a moral crisis. A price is a poor sole allocator for organ transplants. Peak road pricing can reduce congestion and improve travel times, but a city may still need protections so essential workers are not priced out of movement. High water prices can conserve a depleted resource, but a baseline household quantity should not become unaffordable.

The mature post-scarcity economy stopped asking whether markets were good or bad in the abstract.

It asked which scarcity the market was pricing.

That question became the core of economic design.

If the scarcity is real, a price can carry useful information.

If the scarcity is artificial, a high price may be a symptom of restriction.

If the good is essential, the system may need a universal floor underneath the price.

If the market is concentrated, the price may reflect power rather than cost.

If the transaction creates large externalities, the private price may omit social cost.

If the service has near-zero marginal cost, metering every use may reduce welfare more than it improves allocation.

This sounds like standard economics because it is.

Post-scarcity did not invent a new discipline.

It made old distinctions politically central.

Housing was the obvious example. A rising rent can mean a neighborhood became more desirable. It can also mean the city refused to let supply respond. Treating both cases as the same market signal protects the bottleneck from scrutiny.

Digital platforms created a different problem.

A service could have a money price of zero while users paid through data, attention, lock-in, advertising exposure, reduced privacy, or dependence. The OECD's 2026 work on competition and consumer policy in digital markets emphasized that traditional analysis based on price and output can miss harms to autonomy, choice, privacy, and trust.

Post-scarcity made this problem larger because more baseline services had digital interfaces.

A free service can be expensive if exit is difficult.

This is why the abundance stack treated portability and interoperability as part of market design. Competition cannot discipline a provider if switching requires abandoning history, identity, social relationships, or essential records.

The ability to leave became a price mechanism of its own.

A provider facing credible exit has to compete.

A provider with captive users can turn abundance into rent.

The same logic applied to public provision. A universal service can become complacent when users have no alternative and no appeal. The answer was not to privatize everything. It was to preserve contestability where possible.

Some services supported multiple providers paid under common rules.

Some had public options competing with private ones.

Some were natural monopolies regulated for performance and price.

Some were public systems with democratic rather than consumer exit.

Different market structures fit different physical realities.

Electric distribution is not sneakers.

The mistake of late twentieth-century policy had sometimes been to force unlike sectors into the same institutional model because competition was presumed to work everywhere in the same way. The opposite mistake was to treat every essential service as a reason for monopoly.

Post-scarcity became pragmatic about structure.

Where duplication was cheap, competition was useful.

Where duplication was wasteful, regulation and public accountability mattered more.

Where interoperability could create competition on top of shared infrastructure, the infrastructure became common and the services differentiated above it.

Telecommunications provided an old example. Roads provided another. Payment systems, electric grids, data standards, and AI capacity increasingly followed variations of the same pattern.

This layered market design helped separate infrastructure rents from service innovation.

A company could compete to build the best tool without controlling the only road the tool traveled on.

The distinction mattered because abundance can create winner-take-most dynamics.

Low marginal costs favor scale. Data can improve products. Networks become more useful when more participants join. Capital-intensive systems can be cheaper per unit at large size. These forces can produce concentration even while consumer prices fall.

A monopoly can be materially generous for a while.

That does not remove the power problem.

A dominant provider can change terms after competitors disappear. It can decide who participates. It can bundle unrelated services. It can favor its own products. It can purchase challengers. It can turn a standard into a private gate.

The 2030 economy treated competition as a resilience policy as much as a price policy.

Multiple providers created alternatives when one failed.

Open standards lowered the cost of entry.

Portability reduced the penalty for switching.

Merger policy considered the future option value of competitors, not only immediate price effects.

This was especially important in intelligence markets, where rapid technical progress could make today's weak competitor tomorrow's substitute.

The economy also became more comfortable with public procurement as a market maker.

A government guaranteeing baseline demand did not have to operate every supplier. It could define an outcome, publish standards, purchase capacity, and allow firms to compete for the contract.

This happened in food, housing, connectivity, mobility, health, and intelligence.

The model had risks.

Large procurement markets can become oligopolies. Firms become expert at selling to governments rather than serving users. Specifications can freeze innovation. Political favoritism can determine winners. Contractors can underbid and renegotiate later. Government buyers can demand bespoke features that destroy standardization.

The better systems learned to purchase interfaces and outcomes rather than custom kingdoms.

A connectivity provider had to meet speed, reliability, coverage, portability, and privacy requirements. It did not need to use one prescribed technical stack.

A housing provider had to meet safety, stability, access, and cost standards. It could build using different systems.

An intelligence service had to meet auditability, availability, migration, security, and capability requirements while routing among different underlying models.

This preserved room for competition above the guarantee.

The baseline itself created private markets that had not existed before.

A person guaranteed modest housing could pay for more space.

A universal local mobility account could be topped up for premium travel.

A basic intelligence service could be supplemented with specialized compute.

A public health system could coexist with private amenities as long as the private tier did not drain the public tier of all scarce capacity.

That last caveat became a recurring political problem.

When a scarce professional can serve either the baseline or a luxury market, high private prices can pull capacity away from universal access. A post-scarcity system cannot guarantee a service by declaring it universal if every clinician, teacher, or engineer can earn far more serving premium users.

The answer varied.

Training capacity expanded.

Automation reduced routine labor.

Some professionals accepted public-service obligations in exchange for subsidized education or licensing benefits.

Some systems limited the amount of scarce public capacity that could be sold privately.

Some simply paid more.

Again, the real scarcity had to be priced.

A mature abundance system is not cheap because it refuses to pay scarce people.

It is cheap because it avoids wasting them on work that does not require scarcity.

Markets became more dynamic in other ways.

Starting a small business became easier when founders did not need to purchase every kind of expertise, when computing and intelligence were inexpensive, when health coverage was portable, and when personal ruin was less likely to follow failure.

This lowered the downside of entrepreneurship.

One of the old arguments against a strong social floor had been that it would make people less enterprising. The post-scarcity period often produced the opposite in sectors where the baseline acted as risk insurance.

A person could leave an employer to start a company without losing health care.

A failed founder could remain housed.

A creator could spend six months testing a project without immediately converting every hour into wages.

A small manufacturer could rent robotic capacity instead of buying a factory.

A local service business could access finance, legal guidance, software, marketing, and scheduling tools once reserved for larger firms.

The social floor lowered the minimum viable courage.

It did not remove risk.

Investors still lost money. Businesses still failed. Reputation still mattered. Entrepreneurs could spend years on ideas nobody wanted. The difference was that private risk no longer required the public system to threaten destitution as proof of seriousness.

This changed bankruptcy culture too.

A company could fail without taking a worker's medical access with it. A household losing investment capital did not automatically lose the baseline. Business entities became easier to let die because their social functions were less tightly bundled with them.

Creative destruction became less literally destructive to the person.

This made markets politically easier to tolerate.

That may be one of the most important institutional achievements of post-scarcity.

Capitalism's dynamism comes partly from allowing resources to move away from failed arrangements. Its politics become poisonous when the failure of an arrangement destroys the people attached to it.

The baseline separated the two.

A bad company could close.

A bad job could vanish.

A failed technology could lose financing.

The person remained inside society.

This did not produce ideological peace.

Critics on the left argued that the system preserved too much private power above the floor. Critics on the right argued that universal services distorted prices and reduced responsibility. Libertarians objected to taxes and public ownership. Socialists objected to inherited wealth and concentrated capital. Environmentalists worried that cheap production would accelerate consumption. Communities resisted development. Firms lobbied against competition. Consumers demanded cheap prices and high wages at the same time.

Markets after need remained politics.

They simply operated under a different moral constraint.

Nobody should have to lose the essentials for a market to discover a price.

Once that constraint was accepted, markets became easier to defend where they worked.

You could allow prices to allocate beachfront property because nobody's survival depended on beachfront property.

You could let a fashionable restaurant charge what demand supported because nutrition was not riding on the reservation.

You could let a premium intelligence provider charge for specialized capability because useful baseline intelligence remained available.

You could let people compete fiercely for status, wealth, and extraordinary experiences because the loser did not lose ordinary membership in civilization.

The post-scarcity economy did not end competition.

It civilized the stakes.

This was the paradox the twentieth century had struggled to resolve.

A market works best when participants can walk away.

The social floor made walking away cheaper.

Paradise did not replace the market.

It gave more people enough ground beneath their feet to enter one voluntarily.
