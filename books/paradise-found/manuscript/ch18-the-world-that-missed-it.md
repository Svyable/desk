# The World That Missed It

Post-scarcity arrived in 2030.

Humanity did not arrive together.

The title of this book cheats in the way all historical titles cheat. We say humanity reached the Moon even though almost every human remained on Earth. We say the world entered the internet age while billions remained offline for years afterward. We say electricity transformed civilization while hundreds of millions still lacked a connection more than a century after commercial grids began spreading.

Civilizations cross thresholds unevenly.

The threshold can still be real.

This became the most uncomfortable qualification in the post-scarcity story because the places proving abundance was possible were often the places already richest in capital, institutions, infrastructure, and technical capability.

In 2026, before the future history in this book begins, the inequality was already stark.

The International Telecommunication Union reported that almost three-quarters of the world's population was online in 2025, which was an extraordinary expansion in historical terms. It also reported that 2.2 billion people remained offline. Mobile-broadband coverage was nearly universal in a technical sense, but affordability and quality gaps persisted. The 2026 ICT Development Index still found large differences among countries in meaningful connectivity.

Electricity told a similar story. The 2026 *Tracking SDG 7* update reported that 655 million people lacked electricity in 2024, with more than 560 million of them in sub-Saharan Africa. About two billion people still relied on polluting fuels and technologies for cooking. The global access rate had reached ninety-two percent and then slowed.

A technology can be mature enough to transform one part of the world and remain inaccessible in another.

This was the first warning against describing post-scarcity as a universal technical stage.

The cost curve does not install itself.

A solar module can be cheap while financing is expensive. A model can be capable while connectivity is unaffordable. A medicine can be effective while the clinic lacks staff. A robot can reduce labor cost while the importer cannot obtain foreign exchange. A modular housing system can produce units cheaply while roads, water, sewer, land titles, and electricity are missing.

The abundance stack is only as strong as the institutional and physical layers already underneath it.

This created a development paradox.

The countries that could benefit most from skipping expensive legacy systems often had the least capital to finance the new systems.

A rich country can borrow cheaply to build a grid that lowers electricity cost. A poor country may pay high interest precisely because its infrastructure is weak and its fiscal position uncertain. The result is that the country with the greater need faces the higher capital cost.

This is not merely unfair.

It changes which technology is cheapest.

A solar project with inexpensive equipment can produce expensive electricity if financing costs dominate. A housing project can use efficient methods and remain unaffordable if land title is disputed and lenders price legal risk. A data center can use advanced hardware and remain uneconomic if the grid fails regularly and backup generation must be built around it.

Post-scarcity therefore turned development finance into abundance infrastructure.

The late 2020s produced new attempts to solve this through blended finance, guarantees, multilateral development banks, debt restructuring, public-private investment, pooled procurement, regional grids, and standardized project structures. Some succeeded. Others reproduced old problems under new names.

The fundamental issue was risk allocation.

Who bears the risk that a government changes policy?

Who bears currency risk?

Who bears construction risk?

Who pays if a project serves poor households whose ability to pay is limited?

Who absorbs the loss from a climate disaster?

Rich countries had spent generations building institutions that made many of these risks boring enough for private capital. Poorer countries were sometimes asked to pay a premium for not yet having the institutions that the investment was supposed to help create.

The abundant regions discovered that exporting machines was easier than exporting cheap capital.

This became one of the central moral tests after 2030.

If the marginal cost of intelligence was low, should wealthy societies treat global access as a public good?

If excess renewable capacity and cheap storage could improve health, education, and commerce, how much should international finance subsidize deployment?

If open technical standards reduced dependence on one vendor, should public development institutions require them?

If climate damage threatened infrastructure in countries that had contributed little to cumulative emissions, who should pay for resilience?

These were no longer charity questions.

They were questions about whether post-scarcity was a civilization or an enclave.

The technology itself created some new development paths.

Artificial intelligence reduced the value of proximity to certain categories of expertise. A small business in a country with few lawyers, engineers, translators, or software developers could access useful first-pass assistance without building the full professional labor pool first. Students could obtain explanations in languages poorly served by traditional educational publishing. Health workers could receive decision support and translation. Governments could automate parts of administration without hiring bureaucracies proportional to population.

UNDP's 2025 Human Development Report had framed AI around precisely this kind of choice: the technology's development value depended less on whether it looked human and more on whether institutions used it to expand people's capabilities and agency.

The opportunity was real.

So was the dependence risk.

A country that built essential services on foreign proprietary models, foreign clouds, foreign payment systems, foreign satellites, foreign energy equipment, and foreign finance could become more capable and more vulnerable at the same time.

Sovereignty returned through the back door of abundance.

The response was not technological autarky. Few countries could rationally build every layer domestically.

The durable strategy was multi-dependence.

Use global systems, but avoid one supplier where failure would be catastrophic.

Train local operators.

Retain local copies of essential data.

Use portable standards.

Develop domestic capacity in selected strategic layers.

Maintain regional alternatives.

Preserve manual or local fallback modes for critical services.

The same resilience principle that applied to households and cities applied to nations.

No single narrow path.

Regional integration became more important for small countries.

An individual country might not justify a large data-center ecosystem, advanced medical manufacturing, high-capacity electricity balancing market, or specialized industrial base. A region could.

Cross-border grids allowed countries with different generation profiles to share capacity. Regional procurement enlarged markets for medicines and infrastructure. Common digital standards reduced fragmentation. Trade corridors improved logistics. Mutual recognition of credentials expanded scarce professional capacity.

The post-scarcity world became more regional at the same time nationalism was politically powerful.

This tension produced uneven results.

Places that integrated gained scale.

Places that closed themselves off sometimes gained strategic control and paid higher prices.

Places in conflict lost regardless of technology.

War remained abundance's oldest enemy.

A missile can turn a power plant into scarcity in seconds. A blockade can make a globally abundant commodity locally unavailable. A government can conscript labor, restrict movement, seize assets, and redirect production. Refugees can arrive faster than housing and services can expand. Insurance can disappear. Private capital can flee.

The future history of 2030 is impossible to write honestly without this sentence:

Post-scarcity did not survive war equally well.

Some of the abundance architecture improved resilience. Distributed energy could keep critical loads running after centralized failures. Local manufacturing could produce replacement parts. digital identity could help displaced people recover records. Portable benefits could travel across jurisdictions. AI translation could reduce language barriers. Prepositioned inventories and better logistics could improve humanitarian response.

None of this made war abundant.

Destruction still beats production when destruction is deliberate enough.

This is why the book's thesis is conditional on peace at a scale sufficient for systems to compound.

The same was true of state capacity.

A government can possess money and technology while failing to deliver because corruption, patronage, weak procurement, poor maintenance, or political conflict consume the system.

Abundance requires boring competence.

Invoices paid correctly.

Contracts enforced.

Equipment maintained.

Standards published.

Workers trained.

Appeals answered.

Corruption investigated.

Budgets audited.

Land records usable.

Electric meters functioning.

Water systems repaired before collapse.

These tasks rarely appear in visions of technological transformation because they have no singular breakthrough moment.

They decide whether the breakthrough becomes ordinary life.

Countries with weak institutions faced a difficult sequencing problem. Should they automate administration first, hoping better systems reduce corruption and delay? Or strengthen institutions first, knowing weak institutions can corrupt the automated systems too?

There was no universal answer.

Some digital public infrastructure projects improved state capacity by making transactions more transparent, reducing cash handling, simplifying identity, and lowering administrative friction. Others centralized power, excluded people with weak documentation, or created new surveillance capabilities.

The technology amplified the state it entered.

A capable state could become more capable.

A predatory state could become more predatory.

This is one reason the post-scarcity transition widened some political gaps before narrowing material ones.

Democracies with strong institutions could use abundance to expand choice.

Authoritarian systems could use abundance to make compliance comfortable and surveillance cheap.

Fragile states could struggle to convert productive potential into either.

The global inequality of capital ownership made the problem worse.

The World Inequality Report 2026 estimated that the richest tenth of the global population owned roughly three-quarters of wealth while the bottom half owned only a tiny fraction. Automated productive capital therefore entered the abundance era from an unequal starting point.

If capital returns compounded faster than wages, countries and households without ownership could become consumers of abundance financed by someone else's assets.

This might still improve material life.

It could also create permanent dependency.

The development question became not only whether poorer countries could access cheap goods, but whether they could own productive systems that generated income, bargaining power, and fiscal capacity.

This is where the abundance dividend became global strategy.

Sovereign funds were not only for oil-rich countries. Some governments built public investment vehicles from privatization proceeds, natural-resource rents, spectrum auctions, land value, carbon revenues, and fiscal surpluses. Development institutions structured projects so host countries retained equity or revenue shares rather than accepting only debt. Pension systems accumulated domestic and global assets.

The amounts were often small relative to rich-country funds.

The principle mattered.

Do not finance every abundance asset by selling the upside forever.

This became especially important in countries with young populations. Demography created both pressure and possibility. A rapidly growing labor force needed jobs, but it also created demand large enough to support new infrastructure and markets. Automation could threaten low-wage manufacturing development paths that earlier Asian economies had used. At the same time, cheap intelligence and flexible production could let smaller firms move into higher-value activities earlier.

The old development ladder was changing while countries were still climbing it.

This made predictions dangerous.

Some economists expected automation to destroy the comparative advantage of cheap labor. Others expected abundant tools to raise worker productivity and make labor more valuable. Both occurred depending on sector.

A call center could be automated.

A nurse with machine support could serve more patients.

A small manufacturer could use design and robotics tools to produce goods previously beyond its capability.

A farmer could gain precision tools.

A routine back-office service could disappear.

Development became less about moving a population from agriculture to one standard factory model and more about building capabilities across a changing portfolio.

Education became even more important.

Not because every person needed to become a programmer.

Because tool-rich economies reward the ability to adapt when the tool changes.

Universal intelligence access helped, but connectivity and language remained bottlenecks. A model that speaks the national language poorly or fails to understand local law, culture, crops, diseases, and institutions provides a thinner abundance than one built around local context.

This created a new form of data inequality.

Languages, regions, and populations with more digitized material became easier for systems to serve. Communities with less data risked receiving systems whose apparent universality hid foreign assumptions.

The response required local knowledge production.

Digitization.

Translation.

Local research.

Open datasets with governance.

Community participation.

Not every data gap should be filled by surveillance.

A culture can be underrepresented in models without owing the world a complete machine-readable record of itself.

The post-scarcity development project had to respect the right to remain partially illegible.

Climate change created another unevenness.

The countries least responsible for historical emissions were often more exposed to heat, drought, storms, agricultural disruption, and limited fiscal space for adaptation. Cheap renewable energy helped reduce future emissions and sometimes lowered costs, but it did not repair climate damage already locked in.

Abundance therefore had a reparative cost.

Sea walls.

Cooling.

Water infrastructure.

Drought-resistant agriculture.

Relocation.

Disaster insurance or public replacement when insurance failed.

Heat-resilient housing.

Disease surveillance.

Grid hardening.

These expenditures did not create glamorous new consumption. They preserved an old baseline under a harsher climate.

This is another reason the cost of post-scarcity differed by geography.

Enough in a stable temperate city might be cheaper than enough in a flood-prone coastal region facing extreme heat.

Universalism therefore could not mean identical cost per person.

It meant comparable capability under different conditions.

The world that missed post-scarcity did not miss it for one reason.

Some lacked capital.

Some lacked peace.

Some lacked state capacity.

Some lacked infrastructure.

Some were trapped by debt.

Some had authoritarian institutions that delivered material goods while constraining freedom.

Some had democratic institutions unable to build.

Some had natural resources and weak ownership structures.

Some had excellent technology and terrible housing policy.

Some were simply unlucky in geography or climate exposure.

The threshold was not a stage in a textbook.

It was a configuration.

This was hopeful for one reason.

Configurations can be copied selectively.

A country did not need to reproduce the historical path of a rich nation. It could use cheap solar without building a fossil-heavy grid first. It could use mobile networks without universal landlines. It could deploy AI tutoring without waiting to produce enough elite teachers for one-to-one instruction. It could use digital payments without reproducing the full branch-banking system.

Leapfrogging was real where the missing legacy system was a burden.

It was not real where the legacy system was the foundation.

You can skip copper telephone lines.

You cannot skip reliable institutions entirely.

You can skip some centralized generation.

You cannot skip electrical safety.

You can skip some physical paperwork.

You cannot skip legitimacy.

The abundant world learned that development was no longer primarily a question of transferring objects.

It was a question of transferring capability without transferring dependency.

That is much harder.

By the end of 2030, the phrase *global post-scarcity* remained aspirational.

Some places had crossed the baseline threshold decisively. Others had crossed parts of it. Some were moving quickly. Others were going backward under conflict, debt, authoritarian collapse, climate shock, or institutional failure.

The moral achievement was not that humanity had finished the work.

It was that humanity had lost one old excuse.

We could no longer say deprivation was technically necessary because civilization did not know how to produce enough.

The remaining question was whether we could build the institutions, finance, peace, and power-sharing required to make enough arrive everywhere.

After 2030, poverty looked less like fate.

That made every failure harder to defend.
