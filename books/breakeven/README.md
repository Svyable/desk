For seventy years, "fusion is thirty years away" was the safest bet in energy journalism. On December 5, 2022, a laser facility built to certify nuclear weapons without underground testing put more energy out of a small gold-capsule fusion target than its lasers put into it, and the joke stopped being simply true.

*Breakeven* argues that the reasons fusion stayed thirty years away were never purely a physics problem. They were funding cycles, Cold War secrecy, an international treaty organization built to manage politics as much as plasma, and the absence of demand urgent enough to justify the capital. Four things changed within about three years of each other: a real physics threshold got crossed at the National Ignition Facility; high-temperature superconductors made smaller, cheaper magnetic-confinement machines plausible; a U.S. regulator decided fusion did not need to be licensed like a fission reactor; and the AI data-center buildout created demand for firm, carbon-free power urgent enough that hyperscale companies began signing power-purchase agreements for electricity from plants that do not yet exist.

None of that guarantees fusion works as a business. It changes why intelligent, skeptical people are spending billions on it anyway. The book tries to hold both facts at once: this is the most genuine physics progress fusion has made in seventy years, and nearly every specific near-term date given by the people building these machines has a history of arriving late.
