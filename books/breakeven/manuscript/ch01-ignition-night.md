# Ignition Night

Shortly after 1 a.m. on December 5, 2022, the physicist Dave Schlossberg's phone rang in the dark. He had told a colleague, Alex Zylstra, to call him if anything interesting happened during that night's experiment at the National Ignition Facility. Zylstra was excited. The data coming off the diagnostics did not look like anything the facility had recorded in its fourteen years of trying.

The facility in question sits on the grounds of Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, forty miles east of San Francisco, inside a building the width of three football fields and ten stories tall. Most of that volume is laser. Two long rooms called laser bays, each four hundred feet end to end, hold optical amplifiers that take a single infrared pulse smaller than a printer's flash and split it, boost it, and route it through mirrors and lenses until it becomes 192 separate beams converging, in the space of a few billionths of a second, on a target smaller than a pea.

The target that night was a gold cylinder about the size of a pencil eraser, called a hohlraum, with a diamond capsule suspended at its center. The capsule was roughly the size of a peppercorn, its shell about as thick as a human hair, filled with a frozen layer of deuterium and tritium — two heavier isotopes of hydrogen — cooled to a few degrees above absolute zero. The 192 beams did not strike the fuel directly. They struck the inside wall of the gold cylinder, which flashed into X-rays, and it was those X-rays, arriving from every direction at once, that crushed the capsule inward. In less than a hundred-trillionth of the time it takes to blink, the shell collapsed to a fraction of its original size, compressing the frozen hydrogen at its core to a density and temperature the target's designers had spent decades trying to reach.

By the time Schlossberg picked up the phone, the machine had already done its work and gone quiet. What remained was a set of numbers on a screen, and what the numbers were saying, once the team had checked and rechecked the diagnostics, was that the fuel had briefly ignited. The laser had delivered 2.05 megajoules of energy to the target. The fusion reactions inside that imploding capsule had released 3.15 megajoules back out — about 54 percent more energy than the lasers put in. It was the first time any fusion experiment on Earth had produced more energy from its fuel than the energy used to drive the reaction. Not the first time humans had triggered fusion; thermonuclear weapons had been doing that violently and briefly since 1952, and NIF itself had spent years working toward this exact threshold. It was the first time the reaction had paid for itself, however briefly, in a laboratory built to study it rather than detonate it.

The shot was not a surprise in the sense that no one had seen it coming. It was the payoff of a specific, traceable engineering decision, laid over a much longer history of falling short. Construction on the facility had begun in 1997, and NIF's early planners had once hoped to reach ignition by around 2012, a date that came and went along with several that followed it, while an entire subfield of plasma physics learned to live with the gap between the facility's name and what the facility could actually do. A year before the December shot, in August 2021, an experiment had come startlingly close — 1.3 megajoules of fusion yield, a result so far beyond prior shots that some on the team initially suspected an instrument error. It wasn't ignition, not quite, but it proved the regime was reachable, and it reset expectations inside the lab even as it went almost unnoticed outside it. Andrea "Annie" Kritcher, the physicist who led the target design campaign, and her colleagues spent the following months adjusting the geometry: a slightly larger hohlraum, a thicker capsule shell to suppress the small instabilities that had been robbing the 2021 shot of performance, changes to how the laser's energy was paced over the course of the pulse. None of that reads as dramatic on paper. In an experiment where success and failure sit on either side of a threshold measured in micrometers and nanoseconds, it was enough.

Word did not reach the public for eight days. Verifying a result like this meant independent reanalysis of the diagnostic data, confirmation that the neutron and X-ray measurements agreed with each other, and the internal choreography of a national laboratory deciding how to announce something it had chased since the facility's groundbreaking in 1997, a project originally budgeted at $1.2 billion that had eventually cost more than $3.5 billion and missed its original ignition target date by over a decade. On December 13, 2022, Secretary of Energy Jennifer Granholm stood before reporters at DOE headquarters in Washington and called it "one of the most impressive scientific feats of the 21st century." Arati Prabhakar, then director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, described it as "an engineering marvel beyond belief" and "a tremendous example of what perseverance really can achieve." Kim Budil, the director of Lawrence Livermore, tried to hold the two true things in the room at once: that ignition was real, and that a power plant was not around the corner. Getting there, she said, would take "a few decades of research on the underlying technologies," assuming sustained investment — not six decades, she added, but not next year either. Jill Hruby, the head of the National Nuclear Security Administration, framed the result differently again, calling it the opening of "a new chapter" in the program that had funded NIF's construction in the first place: the stewardship of America's nuclear weapons stockpile, absent the underground testing the United States stopped doing in 1992.

Every outlet covering the announcement reached for the same image, because it happened to be true: for a fraction of a second, inside a gold cylinder smaller than a fingertip, physicists had recreated conditions that exist naturally only at the centers of stars. It was a good sentence, and the physics underneath it was sound. It was also, on its own, an incomplete account of what had happened, and the incompleteness would define the argument that followed for years.

The 2.05 megajoules of laser light that struck the target on December 5 did not appear from nowhere. NIF's laser system is built around flashlamp-pumped glass amplifiers, a technology chosen in the 1990s for its ability to deliver an enormous, precisely shaped pulse rather than for its efficiency at converting electricity into light. To deliver those 2.05 megajoules to the hohlraum, the facility drew something on the order of 400 megajoules from the electrical grid — roughly two hundred times more energy than the laser ultimately put on target, for a wall-plug efficiency of about half a percent. Counted against the fusion yield of 3.15 megajoules, the honest ledger for that single shot was not a gain at all. It was a loss, and a large one, when measured from the wall socket rather than the target chamber.

None of the officials at the December 13 press conference hid this. Every one of them, asked directly, said plainly that NIF was not a power plant and was never designed to be one — that its lasers exist to study the physics of a hydrogen bomb's secondary stage without detonating one, and that a commercial reactor would need a completely different, far more efficient way of driving the same reaction, fired many times a second rather than once every few hours. But a press conference compresses. A headline compresses further. Within days, a specific and defensible claim — the fusion reactions inside that capsule released more energy than the laser light that struck it — had drifted, in a great deal of the coverage that followed, into a vaguer and much larger one: scientists had cracked fusion, and cheap, limitless energy was coming. Scientists who had spent their careers on the difference between those two claims spent the following weeks explaining it to journalists, and some of them are still explaining it now.

The distance between those two claims is not a technicality. It is close to the whole argument of the years that followed December 2022, and it will recur throughout this book in a more precise form: the difference between the energy a fusion target releases and the energy an entire fusion system consumes to make that release possible — what specialists came to call target gain versus wall-plug gain. Every fusion company founded or funded in the years since has had to answer, implicitly or explicitly, which side of that line its technology sits on, and how far it has to travel to cross it in both directions at once. NIF crossed the first line on December 5, 2022, convincingly and reproducibly — it would do it again in February 2024, and again, at nearly three times the gain, in April 2025. It has not come close to the second, has never claimed to be trying to, and was not built to.

That distinction did not stop the shot from mattering. It is possible for a result to be simultaneously overhyped in its retelling and genuinely important in its substance, and December 5, 2022 was both. For seventy years, controlled fusion research had operated under a joke so durable it had become a kind of institutional weather: fusion is thirty years away, and it always will be. The physicist Robert L. Park, who had watched the field's funding cycles rise and collapse since the 1970s, put a version of that line on record with BusinessWeek in 1990, and by the time NIF fired its shot, the joke had outlived several generations of scientists who had spent entire careers inside its shadow. What made ignition different was not that it disproved the joke. Nobody serious argued, in December 2022, that a fusion power plant had suddenly become imminent. What ignition did was remove one specific, long-standing excuse. For decades, skeptics of fusion's timeline could point to an open physics question: nobody had ever shown, in a laboratory, that the basic reaction could release more energy than was delivered to the fuel itself. After December 5, that particular question had an answer, and the answer was yes.

What replaced it was a different, harder set of questions — economic, engineering, regulatory, and political ones that this book spends its remaining chapters trying to take seriously without either dismissing them or resolving them into false comfort. Whether a laser system can ever be made efficient enough, or whether magnetic confinement machines using an entirely different approach can get there faster. Whether tritium, the radioactive hydrogen isotope that fusion's easiest reaction depends on, can be produced in the quantities a fleet of power plants would need, when the world's entire usable stockpile currently sits somewhere around 25 to 30 kilograms. Whether the neutrons a working reactor would produce — the same neutrons whose energy this book will explain in the next chapter — can be contained without destroying the materials meant to contain them, an objection the retired Princeton plasma physicist Daniel Jassby has pressed for years and that deserves a harder hearing than it usually gets in press-release coverage of the field. Whether an international project like ITER, which had already been promising "first plasma" imminently for the better part of two decades by the time NIF fired its shot, offers a reasonable model for how long these things take, or a cautionary one.

And underneath all of that, a newer question that did not exist in anything like its current form in December 2022: whether the world's sudden, voracious appetite for electricity — driven in large part by artificial intelligence data centers that by 2025 were consuming power at a scale comparable to entire mid-sized countries — would change the calculus around fusion investment regardless of how those older questions got answered. Four things moved roughly together in the years after ignition night: the physics threshold NIF crossed, a new generation of high-temperature superconducting magnets that made smaller and cheaper alternatives to NIF's laser approach look plausible, a 2023 decision by U.S. regulators not to treat fusion power plants like fission reactors, and a demand signal from technology companies large enough to sign contracts for electricity from plants that do not yet exist. None of those four things, individually, would have been enough to change fusion's seventy-year reputation. Together, they are the reason this book exists rather than being, like so many fusion books before it, a history of the joke.

Kim Budil, at that December press conference, was careful in a way that the coverage around her often was not. Asked what ignition meant for when the public might actually get electricity from fusion, she declined to give a specific year, and she was right to decline. Every specific year anyone in this field has offered, across seven decades of trying, has a track record of arriving late, and this book will not add a new one to the pile. What it will try to do is take the question seriously on its own terms: not whether fusion is coming, and not whether it isn't, but what changed, measurably, in the years bracketing that phone call in the dark, and why intelligent, skeptical people who had heard the thirty-years-away joke as many times as anyone are now spending billions of dollars behaving as though this time some of the old excuses no longer apply.

Schlossberg's phone call ended before dawn. It would be another eight days before the rest of the world heard about it, and years before anyone could say with confidence what the shot had actually changed. But something had, in fact, happened in the dark that night at Lawrence Livermore that had never happened before in a laboratory: a gram-scale amount of hydrogen, compressed and heated by light, had briefly burned hotter than it was made to burn, and had given back more than it was given. What that fact is actually worth — to physics, to energy policy, to the people now betting billions that it is the hinge point of a technology that finally works — is the question the rest of this book tries to answer honestly, including the parts of the answer that are still, as of this writing, unknown.
