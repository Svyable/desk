What Happens When the Actuaries Are Wrong About Everything at Once.

Every institution built around a human lifespan — a pension fund, a life insurer, a retirement age, a Social Security trust fund, a mortgage term, a marriage, a career ladder — was priced against a mortality table. For most of the twentieth century those tables moved slowly and predictably enough that the institutions built on top of them could be adjusted a little at a time. That is ending on two fronts at once. Biotechnology is starting to treat aging itself as a target rather than a background fact, turning slow, engineered gains in life expectancy into something that could compress or accelerate unpredictably. At the same time, most of the world's large economies are having far fewer children than they need to replace themselves, which means the number of long lives being produced is falling even as the length of any individual life becomes less certain.

The working thesis is that this is a repricing event of the kind societies have handled badly before: correctly recognizing that something fundamental has changed while mispricing the timetable, the distribution of the gains, and who is left holding the institutions built for the old assumptions. The book follows that repricing through the science (GLP-1 drugs, senolytics, cellular reprogramming, and the graveyard of longevity biotech promises that didn't pan out), the demographics (a fertility collapse now touching most of East Asia and much of Europe), and the money (actuaries, pension funds, insurers, and states that have to hold a number for how long you will live, and are finding out how much of that number was always a guess).

It is not a book about immortality. It tries to hold two facts that are both true and inconvenient together: the science of aging is more active and better funded than at any point in human history, and most of what has been promised about it in the last decade has either failed outright or not yet been shown to work in humans. The chapters that follow try to price that gap honestly.
