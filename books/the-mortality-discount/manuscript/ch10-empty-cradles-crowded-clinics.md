# Empty Cradles, Crowded Clinics

By the South Korean government's own accounting, the country has spent somewhere between roughly two hundred and ten billion and two hundred and seventy billion dollars — reported cumulative totals vary by outlet, by exactly which years and programs are counted, and by exchange-rate conventions, landing anywhere from about two hundred and eighty to three hundred and eighty trillion won depending on the tally — on programs explicitly aimed at reversing its birth-rate decline since a 2006 policy baseline. Over that same span, the country's total fertility rate fell from roughly 1.08 to the record low of 0.72 recorded in 2023, before the modest, likely temporary rebound described in the previous chapter. Whatever the precise dollar figure, and this book will not pretend more precision exists than the public record actually supports, the order of magnitude is not in dispute: a wealthy, technologically sophisticated government spent a sum comparable to a small country's entire annual economic output trying to make its citizens have more children, and its fertility rate kept falling anyway, for most of the period the spending was occurring.

The per-child mechanics behind that aggregate figure are concrete enough to convey what "spending" actually meant in practice. Reporting from the *Korea Herald* on 2024 benefit levels found that a child born that year came with roughly twenty-nine point six million won — on the order of twenty-one to twenty-two thousand dollars — in combined cash and in-kind support from national and local programs layered on top of each other: birth grants, monthly childcare stipends, parental leave benefits, housing assistance for young families. That is not a token gesture. It is a substantial sum by the standard of comparable family-support programs anywhere in the developed world, attached to every single birth, sustained over nearly two decades, and it moved the national fertility rate only modestly, and only after roughly seventeen years of decline, in a rebound that demographers studying the pattern have attributed at least as much to a post-pandemic marriage catch-up as to the cumulative effect of the spending itself.

It is worth being fair to the South Korean government's own record here, rather than treating nearly two decades of spending as a story of pure failure: the counterfactual question of how much lower the fertility rate might have fallen without any of this spending is genuinely unanswerable, and it is entirely possible the country's decline would have been sharper, or arrived faster, absent the cumulative support programs described above. What the record does establish, without needing to resolve that unanswerable counterfactual, is a narrower and still highly consequential claim: this scale of spending, sustained this long, was not sufficient to prevent South Korea's fertility rate from falling to the lowest level ever recorded for any country, nor to prevent it from staying there for several consecutive years before the recent, likely temporary uptick. A policymaker in any other country now considering a comparably generous fertility-incentive program should treat South Korea's experience as real evidence about the probable scale of return on that kind of investment — a meaningful, non-zero effect, almost certainly, but one that has so far proven far smaller than the scale of spending required to produce it, at least at the levels South Korea itself has been willing to commit.

## Hungary's experiment, run the other direction

Hungary offers the closest thing available to a controlled test of the opposite policy philosophy: rather than direct cash payments scaled to South Korea's approach, Prime Minister Viktor Orbán's government, since 2010, has built an unusually comprehensive suite of structural incentives aimed specifically at family formation — a personal income-tax exemption for women who have four or more children, effectively for life; zero-interest "baby-expecting" loans available to married couples, with a portion of the loan forgiven outright for each child subsequently born; and housing subsidies tied to marriage and childbearing. Hungary now spends an estimated five percent of its gross domestic product on family benefits of this kind, among the highest shares of any economy in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development.

The results, tracked carefully by demographers on both sides of the policy debate, show a genuine but modest and, more recently, reversing effect. Hungary's total fertility rate rose from roughly 1.2 to 1.3 in the early 2010s to a peak of about 1.59 in 2021, before falling back to approximately 1.39 by 2024 — described in subsequent analysis as Hungary's lowest fertility rate in over a decade, essentially erasing much of the gain the policy suite appeared to have produced over its first decade in effect. Academic estimates of the program's earlier tax-exemption changes specifically, cited in a summary published by the Institute for Family Studies, suggest those measures may have caused somewhere between six thousand and eighteen thousand additional births in the 2011-to-2013 period — a real, measurable effect, and also nowhere close to enough additional births to move Hungary's overall trajectory toward the replacement rate of roughly 2.1 that would let its population stabilize on its own. Even sympathetic analysts have begun describing the Hungarian program in more measured terms than its early advocates once did. A 2025 assessment published by the American Enterprise Institute — an organization not reflexively hostile to pronatalist policy — was titled, without much diplomatic softening, "Hungary's Fertility Outcomes Highlight Pro-Natal Policy Limitations," and argued that much of the program's apparent early success reflected what demographers call a tempo effect: couples having children somewhat sooner than they otherwise would have, in response to the incentives, rather than a lasting increase in how many children they ultimately chose to have across their full reproductive years. A tempo effect produces a real, temporary bump in the annual fertility rate, followed by a return closer to the underlying trend once the pool of couples who simply moved their timing forward has been exhausted — which is a reasonably good description of the shape Hungary's own data now show.

Read together, South Korea's roughly quarter-trillion-dollar direct-payment approach and Hungary's structurally different, more comprehensive incentive suite arrive at a similar, uncomfortable conclusion from two different policy directions: government spending on pronatalist incentives, even at a scale large enough to be a serious fraction of national economic output, appears capable of producing real but modest effects — some additional births, some shift in timing — without coming close to reversing the underlying trajectory toward well-below-replacement fertility in either country. That is a specific, falsifiable claim, not a broad assertion that "policy doesn't matter," and it is worth stating the boundary of what the evidence actually supports: it does not establish that no policy intervention, of any design, at any scale, could meaningfully move fertility rates. It establishes that two of the most sustained, well-funded, differently designed national experiments run so far have not managed to do so, which is a meaningfully different and more modest claim, but one directly relevant to any government currently weighing whether a similar program, in a similar country, is likely to succeed where these two largely have not.

## The valve nobody wants to fully open

If direct fertility incentives have a poor track record of reversing demographic decline on their own, the most obvious economic alternative — inviting in enough working-age immigrants to offset a shrinking native-born population — carries a different kind of cost, one measured in domestic political stability rather than treasury outlays.

Germany and Japan make an instructive comparison precisely because both countries carry among the highest shares of elderly population in the world, and yet have chosen visibly different paths. Germany has, over the past decade in particular, used immigration proactively and at real scale to stabilize and partly rejuvenate both its population and its labor force; roughly one-fifth of the country's total population now has a migrant background, by most current estimates. Japan has moved more slowly and more reluctantly, but it has moved. A 2019 legal reform created the Specified Skilled Worker visa category — the first formal Japanese government acknowledgment that the country needs meaningful numbers of foreign workers in blue-collar sectors such as construction, logistics, hospitality, and long-term care — explicitly designed, in its structure and its public messaging, to avoid being described domestically as an "immigration policy," since the initial tier of the program offers no path to family reunification or permanent settlement. Japan's foreign resident population nonetheless reached more than three point seven million people by 2024, and government projections have discussed admitting several hundred thousand additional workers under expanded versions of the same program over the coming decade, aimed at closing a projected shortfall estimated, in various government and industry analyses, at somewhere on the order of eleven million workers by 2040 across the sectors aging most acutely strains.

The political cost of leaning on immigration this way, even in a country managing it as carefully and gradually as Japan has, is becoming visible in real time across both societies, and it is worth describing plainly rather than editorializing about which side of the underlying debate is correct. In Germany's February 2025 federal election, the anti-immigration Alternative für Deutschland party won 20.8 percent of the national vote — the strongest result for a party of the German far right since the Second World War — with subsequent polling through 2026 showing support climbing further still, and immigration consistently ranked by voters, including a large majority of the party's own supporters, as their top political concern. In Japan, the July 2025 upper house election saw a new nationalist party, Sanseito, running on an explicit "Japanese First" platform, perform well enough to unsettle the long-dominant Liberal Democratic Party's own rhetoric, prompting the ruling party to visibly toughen its public language on immigration even as the underlying economic dependence on foreign labor, described above, continued to deepen in the background. Neither election result overturned the countries' actual policy trajectories — Germany continues to rely on immigration at scale, and Japan continues to quietly expand its Specified Skilled Worker program — but both results demonstrate the same structural bind clearly: the demographic arithmetic increasingly requires immigration at a scale that a meaningful and, in some places, growing share of the domestic electorate is prepared to resist, sometimes forcefully, at the ballot box. This is precisely the kind of tension this book keeps finding at every institutional layer it examines: the economically indicated adjustment and the politically survivable adjustment are not the same thing, and the gap between them does not resolve itself simply because the underlying demographic pressure keeps building regardless of which side wins a given election cycle.

## What the strain actually looks like in the numbers

The economic pressure driving both the fertility-incentive spending and the immigration debate is not abstract. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's own tracking shows the old-age dependency ratio across its member countries — the population aged sixty-five and older, measured against the working-age population that supports it through taxes and productive labor — rising from roughly nineteen percent in 1980 to about thirty-one percent by 2023, and projected to reach approximately fifty-two percent by 2060, effectively doubling again within about three and a half decades, with the ratio projected to nearly triple in some member countries. The OECD's working-age population as a whole is projected to shrink by roughly eight percent across the organization's members by 2060, and by more than thirty percent in over a quarter of individual member countries — a contraction of the tax base and labor supply large enough, on the International Monetary Fund's own analysis in its April 2025 World Economic Outlook, to directly dampen potential economic growth across the affected countries, independent of any other economic headwind those countries might separately face.

The dependency-ratio arithmetic is worth translating into a more concrete, human-scale image, the same way this book's chapter on South Korea's fertility collapse translated a decimal-point birth rate into an ordinary classroom's terms. An old-age dependency ratio of nineteen percent, roughly where the OECD average stood in 1980, means something like five working-age adults for every person aged sixty-five and older — a ratio comfortable enough that the tax and social-security burden of supporting each retiree could be spread thinly across a large base of active workers. A ratio of fifty-two percent, the OECD's own projection for 2060, means fewer than two working-age adults for every person over sixty-five, a roughly two-and-a-half-fold tightening of the support ratio within a single working lifetime. Every one of the institutions examined throughout this book — a pension system, a national health service, a family expecting to help support aging parents directly — was built, staffed, and funded around some assumption about how many working-age contributors would be available to support each dependent retiree, and none of them were built around a ratio anywhere close to two-to-one, because until very recently in human history no wealthy society had ever needed to plan for one.

Japan's response to its own version of this pressure offers a genuinely constructive, if partial, counterexample worth including alongside the more alarming numbers, because this book's discipline about counter-evidence applies as much to unrelieved pessimism as it does to unearned optimism. The country's Silver Human Resource Center system, established in Tokyo in 1974 and expanded nationally since, now operates roughly thirteen hundred centers with more than seven hundred thousand registered elderly members, matching retirees with part-time, community-based paid work — facility maintenance, office support, skilled trades, customer service — that keeps them economically and socially engaged well past conventional retirement age. Academic research on the centers frames their core purpose, revealingly, less around raw economic necessity than around *ikigai*, a Japanese concept roughly translating to a sense of purpose or reason for being, suggesting the institution succeeds as much by giving retirees somewhere meaningful to direct their remaining working years as by plugging a literal labor shortage. The scale should be kept honest, though: seven hundred thousand participants is a modest fraction of Japan's more than thirty-six million residents aged sixty-five and older, meaning the program mitigates the labor-force strain at the margins rather than resolving it, alongside a separate 2020 legal reform requiring Japanese employers to make a good-faith effort — not a binding legal guarantee, an important distinction the law itself preserves — to extend employment opportunities to workers up to age seventy.

The *ikigai* framing deserves a moment of its own attention, because it points toward something this chapter's more purely economic analysis can otherwise miss. A retiree who joins a Silver Human Resource Center is not, typically, doing so purely out of financial necessity; the part-time wages on offer are generally modest relative to what a full-time career would have paid. What the centers appear to supply, on the academic research examining participants' own stated motivations, is closer to structure and social contact: a reason to leave the house on a given morning, a small team of colleagues, a task with a visible beginning and end, the kind of ordinary social infrastructure a career otherwise provides by default and that a sudden, complete retirement can abruptly remove. This is a genuinely different value proposition than the pure labor-supply framing that dominates most policy discussion of aging workforces, and it suggests a possible model — modest paid work calibrated more toward social engagement than toward filling a critical staffing gap — that other aging societies examined in this book might reasonably adapt even where, as the honest accounting above makes clear, it cannot come close to closing the underlying dependency-ratio gap on its own.

## Two ways to read the same numbers

It would be a disservice to this chapter's subject to end it as though the demographic and economic trajectory it has just laid out speaks with one voice, because serious, credentialed researchers looking at exactly this evidence do not agree with each other, and the disagreement is worth preserving rather than resolving artificially for the sake of narrative closure. Jennifer Sciubba, a political demographer affiliated with the Population Reference Bureau and the author of a 2022 book on global demographic change, has argued explicitly and publicly — in an interview headlined, pointedly, "There Is No Demographic Crisis. Only a Crisis of Care" — that framing aging and low fertility as a civilizational emergency is itself a category error; the real, addressable problem, in her account, is the political and institutional response to an aging population, particularly around care infrastructure and labor policy, not the underlying population numbers considered in isolation, and she has said plainly that the shrinking population trajectories many countries now face are, in her assessment, no reason to panic.

Set against that view, the economists Dean Spears and Michael Geruso published a 2025 book arguing close to the opposite: they explicitly reject comparison to the discredited overpopulation alarmism of the mid-twentieth century, arguing writers like Paul Ehrlich were wrong both in their values and their facts, but contend that today's low-fertility trend deserves serious concern precisely because, in their formulation, fewer people ultimately means fewer of the ideas, discoveries, and innovations that have driven most of human material progress. Their book has itself drawn direct pushback from population-focused nonprofits, who argue it overstates the case in the opposite direction from Sciubba — a genuine three-way disagreement, not the two-sided debate popular coverage usually compresses this subject into, between an alarmed camp exemplified by Eberstadt's projections in the previous chapter, a "this is a care-policy problem, not an existential one" camp exemplified by Sciubba, and an "this is a real and underrated crisis, but not the one you're picturing" camp exemplified by Spears and Geruso.

This book does not resolve that three-way argument, because the honest evidence available does not yet resolve it either. What the evidence does establish, across South Korea's spending, Hungary's tax incentives, Germany and Japan's contrasting migration strategies, and the OECD and IMF's own dependency-ratio projections, is narrower and more useful than picking a winner among the demographers: the institutions built around a growing or at least stable working-age population are now operating in a world where that assumption no longer reliably holds in a large and growing share of the global economy, the policy tools tried so far to restore it have worked only partially at best, and the tools that do show real economic promise — immigration chief among them — carry political costs that are, themselves, now measurably reshaping the electoral map of the countries that need them most.
