# The State's Dilemma

Strip away the details particular to any single country, and every government confronting the demographic and biological pressures examined across this book's earlier chapters is choosing, in practice, among a fairly short menu of options, none of which is free, and none of which, on the evidence assembled so far, reliably solves the underlying problem on its own. A state can raise the age at which people become eligible for a pension. It can try to pay people to have more children. It can open its borders to more working-age immigrants. It can invest in automation to let a shrinking workforce produce more per worker. Or it can simply do none of these decisively enough, and let the arithmetic resolve itself eventually through reduced benefits, higher taxes, or some blend of both, once the accumulated reserves this book's earlier chapters have already found ticking down toward specific, published depletion dates finally run out. Every country examined in this book is, in practice, doing some combination of the first four, in proportions that reveal a great deal about each country's specific political constraints, while the fifth option — inaction, followed by an eventual forced reckoning — remains the default outcome for any government that fails to move decisively enough on the others in time.

A structural choice sitting underneath all four active options deserves to be named before turning to each in turn, because it shapes how much room a given government actually has to maneuver: whether a state funds its retirement promises on a pay-as-you-go basis, using current workers' tax contributions to pay current retirees' benefits directly, or on a pre-funded basis, setting aside and investing contributions in advance so that a retiree's eventual benefit is backed by an actual accumulated pool of invested assets rather than a promise to tax future workers. The American Social Security system examined throughout this book operates predominantly on the pay-as-you-go model, which is precisely why its finances are so directly and immediately exposed to the dependency-ratio arithmetic this book's chapters have traced: when the ratio of workers to retirees shifts unfavorably, a pay-as-you-go system feels the strain immediately, in the form of the specific, published trust-fund depletion dates this book's earlier chapter examined in detail, because there is no substantial pool of pre-invested assets standing between the current demographic reality and the government's ability to pay promised benefits. A more fully pre-funded system, by contrast, is exposed to a different and in some ways more forgiving set of risks — primarily investment-return risk rather than pure demographic risk — since a large enough pool of accumulated, invested assets can continue funding promised benefits for a period even if the underlying worker-to-retiree ratio deteriorates sharply, provided the fund's investment returns hold up. Very few countries examined in this book's chapters run a purely pre-funded national retirement system; most, including the United States, rely on some blend of the two models, with the pay-as-you-go component bearing the most direct and immediate exposure to exactly the demographic pressures this chapter's four options are meant to address.

## Raising the age, carefully

France's 2023 pension reform, examined in an earlier chapter, offers the clearest recent illustration of the first option's real cost: President Macron's government raised the country's minimum retirement age from sixty-two to sixty-four, a two-year adjustment phased in gradually over seven years, and still needed to invoke a special constitutional provision to force the bill through without a parliamentary vote, against nationwide strikes that ran for months and crowd estimates for individual protest days that reached into the millions by some counts. Two years, gained at that level of sustained political cost, is a small fraction of the gap this book's earlier chapters have documented between the retirement ages most pension systems still assume and the life expectancy gains, income-based life-expectancy disparities, and demographic pressures those systems are actually now facing. The United States, examined in the same earlier chapter, offers the opposite lesson: a Social Security system whose trustees have published, with unusual bureaucratic candor, a specific projected trust-fund depletion date — 2033 for the retirement-specific fund, 2034 for the combined trust funds — and a specific, automatic benefit cut, on the order of a fifth to a quarter of scheduled payments, that would take effect at that date absent congressional action, with no legislative move toward raising the retirement age or otherwise closing the gap yet enacted as of this writing, despite the depletion date having been publicly known and republished annually for years. Raising a retirement age is available to every government facing this problem. Actually doing it, at anything like the scale the underlying demographic and actuarial pressure calls for, has proven to be one of the most politically costly domestic policy moves a modern government can attempt — which helps explain why so few governments have moved as far, or as fast, as their own trustees' and actuaries' projections suggest they eventually will need to.

## Paying for babies, and getting a fraction of what was paid for

The second option, examined at length in an earlier chapter, has the clearest and most discouraging track record of the four. South Korea's cumulative spending on fertility-incentive programs, variously tallied at somewhere between roughly two hundred and ten and two hundred and seventy billion dollars since 2006, produced a fertility rate that kept falling for most of that period, reaching a global record low of 0.72 in 2023 before a modest, likely partly temporary rebound. Hungary's more structurally ambitious program — tax exemptions, forgivable loans, housing subsidies, consuming an estimated five percent of the country's entire economic output — lifted the country's fertility rate from roughly 1.2 to a peak of 1.59 in 2021, only to see it fall back to about 1.39 by 2024, a reversal serious enough that even sympathetic policy analysts have begun describing the program's outcomes in terms of its limitations rather than its success. Both examples, taken together, support a narrow but important conclusion already established in this book: substantial, sustained government spending on fertility incentives appears capable of producing real, measurable, but modest effects, well short of what would be needed to restore either country's population to a self-replacing trajectory, which means a government choosing to lean heavily on this option should expect to spend a very great deal of money for a comparatively small demographic return, rather than a solution to the underlying problem.

## Importing workers, and importing a political fight along with them

The third option carries the strongest evidence of actually working, on strictly economic terms, alongside the clearest evidence of political backlash. Germany's proactive, sustained use of immigration to stabilize its aging population and workforce, examined in an earlier chapter, has demonstrably kept the country's demographic and labor-market position stronger than Japan's more cautious approach over the same period, even as both countries carry similarly severe underlying aging ratios. But the same chapter documented the cost: a far-right, explicitly anti-immigration party winning the strongest electoral result for the German far right since the Second World War in February 2025, with immigration consistently ranked as voters' top concern, and support continuing to climb in subsequent polling. Japan's own, more cautious embrace of labor migration through its Specified Skilled Worker visa program — carefully, deliberately not called an immigration policy in its domestic political framing, even as it has grown to cover several million foreign residents and is slated to expand further — has nonetheless coincided with the rise of its own nationalist political movement running on an explicit "Japanese First" platform. The pattern across both countries is consistent enough to state as a general finding rather than a coincidence specific to either: the economically indicated policy response to a shrinking working-age population increasingly runs directly against a hardening political constraint in the same countries that need the response most, which means a government choosing this option is choosing not just an economic policy but a sustained political fight, with no guarantee of winning it cleanly enough to sustain the policy at the scale the underlying demographic arithmetic requires.

The political economy of this specific tension deserves one further observation, because it helps explain why the backlash has proven so durable rather than fading as the underlying demographic need becomes more widely understood. Immigration's economic benefits to an aging society — a larger tax base, more workers per retiree, continued growth in sectors that would otherwise contract — are diffuse, aggregate, and often invisible to any single voter in their day-to-day life, showing up mainly in macroeconomic statistics and long-run fiscal projections of exactly the kind this book's chapters on dependency ratios and trust-fund depletion dates have examined. Immigration's visible, local costs and disruptions — competition for housing and public services in specific neighborhoods, cultural and linguistic change experienced directly in daily life, the strain on schools and healthcare systems in areas receiving large numbers of new arrivals quickly — are concentrated, immediate, and experienced directly by specific communities, often ones that did not choose or particularly want the change. This asymmetry, between diffuse aggregate benefit and concentrated local cost, is a familiar pattern in political economy well beyond immigration policy specifically, but it is worth naming here because it means the aging-driven economic case for immigration, however sound at the level of national statistics, is structurally disadvantaged in ordinary democratic politics against the more vivid, more immediately felt costs that mobilize the opposition this chapter has documented in Germany and Japan alike.

## Automating around the shortage, imperfectly

The fourth option is the newest, the least tested at scale, and, on the evidence examined in an earlier chapter, the most mismatched to where the actual labor shortage is concentrated. Artificial intelligence, per the OECD's own 2025 Employment Outlook, is explicitly framed by the organization as a tool that could help member economies offset the aging-driven contraction in their working-age populations. But the same body of labor-market research finds the technology's current strengths concentrated in knowledge work — the kind of white-collar tasks that AI tools can draft, analyze, and summarize — while the occupations projected to shrink fastest and most painfully under population aging, elder care and other hands-on caregiving chief among them, remain occupations current AI tools are poorly suited to perform. A government betting heavily on automation as its primary answer to workforce aging is, in effect, betting that a technology best suited to augmenting comparatively well-staffed white-collar sectors will somehow also resolve a shortage concentrated in a different, physically hands-on part of the economy that the same technology, as currently deployed, barely touches — a mismatch this book's earlier chapter on career and labor-market redesign already flagged as a genuine, unresolved structural problem rather than a temporary gap that better AI models will straightforwardly close.

There is a narrower, less discussed version of the automation option worth distinguishing from the broad AI-as-labor-offset framing above: dedicated robotics aimed specifically at the physical caregiving tasks this chapter has just identified as the hardest for current AI systems to address. Japan, examined throughout this book's demographic chapters as the country furthest along in confronting these pressures, has invested in eldercare robotics research for longer and at greater relative scale than most other governments, producing real, deployed assistive technology — robotic lifting devices that reduce physical strain on human caregivers, monitoring systems that detect falls or changes in a resident's condition, simple conversational companion robots aimed at reducing social isolation among residents living alone. None of this technology, as of this writing, comes close to replacing the hands-on core of caregiving work the way this chapter's broader automation discussion might optimistically suggest; it functions, at its current state of development, as a genuine but partial productivity aid for human caregivers rather than a substitute for them, extending how many residents a given number of human care workers can adequately serve rather than eliminating the need for those workers outright. This is a meaningfully different and more modest claim than the OECD's broader AI-as-offset framing implies, and a government weighing this specific, narrower option should calibrate its expectations accordingly: a genuine, worthwhile productivity gain at the margins, not the kind of wholesale technological substitution that would meaningfully close the caregiving-labor gap this book's demographic chapters have documented on its own.

## A fifth option this book has not yet named directly

There is a fifth genuine policy lever available to states confronting this pressure, distinct from the four examined above and from the passive "do nothing and let the reserves run out" default this chapter treats as the effective outcome of failing at the other four: directly funding the kind of foundational, patent-free longevity and public-health research this book's earlier chapters found chronically and specifically underfunded, on the logic that a state carrying the largest aggregate financial exposure to the mortality discount described throughout this book has the strongest possible fiscal incentive to close the scientific uncertainty at the center of it. The TAME trial, examined in an earlier chapter, is the clearest illustration of this option's current absence rather than its presence: a trial designed specifically to test whether a ten-cent generic pill could delay the onset of several major age-related diseases at once, structured explicitly to open a regulatory pathway that could benefit every future intervention in the same category, has spent the better part of a decade without securing the tens of millions of dollars its own investigators estimate it needs — a sum that is a rounding error against the scale of the entitlement exposure this chapter's earlier sections describe, and one that no government examined across this book's chapters has yet chosen to simply provide directly, despite each of those same governments carrying, through its own pension and healthcare obligations, a far larger direct financial stake in the trial's outcome than any private investor considering the same funding gap.

This is worth naming as a distinct, missed option rather than folding it into the fourth option's discussion of automation and technology, because the logic is different in an important way: raising a retirement age, funding fertility incentives, and opening immigration are all attempts to adjust an institution's structure to match an unchanged demographic and biological reality, while directly funding foundational aging research is an attempt to change the underlying reality itself, at a funding scale that is, by the standard of the other four options examined in this chapter, remarkably modest — the TAME trial's full funding gap, at the higher end of the estimates examined in an earlier chapter, is a small fraction of what South Korea alone has spent on fertility incentives with comparatively little demographic return. That no government has stepped in to close a funding gap this small, for a trial with this much potential public benefit and this much direct relevance to those same governments' own fiscal exposure, is itself one of the more pointed data points this book has assembled about the gap between the scale of the problem governments say they understand and the scale of the response they have actually been willing to fund.

## Choosing all four badly, rather than one well

What the evidence assembled across this book actually shows, country by country, is not that any single government has committed decisively to one of these four options and either succeeded or failed cleanly. It is that most governments facing this pressure are doing some uncoordinated blend of all four, simultaneously, each pursued with less political capital, less funding, and less follow-through than the scale of the underlying demographic and actuarial pressure would justify: a modest, incremental retirement-age adjustment here, a fertility-incentive program there that everyone privately expects to underperform its stated goals, an immigration policy calibrated as much to minimize domestic political blowback as to close the actual labor-market gap, and a general, largely rhetorical faith that AI and automation will eventually pick up whatever slack the other three options leave behind. This is not, on the whole, a story of governments failing to understand the problem; the published trust-fund depletion dates, dependency-ratio projections, and pronatalist-spending tallies examined throughout this book demonstrate that the relevant state institutions, in country after country, understand the arithmetic in granular, well-documented detail. It is a story of the four available responses each carrying a distinct, serious political or fiscal cost, in political systems that remain, for entirely ordinary and recognizable democratic reasons, more responsive to the visible, near-term cost of any one of these choices than to the diffuse, compounding, multi-decade cost of choosing none of them decisively enough — which leaves the fifth, unstated option, a benefit cut or tax increase eventually forced by depleted reserves rather than chosen deliberately in advance, as the default outcome nearly every government examined in this book appears, in practice, to be drifting toward, one modest and insufficient policy adjustment at a time.
