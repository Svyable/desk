# After the Repricing

Edmond Halley never found out whether his Breslau table would still describe the world a century after he built it. He couldn't have; the question only answers itself in retrospect, decade by decade, as new records accumulate and someone eventually sits down to compare them against the old assumption. That is still, after three and a third centuries of increasingly sophisticated actuarial science, the only way this kind of question ever gets answered. Nobody alive today will know, with certainty, whether this book's central argument was right until long after most of its readers have found out the answer to the much more personal version of the same question: how long they, individually, actually got to live, and in what condition.

That is not a reason to stop asking the aggregate question in the meantime, and it is not, this closing chapter will argue, a reason for the answer to remain as invisible, institution by institution, as this book has found it to be. What follows is not a confident forecast of exactly how the twin pressures this book has traced — a biotechnology industry beginning, unevenly and with a long trail of expensive failures, to treat aging as a target; a demographic collapse already well underway across a meaningful share of the world's largest economies — will resolve over the coming decades. It is a closer look at the handful of plausible shapes that resolution could take, and at what each of them would actually require from the institutions this book has spent its chapters examining.

## The scenario where mostly nothing changes

The least dramatic outcome, and on the evidence assembled in this book's own falsification chapter, a genuinely plausible one, is that the coming decades look a great deal like the last one: continued incremental gains in life expectancy in wealthy countries, punctuated by occasional setbacks like the American opioid crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic; a longevity biotech sector that continues to raise substantial capital and continues to produce mostly disappointing clinical results, with perhaps one or two genuinely useful but narrow therapies — a refined GLP-1 successor, an approved senolytic for a specific indication — reaching the market without fundamentally altering the pace of human aging itself; and a demographic decline across East Asia and parts of Europe that continues, slowly, generating real and rising fiscal strain without a single dramatic collapse, absorbed the way this book's chapter on actuarial history found the profession absorbing mortality shocks for three centuries: unevenly, with real political friction, but without full institutional failure. This is, roughly, the scenario in which the mortality discount this book has described turns out to have needed only ordinary, continuous revision rather than the kind of dramatic repricing its more attention-grabbing chapters have traced the early signs of. It is also, worth noting plainly, the scenario the actuarial profession's own mortality-improvement scales and the pension risk transfer market's continued, orderly growth through 2024 and 2025 both seem to be pricing in as their base case, which is itself meaningful evidence, from the institutions with the most direct financial stake in getting this question right, that this outcome deserves to be taken seriously as the single most likely path rather than dismissed for the sake of a more dramatic ending.

## The scenario where the science actually delivers

A second, less likely but non-trivial scenario is that one or more of the biological programs examined across this book's middle chapters — partial cellular reprogramming clearing its safety hurdle, a senolytic drug finally demonstrating clear benefit in a well-designed trial, or some combination of interventions targeting several of the hallmarks-of-aging mechanisms at once — actually works, at meaningful scale, within the next fifteen to twenty years. If that happens, this book's fourteenth chapter's warning becomes the central story rather than a cautionary aside: the gains would very likely arrive first, and disproportionately, for the wealthiest people and countries, widening a life-expectancy gap between rich and poor that the Chetty study examined in that chapter already found comparable in size to the entire twentieth century's average improvement. The institutions least prepared for this scenario are not, on this book's evidence, the ones most people would guess. Pension funds and insurers, despite the friction and occasional high-profile failures this book has documented — the collapsed 2004 longevity bond, Life Partners Holdings' mispriced life settlements — have the most direct financial exposure to exactly this outcome and the strongest professional incentive to see it coming and reprice accordingly, which is precisely why the pension risk transfer market examined in an earlier chapter has grown as large and as fast as it has: it is, in effect, the financial system's own hedge against this scenario materializing faster than expected. The institutions this book found least prepared are the ones whose exposure is diffuse, political, and resistant to the kind of continuous, professional revision an actuary applies to a mortality table: a national retirement age set once and defended for generations, an entitlement program whose depletion date is published annually without triggering the legislative response its own numbers imply, a labor market and career structure still built around a four-decade working life even as the biological plausibility of a much longer one grows.

## The scenario where the demographic story dominates

A third scenario, and on the currently available evidence perhaps the most likely of the three to matter most over the next two decades specifically, is that the biological story remains largely where this book's falsification chapter found it — real, serious, underfunded, mostly unproven — while the demographic story continues to worsen largely on its existing trajectory, with South Korea's recent modest rebound proving to be the temporary, marriage-driven blip most of the demographers examined in this book's chapters expect it to be, Japan's population continuing its now-sixteen-year streak of annual decline, and China's fertility rate continuing to fall toward, and perhaps below, the extreme lows East Asia's other major economies have already reached. In this scenario, the institutional strain this book has traced arrives less from any single dramatic biological breakthrough and more from the slower, already-underway mechanism this book's chapters on South Korea, Japan, and China documented directly: fewer workers supporting more retirees, a labor market straining under the specific mismatch between where AI can help and where the caregiving shortage is actually concentrated, and governments continuing to choose, as this book's nineteenth chapter found most of them already doing, an uncoordinated blend of modest, insufficient responses across raising retirement ages, funding fertility incentives, expanding immigration, and hoping automation closes whatever gap remains, rather than committing decisively to any single adequate response.

A fourth possibility deserves to be named separately, distinct from the three scenarios above precisely because it does not resolve cleanly into either the biological or the demographic story dominating: the two forces this book has traced could continue moving in their current, only loosely correlated directions simultaneously, at broadly their current uneven paces, for long enough that the institutions this book has examined face neither a single decisive crisis nor a single decisive breakthrough, but a slow, compounding, multi-decade drift in which pension systems, insurers, labor markets, and family-formation patterns each absorb a little more strain every year without any single year looking like the crisis year this book's more dramatic passages might lead a reader to expect. This is, in some ways, the least satisfying scenario to write about, because it offers no clean resolution and no obvious turning point a reader could watch for the way this book's twenty-first chapter's falsification tests describe. It may also be the most likely scenario of all, on the balance of evidence this book has assembled: not a single dramatic repricing event, but the same kind of slow, uneven, individually modest drift this book has documented in South Korea's fertility spending, in America's unaddressed Social Security depletion date, and in Japan's decades-long rural depopulation sequence — each, on its own, too gradual to force the kind of decisive institutional response this book has argued is genuinely warranted, and each, together, adding up to exactly the kind of accumulated, underpriced gap this book's opening chapter set out to trace.

## What an individual reader, as distinct from an institution, might actually do

This book has been written primarily about institutions, deliberately, because an individual reader's own choices about diet, exercise, savings, career, and family formation are already covered exhaustively by an entire publishing genre this book has tried not to duplicate. But it would be an evasion to close a book about the mortality discount without saying anything at all about what an individual reader, sitting with this book's evidence rather than an institution's, might reasonably take from it.

The first, most modest lesson is a version of the same discipline this book has asked of pension funds and governments, scaled down to a single life: hold your own assumptions about how long you are likely to live, and in what condition, as a working estimate rather than a fixed fact, and revisit that estimate periodically against genuinely new evidence rather than either the pessimism of a bad year or the optimism of a longevity headline. A fifty-year-old reading this book should not assume, on the strength of Bryan Johnson's protocol or Altos Labs' funding round, that a dramatically extended healthy lifespan is a safe planning assumption for their own retirement savings; this book's biology chapters have been consistent and specific about how far that science remains from a proven, broadly available result. The same reader should also not assume, on the strength of Olshansky's skepticism or Unity Biotechnology's failed trial, that no further gains are coming at all; this book's second chapter found the GLP-1 drug class alone delivering a real, already-proven mortality benefit that has nothing to do with the more speculative geroscience research examined later in this book, and a diet and exercise regimen consistent with unglamorous, well-supported public health advice — plausibly including the genuine, if evidentially contested, lessons the Blue Zones brand popularized even where its most extreme longevity claims have been challenged — remains one of the better-evidenced individual interventions available regardless of how the more speculative biotechnology examined in this book eventually resolves.

The second lesson concerns institutions this reader does not control, but does depend on: a retirement age, a pension formula, a Social Security benefit, an insurance policy's terms. This book's evidence suggests these institutions are, on the whole, moving more slowly than the underlying reality justifies, which argues for an individual reader treating any single institution's current promise with the same working-estimate skepticism this book has asked of the institutions themselves — not paranoid distrust, but the ordinary, actuarial humility of building a personal financial plan that would survive a modest downward revision to Social Security's promised benefit, described in this book's eighth chapter, or a modest upward revision to a personally relevant retirement age, without being catastrophically dependent on either institution keeping its current promise exactly as currently written. This is not a dramatic prescription. It is the same discipline this book has asked of every larger institution examined across its chapters, applied at the scale of a single household's own financial planning.

## What a society that took this seriously would actually do

Across all three scenarios, and the many blended versions of them that are, in practice, more likely than any single clean outcome, the same underlying prescription holds, and it is worth stating plainly rather than leaving implicit, because it is the one genuinely actionable conclusion this book's evidence supports. The institutions this book has examined do not need to correctly predict, years in advance, exactly how the biology and the demographics will resolve. What they need, and what this book has found most of them still lacking, is the discipline the actuarial profession itself has practiced, imperfectly but genuinely, since Edmond Halley first built a table nobody had thought to build before him: continuous, unflinching revision against the best current evidence, rather than treating a number set once — a retirement age, a fertility projection, a mortality table last meaningfully updated a decade ago — as a fixed fact that only gets revisited once the gap between assumption and reality has grown too large to quietly absorb.

That discipline is not primarily a scientific or actuarial problem; this book's chapters have found the relevant science, however incomplete, and the relevant actuarial methodology, however occasionally strained, largely adequate to the task of estimating where things currently stand. It is a political and institutional problem, of the kind this book's chapters on France's pension protests and America's stalled Social Security reform have documented at length: the visible, immediate cost of revising a promise downward, or raising an age, or funding a hard demographic truth honestly rather than deferring it, falls on people and politicians who can see it coming and organize against it, while the diffuse, compounding cost of not revising falls, unevenly and often invisibly, on people not yet old enough to vote, not yet born, or simply not yet aware that the promise made to them was already, quietly, out of date by the time they were old enough to rely on it.

This asymmetry between visible near-term cost and diffuse long-term cost is, in the end, the closest thing this book has to a single unifying mechanism behind every institution examined across its chapters, more fundamental even than the specific biological and demographic developments this book has spent so many of its pages documenting. Bismarck's Germany could have set its qualifying age lower than seventy in 1889 and simply run a more generous, faster-depleting program; it chose the number it chose partly because the fiscal consequences of choosing wrong would have arrived within a political generation, a cost immediate enough to concentrate the attention of the people setting the number. The American Congress that has, as of this writing, still not acted on Social Security's published 2033 depletion date is not ignorant of the number; it faces exactly the asymmetry described above, in which acting now imposes a cost on people who will notice and vote against it, while the cost of not acting is deferred onto retirees and workers who are not yet organized, not yet aware, or not yet in a position to hold the current Congress accountable for a shortfall that will land on a different Congress's watch. South Korea's government did not fail to notice its fertility rate falling for lack of data or lack of concern; it spent, by this book's own accounting, somewhere in the range of two hundred and fifty billion dollars trying to address it, an amount of money and political attention that reflects genuine alarm rather than indifference, and still found the underlying demographic momentum harder to reverse than the scale of that spending anticipated.

None of these institutions, in other words, failed this book's argument through simple negligence or a failure to understand the underlying mortality discount they were carrying. They failed, where they failed, through the much more ordinary and much harder problem of trying to act on a diffuse, long-horizon truth inside political and financial systems built, for entirely sound and recognizable reasons, to respond most strongly to whatever cost or benefit is nearest and most visible. Recognizing that pattern clearly, across pensions, insurance, retirement ages, immigration policy, and fertility incentives alike, is not the same as solving it. But it is, on the evidence this book has assembled, chapter after chapter, the actual shape of the problem — closer to a problem of institutional attention and political time horizon than a problem of missing science or missing data — and a reader who finishes this book with that distinction clearly in view has understood something this book considers more important than any single prediction about how long human beings, individually or collectively, are actually going to live.

Halley's table held up well enough, for long enough, to be useful — not because it was perfect, but because a profession built up around it kept checking it against the world and revising it when the world disagreed. The institutions this book has traced through pensions, insurance, retirement ages, careers, and family formation are, each in their own way, still deciding whether they are willing to do the same. The evidence collected across this book's chapters suggests most of them are not yet doing so at anything like the pace the underlying reality now requires. That gap — between what the evidence already shows and what the institutions built on top of it have so far been willing to revise — is the actual subject of this book, more than any single prediction about how long anyone, individually, is going to live.
