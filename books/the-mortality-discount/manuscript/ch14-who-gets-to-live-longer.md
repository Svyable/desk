# Who Gets to Live Longer

The richest one percent of American men already live, on average, fourteen and six-tenths years longer than the poorest one percent. Among women, the same comparison shows a gap of ten and one-tenth years. These are not estimates from an advocacy report or a back-of-envelope calculation. They come from one of the largest and most carefully constructed studies ever conducted on the relationship between income and mortality: a team led by the Stanford economist Raj Chetty, publishing in *JAMA* in April 2016, linked one point four billion de-identified tax records from the Internal Revenue Service, covering the years 1999 through 2014, to Social Security Administration death records, producing a dataset large enough to measure life expectancy by exact income percentile across the entire American population, rather than relying on the smaller surveys and cruder income bands that had limited earlier research on the subject.

The scale of the gap the study found — roughly fourteen and a half years, comparable to the entire gain in average American life expectancy across most of the twentieth century, described in an earlier chapter, existing right now, at a single moment in time, between two groups of people living in the same country — is worth sitting with before moving to the study's second, less-quoted finding, which matters at least as much for the argument of this book. Between 2001 and 2014, life expectancy for the top five percent of earners rose by two and thirty-four hundredths years for men and two and ninety-one hundredths years for women. For the bottom five percent of earners over the same period, life expectancy rose by just thirty-two hundredths of a year for men, and by only four hundredths of a year for women — a gain so small it is barely distinguishable from no improvement at all. The gap was not merely large. It was actively widening, in real time, even as the overall American population's life expectancy was, on average, still climbing.

## Geography complicates a purely economic story

The Chetty study's authors were careful, in their own analysis, not to present the income–longevity relationship as a simple, mechanical one, and this book's discipline about preserving genuine complications rather than smoothing them into a cleaner headline requires taking that caution seriously. The size of the gap between rich and poor varied substantially by geography in ways that income alone does not explain. Some high-poverty areas of the country — certain parts of California were specifically noted in coverage of the findings — showed rising life expectancy among low-income residents over the study period, even as other, comparably poor areas elsewhere in the country saw low-income life expectancy stagnate or decline. If income were the whole story, the same income level should produce roughly the same health outcome regardless of location. It doesn't, which points toward local factors — health behaviors, the quality and accessibility of the local healthcare system, environmental conditions, social cohesion, even municipal public health policy — mattering independently of income itself, and interacting with it in ways the aggregate national figures obscure. The honest summary the study's own authors reached, and the one this chapter adopts, is not "money buys a fixed number of extra years of life" but something closer to: the gap between rich and poor Americans' life expectancy is enormous, real, and geographically variable enough that income alone is a necessary but not sufficient explanation for it — a genuinely useful complication for a reader tempted to reduce the whole subject to a single clean number.

## What the gap is actually made of

It is worth pausing on the mechanisms that plausibly drive a gap this large, because the Chetty study itself was better positioned to measure the size of the gap precisely than to fully explain every cause behind it, and later research building on its findings has pointed toward a fairly consistent set of contributing factors. Smoking rates differ substantially by income in the United States, with lower-income Americans smoking at meaningfully higher rates than higher earners, a single behavioral factor with an outsized, well-documented effect on lifespan given smoking's established links to heart disease, cancer, and respiratory illness. Obesity and metabolic disease follow a broadly similar income gradient, connected in turn to food environments that differ sharply by neighborhood income — a well-resourced area with accessible grocery stores and safe places to exercise outdoors offers a materially different set of default choices than a lower-income area with fewer such resources, independent of any individual's personal discipline or health knowledge. Access to consistent, high-quality healthcare tracks income closely in the American system specifically, given its heavy reliance on employer-sponsored insurance, which means a lower-income worker is both more likely to lack insurance entirely and more likely, when insured, to carry a plan with higher out-of-pocket costs that discourage routine preventive care and early treatment of emerging problems. Chronic stress associated with financial precarity, unstable housing, and physically demanding or hazardous occupations compounds all of the above, through biological pathways — elevated cortisol, cardiovascular strain, disrupted sleep — that public health researchers have connected to accelerated aging and earlier mortality independent of any single specific disease. None of these mechanisms is exotic or surprising in isolation; what the Chetty study's scale demonstrated was how large an effect they produce when they compound together, consistently, across an entire adult lifetime, for tens of millions of Americans at the lower end of the income distribution.

## What a fourteen-year head start means for everything else in this book

Set the Chetty findings against the material covered in earlier chapters and a specific, uncomfortable pattern emerges, one that runs through nearly every institution and every scientific development this book has examined so far. A fourteen-year gap in life expectancy that already exists, and was widening even before any of the newer longevity science discussed in this book's biology chapters had reached the market, is the baseline onto which every subsequent development in this field lands — and very little about that baseline suggests the newer developments will narrow it.

Return to Bryan Johnson's roughly two-million-dollar-a-year Blueprint protocol, described in an earlier chapter: a regimen explicitly, structurally unavailable to anyone without personal wealth on a scale that places them, by definition, well inside the income percentiles the Chetty study found already living considerably longer than average. Return to Altos Labs' three-billion-dollar opening funding round and Retro Biosciences' billion-dollar-plus valuation, both chasing therapies that, even if they eventually clear the safety and efficacy hurdles described in this book's chapters on partial reprogramming and senescent-cell clearance, will almost certainly arrive first, and at the highest initial price, in exactly the income brackets that already enjoy the largest life-expectancy advantage — the same pattern that has played out with essentially every major medical innovation in modern history, from early cardiac interventions to the first generations of cancer immunotherapy, where a genuine breakthrough reaches wealthy, well-insured patients years before it becomes broadly accessible, if it ever fully does. Even the GLP-1 drugs examined in an earlier chapter, unusually inexpensive to manufacture relative to their demonstrated cardiovascular benefit, remain, as of this writing, priced high enough in the United States and constrained enough by the insurance-coverage disputes described in that chapter that access still correlates closely with income and employer-provided insurance quality, rather than with medical need alone.

Cancer immunotherapy, invoked briefly above as a comparison, is worth pausing on for a moment longer, because its own recent history is a close enough analogue to be instructive in some detail. The first genuinely transformative checkpoint-inhibitor immunotherapies reached approval in the early 2010s at list prices commonly running well over one hundred thousand dollars for a course of treatment, available in practice to patients with strong insurance coverage or access to a well-resourced academic medical center willing to help navigate the approval and reimbursement process, while patients without that same access frequently could not obtain the same drugs at all, or obtained them only after delays measured in months during which a cancer's own progression made the eventual treatment less likely to succeed. More than a decade later, several of the original checkpoint inhibitors have lost patent protection or face biosimilar competition, broader insurance coverage has become standard across most wealthy-country healthcare systems, and access has widened considerably, even as newer, still-patented immunotherapies repeat the same expensive, unevenly accessible pattern the earlier generation went through. This is a genuine, real-world instance of the Preston-curve-style diffusion this book's second chapter described in the abstract, playing out inside a single medical category over roughly a decade — evidence that the pattern this chapter has warned about is not necessarily permanent, but also evidence, in the decade-long timeline and the immediately following wave of newly expensive successor drugs, of just how long the unequal-access phase can last, and how reliably a new, similarly expensive successor tends to arrive before the previous generation has fully diffused to a broad, equitable level of access.

None of this means every longevity development necessarily widens the gap further; a cheap, mass-manufacturable drug like generic metformin, if the TAME trial described earlier in this book ever secures the funding to actually demonstrate an anti-aging effect, could in principle be one of the more equitably distributable interventions this field has produced, precisely because it is already off-patent and inexpensive at global scale. But the honest pattern across the specific, well-documented developments this book has traced so far — expensive personal protocols, venture-funded biotech chasing patentable therapies, a blockbuster drug class still fighting insurance-coverage battles even after demonstrating a clear mortality benefit — points overwhelmingly toward a longevity dividend that arrives unevenly, layered on top of a baseline inequality that was already, by Chetty's own data, comparable in size to the entire twentieth century's average gain, and still widening at the point the study's data ends.

## An American pattern, not necessarily a universal one

It is worth being careful about how far to generalize the Chetty study's findings beyond the specific country it examined, because the size of an income-longevity gap is not a fixed law of economics; it plausibly depends heavily on how a given country's healthcare and social-support systems are structured. The United States is somewhat unusual among wealthy countries in the degree to which access to routine healthcare depends on employment status and employer-provided insurance quality, rather than being available on a comparatively uniform basis regardless of income, as in most countries with a national health service or a universal single-payer system. This structural difference gives reasonable grounds to expect that a comparable income-longevity study conducted in a country with more universal healthcare access might find a smaller, though almost certainly not zero, gap between rich and poor — since healthcare access is only one of the several mechanisms described above, and factors like housing quality, occupational hazard exposure, and neighborhood food environment plausibly track income to some degree in every developed economy, regardless of how its healthcare system is financed. This book's research did not turn up an equally large, equally rigorous income-longevity study conducted in a country with more universal healthcare coverage, which means this comparison should be read as a plausible hypothesis grounded in the two systems' different structures, not as an independently confirmed empirical finding; a reader interested in how much of the American gap is specifically attributable to the country's particular healthcare-financing model, as opposed to income-linked mechanisms likely to recur across any market economy, should treat that as a genuinely open question this book has not resolved, rather than a settled comparative fact.

## A two-tier future, priced in advance

This chapter's argument connects directly back to the actuarial and institutional material covered earlier in this book, and it is worth drawing that connection explicitly. A pension fund, an insurer, or a national retirement system prices its obligations against an average life expectancy for the population it covers — but averages conceal exactly the kind of internal variation the Chetty study documents. A retirement age set for a national population's average longevity is, by construction, generous to the wealthiest, longest-lived members of that population, who will collect benefits for years longer than the national average implies, and comparatively stingy to the poorest members, who may not live long enough to collect anywhere near the average number of years the system's own actuarial assumptions project for them — a distributional reality obscured by every single-number retirement-age policy this book's earlier chapter on Bismarck's seventy and America's sixty-five described, and one that any future policy response to a genuinely repriced mortality landscape will have to reckon with explicitly rather than continue to paper over with a single national average.

This distributional reality also runs directly through the mechanics of a flat, uniform retirement age in a way worth spelling out concretely, because the effect is regressive in a specific, easily overlooked sense. A wealthy worker who can expect to live, per the Chetty study's findings, into their mid-to-late eighties draws retirement benefits for roughly two decades after reaching a typical retirement age of sixty-five. A poorer worker in the same cohort, with a life expectancy fourteen years shorter, may draw benefits for less than a decade, having paid into the same system, at broadly similar rates relative to income, across an entire working lifetime. A retirement system that collects contributions on broadly similar terms from richer and poorer workers alike, but pays out benefits for a systematically longer period to the richer group simply because they live longer, functions, in this specific respect, as a quiet transfer from shorter-lived to longer-lived populations — which in practice means, given the well-documented income gradient in life expectancy, a transfer that runs disproportionately from poorer contributors to richer ones, the exact opposite of the redistributive direction most such programs are popularly understood to run in. This is not a criticism unique to the American Social Security system or any other single country's program; it is close to a structural feature of any retirement system that ties benefits to survival and sets a single, uniform eligibility age across a population with meaningfully unequal life expectancy, and it is a feature that becomes more consequential, not less, the wider the underlying income-longevity gap grows.

If the science examined throughout this book's biology chapters does eventually deliver meaningful, broadly available gains in healthy lifespan — a real possibility this book has tried to treat honestly rather than dismissively, alongside the equally real possibility that much of the current investment wave fails to deliver much of anything, as the senescent-cell and partial-reprogramming chapters detailed — the Chetty findings are a strong warning against assuming those gains will simply flow evenly across the population that currently lives, works, saves, and eventually retires under institutions built around a single national average. The more likely pattern, based on every precedent this book has examined so far, is a longevity dividend that widens an already substantial and already widening gap, arriving first and most fully for the people who least need an extra fourteen years of head start, and last, if at all, for the people the underlying institutions are, on paper, supposed to serve equally.

There is a genuinely more hopeful counter-history worth holding alongside that conclusion, examined at length in this book's second chapter: the Preston curve's own finding that twentieth-century medical technology, developed initially in the wealthiest countries at the highest cost, did eventually diffuse outward and become available at a small fraction of its original price, letting later-developing countries reach a given level of life expectancy at a much lower income threshold than the countries that pioneered the underlying technology decades earlier. Vaccines, antibiotics, and oral rehydration therapy were, at their moment of introduction, exactly the kind of expensive, cutting-edge medical technology available first to the wealthiest patients and wealthiest countries before broader diffusion made them cheap and universal. If the current wave of longevity biotechnology follows a similar arc — a possibility this book's chapter on venture funding treats as genuinely uncertain rather than either assured or impossible — the widening gap this chapter has documented could eventually narrow again, on a timeline of decades rather than years, the same way the equivalent income-health gradient narrowed for the older generation of medical technologies the Preston curve describes. Nothing in the evidence assembled across this book guarantees that outcome, and the specific economics of longevity biotechnology examined in earlier chapters — costly biologics and cell therapies, rather than the comparatively simple, mass-manufacturable vaccines and antibiotics that diffused most successfully in the twentieth century — give real reason for caution about assuming the same pattern will repeat on the same timeline. But the historical precedent is real, and a fair accounting of this chapter's argument should hold it in view alongside the more immediate, better-documented inequality this chapter has spent most of its pages establishing.
