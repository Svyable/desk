# Who Gets to Live Longer

The richest one percent of American men already live, on average, fourteen and six-tenths years longer than the poorest one percent. Among women, the same comparison shows a gap of ten and one-tenth years. These are not estimates from an advocacy report or a back-of-envelope calculation. They come from one of the largest and most carefully constructed studies ever conducted on the relationship between income and mortality: a team led by the Stanford economist Raj Chetty, publishing in *JAMA* in April 2016, linked one point four billion de-identified tax records from the Internal Revenue Service, covering the years 1999 through 2014, to Social Security Administration death records, producing a dataset large enough to measure life expectancy by exact income percentile across the entire American population, rather than relying on the smaller surveys and cruder income bands that had limited earlier research on the subject.

The scale of the gap the study found — roughly fourteen and a half years, comparable to the entire gain in average American life expectancy across most of the twentieth century, described in an earlier chapter, existing right now, at a single moment in time, between two groups of people living in the same country — is worth sitting with before moving to the study's second, less-quoted finding, which matters at least as much for the argument of this book. Between 2001 and 2014, life expectancy for the top five percent of earners rose by two and thirty-four hundredths years for men and two and ninety-one hundredths years for women. For the bottom five percent of earners over the same period, life expectancy rose by just thirty-two hundredths of a year for men, and by only four hundredths of a year for women — a gain so small it is barely distinguishable from no improvement at all. The gap was not merely large. It was actively widening, in real time, even as the overall American population's life expectancy was, on average, still climbing.

## Geography complicates a purely economic story

The Chetty study's authors were careful, in their own analysis, not to present the income–longevity relationship as a simple, mechanical one, and this book's discipline about preserving genuine complications rather than smoothing them into a cleaner headline requires taking that caution seriously. The size of the gap between rich and poor varied substantially by geography in ways that income alone does not explain. Some high-poverty areas of the country — certain parts of California were specifically noted in coverage of the findings — showed rising life expectancy among low-income residents over the study period, even as other, comparably poor areas elsewhere in the country saw low-income life expectancy stagnate or decline. If income were the whole story, the same income level should produce roughly the same health outcome regardless of location. It doesn't, which points toward local factors — health behaviors, the quality and accessibility of the local healthcare system, environmental conditions, social cohesion, even municipal public health policy — mattering independently of income itself, and interacting with it in ways the aggregate national figures obscure. The honest summary the study's own authors reached, and the one this chapter adopts, is not "money buys a fixed number of extra years of life" but something closer to: the gap between rich and poor Americans' life expectancy is enormous, real, and geographically variable enough that income alone is a necessary but not sufficient explanation for it — a genuinely useful complication for a reader tempted to reduce the whole subject to a single clean number.

## What a fourteen-year head start means for everything else in this book

Set the Chetty findings against the material covered in earlier chapters and a specific, uncomfortable pattern emerges, one that runs through nearly every institution and every scientific development this book has examined so far. A fourteen-year gap in life expectancy that already exists, and was widening even before any of the newer longevity science discussed in this book's biology chapters had reached the market, is the baseline onto which every subsequent development in this field lands — and very little about that baseline suggests the newer developments will narrow it.

Return to Bryan Johnson's roughly two-million-dollar-a-year Blueprint protocol, described in an earlier chapter: a regimen explicitly, structurally unavailable to anyone without personal wealth on a scale that places them, by definition, well inside the income percentiles the Chetty study found already living considerably longer than average. Return to Altos Labs' three-billion-dollar opening funding round and Retro Biosciences' billion-dollar-plus valuation, both chasing therapies that, even if they eventually clear the safety and efficacy hurdles described in this book's chapters on partial reprogramming and senescent-cell clearance, will almost certainly arrive first, and at the highest initial price, in exactly the income brackets that already enjoy the largest life-expectancy advantage — the same pattern that has played out with essentially every major medical innovation in modern history, from early cardiac interventions to the first generations of cancer immunotherapy, where a genuine breakthrough reaches wealthy, well-insured patients years before it becomes broadly accessible, if it ever fully does. Even the GLP-1 drugs examined in an earlier chapter, unusually inexpensive to manufacture relative to their demonstrated cardiovascular benefit, remain, as of this writing, priced high enough in the United States and constrained enough by the insurance-coverage disputes described in that chapter that access still correlates closely with income and employer-provided insurance quality, rather than with medical need alone.

None of this means every longevity development necessarily widens the gap further; a cheap, mass-manufacturable drug like generic metformin, if the TAME trial described earlier in this book ever secures the funding to actually demonstrate an anti-aging effect, could in principle be one of the more equitably distributable interventions this field has produced, precisely because it is already off-patent and inexpensive at global scale. But the honest pattern across the specific, well-documented developments this book has traced so far — expensive personal protocols, venture-funded biotech chasing patentable therapies, a blockbuster drug class still fighting insurance-coverage battles even after demonstrating a clear mortality benefit — points overwhelmingly toward a longevity dividend that arrives unevenly, layered on top of a baseline inequality that was already, by Chetty's own data, comparable in size to the entire twentieth century's average gain, and still widening at the point the study's data ends.

## A two-tier future, priced in advance

This chapter's argument connects directly back to the actuarial and institutional material covered earlier in this book, and it is worth drawing that connection explicitly. A pension fund, an insurer, or a national retirement system prices its obligations against an average life expectancy for the population it covers — but averages conceal exactly the kind of internal variation the Chetty study documents. A retirement age set for a national population's average longevity is, by construction, generous to the wealthiest, longest-lived members of that population, who will collect benefits for years longer than the national average implies, and comparatively stingy to the poorest members, who may not live long enough to collect anywhere near the average number of years the system's own actuarial assumptions project for them — a distributional reality obscured by every single-number retirement-age policy this book's earlier chapter on Bismarck's seventy and America's sixty-five described, and one that any future policy response to a genuinely repriced mortality landscape will have to reckon with explicitly rather than continue to paper over with a single national average.

If the science examined throughout this book's biology chapters does eventually deliver meaningful, broadly available gains in healthy lifespan — a real possibility this book has tried to treat honestly rather than dismissively, alongside the equally real possibility that much of the current investment wave fails to deliver much of anything, as the senescent-cell and partial-reprogramming chapters detailed — the Chetty findings are a strong warning against assuming those gains will simply flow evenly across the population that currently lives, works, saves, and eventually retires under institutions built around a single national average. The more likely pattern, based on every precedent this book has examined so far, is a longevity dividend that widens an already substantial and already widening gap, arriving first and most fully for the people who least need an extra fourteen years of head start, and last, if at all, for the people the underlying institutions are, on paper, supposed to serve equally.
