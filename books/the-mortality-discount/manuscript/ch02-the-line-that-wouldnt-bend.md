# The Line That Wouldn't Bend

An American born in 1900 could expect, on average, to live to about forty-seven. An American born in 2000 could expect to live to about seventy-seven. Plotted on a chart, the twentieth century's gain in life expectancy looks like one of the great uncomplicated success stories of modern civilization: a line climbing steadily upward, decade after decade, as though humanity had simply gotten better, in some general and continuous way, at not dying.

Look closer and the line stops looking smooth. It looks bent, in places nearly broken, by forces that had almost nothing to do with medicine directly extending anyone's old age: a flu pandemic that killed an estimated fifty million people worldwide in 1918 and briefly erased years of gain in a single season; two world wars; the introduction of municipal water chlorination and sewage treatment in city after city in the first decades of the century, which did more to keep people alive than any drug then in existence; the arrival of antibiotics in the 1940s, which took infections that had reliably killed young, otherwise healthy people and turned most of them into a course of pills; vaccination campaigns that, over the second half of the century, made diseases that had once been a routine cause of childhood death into rare events in the countries that could afford them. None of that is a story about aging. It is a story about not dying young — about closing off the ways a person in their twenties, or their first year of life, or their thirties in childbirth, used to die, so that far more people than before survived long enough to reach old age at all.

This distinction matters more than it might seem, because it is the hinge this whole book turns on. Most of the twentieth century's gain in life expectancy came from moving the left side of the survival curve — reducing early and mid-life death — rather than from pushing out the right side, the maximum age a human being can plausibly reach. Demographers sometimes call the resulting shape "rectangularization": instead of a gently sloping line where people die at a fairly even rate across the whole lifespan, the modern survival curve looks more like a rectangle, staying close to one hundred percent survival for decade after decade and then dropping sharply, in a compressed span, once people reach their seventies and eighties. James Fries, the Stanford physician who gave this pattern its most influential name — the "compression of morbidity" hypothesis, first published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1980 — made a version of exactly this point: average American life expectancy had climbed from forty-seven to seventy-three over the century he was writing in, but the maximum human lifespan itself had not obviously moved. People were far more reliably reaching old age. They were not, in any clear way, living dramatically further past it than the small number of very old people always had.

Fries's argument, built on that observation, was optimistic in a specific and careful way. If lifespan has something like a fixed biological ceiling, he reasoned, then medicine's job is not to push the ceiling higher — that may simply not be available — but to compress the period of chronic illness and disability into a shorter span right before that ceiling, so that people live in reasonably good health for nearly their entire lives and then decline quickly at the end, rather than spending their final fifteen or twenty years in slow, expensive deterioration. It's an appealing picture, and it is one this book will keep returning to, because it stakes out one clear end of a spectrum this whole subject sits on. At the other end sits the newer, more ambitious claim that the ceiling itself is not fixed — that biological aging is a process, made of identifiable, potentially addressable mechanisms, and that intervening on those mechanisms directly could do something Fries's framework didn't anticipate: not just compress the bad years, but genuinely extend the good ones. Chapter Three looked closely at how far the regulatory and scientific apparatus has actually gotten toward treating that second claim as real. This chapter stays with the first half of the story — what actually produced the twentieth century's gains, and what has happened to the line since, because the honest recent history complicates both Fries's optimism and the newer, more dramatic claims built against it.

## Picking the fruit within reach

It is worth being specific about what did the work, because "medicine got better" is too vague to be useful and slightly misleading besides. Clean water and sewage separation, engineered into cities across the industrialized world in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, eliminated cholera and typhoid as routine urban killers before most of the twentieth century's famous drugs existed at all. Pasteurization and safer food handling reduced another broad category of infectious death. The introduction of antibiotics in the 1940s took bacterial infections — pneumonia, sepsis, infected wounds — that had killed people at every age, including the young and previously healthy, and made most of them survivable. Vaccination programs, expanding steadily through the century, removed diseases like smallpox, polio, measles, and diphtheria from the list of things a child in a rich country needed to survive to reach adulthood. Advances in obstetric and neonatal care sharply cut maternal and infant mortality, two categories that, when they were common, dragged average life expectancy down disproportionately, because a death in infancy or childbirth erases an entire lifetime of potential years in the statistics, while a death at seventy-five erases relatively few. And rising income, more broadly, funded all of the above and added its own separate effect, through better nutrition, safer housing, and eventually safer workplaces.

Every one of those interventions shares a common shape: they worked by preventing death from something that was not, in any meaningful sense, aging. A twenty-five-year-old who died of typhoid in 1890 was not old. A newborn who died of a preventable infection in 1920 had not aged at all. The extraordinary twentieth-century gain in life expectancy came overwhelmingly from stopping death that had nothing to do with the biological process that eventually kills nearly everyone who is fortunate enough to avoid every other cause first. This is precisely the observation behind the framing, discussed by biodemographers like S. Jay Olshansky and increasingly common across the field, that the twentieth century picked the low-hanging fruit: the categories of death that could be prevented by public health infrastructure, vaccination, and acute-care medicine, rather than by altering the pace of aging itself, were both extremely common and, relatively speaking, easier to address. Once most of that fruit had been picked — once infectious disease, childbirth, and infant mortality had stopped being major causes of death across most of a population's lifespan — further gains in life expectancy had to come increasingly from somewhere else: from managing chronic disease in the old rather than preventing acute death in the young, a much harder and slower kind of medical progress to achieve, and eventually, if the more ambitious geroscience program pans out, from addressing aging itself as later chapters describe. This is not a claim that medicine stopped improving after 1970 or that further gains became impossible. It is a claim about where the easy gains were, and a caution about assuming the same pace of progress will simply continue by extrapolation once the easy gains run out.

## The line breaks

For most of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries in the United States, the line did keep climbing, slowly, a fraction of a year most years, the ordinary background hum of continued public health and medical improvement. Then it didn't.

The reversal that gets the most attention, understandably, is the pandemic one. According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's National Center for Health Statistics, American life expectancy at birth fell for two consecutive years running into 2021, dropping to 76.4 years — the lowest level recorded since 1996 — after an already sharp 1.8-year drop the year before. Taken together, the two-year decline from 2019 to 2021 was the largest such drop in nearly a century, since the double blow of the 1918 influenza pandemic and the years immediately following it. COVID-19 accounted for the clear majority of that fall, an estimated three-quarters of the 2019-to-2020 decline and roughly half of the following year's further drop. But COVID was not the only thing dragging the number down, and the second contributor is the one worth sitting with longest, because it complicates any simple story about pandemics being a one-off, external shock unrelated to the deeper argument of this book. Accidental and unintentional-injury deaths — overwhelmingly drug overdoses — accounted for roughly a sixth of the further decline between 2020 and 2021 on their own. Nearly one hundred and seven thousand Americans died of drug overdoses in 2021 alone, with the age-adjusted overdose death rate climbing more than fourteen percent in that single year and roughly fifty percent across the two years combined.

That is not a story about biology reaching a limit. It is a story about a country's life expectancy being dragged downward by a public health and social crisis entirely within the reach of policy, in the same rough category as the infectious-disease and infant-mortality gains of a century earlier, just moving in the opposite direction. It is, in other words, exactly the kind of force that produced most of the twentieth century's gains, now working in reverse: not a failure of geroscience to bend the aging process, but a failure to prevent deaths among the young and middle-aged that had nothing to do with aging at all, the same category Chapter One's opening argument identified as having driven nearly all of the earlier progress.

The recovery since has been real, and worth stating plainly rather than leaving the story at its lowest point for narrative effect: U.S. life expectancy rebounded to 77.5 years in 2022 as COVID mortality receded, rose again to 78.4 years in 2023, and, according to final 2024 mortality data the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics released in January 2026, reached a record 79.0 years in 2024 — a six-tenths-of-a-year gain the agency attributed largely to declining mortality from unintentional injuries, COVID-19, heart disease, cancer, and homicide. Life expectancy at age sixty-five, the figure that matters most directly to the pension and annuity math examined later in this book, rose more modestly over the same year, to 19.7 additional years. But the rebound has been incomplete, and it has not been a return to a single smooth trend. Drug overdose deaths remained stubbornly elevated through the recovery, topping one hundred and ten thousand in 2023 even as the pandemic's direct toll faded, meaning the honest current picture is neither "life expectancy is collapsing" nor "the pandemic dip is fully behind us and the old upward line has resumed." It is closer to: a historic, multi-year reversal driven by two distinct and only partly related causes, followed by a genuine, multi-year recovery built from several different cause-specific improvements moving together rather than from any single mechanism, that has still not, as of the most recent data available, definitively closed the gap back to where the pre-pandemic trend would have predicted the country would be by now, let alone caught up with the life expectancy of the peer countries the United States had already been falling behind before 2020, for reasons connected to the same opioid crisis and to broader patterns of inequality in American healthcare that a later chapter returns to directly.

## Is the ceiling itself moving

Set the American reversal aside for a moment and ask a different, more fundamental question: even among the healthiest, longest-lived populations in the world — the ones with the best access to modern medicine, the most stable public health infrastructure, none of the specific American problems just described — is life expectancy still climbing at anything like its historical pace, or is it beginning to flatten out the way Fries's compression-of-morbidity logic would predict once a biological ceiling comes into view?

This is genuinely contested among serious researchers, not settled, and a book trying to price the mortality discount honestly needs to sit inside that disagreement rather than resolve it for the sake of a cleaner story. In 2024, the biodemographer S. Jay Olshansky and several coauthors published an analysis in the journal *Nature Aging* examining mortality data from the world's longest-living populations — the handful of countries and territories, including Japan, Hong Kong, and several in Western Europe, that consistently top international life expectancy rankings, plus the United States for comparison. Their finding: life expectancy at birth in these top-longevity populations had risen, on average, only about six and a half years since 1990 — a markedly slower pace of gain than earlier in the twentieth century. Olshansky, who has argued a version of this position since the early 1990s, framed the finding as evidence that humanity is approaching something like a biologically grounded ceiling on lifespan under current medical approaches, predicting in accompanying commentary that maximum population life expectancy would likely stabilize somewhere in the high eighties absent a genuine breakthrough that changes the biology of aging itself, rather than simply continuing to prevent death from disease.

That is not, however, the last word, and it is important that it not be treated as one. A 2025 preprint by other researchers pushed back directly against Olshansky's plateau thesis, arguing on a reanalysis of similar data that human life expectancy is, in fact, still rising, and that the deceleration Olshansky's group identified is more modest or more population-specific than the 2024 paper suggested. Neither side of this argument has, as of this writing, definitively settled it. What the disagreement usefully establishes, for the purposes of this book, is that the question of whether life expectancy is still climbing at anything like its old pace, in the absence of interventions that directly target aging, is a live scientific dispute among credentialed biodemographers working from the same broad category of data, not a settled fact that longevity-industry marketing or its skeptics can simply assert in either direction.

## What the bent line means for the rest of this book

Put the pieces together and a specific, less triumphant shape emerges than either "life expectancy has always climbed and will keep climbing" or "the golden age of medical progress is over" would suggest. Most of the twentieth century's enormous gain came from preventing death that had nothing to do with aging — infectious disease, childbirth, infancy — and that category of gain has now been substantially exhausted in rich countries, leaving progress increasingly dependent on the harder, slower work of managing chronic disease in old age or, if the more ambitious science pans out, intervening on aging itself. Even within that harder frontier, the pace of continued improvement among the world's longest-lived populations is itself disputed among experts, not a smooth continuation of the old trend by default. And at least one major country — the United States, examined closely in this chapter, though hardly the only one — has recently demonstrated that a shrinking, not merely a slowing, line remains entirely possible, driven by causes as mundane and as devastating as a drug crisis and an underprepared public health response to a pandemic, the same category of force that produced most of the previous century's progress, now capable of running the other way.

This is the actual terrain the rest of the book needs to price. Not a guaranteed, ever-climbing life expectancy that biotechnology is merely accelerating, and not a fixed biological ceiling that no amount of science can move. Something messier: a line that climbed steeply for a hundred years by removing early death, that has slowed as the easiest gains ran out, that can still be pushed backward by policy failure and public health crisis on a timescale of a single decade, and whose future trajectory now depends on a genuinely uncertain scientific question — whether the aging process itself, as opposed to the diseases that have historically killed people before they reach it, can be meaningfully and safely delayed. The next several chapters look directly at the most serious attempts anyone has made to answer that question, and at how far, in practice, any of them has actually gotten.
