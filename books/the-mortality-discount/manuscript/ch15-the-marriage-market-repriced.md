# The Marriage Market Repriced

In 2016, the London Business School economist Andrew Scott and his colleague Lynda Gratton, a professor of management practice at the same institution, published a book with a title that stated its argument plainly: *The 100-Year Life*. Its premise was demographic rather than speculative — building on the same steady, historical rise in life expectancy this book's second chapter examined in detail, Gratton and Scott argued that a meaningful share of people born from roughly 2000 onward in wealthy countries could plausibly expect to live past a century, and that the traditional three-stage life course inherited from the twentieth century — a fixed span of education, followed by a single continuous career, followed by retirement — simply does not divide sensibly across a life that long. A retirement lasting thirty or forty years, financed by savings accumulated over a career of roughly the same length, is a fundamentally different financial and psychological proposition than the same arrangement was when it was designed, in the era this book's earlier chapter on retirement-age history described, for lives that ended, on average, only a decade or so past the retirement age itself. The book sold more than a million copies, was translated into fifteen languages, and was shortlisted for the *Financial Times* Business Book of the Year — a commercial and cultural success that reflects how directly its argument spoke to an anxiety a great many readers in wealthy countries were already, if inchoately, feeling about their own working lives.

Gratton and Scott's proposed alternative, a "multi-stage life" built from recurring cycles of work, learning, and rest distributed across adulthood rather than concentrated into three large, sequential blocks, is a genuinely influential piece of thinking, and this book has already drawn on adjacent material — Japan's Silver Human Resource Centers, examined in the chapter on demographic policy responses, are close to a real-world institutional embodiment of exactly this kind of thinking, offering older adults a way to keep working in a lower-intensity, more flexible form well past conventional retirement, organized as much around a sense of continued purpose as around economic necessity. It is worth being honest, though, about what kind of claim the "hundred-year life" framing actually is. It is a projection built by extrapolating the historical mortality-improvement trend forward, not a guaranteed demographic outcome — and this book's second chapter already showed that trend is neither smooth nor uncontested among the demographers and biodemographers who study it most closely, with S. Jay Olshansky's research specifically challenging whether continued gains at anything like the pace assumed by "hundred-year life" framing will actually materialize. A hundred-year life is a real and increasingly plausible outcome for a meaningful, probably growing share of people born in wealthy countries in this century. It is not yet, and should not be marketed as, the demographic default that every institution needs to redesign itself around immediately, and a fair reading of Gratton and Scott's own careful academic framing acknowledges roughly this much, even where the more popular, secondary retellings of their argument sometimes flatten the nuance away.

## Stanford's case for redesigning the map

A parallel and complementary institutional effort has developed at Stanford University's Center on Longevity, founded and directed by the psychologist Laura Carstensen. In 2019, Carstensen launched an initiative called the New Map of Life, built around the argument that a genuine, structural mismatch now exists between how long people actually live and the institutions, economic policies, and cultural norms that were designed for much shorter lifespans — precisely the argument this book has made, chapter by chapter, about pensions, retirement ages, and insurance, extended here to the more personal terrain of education, career structure, and family formation. The initiative's stated goal, in its own published materials, is to engineer what it describes as at least thirty additional satisfying years of vitality and engagement into the human life course, by replacing the traditional three-stage model with one built around recurring cycles of education and work distributed across adulthood, rather than treating education as something that ends at twenty-two and work as something that begins immediately afterward and continues unbroken for four decades.

It is worth being clear-eyed about what kind of institution is making this argument and why, in the same spirit this book has applied to every other advocacy-adjacent source it has drawn on. The Stanford Center on Longevity is an academic research center whose explicit mission is to promote longer, more engaged lives; its "thirty more years" framing is the center's own normative goal and rhetorical framing, not a neutral demographic projection independent of the institution's own advocacy purpose. That does not make the underlying argument wrong — the structural mismatch Carstensen describes between extended lifespans and institutions still calibrated to much shorter ones is, on the evidence this book has already assembled across its chapters on retirement ages, pension funding, and Social Security's own published depletion projections, a real and well-documented phenomenon, not merely an academic center's talking point. But a careful reader should hold the argument and its institutional source in view together, the same way this book has asked readers to hold Bryan Johnson's Blueprint claims against the fact that Johnson himself is both the experiment's subject and its primary marketer.

## Time horizon as a psychological variable, not just a chronological one

Carstensen's own most enduring scientific contribution, developed across several decades of peer-reviewed research predating the New Map of Life initiative, is a framework called socioemotional selectivity theory, and it offers something genuinely useful to this chapter's subject: a mechanism for understanding how a longer or shorter expected lifespan might reshape not just financial planning, but the emotional and relational choices people make well before they reach old age. The theory holds that people's behavior and priorities shift according to how much time they perceive themselves as having left, rather than according to their chronological age directly — as a person's perceived time horizon narrows, whether through advancing age or through other circumstances entirely, they tend to become more selective, prioritizing emotionally meaningful relationships and goals over the pursuit of novelty, new information, or an expanding social network. Experimentally, researchers working within this framework have shown they can shift younger people's stated preferences to resemble older people's simply by artificially shortening their perceived remaining time horizon, and can shift older people's preferences in the other direction by lengthening it — evidence that the underlying psychological mechanism tracks perceived time remaining specifically, not age as such.

This matters for the subject of this chapter because it suggests, at minimum, a genuine open question about what happens to people's relationship and family-formation choices in a world where the honestly expected, though still uncertain, span of a healthy adult life meaningfully lengthens. A well-established and widely replicated psychological theory built to explain emotional and social behavior does not, on its own, tell us how people will actually reorganize marriage timing, childbearing decisions, or career-versus-family tradeoffs in response to a longer expected horizon — extending socioemotional selectivity theory to make specific predictions about financial or family-formation behavior would require research that directly tests it in that domain, which, as far as this book's research was able to establish, remains a thinner and less settled body of evidence than the theory's well-established core findings about emotional prioritization. What the theory does responsibly support is a more modest but still useful claim: perceived time horizon, not simply chronological age, shapes how people prioritize their relationships and their remaining years, which means a genuine, durable shift in expected healthy lifespan — should the science examined in this book's earlier chapters eventually deliver one at broad scale, rather than remaining confined to the well-funded and well-insured populations described in the previous chapter — would plausibly reshape not just retirement planning, but the entire sequence of decisions, including marriage and childbearing timing, that people make across the whole of their adult lives, not merely at its end.

## The demographic chapters, meeting the institutional ones

This chapter sits at a genuine intersection of two threads this book has developed separately until now. The demographic chapters on South Korea, Japan, and the broader global fertility collapse described a world in which people, especially in the countries recording the lowest fertility rates, are already marrying and having children later, or not at all, for reasons that research in those specific countries attributes mainly to housing costs, labor-market precarity, and shifting social expectations around work and gender roles — not, primarily, to any change in expected lifespan. The institutional chapters on pensions, insurance, and retirement ages described a set of systems built around a three-stage life course that a longer expected healthy lifespan, whether from ordinary continued medical progress or from the more speculative geroscience examined in this book's biology chapters, is straining in ways those systems were not originally designed to absorb.

Gratton, Scott, and Carstensen's work, taken together, makes the case that these two threads are not actually separate: a society redesigning its retirement age, its pension funding assumptions, and its healthcare system around a longer expected lifespan will, whether deliberately or not, also be redesigning the informal life-stage template — when people are expected to finish their education, start a career, marry, have children, and eventually retire — that has quietly organized personal decision-making in wealthy countries for the better part of a century. Whether that redesign happens thoughtfully, through the kind of multi-stage-life institutional reform Gratton, Scott, and Carstensen are each separately advocating for, or simply accumulates as an uncoordinated pile of individual adjustments — people marrying later because a first career now stretches longer, having fewer children because the working years available for career-building have expanded but the biologically fertile years available for childbearing largely have not, retiring later because a pension system recalibrated its promises around a longer expected lifespan without correspondingly redesigning the working decades that precede it — is, itself, exactly the kind of institutional choice this book has tried to make visible throughout: not a question the science alone answers, but a question about which existing assumptions get revisited deliberately, and which simply keep operating on inertia until the gap between what they promise and what the underlying demographic reality actually delivers becomes too large to ignore any longer.
