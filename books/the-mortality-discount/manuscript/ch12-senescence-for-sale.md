# Senescence for Sale

On August 17, 2020, shares of Unity Biotechnology fell by roughly sixty-three to sixty-six percent in a single trading session, wiping out the large majority of the company's market value in a matter of hours. The trigger was a set of twelve-week clinical trial results for the company's lead drug candidate, UBX0101, a small molecule designed to treat knee osteoarthritis by selectively killing a specific category of cell that had, by then, become one of the most closely watched targets in the entire biology of aging: senescent cells.

Senescent cells are cells that have stopped dividing but, instead of dying off in the ordinary way, linger in the body, secreting a mix of inflammatory signaling molecules that appear to accelerate aging and disease in the surrounding tissue — a phenomenon researchers sometimes describe, evocatively, as cells that have refused to leave the party after the lights came up. The theory behind senolytic drugs, the category UBX0101 belonged to, is straightforward in outline: if you can selectively clear out these lingering senescent cells without harming healthy ones, you should be able to reduce the chronic, low-grade inflammation associated with a wide range of age-related conditions, potentially treating several different diseases of aging with the same basic mechanism. UBX0101 was, at the time of its Phase 2 trial, one of the furthest-along, most closely watched attempts to prove that theory worked in actual human patients rather than in the mouse studies where the underlying senescent-cell biology had first been demonstrated. When the trial reported no dose showing a statistically significant improvement over placebo at the twelve-week mark, Unity's stock collapse was not merely a bad day for one company's shareholders. It was widely read, across the biotech industry and the longevity field specifically, as a serious setback for the entire senolytic drug category — one of the first large, well-funded, publicly reported human failures of a therapy built directly on the hallmarks-of-aging framework this book examined in an earlier chapter.

Unity's own public explanation for the failure pointed to an unusually strong placebo effect among trial participants, a genuine and recognized problem in osteoarthritis research specifically, where subjective pain reporting is highly susceptible to expectation effects regardless of what the injected drug actually contains. Independent post-mortem analyses published by science-focused outlets in the aging research community offered a more technical and, in some ways, more forgiving explanation: the drug's poor bioavailability and delivery within the joint itself, rather than a fundamental flaw in the underlying senescent-cell hypothesis, may have been the real culprit — meaning UBX0101 may have failed to adequately reach and clear the target cells at all, rather than reaching them and having no effect. This distinction matters enormously for how the field should have responded to the failure, and it is worth being precise about it: a drug failing because it couldn't be delivered effectively to its target is a very different kind of setback than a drug failing because the underlying biological theory itself was wrong, and conflating the two — treating UBX0101's collapse as proof that senescent-cell clearance doesn't work in humans, full stop — would overstate what the evidence actually shows. The honest reading is narrower and less dramatic than either the initial market panic or a defensive industry rebuttal would prefer: this specific molecule, delivered this specific way, for this specific condition, did not outperform placebo in this specific trial. Unity itself responded by deprioritizing UBX0101 entirely and redirecting its research toward ophthalmology and neurology programs, an implicit acknowledgment that whatever the precise cause, the osteoarthritis bet had not paid off as hoped.

## Five years of small, honest, underwhelming results

If UBX0101 was the field's most dramatic public failure, the combination of dasatinib and quercetin — two existing, already-approved drugs repurposed as a senolytic cocktail, dasatinib originally developed as a cancer treatment and quercetin a naturally occurring plant compound sold as a dietary supplement — has become its most closely studied and longest-running human research program, precisely because both components were already approved for other uses and considered safe enough to test relatively quickly in small human trials, without needing to develop an entirely new molecule from scratch the way UBX0101 required.

The first human evidence came from a small pilot study led by researchers at the Mayo Clinic, published in 2019, testing just three days of oral dasatinib and quercetin in nine patients with diabetic kidney disease. The results were genuinely encouraging at the mechanistic level: senescent-cell markers in fat tissue samples dropped measurably within eleven days of treatment, exactly the kind of biological signal a senolytic drug should produce if the underlying theory holds. But a nine-person study with no placebo control is, by design, a proof-of-mechanism exercise rather than evidence of clinical benefit, and the studies that followed, testing the same drug combination against real clinical endpoints in larger and better-controlled trials, have told a consistently more modest story. A Phase 2 trial testing dasatinib and quercetin for osteoporosis, reported in 2024, failed to show a statistically significant benefit across the trial's primary endpoint for the treatment group as a whole, though researchers reported a positive signal in bone mineral density within certain subgroups of participants — a pattern that recurs often enough across this entire drug class to be worth naming explicitly rather than treating each instance as an isolated disappointment: a failed primary endpoint, paired with a hopeful-looking subgroup finding that the trial was not statistically powered to properly confirm, is close to the standard shape of a senolytic trial result so far, rather than the exception.

A subsequent, small twelve-week trial of the same drug combination in patients with mild cognitive impairment and early Alzheimer's disease, reported in 2025, produced results a research-tracking outlet in the field summarized bluntly as showing very few statistically significant results and no clear suggestion of effectiveness on the biomarkers most directly associated with Alzheimer's pathology, such as tau and amyloid protein levels — though a related, separate study focused specifically on patients with mild cognitive impairment found small, inconsistent improvements in a standard cognitive assessment score, moving by roughly one to two points on a scale where that magnitude of change does not reliably indicate a meaningful clinical difference for an individual patient. Taken together, five years of human trials testing dasatinib and quercetin across diabetic kidney disease, osteoporosis, mild cognitive impairment, and Alzheimer's disease have produced a remarkably consistent pattern: small, real, mechanistically plausible biological signals, paired with modest, mixed, and often statistically underpowered clinical results that fall well short of anything resembling a breakthrough — nothing close to what the more excited popular coverage of "senolytics" as a category has led a general audience to expect, and nothing close to a disproof of the underlying biology either.

## A graveyard, and what it actually proves

It would be easy, cataloguing UBX0101's collapse alongside five years of underwhelming dasatinib-and-quercetin results, to conclude that senolytic science has simply failed to deliver on its promise, and some skeptical commentary on the broader longevity field has drawn close to that conclusion. That reading moves too fast in the opposite direction from the industry's own hype, and it is worth resisting for the same reason this book resists overselling the science in the other direction: neither a single high-profile stock collapse nor a string of modest, mixed trial results across a handful of small studies constitutes a definitive test of whether clearing senescent cells can meaningfully improve human health. What has actually been tested, so far, is a specific molecule with plausible delivery problems in one disease, and one specific two-drug combination, repurposed from existing approved medications rather than purpose-built for this application, across a handful of conditions and trial sizes too small to detect anything but a fairly large effect if one exists. That is a real, honest, and so far disappointing track record. It is not the same thing as a comprehensive test of the underlying cellular-senescence hypothesis itself, which remains, at the level of basic biology, one of the better-supported mechanisms in the aging field — well short of clinically proven, but also well short of clinically disproven.

The gap between those two honest conclusions — real biological plausibility, alongside a consistent human clinical record of modest-to-null results across every trial run so far — is precisely the terrain this book keeps finding across the entire longevity science landscape, chapter after chapter: genuine, serious mechanistic science, funded at real scale, running years or decades ahead of the clinical proof that would justify treating any of it as a solved problem. A reader trying to price the actual state of senolytic medicine honestly should hold both facts in view at once, the way this chapter has tried to: an idea worth continuing to fund and test, and a field that has, so far, mostly delivered a graveyard of underwhelming trial results rather than the breakthrough its most enthusiastic advocates continue to promise just a few years out.
