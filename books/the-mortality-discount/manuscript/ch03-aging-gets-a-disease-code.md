# Aging Gets a Disease Code

Nir Barzilai has spent the better part of a decade trying to give away a cheap pill.

Metformin costs a few cents a dose. It has been prescribed to people with type 2 diabetes since the late 1950s, it is off patent in every market that matters, and no pharmaceutical company earns meaningful margin from selling it. That fact alone should make it a strange candidate for the most consequential drug trial nobody has been able to fully fund. But metformin keeps turning up in the wrong place in observational studies: diabetics who take it appear, in some analyses, to develop cancer, cardiovascular disease, and dementia later than people who don't have diabetes at all. Not just later than untreated diabetics, which would be unremarkable. Later than the general population, which manages its blood sugar the old-fashioned way, by not having a disease that damages it.

Barzilai, a geroscientist at the Albert Einstein College of Medicine, wanted to test whether metformin was doing something to the pace of aging itself, and not merely fixing diabetes. The trial he designed, TAME — Targeting Aging with Metformin — would enroll around three thousand people between the ages of sixty-five and seventy-nine, none of them diabetic, and follow them for roughly six years to see whether a generic drug that costs about the same as a cup of coffee could delay the composite onset of cancer, heart disease, cognitive decline, and death. It has been described, accurately, as one of the most important clinical trials never conducted at scale. As of 2024, TAME remained what the longevity-research press calls "awaiting funding" — full financing pledged and re-pledged, restructured, and still short. Estimates of the total budget required vary by source and by year: reporting in 2024 put the figure at thirty to fifty million dollars; other tallies, including from the Alliance for Aging Research, cite a seventy-five-million-dollar budget with tens of millions already committed by philanthropic donors. The exact number moves depending on who is counting and when. What has not moved is the outcome: a trial serious enough to be built around the FDA's own procedural logic has spent most of a decade unable to close a funding gap that a single moderately successful biotech IPO could cover in an afternoon.

That gap is not really about metformin. It is about what happens when a scientific field tries to get an institution — in this case, a drug regulator — to recognize something it was not built to recognize.

## The problem with a disease that isn't one

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration approves drugs for indications: diagnosed conditions with defined endpoints. A cholesterol drug lowers LDL and, ideally, reduces heart attacks. A cancer drug shrinks tumors or extends survival. Aging is not on that list, and it is not obvious that it could be. There is no FDA-recognized diagnosis of "aging." A drug company cannot currently bring a compound to the agency and say, in effect, we would like to treat the patient's calendar.

This is not bureaucratic obstinacy so much as a genuine definitional problem that biology has not settled either. TAME's design gets around it with a workaround rather than a breakthrough: instead of asking the FDA to accept "aging" as an endpoint, Barzilai's trial measures time to a first occurrence of any of several age-related diseases — a composite endpoint built from things the FDA already knows how to evaluate. If metformin delays that composite outcome in a statistically convincing way, it would not create an approved indication for "aging." It would do something narrower and, in regulatory terms, more radical: it would establish a template. A future drug aimed explicitly at biological aging could run a trial against a similar composite endpoint and, for the first time, have a precedent-backed path through the agency that funded it. That is the real reason TAME matters more than its ten-cent-a-day study drug would suggest. It is not primarily a bet on metformin. It is a bet on getting a regulatory door to open, once, so that everything built to walk through it afterward doesn't have to argue from scratch.

The scientific case underneath that bet has its own history, and it is worth being precise about what the field currently agrees on, because popular coverage of "the biology of aging" tends to compress a contested, evolving research program into something that sounds more settled than it is.

## Nine hallmarks, then twelve

In 2013, a team led by Carlos López-Otín published a paper in the journal *Cell* titled, plainly, "The Hallmarks of Aging." It proposed nine cellular and molecular processes that recur across aging organisms: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of protein-quality control, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem-cell exhaustion, and altered communication between cells. The paper did not claim to have discovered a single cause of aging. It claimed something more useful for a field trying to organize itself: a shared taxonomy, a list that different labs studying different organisms and different diseases could use to describe what they were each seeing, in language everyone else in the field would recognize.

It worked, by the ordinary measure of how ideas succeed in science. The 2013 paper became one of the most cited papers in the biology of aging, credited in later commentary with helping seed something like three hundred thousand subsequent publications that use its framework, directly or indirectly. Ten years later, in January 2023, the same core group of authors published an update, "Hallmarks of Aging: An Expanding Universe," which did something quietly significant: it grew the list from nine hallmarks to twelve, adding disabled autophagy (the cell's internal recycling and quality-control system breaking down), chronic low-grade inflammation often called "inflammaging," and dysbiosis, disruption of the microbial communities that live in and on the body. The 2023 paper also tried to formalize what counts as a hallmark at all, proposing that a legitimate entry on the list has to satisfy three tests: it has to show up as organisms age, artificially worsening it has to accelerate aging, and — the test that matters most for a drug developer — reducing it has to be shown to slow or reverse aging in at least some experimental system.

That third test is the tell. The 2013 and 2023 papers were not written primarily as neutral natural history. They were written, at least in part, as a target list for people trying to build therapies. Growing from nine hallmarks to twelve within a decade is honest evidence that the list is not a finished inventory of aging's causes; it is a live working document, and it will likely keep growing as the underlying biology gets messier under closer study. A published, peer-reviewed critique of the framework — one memorably titled "The Hoverfly and the Wasp," after an insect that survives by imitating a more dangerous one — has argued the Hallmarks paper has the same weaknesses it was meant to fix in earlier, more piecemeal theories of aging: an overreliance on treating aging as accumulated damage, some circularity in how a "hallmark" gets defined, and a structure that functions as well as a way for disparate labs to feel like they are part of one field as it does as a rigorous causal theory. That critique does not mean the Hallmarks framework is wrong. It means the framework's main achievement so far has been organizational and rhetorical as much as it has been explanatory — which is a genuinely different, and more modest, kind of achievement than "cracking the code of aging," the phrase that tends to appear in the press coverage.

It is worth pausing on a handful of the individual hallmarks, because the later chapters of this book each pick up a different one of them as their organizing thread, and the connections are easier to see once the underlying biology has a little more texture than a bare list provides. Cellular senescence, examined at length in this book's chapter on senolytic drugs, refers to cells that stop dividing in response to stress or damage but do not die off the way a normally aging cell would; instead they linger, secreting inflammatory signals that spread dysfunction to the tissue around them, a bit like a smoke detector that never stops beeping long after the fire that triggered it has gone out. Epigenetic alterations, the hallmark most directly relevant to this book's later chapter on cellular reprogramming, refer not to changes in the underlying DNA sequence itself but to changes in which genes get switched on or off as a cell ages — a layer of regulation sitting on top of the genome, sometimes compared to sheet music sitting on top of an unchanged set of piano keys, that reprogramming therapies attempt to reset toward a more youthful pattern. Stem-cell exhaustion refers to the gradual decline, across a lifetime, in the body's reserve of cells capable of replacing damaged or worn-out tissue, part of why a wound or a broken bone heals more slowly and less completely in old age than in youth. None of these hallmarks operates in isolation; a substantial part of the difficulty in developing an effective anti-aging therapy, as later chapters document in more concrete clinical terms, is that intervening on any single hallmark in isolation tends to produce only a modest effect, because the others continue driving dysfunction largely unchecked, which is part of why serious geroscientists increasingly talk about combination approaches rather than a single silver-bullet drug.

## A hypothesis with a name of its own

The regulatory workaround built into the TAME trial's design has a formal scientific name in the geroscience literature: the geroscience hypothesis, the proposition that because so many of the diseases of old age — heart disease, cancer, dementia, osteoarthritis, type 2 diabetes — appear to share upstream biological drivers among the hallmarks just described, a single intervention that slows the underlying aging process should, in principle, delay the onset of several different named diseases simultaneously, rather than needing a separate drug developed and approved for each one individually. This is a genuinely different model of drug development than the one that has dominated the pharmaceutical industry for most of its modern history, in which a company identifies a single disease, develops a molecule that addresses a specific mechanism understood to cause or worsen that disease, and runs a trial measuring that disease's specific endpoints — a cancer drug measured by tumor shrinkage or survival time, a cardiovascular drug measured by heart attacks and strokes prevented, a diabetes drug measured by blood sugar control. The geroscience hypothesis proposes, instead, that a drug like metformin, or a future compound developed specifically to target one of the twelve hallmarks directly, could be evaluated against a basket of unrelated-looking diseases at once, because the underlying biological clock driving all of them forward is, on this hypothesis, the same clock.

If the geroscience hypothesis is right, even partially, the economic and regulatory implications extend considerably further than metformin's own patent-free economics. A pharmaceutical industry built for a century around single-disease drug development would need, in principle, an entirely new category of trial design, a new category of regulatory endpoint, and a new way of thinking about which diseases belong in the same clinical development program — exactly the kind of institutional restructuring this book's later chapters on actuarial and pension institutions find happening far more slowly, in every setting this book examines, than the underlying science that would justify it. TAME's composite endpoint, tracking time to a first occurrence of any of several different age-related diseases rather than a single named disease, is the first serious, large-scale attempt to build a clinical trial around the geroscience hypothesis rather than around the traditional single-disease model — which is precisely why its outcome, whenever it finally happens, carries weight for the entire field regardless of how modest metformin's own individual effect turns out to be.

## A code that isn't quite a diagnosis

The regulatory side of this story has its own quieter, more contested version of the same pattern: real change, oversold as more settled than it is.

In 2022, the World Health Organization's eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases — ICD-11, the coding system doctors and insurers around the world use to record diagnoses — added an extension code, XT9T, covering conditions described as caused by "pathological processes which persistently lead to the loss of organism's adaptation and progress in older ages." The proposal came from the Biogerontology Research Foundation and the International Longevity Alliance, advocacy groups that had spent years pushing the WHO to formally recognize aging within its disease classification.

It is easy to read a headline about this — "WHO adds aging to its disease list" — and conclude that a major global health authority has declared old age itself a treatable pathology. That is not, in fact, what happened, and the distinction matters for anyone trying to understand how far the reclassification of aging has actually gone. XT9T is an extension code, not a standalone diagnosis. In ICD-11's structure, extension codes are modifiers: they attach to an existing diagnosis to add information about it, the way a coder might note that a fracture happened in a particular location. A clinician can use XT9T to tag an existing diagnosis — arthritis, say — as age-related, but the tag does not, by itself, make "aging" something a doctor diagnoses and a drug treats. There is no ICD-11 code that says, simply, "this patient has aging."

The more interesting part of the story is what didn't survive. An earlier WHO proposal would have gone further, creating a standalone code — MG2A, informally discussed as "old age" — that could function as a primary diagnosis in its own right. That proposal drew sustained criticism from clinicians and bioethicists, some of whom warned it risked encouraging doctors to write off treatable conditions in older patients as simply "old age" and stop looking for an actual, addressable cause — a documented and long-standing problem in geriatric medicine sometimes called diagnostic overshadowing. The WHO withdrew the standalone old-age code. What survived was the narrower extension modifier. The honest summary of the regulatory picture, as of the writing of this book, is not "the WHO declared aging a disease." It is closer to: a limited, contested, partial recognition survived a more sweeping version of itself that didn't. That is a real shift in how global health bureaucracy talks about aging. It is also a much smaller shift than the more triumphant longevity-industry press releases tend to suggest, and the difference between those two descriptions is precisely the kind of gap this book keeps finding between the pace of the underlying science and the pace of the institutions meant to act on it.

## Why a classification code is not a footnote

It is worth pausing on why a seemingly bureaucratic detail like an extension code buried inside a global disease classification system matters enough to warrant its own section in a book about institutions and money, rather than being treated as a minor technical curiosity. The World Health Organization has maintained the International Classification of Diseases, in successive revisions, since shortly after the organization's own founding in the late 1940s, and the system now does considerably more work in the world than its dry bureaucratic name suggests. Every cause of death recorded on a death certificate anywhere in the world that follows WHO reporting conventions gets coded against it, which means ICD codes are the raw material underneath national and international mortality statistics — including, directly, the life-expectancy figures this book's previous chapter spent an entire discussion establishing and complicating. Health insurers, in the United States and many other countries, use ICD codes to determine what a claim is for and, in many reimbursement systems, how much a provider gets paid for treating it. Medical researchers use ICD codes to define study populations and track disease prevalence over time. Public health agencies use them to decide where to direct funding and screening programs. A code that does not exist, in this system, is not merely an absent label. It is, in practice, a condition that is harder to study systematically, harder to bill an insurer for treating, and harder to track at a population level — which is precisely why the advocacy organizations that spent years pushing for the XT9T extension code framed the effort in terms considerably more consequential than semantics: without some formal way to flag a condition as aging-related within the system essentially every national health bureaucracy and research funder already uses, aging-related research and aging-related clinical documentation remain structurally harder to aggregate, fund, and study at scale than research and documentation attached to a disease with its own established code.

This is also why the WHO's decision to withdraw the more sweeping standalone "old age" code, described earlier in this chapter, was not merely a matter of avoiding an offensive label. The clinical worry driving that withdrawal — diagnostic overshadowing, in which a treatable condition in an older patient gets attributed reflexively to "just getting old" rather than properly diagnosed and treated — is a well-documented, long-standing problem in geriatric medicine, predating the ICD-11 debate by decades. A doctor who can write "old age" on a chart, as a sufficient explanation for a patient's declining mobility or cognitive change, has less institutional pressure to investigate whether that decline actually traces back to a specific, treatable cause: a medication interaction, an untreated infection, a vitamin deficiency, depression, or an early-stage disease that would respond to intervention if caught in time. Removing the standalone code did not remove the underlying clinical temptation to reach for "old age" as an explanation, but it did remove the specific bureaucratic permission structure — a designated code a clinician could select instead of doing the more careful diagnostic work — that made the shortcut easiest to take at scale, across every health system in the world using the WHO's classification. The extension code that survived review, by contrast, does not offer that shortcut; it can only be attached to an already-specified diagnosis, preserving the requirement that a clinician identify what is actually wrong before noting that the underlying cause appears to be aging-related.

## What a decade of stalling looks like from the inside

Return to Barzilai's ten-cent pill.

By any reasonable account, TAME sits at an unusual intersection: serious geroscience credentials, a design built specifically to solve the regulatory problem described above, an off-patent drug with a multi-decade safety record in millions of diabetic patients, and a lead investigator with the standing to get major journals and funders to take the pitch seriously. And still, as of recent reporting, the trial remains only partially funded, propped up by philanthropic pledges rather than pharmaceutical-industry capital — for the specific and almost darkly funny reason that no company can patent metformin, and therefore no company stands to recoup the tens of millions a Phase 3-scale trial would cost. The economics that would normally fund a trial like this run backward here. A drug this cheap is, from an investor's standpoint, a bad bet regardless of what it might do, because there is no way to own the result.

This is worth sitting with, because it complicates a story that longevity coverage often tells in a much cleaner shape: breathless claims that "science is on the verge of curing aging," met by billions of dollars in venture capital chasing the cure. Both halves of that story are true in the sense that the money and the ambition are real — later chapters will look closely at where and how much of it has been raised. But the money has mostly not gone toward the specific, unglamorous, regulation-clearing work TAME represents. It has gone disproportionately toward patentable interventions with a defensible commercial moat: novel molecules, gene therapies, cell-reprogramming platforms, things a company can own outright. A trial that would benefit every future longevity drug by establishing that a composite-disease endpoint can get an intervention through the FDA — a trial that is, in a real sense, public infrastructure for the entire field — has had a harder time raising forty or fifty or seventy-five million dollars, depending on whose estimate you use, than individual companies have had raising ten times that for a single proprietary candidate.

That asymmetry is a small, specific instance of the book's larger argument. The institutions that would need to reprice human aging — regulators, insurers, pension systems, health systems — are not simply waiting for the science to arrive so they can act on it. The science itself is being shaped, funded, and sequenced by an overlapping set of institutions — venture capital, patent law, a regulatory apparatus built around discrete disease categories — that were not designed with a diffuse, whole-organism target like "aging" in mind. Barzilai is not fighting a purely scientific battle. He is fighting an incentive structure that rewards ownership over public good, and a regulatory category system that was built, reasonably enough, around diseases you can name, not around the clock that produces most of them.

## Provisional reclassification

None of this means the reclassification of aging is fake or that it will not eventually matter. The 2013 and 2023 Hallmarks papers gave a scattered field a shared vocabulary, and shared vocabulary is often the precondition for coordinated funding and coordinated regulation, even when it arrives years before either. The ICD-11 extension code, modest as it is, gives clinicians and researchers a formal way to flag age-relatedness in medical records at global scale, which is the kind of unglamorous infrastructure that later, more dramatic changes tend to depend on — you cannot study a category of disease systematically if there is no consistent way to record that a given case belongs to it. And TAME's design, whatever its funding troubles, represents a genuine and clever piece of regulatory engineering: a plan for how a drug aimed at aging itself could, in principle, clear the FDA without the agency first having to solve the harder philosophical question of what aging technically is.

What this chapter is not going to do is tell you the reclassification is complete, or that it is a formality now working its way through the pipeline. It is provisional, partial, underfunded relative to its stated importance, and still capable of being undone or narrowed further, the way the standalone "old age" ICD code was narrowed to an extension modifier after review. A reader trying to price the actual state of this field — which is, after all, the project of this book — should hold two facts at once without letting either one cancel the other: aging is being treated, by a real and growing part of serious biomedical science and by parts of global regulatory infrastructure, as something closer to a target than a fact of life; and the machinery for turning that reclassification into approved treatments, insurance codes, and clinical standard of care remains years, possibly decades, behind the ambition of the scientists making the case for it.

The following chapters look at what has been built in the space between those two positions — some of it substantiated, much of it not, and nearly all of it moving faster in press coverage than in the clinic.
