# Sixty-Five Was a Guess

The story gets told so often that it has acquired the smoothness of a fact: Otto von Bismarck invented the modern pension, picked sixty-five because hardly anyone lived that long, and thereby discovered the neatest trick in social insurance — promise people retirement after most of them are dead.

It is wrong in the most useful possible way.

Germany’s 1889 old-age and disability insurance law set the qualifying age at seventy, not sixty-five. Bismarck was seventy-four when the law took effect. The threshold did not fall to sixty-five until 1916, twenty-seven years after the original law and eighteen years after Bismarck’s death. The legend survives because it has the shape of something we already believe about government numbers: somebody chose one once, for reasons that have been forgotten, and the number outlived the world that produced it.

That part is true. The mistake is assuming that contingency means the number was never calculated.

When the United States created Social Security in 1935, its Committee on Economic Security also chose sixty-five. The Social Security Administration’s own history says the choice was pragmatic. Roughly half the state old-age pension systems then operating used sixty-five; the federal Railroad Retirement System adopted in 1934 used sixty-five too. The committee considered seventy and judged sixty-five the more reasonable standard. Then the actuaries checked the choice. Their studies indicated that a system beginning benefits at sixty-five could be made self-sustaining with what they considered modest payroll taxation.

So sixty-five was neither revealed by nature nor scribbled onto a napkin. It was a political choice that survived an actuarial test.

That is more interesting than the myth.

A retirement age has to do several incompatible jobs at once. It needs to be simple enough for millions of people to plan around. It needs to keep a pension system financeable. It needs to approximate something biological about how long people can work and how long they will draw benefits. It also has to survive contact with voters who do not experience an actuarial average as an abstraction. They experience it as Tuesday morning at a warehouse, a bad knee at sixty-two, a mortgage not yet paid off, a desk job they could plausibly do for another decade, or a promise they were told for forty years would still be there when they arrived.

One number compresses all of that.

The problem is not that the number was once a guess. The problem is that even a defensible number becomes political infrastructure once enough lives are arranged around it.

The other American retirement-age myth makes the same mistake from the opposite direction. Average U.S. life expectancy at birth in the mid-1930s was below sixty-five, which is sometimes used to argue that Social Security was designed for a population expected to die before collecting. The arithmetic is technically seductive and demographically misleading. Infant and childhood mortality pulled life expectancy at birth sharply downward. Adults who had already survived into working age had much better odds of reaching sixty-five, and people who did reach sixty-five could expect years of benefits afterward.

The useful question is not whether Franklin Roosevelt’s advisers secretly built a scam around premature death. They did not. It is what happens when a coordination rule built for one population survives into another.

Congress did move the rule. The 1983 Social Security amendments gradually raised the full retirement age from sixty-five to sixty-seven. But the timetable reveals as much as the destination. The increase was stretched across decades, protecting people already near retirement while pushing most of the adjustment onto younger cohorts. That was not cowardice. It was part of the policy design. Retirement rules are unusually hard to change quickly because people make unusually long plans around them.

This is one reason calls to simply “index the retirement age to longevity” sound cleaner in spreadsheets than they do in lives. Average longevity is not evenly distributed. A sixty-seven-year-old surgeon, electrician, home health aide, accountant and warehouse picker do not arrive at the same birthday with the same body, income, savings, disability risk or bargaining power. Higher-income Americans tend to live longer than lower-income Americans. White-collar work is often easier to extend than physically punishing work. A mechanical rule that moves the national retirement age upward whenever average life expectancy rises can therefore transfer more of the adjustment onto people who received less of the longevity gain.

The blunt national age is not merely an outdated relic. It is also a crude redistribution rule.

That is the strongest argument against this chapter’s instinct to keep the number moving. A retirement system needs legibility. A different retirement age for every occupation, health history and lifetime earnings profile would be more individualized and possibly more just, but it would also be harder to administer, easier to game and much more difficult to explain before people make decisions around it. Simplicity has value. The question is whether the simplicity is still buying enough coordination to justify the inequities it hides.

Social Security’s finances make that tradeoff unusually visible because the government republishes the arithmetic every year.

The 2026 Trustees Report projects that the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund, which pays retirement and survivor benefits, will be able to pay scheduled benefits in full until the fourth quarter of 2032. At depletion, continuing income would cover about seventy-eight percent of scheduled OASI benefits. The Disability Insurance trust fund is in a different position: its reserves are projected to remain positive through the seventy-five-year projection period. If the two legally separate funds are looked at together, their combined reserves are projected to reach depletion in the third quarter of 2034, with about eighty-three percent of scheduled benefits payable from continuing income at that point.

Those dates are dramatic enough that they invite a simple story: fewer workers, more retirees, longer lives, arithmetic eventually wins. Demography does matter enormously. But the Trustees’ own 2026 decomposition makes it difficult to pretend the countdown is a pure demographic clock.

The combined seventy-five-year actuarial balance worsened by 0.60 percentage points of taxable payroll from the prior report. Changes in demographic data and assumptions accounted for the largest single deterioration, 0.44 points, driven principally by lower assumed fertility and lower projected net immigration, partly offset by higher estimated post-pandemic death rates. But legislation and regulation also worsened the balance. Advancing the valuation period by a year worsened it. Changes in methods and program data worsened it. Updated economic assumptions, including stronger near-term productivity and real-earnings growth, improved it.

That is what a real long-horizon institution looks like. Mortality matters. Fertility matters. Immigration matters. Wages matter. Productivity matters. Tax law matters. Program design matters. The discount is not hiding in a single cell.

There is a temptation, especially in a book about longevity, to treat Social Security’s depletion date as a meter measuring how wrong society has become about lifespan. It is not. It is a fiscal projection assembled from several moving systems, one of which is human survival. That makes it less rhetorically tidy and more useful. A number can be transparent without being simple.

France has made the same point in public, with considerably more shouting.

In 2023, President Emmanuel Macron’s government enacted a pension reform intended to raise the legal minimum retirement age from sixty-two toward sixty-four. The reform triggered months of strikes and demonstrations and survived a political process so contentious that the government used Article 49.3 of the French Constitution to advance the legislation without a final vote in the National Assembly. Two no-confidence motions followed. The Constitutional Council upheld the core age increase.

If that were the end of the story, France would be a clean example of how difficult it is to change a retirement age even when a government decides the arithmetic requires it.

It was not the end.

France’s 2026 Social Security financing law revised the timetable for affected cohorts, partially suspending the path created by the 2023 reform. The resulting schedule is not one national number but a staircase: sixty-two years and nine months for some people born from 1963 through early 1965, then sixty-three for later 1965 births, sixty-three and three months for 1966, sixty-three and six months for 1967, sixty-three and nine months for 1968, and sixty-four for those born from 1969. A May 2026 implementing decree explicitly ties the new provisions to the suspension of the 2023 timetable.

The fascinating part is not whether sixty-two, sixty-three or sixty-four is the correct French answer. It is that a retirement age can be enacted, defended through mass protest, upheld by a constitutional court, placed on a long implementation schedule, and still become negotiable again before the schedule finishes unfolding.

That is what happens when a technical parameter becomes a social promise.

The usual reform debate treats retirement age as though the country were choosing a thermostat setting: identify the new demographic reality, turn the dial, endure the complaints. But a pension threshold is closer to a compression algorithm for heterogeneous lives. It takes differences in health, occupation, wealth, longevity, savings and capacity to work and crushes them into a rule legible enough for a state to administer. The rule cannot perfectly represent the population. It can only decide which differences it is willing to ignore.

That reframes the argument this book has been making about mortality assumptions. The danger is not merely that old numbers stay fixed while life expectancy rises. Sometimes the old number remains defensible. Sometimes increasing it would push the burden onto people least able to absorb it. Sometimes better policy lies elsewhere: taxes, contribution rates, immigration, benefit formulas, disability rules, occupational carve-outs, later-retirement incentives, or some combination that spreads the adjustment more deliberately.

The real failure is allowing the public number to become detached from the evidence and tradeoffs that once justified it.

Sixty-five survived because it was understandable, administrable and, at a particular moment, actuarially workable. Those are serious virtues. They are not a permanent warranty.

The next chapter turns to the demographic force making every retirement-age argument harder: not simply that people may live longer, but that in many of the countries carrying the largest old-age promises, fewer people are arriving behind them to share the bill.
