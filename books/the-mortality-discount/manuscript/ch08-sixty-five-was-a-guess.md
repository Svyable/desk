# Sixty-Five Was a Guess

The story gets told so often, in so many management books and opinion columns, that it has the texture of settled fact: Otto von Bismarck, building the world's first national old-age pension system in Germany in 1889, picked sixty-five as the qualifying age because his actuaries told him hardly anyone would live long enough to collect, making the whole program cheap and politically safe. It is a good story. It is also wrong, in a specific and checkable way, and the correction matters for understanding how thoroughly arbitrary — and how thoroughly overdue for revision — the retirement ages built into modern welfare states actually are.

A 2014 paper by the physician and medical historian Axel von Herbay, published in the journal *The Gerontologist* under the blunt title "Otto Von Bismarck Is Not the Origin of Old Age at 65," lays out the documented record. Germany's 1889 old-age and disability insurance law, passed by the Reichstag on May 24 of that year and signed into law the following month, set the qualifying age at seventy, not sixty-five. Bismarck himself was seventy-four when the law took effect — a detail that undercuts the popular version of the story on its own, since a seventy-year qualifying age set by a man already four years past it is a less obviously self-serving arrangement than the sixty-five-year legend implies. The age was not lowered to sixty-five until 1916, twenty-seven years after the original law and eighteen years after Bismarck's own death, making it historically impossible for the sixty-five figure — the number that later became the near-universal template for state pension systems around the world, including the United States' — to have originated with him at all. The myth persists anyway, repeated in enough secondhand retellings that it has effectively become common knowledge, which is itself a small, useful illustration of how easily a plausible-sounding number can calcify into an unexamined assumption once enough institutions build on top of it.

The correction matters for a reason beyond simple historical accuracy. If Bismarck's seventy, not sixty-five, was the real founding number, then the sixty-five that eventually became the global standard was not a carefully calculated actuarial threshold at all. It was itself a later revision — one country's political adjustment to an earlier country's political adjustment — that happened, by whatever combination of coincidence and convenience, to become the number nearly every subsequent pension system in the industrialized world would eventually converge on, including, more than two decades later, the United States.

## Sixty-five, again, for reasons that were also mostly political

The U.S. Social Security Act of 1935 set its own qualifying age at sixty-five, and this number has generated its own durable piece of folk mythology: the claim, repeated periodically in political debate, that average American life expectancy at birth in 1935 was below sixty-five, meaning the program's designers expected most workers to die before ever collecting a benefit, making the whole system a kind of accounting trick dressed up as social insurance.

The Social Security Administration's own historical materials address this claim directly, and the honest answer, as an independent 2016 PolitiFact fact-check concluded after reviewing the same data, is that the popular version is technically true but meaningfully misleading. Life expectancy at birth in the United States around 1935 was indeed below sixty-five — roughly sixty years for men and sixty-four for women, according to figures drawn from the Administration's own historical materials — but that birth-cohort figure is heavily distorted downward by high rates of infant and childhood mortality, which were still common enough in the mid-1930s to pull the average sharply below what an adult who had already survived childhood could actually expect. A person who had already reached adulthood in 1935 had meaningfully better odds of eventually reaching sixty-five than the raw birth-cohort number suggests — roughly half of American men who reached age twenty-one in that era, according to the same historical materials, could expect to live to sixty-five — and someone who did reach sixty-five could then expect, on average, a further twelve to thirteen years of life to collect benefits, not the brief coda the "life expectancy was under sixty-five" framing implies. The honest summary sits between the two competing simplifications: Social Security's designers were not building a program they expected almost nobody to collect, but they were building it around a population whose survival to old age, while common enough to matter, was less reliably assured than it has since become — which is precisely the kind of assumption a retirement-age policy should, in principle, be revisited as it changes, and precisely the kind of assumption that, in practice, tends to calcify into permanence once enshrined in a large federal program with tens of millions of stakeholders who have built their own retirement planning around it.

Sixty-five as a Social Security retirement age has, in fact, been revised since 1935, gradually and with considerable political friction — the full retirement age for Americans born in 1960 or later is now sixty-seven, phased in slowly over decades of legislative adjustment — but the pace of that revision has been glacial relative to the pace at which the underlying life-expectancy assumptions it was meant to track have actually moved, a mismatch this chapter's closing sections examine directly.

## The last time Washington moved the number

The single most consequential adjustment to Social Security's retirement age came in 1983, and its own history is instructive about just how much political difficulty even a modest, well-telegraphed change requires. By the early 1980s, the program's trust fund was projected to run short of money within months, not decades, forcing a genuine fiscal emergency rather than the slower-moving, further-off depletion date this book's later section on the current trustees' report describes. Congress responded with a bipartisan package of reforms, developed with input from a commission chaired by the economist Alan Greenspan, that combined several different adjustments at once: a gradual increase in the payroll tax rate, a new tax on Social Security benefits for higher-income recipients, and, central to this chapter's argument, a gradual increase in the full retirement age from sixty-five to sixty-seven, phased in so slowly that the change did not begin taking effect until people born in 1938 started reaching retirement age in the early 2000s, and did not finish phasing in completely until the cohort born in 1960 began retiring in the 2020s — a more than four-decade implementation window for a two-year adjustment, negotiated specifically to insulate the reform from the immediate political backlash that a faster-moving change would have provoked, by ensuring nobody already near retirement at the time of the law's passage would see their own expected benefits change.

That design choice, whatever its short-term political wisdom, created the exact structural problem this book keeps finding across the institutions it examines: a genuinely evidence-based adjustment, once made, gets treated as a new fixed point rather than the first of a series of adjustments that continued, real-world change would eventually require. The 1983 reform was itself built around life-expectancy and demographic projections current to the early 1980s; those projections have since been revised repeatedly, in directions that have sometimes made the fund's finances better and sometimes worse, without triggering anything resembling the scale of legislative attention the 1983 crisis provoked, because the current situation — a trust fund with a still-comfortable, if shrinking, reserve and a well-telegraphed depletion date years rather than months away — lacks the acute, immediate emergency quality that forced Congress's hand four decades ago. The 1983 reform, in other words, was not evidence that the American political system revises retirement-age assumptions readily. It was evidence that it takes something close to an acute fiscal crisis, months rather than years from actually running out of money, to produce even a slow, heavily insulated, decades-delayed adjustment — a bar the current, slower-moving 2033 depletion date, discussed in the next section, has not yet cleared, despite being publicly known for years.

## The bill comes due, on a schedule the government itself now publishes

Unlike the more abstract, decades-out risk sitting on private pension funds' and insurers' books, discussed in the previous chapter, Social Security's own exposure to an aging population is not hidden or speculative. It is published annually, in plain numbers, by the program's own trustees, and it currently reads as a countdown.

The 2025 Social Security Trustees Report, released by the Social Security Administration on June 18 of that year, projects that the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund — the specific fund that pays retirement benefits, as distinct from the separate Disability Insurance fund — will be depleted in 2033, unchanged from the prior year's projection. At that point, absent congressional action before then, the report projects the program would be able to pay only about seventy-seven percent of scheduled benefits from ongoing payroll tax revenue, an automatic across-the-board benefit cut of roughly twenty-three percent for every retiree then drawing on the system, regardless of individual need or circumstance. The combined OASI and Disability Insurance trust funds together, a broader measure sometimes used in political discussion of the program's finances, are projected to be depleted in 2034 — one year sooner than the prior year's report had projected — with roughly eighty-one percent of combined benefits still payable at that point. Independent verification from organizations across the political spectrum, including the nonpartisan Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget and the Bipartisan Policy Center, has corroborated these figures without meaningful dispute over the trustees' core numbers, even where they differ sharply on what policy response the numbers imply.

The mechanical detail of how that roughly twenty-three-percent cut would actually be applied is worth spelling out, because it clarifies exactly what kind of institutional failure this scenario represents. Current law contains no mechanism for the Social Security Administration to protect any particular group of beneficiaries from an automatic, revenue-triggered benefit reduction of this kind; the cut, should it occur, would apply as a uniform percentage reduction across every retiree then drawing benefits, from a retired schoolteacher relying on Social Security as her primary source of income to a wealthy former executive for whom the payment is a comparatively minor supplement to a much larger retirement portfolio. There is no means test built into the automatic-reduction mechanism, no provision to shield the poorest beneficiaries, and no graduated scale that reduces higher benefits by more than lower ones — an indiscriminate cut, in other words, falling hardest in relative terms on precisely the retirees with the least other savings to fall back on, since a twenty-three-percent reduction in a benefit that constitutes someone's entire retirement income is a far more severe blow than the same percentage reduction taken out of a benefit that represents a small fraction of a comfortable retirement portfolio. This is not a minor technical footnote to the depletion-date story; it is the actual, concrete shape of what "doing nothing" means as a policy choice, spelled out by the same trustees' report that publishes the depletion date itself, available to any member of Congress or member of the public willing to read past the headline percentage.

It is worth being precise about what this depletion date does and does not mean, since it gets garbled in popular discussion more often than almost any other number in American fiscal policy. Trust fund depletion does not mean Social Security runs out of money entirely and stops paying benefits altogether; ongoing payroll tax revenue would continue to fund the large majority of scheduled benefits indefinitely, even after the trust fund's reserves are exhausted. It means the program would, for the first time in its history absent legislative intervention, be legally required to cut benefits automatically to match incoming revenue, rather than drawing down accumulated reserves to bridge the gap the way it has for years. That distinction matters for how alarming the number should sound, but it does not make the underlying mechanism any less directly connected to this book's subject: the trust fund's depletion date moves earlier or later almost entirely as a function of demographic assumptions — how many workers are paying into the system relative to how many retirees are drawing benefits, and for how long those retirees are expected to keep drawing them — which means every one of the demographic and biological trends examined elsewhere in this book, from the fertility collapse discussed in the chapters ahead to the mortality effects of GLP-1 drugs discussed earlier, feeds directly and mechanically into a number the federal government is required to recalculate and publish every single year, unlike almost any other institution examined in this book, whose exposure to the same underlying uncertainty is far less publicly visible.

## What happens when a government actually moves the number

France offers the clearest recent example of what it looks like when a government tries to update a retirement age on a timeline that more closely tracks its actual fiscal exposure, rather than leaving the number frozen for generations at a time the way the United States effectively did with sixty-five for most of the twentieth century.

France's own starting point is worth noting before turning to its 2023 reform, because it illustrates how differently individual wealthy countries had already calibrated the same basic tradeoff well before this book's central argument became newly urgent. A legal minimum retirement age of sixty-two, the figure France was reforming away from, was already, by the standard of most other wealthy economies, an unusually generous one — several years below the sixty-five-to-sixty-seven range most other rich countries, including the post-1983 United States described above, had already converged toward. That gap had been sustained for decades through a combination of comparatively high payroll taxes, a political culture in which the retirement age has functioned as something close to a sacred entitlement rather than an ordinary adjustable policy parameter, and a series of previous French governments choosing, repeatedly, to fund the resulting gap through other means rather than directly confronting the age itself — which is part of why the 2023 reform, when it finally came, provoked the scale of resistance it did: it was not simply asking the French public to accept an ordinary incremental adjustment, but to give up a specific, long-defended national exception that had come to be understood, across a wide swath of French political culture, as a settled achievement rather than a negotiable policy input.

In 2023, President Emmanuel Macron's government pushed through a law raising France's legal minimum retirement age from sixty-two to sixty-four, phased in gradually — three months added per birth-year cohort — from September 2023 through 2030. The reform did not pass through ordinary parliamentary approval; Macron's government invoked a special constitutional provision, Article 49.3, that allowed the bill to become law without a full floor vote in the National Assembly, prompting two separate no-confidence motions against the government, both of which narrowly failed on March 20, 2023. France's Constitutional Council upheld the core of the reform on April 14, clearing the way for the law's promulgation the following day, while striking several ancillary provisions on procedural grounds unrelated to the central age increase.

The public response was not a quiet, technocratic adjustment absorbed without notice. Nationwide strikes and protests ran for months, from January through the reform's final passage in the spring, with individual strike days drawing crowds that different sources measured very differently depending on who was counting: the French Interior Ministry put turnout at a single early-March strike day at roughly one point two eight million people nationwide, while the CGT trade union's leadership disputed that figure as a significant undercount, putting the real number closer to two million — a familiar and recurring pattern in French protest reporting, where police and union crowd estimates routinely diverge by thirty to fifty percent or more, and where a fair account needs to attribute a specific figure to its specific source rather than presenting either side's number as a neutral fact.

Whatever the precise headcount, the scale and duration of the opposition illustrate something this book keeps returning to from a different angle: raising a retirement age, even by two years, even in a wealthy country with a government determined enough to force the change through over sustained mass protest, is one of the most politically costly adjustments a modern state can attempt to make. That cost is not primarily about the specific number two years; it is about the fact that a retirement age, once set, functions less like a technical actuarial parameter that can be quietly recalibrated as life expectancy and demographics shift, and more like an entitlement — a promise citizens plan entire decades of their working and personal lives around, and one they will fight hard, in the street if necessary, to defend against revision, regardless of how thoroughly the original number's underlying assumptions have since moved.

## An arbitrary number, defended as though it weren't

Put the three strands of this chapter together and a specific, uncomfortable picture emerges. The sixty-five that anchors most of the world's pension systems traces back not to careful actuarial calculation but to a chain of political convenience: Bismarck's seventy, revised downward to sixty-five for reasons of German domestic politics in 1916, adopted by the United States two decades later under its own distinct set of political and demographic assumptions, themselves already imperfectly matched to the underlying mortality data even at the moment of adoption. Once set, that number has proven extraordinarily difficult to move again in line with how much the underlying reality has actually shifted — American life expectancy at birth roughly matching the original 1935 retirement age, then rising by close to two decades over the following century, while the legislated retirement age itself crept upward by only two years across that same span, and even that modest adjustment took decades of legislative negotiation to enact. France's 2023 experience shows what it costs a government willing to move faster than that glacial American pace, and the Social Security Trustees' own annually published depletion projections show, with unusual bureaucratic candor, exactly what continuing not to move costs instead.

This is the retirement-age version of the mortality discount this book keeps returning to from different institutional angles. A number set once, under one set of demographic assumptions, gets treated ever afterward less as a variable to be periodically recalculated against new evidence and more as a fixed entitlement, defended by the people who have built their retirement plans around it, right up until the fund actually meant to honor that promise runs low enough for the government itself to publish a countdown to the day the arithmetic stops working. Sixty-five was never a carefully derived number. It has simply been defended as though it were one, for far longer than the assumptions underneath it have actually held.
