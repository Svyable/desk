# The Career That Doesn't End

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's own Employment Outlook, published in 2025, makes an argument that would have sounded strange in most earlier decades of writing about automation and jobs: it frames artificial intelligence explicitly as a tool that could help member economies overcome the aging challenge described throughout this book's demographic chapters, by boosting output per worker precisely as the working-age population available to produce that output shrinks. This is a notable reversal of the framing that dominated public discussion of AI and employment for most of the previous decade, in which the technology was treated almost exclusively as a threat to jobs rather than as a potential offset to a labor shortage that, on the dependency-ratio projections described in an earlier chapter, is already substantial and set to grow considerably worse across most wealthy economies over the coming decades.

The same body of research complicates its own optimistic framing almost immediately, in a way this book's discipline about counter-evidence requires taking seriously rather than glossing over. A May 2026 labor-market analysis from the job-search platform Indeed's Hiring Lab, examining where workforce shrinkage from aging is projected to hit hardest over the next fifteen years, found a structural mismatch sitting at the center of the AI-as-offset argument: the labor-market segments expected to shrink fastest due to population aging — elder care, manual trades, healthcare support roles — are overwhelmingly not the segments where current-generation AI tools are effective. Artificial intelligence, as currently deployed, is disproportionately good at augmenting knowledge work: drafting documents, analyzing data, writing code, summarizing information. It is, as of this writing, considerably less capable of bathing an elderly patient, repairing a residential electrical panel, or providing the physical presence and hands-on judgment a home health aide brings to a frail client's care. This means the technology most confidently proposed as a solution to aging-driven labor scarcity is well positioned to help offset shortages in exactly the white-collar occupations that are, relatively speaking, less acutely affected by the demographic shift, while doing comparatively little for the caregiving and manual-labor shortfalls that aging societies most urgently and directly need filled. It is not that AI offers no help; it is that the help arrives disproportionately in the wrong place, relative to where the demographic pressure is actually concentrated — a mismatch worth taking as seriously as the more familiar worry that AI displaces workers, since here the concern runs the opposite direction: not that a technology will do too much of the newly scarce work, but that it may not be able to do nearly enough of the specific kind that's actually running short.

## A career built to last forty years, asked to last sixty

Set that mismatch alongside the argument developed in this book's earlier chapter on Andrew Scott, Lynda Gratton, and Laura Carstensen's work, and a second, related strain becomes visible: the institutions that shape a working career — professional credentialing, hiring norms, skills training, pension accrual structures — were built, in nearly every developed economy, around an assumption of roughly four decades of continuous employment between the end of formal education and a fixed retirement age. A career genuinely expected to run for fifty or sixty years, whether because a longer healthy lifespan makes that many working years physically viable, because a strained pension system requires it financially, or both at once, does not simply need more of the same career. Skills learned in a first professional training in one's early twenties have a shelf life; entire industries rise and are automated away within a single such extended career, a pattern this book's own subject matter — the rapid recent transformation of drug development, insurance risk modeling, and demographic forecasting itself by AI tools — makes vivid rather than abstract. A worker whose career is expected to span six decades needs, functionally, several distinct careers stitched together, with genuine re-training and re-credentialing built in at multiple points along the way, not a single continuous accumulation of expertise in one field from twenty-five to sixty-five.

Very few institutions, in any country examined across this book's chapters, have actually been redesigned around that premise. Universities remain built overwhelmingly around educating people in their late teens and early twenties, with continuing-education and mid-career retraining programs typically treated as a smaller, secondary offering rather than a core, expected part of every worker's life course. Employer-sponsored pension and retirement-benefit structures, in most countries, still calculate accrual and vesting on the assumption of a fairly continuous, single-employer or single-industry career trajectory, penalizing the kind of career interruption a genuine mid-life retraining period would require. And hiring practices, across a wide range of industries and countries, continue to exhibit a well-documented preference for candidates in a fairly narrow mid-career age band, even in labor markets that are, by their own demographic arithmetic, growing more dependent on retaining and re-deploying older workers with each passing year — an inconsistency between what the aggregate labor-market data says employers will increasingly need and what individual hiring managers, evaluating individual resumes, still appear to select for in practice.

## Japan's answer, and its limits

This book's earlier chapter on demographic policy responses already described Japan's most direct institutional attempt to address this specific problem: the Silver Human Resource Center system, a network of roughly thirteen hundred centers nationwide matching more than seven hundred thousand retirees with part-time, community-based paid work, organized as much around a sense of continued purpose — the Japanese concept of *ikigai* — as around raw economic necessity, alongside a 2020 legal reform requiring Japanese employers to make a genuine, good-faith effort, though not a binding legal guarantee, to extend employment opportunities to workers up to age seventy. It is worth returning to that example here specifically because it illustrates, in miniature, both halves of this chapter's argument at once: a real, functioning institutional response exists, has operated at meaningful scale for half a century, and demonstrably keeps a substantial number of older workers economically and socially engaged well past the age at which most other developed countries' labor markets would have already pushed them out entirely. And that same institution, examined honestly, reaches a modest fraction of the population it would need to reach to fully close the gap this chapter describes — seven hundred thousand participants against more than thirty-six million Japanese residents aged sixty-five and older, a program that mitigates the strain at the margins rather than resolving it, exactly the kind of partial, real-but-insufficient response this book has found in nearly every other institution it has examined, from South Korea's pronatalist spending to the TAME trial's decade-long funding shortfall.

## What redesigning a career for sixty years would actually require

The chapter's honest conclusion is neither the techno-optimist version, in which AI simply absorbs the labor gap aging economies are creating, nor the purely pessimistic version, in which extended lifespans mostly translate into extended financial precarity for older workers pushed out of careers that were never designed to last as long as their own bodies and the actuarial tables described in this book's earlier chapters now suggest they might. It is a narrower, more specific claim: the institutions that shape how a career unfolds — education, credentialing, pension accrual, hiring practice — were built around a life-stage template substantially shorter than the one an increasing share of workers in wealthy countries are likely to actually experience, whether from ordinary continued medical progress, the more speculative geroscience examined in this book's biology chapters, or simply the blunt fiscal necessity of a retirement system whose trust fund depletion dates, described in an earlier chapter, are already published and counting down. Closing that gap requires more than a single new government program or a widely deployed AI tool, however useful either might be at the margins. It requires the kind of deliberate, multi-institutional redesign — of universities, of pension accrual rules, of hiring norms, of the entire cultural expectation about what a career even is — that Gratton, Scott, and Carstensen have each separately called for, and that, on the evidence assembled across this book so far, almost no country has yet undertaken at anything like the scale its own demographic and actuarial projections already say it will eventually need.
