# The Career That Doesn't End

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's own Employment Outlook, published in 2025, makes an argument that would have sounded strange in most earlier decades of writing about automation and jobs: it frames artificial intelligence explicitly as a tool that could help member economies overcome the aging challenge described throughout this book's demographic chapters, by boosting output per worker precisely as the working-age population available to produce that output shrinks. This is a notable reversal of the framing that dominated public discussion of AI and employment for most of the previous decade, in which the technology was treated almost exclusively as a threat to jobs rather than as a potential offset to a labor shortage that, on the dependency-ratio projections described in an earlier chapter, is already substantial and set to grow considerably worse across most wealthy economies over the coming decades.

The same body of research complicates its own optimistic framing almost immediately, in a way this book's discipline about counter-evidence requires taking seriously rather than glossing over. A May 2026 labor-market analysis from the job-search platform Indeed's Hiring Lab, examining where workforce shrinkage from aging is projected to hit hardest over the next fifteen years, found a structural mismatch sitting at the center of the AI-as-offset argument: the labor-market segments expected to shrink fastest due to population aging — elder care, manual trades, healthcare support roles — are overwhelmingly not the segments where current-generation AI tools are effective. Artificial intelligence, as currently deployed, is disproportionately good at augmenting knowledge work: drafting documents, analyzing data, writing code, summarizing information. It is, as of this writing, considerably less capable of bathing an elderly patient, repairing a residential electrical panel, or providing the physical presence and hands-on judgment a home health aide brings to a frail client's care. This means the technology most confidently proposed as a solution to aging-driven labor scarcity is well positioned to help offset shortages in exactly the white-collar occupations that are, relatively speaking, less acutely affected by the demographic shift, while doing comparatively little for the caregiving and manual-labor shortfalls that aging societies most urgently and directly need filled. It is not that AI offers no help; it is that the help arrives disproportionately in the wrong place, relative to where the demographic pressure is actually concentrated — a mismatch worth taking as seriously as the more familiar worry that AI displaces workers, since here the concern runs the opposite direction: not that a technology will do too much of the newly scarce work, but that it may not be able to do nearly enough of the specific kind that's actually running short.

It is worth being precise about why elder care in particular resists this kind of technological substitution so far more than an office job does, because the reasons are instructive rather than simply a temporary gap current-generation AI tools will eventually close. Caring for a frail older adult combines physical tasks — lifting, bathing, mobility assistance — that require robotic dexterity and physical presence current robotics remains far from matching outside narrow, controlled laboratory demonstrations, with judgment tasks — recognizing subtle changes in a patient's condition, providing genuine emotional reassurance, navigating a family's difficult decisions about a loved one's care — that current AI systems, built overwhelmingly around processing and generating text and other digital information, are not well suited to performing directly, however useful they may become as a supporting tool for the human caregivers who remain necessary. Japan, the country this book's earlier chapters have found furthest along in confronting an aging population's care needs, has invested more heavily and for longer in eldercare robotics research than almost any other country, producing genuinely useful assistive devices — robotic lifting aids that reduce physical strain on human caregivers, monitoring systems that alert staff to a resident's fall or distress — without, as of this writing, having deployed anything resembling full robotic replacement for the hands-on, judgment-intensive core of elder care work. This is not a failure of Japanese engineering; it is evidence that the specific combination of physical dexterity and situational judgment elder care requires sits at the harder end of what current robotics and AI can address, a harder end than the drafting, analysis, and summarization tasks dominating AI's most visible successes elsewhere in the economy.

## A career built to last forty years, asked to last sixty

Set that mismatch alongside the argument developed in this book's earlier chapter on Andrew Scott, Lynda Gratton, and Laura Carstensen's work, and a second, related strain becomes visible: the institutions that shape a working career — professional credentialing, hiring norms, skills training, pension accrual structures — were built, in nearly every developed economy, around an assumption of roughly four decades of continuous employment between the end of formal education and a fixed retirement age. A career genuinely expected to run for fifty or sixty years, whether because a longer healthy lifespan makes that many working years physically viable, because a strained pension system requires it financially, or both at once, does not simply need more of the same career. Skills learned in a first professional training in one's early twenties have a shelf life; entire industries rise and are automated away within a single such extended career, a pattern this book's own subject matter — the rapid recent transformation of drug development, insurance risk modeling, and demographic forecasting itself by AI tools — makes vivid rather than abstract. A worker whose career is expected to span six decades needs, functionally, several distinct careers stitched together, with genuine re-training and re-credentialing built in at multiple points along the way, not a single continuous accumulation of expertise in one field from twenty-five to sixty-five.

Very few institutions, in any country examined across this book's chapters, have actually been redesigned around that premise. Universities remain built overwhelmingly around educating people in their late teens and early twenties, with continuing-education and mid-career retraining programs typically treated as a smaller, secondary offering rather than a core, expected part of every worker's life course.

The specific mechanics of employer-sponsored pension design deserve a closer look here, because the shift already underway in most wealthy economies' pension systems has, largely by accident rather than deliberate design, actually made mid-career mobility somewhat easier than it once was, even as other institutions in this chapter's account have lagged. Traditional defined-benefit pensions, the dominant model through most of the twentieth century, calculate a retiree's benefit based on years of service and final salary at a single employer, which creates a strong financial penalty for leaving that employer before reaching full vesting — a worker who switches employers, or takes an extended break to retrain in a new field, can permanently sacrifice a large share of the pension benefit a defined-benefit formula would otherwise have delivered, precisely the kind of institutional friction that discourages the career-interruption-and-retraining pattern this chapter argues a longer working life will increasingly require. The broad shift across most wealthy economies, over the past several decades, from defined-benefit toward defined-contribution retirement accounts — individual, portable accounts that an employee can carry with them from job to job, rather than a benefit formula tied to a single employer's tenure calculation — has, largely as a side effect of cost-shifting decisions employers made for their own financial reasons rather than as a deliberate response to the longevity pressures this book examines, removed at least one significant structural penalty against the kind of mid-career mobility and reinvention a genuinely multi-stage working life requires. This is a rare example, in the material this book has assembled, of an institutional shift already underway for unrelated reasons happening to move in the direction this chapter's argument would recommend — though it is worth noting plainly that the same shift has also transferred investment risk from employer to individual employee, a separate and not uniformly positive consequence of the same underlying change that falls outside this chapter's narrower focus on career mobility specifically.

And hiring practices, across a wide range of industries and countries, continue to exhibit a well-documented preference for candidates in a fairly narrow mid-career age band, even in labor markets that are, by their own demographic arithmetic, growing more dependent on retaining and re-deploying older workers with each passing year — an inconsistency between what the aggregate labor-market data says employers will increasingly need and what individual hiring managers, evaluating individual resumes, still appear to select for in practice.

## The specific shape of the age bias

The hiring-practice friction this chapter raised above deserves closer examination than a single sentence can give it, because "age discrimination" is often discussed as though it were a single, uniform prejudice, when the pattern documented across labor-economics research is considerably more specific and, in some ways, more revealing about what would actually need to change to close it. Age discrimination in hiring, where researchers have been able to study it through controlled methods such as sending closely matched fictitious resumes differing only in the implied age of the applicant, tends to show up most strongly at the point of initial screening and interview selection — older applicants are called back for interviews at meaningfully lower rates than younger applicants with functionally identical qualifications — rather than at the point of an actual job offer once an older candidate has made it through to a final interview stage. This distinction matters for what kind of institutional fix might actually work: it suggests the bias operates substantially through an early, often unconscious heuristic applied to a resume or an application before an employer has any real information about the specific candidate's actual capability, energy, or fit for the role, rather than through a considered judgment made after genuine evaluation. A hiring process redesigned to delay revealing age-indicating information — graduation dates, early-career history predating a certain year — until later in the evaluation process is a comparatively modest, low-cost institutional change that some employers and researchers have proposed specifically to address this pattern, distinct from the much larger, more expensive reforms to pension accrual, credentialing, and continuing education this chapter's other sections describe. That such a comparatively simple intervention remains uncommon across most labor markets examined in this book is itself a small, telling data point: even the cheapest, least structurally demanding fixes to age-biased hiring have not been widely adopted, which is not an encouraging sign for how quickly the far more expensive and structurally demanding reforms — redesigning pension accrual rules, rebuilding mid-career education pathways — are likely to arrive on their own, absent the kind of deliberate policy push this chapter's closing section calls for.

## Japan's answer, and its limits

This book's earlier chapter on demographic policy responses already described Japan's most direct institutional attempt to address this specific problem: the Silver Human Resource Center system, a network of roughly thirteen hundred centers nationwide matching more than seven hundred thousand retirees with part-time, community-based paid work, organized as much around a sense of continued purpose — the Japanese concept of *ikigai* — as around raw economic necessity, alongside a 2020 legal reform requiring Japanese employers to make a genuine, good-faith effort, though not a binding legal guarantee, to extend employment opportunities to workers up to age seventy. It is worth returning to that example here specifically because it illustrates, in miniature, both halves of this chapter's argument at once: a real, functioning institutional response exists, has operated at meaningful scale for half a century, and demonstrably keeps a substantial number of older workers economically and socially engaged well past the age at which most other developed countries' labor markets would have already pushed them out entirely. And that same institution, examined honestly, reaches a modest fraction of the population it would need to reach to fully close the gap this chapter describes — seven hundred thousand participants against more than thirty-six million Japanese residents aged sixty-five and older, a program that mitigates the strain at the margins rather than resolving it, exactly the kind of partial, real-but-insufficient response this book has found in nearly every other institution it has examined, from South Korea's pronatalist spending to the TAME trial's decade-long funding shortfall.

It is worth asking, briefly, why an institution this genuinely useful, operating for half a century, has not scaled further or been more widely copied by other aging economies confronting closely similar pressures — South Korea and China, examined in this book's demographic chapters, each face old-age dependency pressures that will likely rival or exceed Japan's own within a generation, and neither has built anything resembling the Silver Human Resource Center network at comparable scale. Part of the answer, consistent with a pattern this book keeps finding, appears to be cultural and institutional specificity rather than any inherent flaw in the underlying model: the *ikigai* framing that gives the Japanese system much of its social legitimacy and participant motivation draws on a particular cultural vocabulary around purposeful engagement in old age that does not necessarily translate directly into other countries' social and institutional context, and building an equivalent network from scratch requires sustained, patient public investment in matching infrastructure — local offices, employer relationships, a trusted intermediary institution between retirees and part-time work opportunities — of exactly the unglamorous, multi-decade kind that the fertility-incentive spending examined in this book's demographic chapters shows governments are willing to fund at large scale, but that a comparatively modest, low-profile labor-market institution like a nationwide part-time-work-matching network has evidently found harder to attract the same sustained political attention and funding commitment toward, despite plausibly offering a better-demonstrated return on investment than some of the more heavily funded fertility-incentive programs this book has separately examined.

## What redesigning a career for sixty years would actually require

## What a mid-career sabbatical would actually cost

It is worth costing out, in rough terms, what a genuine mid-career retraining period would require of an ordinary worker under current institutional arrangements, because the abstraction of "redesigning careers" can otherwise obscure just how large a barrier current institutions actually present. A worker in their mid-forties who wanted to take a genuine year away from full-time employment to retrain into a new field — the kind of deliberate, planned interruption the multi-stage-life model this chapter has examined would treat as an ordinary, expected feature of a normal career rather than an unusual disruption — faces, under most current employer-sponsored benefit structures, the loss of a full year of employer retirement-plan contributions, a likely gap in employer-sponsored health insurance coverage in countries like the United States where health insurance is tied to employment, a pause in Social Security-covered earnings that can affect the eventual benefit calculation depending on which years of a worker's earnings history end up counting toward it, and, in many white-collar fields, a resume gap that hiring managers, per the age-bias research described earlier in this chapter, tend to view with some suspicion regardless of the underlying reason for it. None of these costs is enormous in isolation. Together, they function as a meaningful financial and career penalty attached to exactly the kind of retraining interruption this chapter has argued a genuinely multi-stage working life requires — which helps explain why, even among workers who intellectually accept the case for a mid-career reinvention, relatively few actually take one, and why the institutions this chapter has found best positioned to support this kind of transition are disproportionately public, tenure-protected, or unionized positions with built-in sabbatical provisions, rather than the ordinary private-sector employment that covers most of the workforce in every country this book examines.

The chapter's honest conclusion is neither the techno-optimist version, in which AI simply absorbs the labor gap aging economies are creating, nor the purely pessimistic version, in which extended lifespans mostly translate into extended financial precarity for older workers pushed out of careers that were never designed to last as long as their own bodies and the actuarial tables described in this book's earlier chapters now suggest they might. It is a narrower, more specific claim: the institutions that shape how a career unfolds — education, credentialing, pension accrual, hiring practice — were built around a life-stage template substantially shorter than the one an increasing share of workers in wealthy countries are likely to actually experience, whether from ordinary continued medical progress, the more speculative geroscience examined in this book's biology chapters, or simply the blunt fiscal necessity of a retirement system whose trust fund depletion dates, described in an earlier chapter, are already published and counting down. Closing that gap requires more than a single new government program or a widely deployed AI tool, however useful either might be at the margins. It requires the kind of deliberate, multi-institutional redesign — of universities, of pension accrual rules, of hiring norms, of the entire cultural expectation about what a career even is — that Gratton, Scott, and Carstensen have each separately called for, and that, on the evidence assembled across this book so far, almost no country has yet undertaken at anything like the scale its own demographic and actuarial projections already say it will eventually need.
