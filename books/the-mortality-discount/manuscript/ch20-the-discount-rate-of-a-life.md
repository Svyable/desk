# The Discount Rate of a Life

Return, for a moment, to the discount rate this book's opening chapter used to frame everything that followed: the idea, familiar from ordinary finance, that a future sum of money is worth less today than the same sum in hand, and that the gap between the two can be expressed as a single number, compounding, that lets an investor translate an uncertain future cash flow into a price. A life table does something structurally similar with an uncertain future lifespan, translating it into a present number — expected years remaining, at this age, for this population — that a pension fund, an insurer, or a government can build a promise on top of. Every institution examined across this book's chapters carries some version of that translation buried inside its ordinary operation, usually without describing it in those terms, and usually without revisiting it as often as the underlying reality has actually moved.

The metaphor is worth pressing a little further than this book's opening chapter had room to, because an ordinary financial discount rate and a mortality discount actually diverge in one important respect that matters for everything that follows. A financial discount rate is, in principle, a single, freely chosen number — an investor or an institution can select a higher or lower rate depending on how much risk and uncertainty they want to build into their valuation, and different institutions routinely apply different discount rates to the same future cash flow based on their own risk tolerance and cost of capital, without either being simply right or wrong. A mortality discount is not freely chosen in the same sense; it is meant to track an actual, external, biological and demographic reality that exists independent of any institution's preference for how to model it. An insurer can choose to be conservative or aggressive in how it prices longevity risk, the same way it can choose a higher or lower financial discount rate, but the underlying mortality experience itself will eventually resolve to a specific, objective number regardless of what any institution assumed — a fact this book's account of the 2004 longevity bond's basis risk, and of Life Partners Holdings' mispriced life-expectancy estimates, has already shown can inflict real financial damage on institutions that got the underlying number wrong, in either direction, no matter how sophisticated their surrounding financial engineering. This is what makes the mortality discount, unlike an ordinary financial discount rate, ultimately a forecasting problem rather than a pure risk-preference problem — which is exactly why this book has spent so many of its chapters on the underlying science and demography themselves, rather than simply on the financial instruments built on top of them.

It is worth being explicit, now that the book's evidence has been laid out chapter by chapter, about exactly where that translation currently sits, and how far its assumptions have already drifted from the ground truth this book has spent its chapters documenting.

## What has actually moved, and what hasn't

Start with the plainest, most conservative summary the evidence supports. Life expectancy in the world's wealthiest countries is not, on the whole, exploding upward in a way that has already outrun the actuarial tables built to price it. The United States, examined in this book's second chapter, saw a historic multi-year decline driven by COVID-19 and a persistent drug-overdose crisis, followed by a genuine but still-incomplete recovery that reached a record 79.0 years at birth in 2024 — a meaningful, real improvement, and also not, on its own, the kind of dramatic biological breakthrough that would force pension actuaries to tear up their models overnight. The most ambitious biotechnology examined across this book's middle chapters — partial cellular reprogramming, senolytic drugs, biological age clocks of contested validity — has not yet produced a single approved therapy demonstrated, in a large, well-controlled human trial, to meaningfully extend healthy human lifespan. Unity Biotechnology's senescence-clearing drug failed its pivotal trial outright. The TAME trial, designed specifically to test whether a cheap, generic drug could delay the onset of age-related disease, remains, a decade after its conception, without the full funding it needs to run at the scale its own design requires. Altos Labs and its well-funded peers are pursuing a genuinely serious scientific program, backed by billions of dollars of real capital, that has not yet cleared the basic safety hurdle — controlling the cancer risk inherent in the reprogramming mechanism itself — that stands between its current laboratory results and a single human clinical trial.

If this were the whole picture, the honest conclusion would be a comparatively boring one: the mortality discount most institutions currently carry is roughly right, or at least not yet dramatically wrong, and this book's opening chapter's warning about a coming repricing would turn out to have been premature. That is not, however, the whole picture, and the reason it isn't has less to do with any single dramatic biological breakthrough than with two more modest, better-documented developments this book has traced in detail: a genuine, if uneven and contested, reclassification of aging itself as a target for medical intervention, moving through the regulatory and scientific apparatus more slowly and more partially than popular coverage suggests, but moving nonetheless; and a demographic collapse, already well underway and considerably further along than most public discussion outside demography departments has absorbed, that is straining the same institutions from an entirely different direction than a longevity breakthrough would, and doing so with numbers that are not speculative or years away, but published, current, and in several cases — South Korea's fertility rate, China's population decline, the Social Security trustees' depletion projections — already worse than most casual observers assume.

## Why "on average" was always doing too much work

Before turning to the two-directional pull this chapter's title describes, it is worth pausing on a simplification this book has necessarily made throughout, in service of tracing a clear argument across many institutions and countries at once, and correcting for it explicitly here near the book's close: a single national mortality discount, priced against a single national average life expectancy, was always a considerable simplification of the underlying reality, even before any of the developments this book documents began straining it. This book's fourteenth chapter, on income and life expectancy, already showed how much variation a single national average conceals — a fourteen-year gap between the richest and poorest Americans, real and substantial geographic variation even at similar income levels, a gap that has been widening rather than narrowing. The same kind of concealment runs through every other national average this book has cited: a country's fertility rate is itself an average across regions, income levels, and education levels that can vary by a factor of two or more within a single country's borders; a national retirement age applies uniformly across occupations with radically different physical demands and radically different life expectancies, from a desk-based professional career to physically hazardous manual labor; a pension system's mortality table, even when regularly revised, typically represents a national or industry-wide average rather than the specific, granular mortality experience of any single group of beneficiaries, the exact basis-risk problem this book's chapter on the failed 2004 longevity bond found sinking that specific financial instrument. Every average this book has cited, in other words, is doing real interpretive work, standing in for a distribution of outcomes considerably wider and more unequal than the single number usually gets credit for capturing — a caveat worth stating plainly here, near the end of this book's argument, rather than leaving implicit throughout.

## Two discount rates, moving in opposite directions

This is the shape this book's opening chapter promised to trace, and it is worth stating plainly now that the evidence is fully assembled: the mortality discount is not simply wrong in one direction. It is being pulled in two different directions by two different forces, examined separately across this book's chapters, whose combined effect on any single institution depends heavily on which side of the ledger that institution actually sits on.

An institution exposed primarily to longevity risk — a pension fund, an annuity provider, the Social Security retirement trust fund — is threatened by any development that extends how long its beneficiaries actually live relative to what its tables assumed, whether that development comes from a genuine geroscience breakthrough or, less dramatically but more immediately relevant given the evidence assembled in this book's middle chapters, from the more modest, already-proven mortality benefit of drugs like the GLP-1 class examined in the chapter on the Ozempic economy, whose documented cardiovascular benefit is real, current, and already large enough to represent a measurable actuarial fact rather than a speculative future one. An institution exposed primarily to demographic risk — a national economy's tax base, an employer's future labor supply, a housing market built around a growing population of young buyers — is threatened by the opposite force: not people living too long, but too few new people existing in the first place to replace the workers, taxpayers, and consumers the institution was built around. A country like Japan or South Korea, examined in this book's demographic chapters, is being squeezed from both directions simultaneously: a population living longer, on average, than its pension system's founding assumptions anticipated, replaced by too few children to fund the resulting gap through ordinary economic growth — precisely the double bind this book's opening chapter described as the genuinely novel feature of the present moment, distinct from either challenge examined in isolation.

## A quantitative sketch, held loosely

It may help to translate the two-directional pull described above into something closer to a rough, deliberately imprecise numerical sketch, in the spirit of the actuarial discipline this book keeps returning to — a working estimate, openly labeled as such, rather than a false precision this book has criticized elsewhere. Consider a national retirement system, broadly resembling the ones this book has examined, whose actuaries currently assume a sixty-five-year-old will live, on average, a further nineteen to twenty years, roughly matching the figure this book's second chapter found in the CDC's most recent published American life tables. Now consider two separate, partial adjustments this book's evidence suggests such a system may need to make simultaneously, moving in opposite directions on the underlying finances even though both stem from the same broad category of mortality and demographic change this book has traced. On the longevity side, a documented, already-proven mortality benefit like the GLP-1 drug class's cardiovascular effect, examined in this book's fourth chapter, plausibly extends life expectancy at older ages by some still-uncertain but non-trivial fraction of a year across a large population, once the drugs reach broad enough adoption — a real, incremental addition to the system's expected payout duration per beneficiary, arriving on a timeline of years rather than decades. On the demographic side, a fertility rate persisting well below replacement, of the kind this book's ninth and tenth chapters documented across South Korea, Japan, and China, shrinks the number of future workers paying into the same system relative to the number of retirees drawing from it, a separate and, on the evidence assembled in this book's tenth chapter on dependency ratios, considerably larger source of financial strain than the longevity effect alone.

The point of this sketch is not to produce a specific, defensible forecast — this book has been consistently skeptical of exactly that kind of false precision, and a genuine actuarial projection would require far more data and far more careful modeling than a single illustrative paragraph can respectably provide. The point is to make concrete something this chapter's more abstract discussion of "two discount rates moving in opposite directions" can otherwise leave too vague to act on: an institution examined honestly through this book's evidence is not simply facing one clean, single-direction correction it can plan for once and be done with. It is facing at least two separate, only loosely correlated sources of revision to its founding assumptions, moving on different timelines, driven by different underlying mechanisms — one from the biology chapters, one from the demographic chapters — that a single retirement-age adjustment or a single funding formula recalibration is unlikely to address both of at once, which is itself a strong argument, examined directly in this book's next chapter, for the kind of continuous, multi-dimensional monitoring and revision this book has found most institutions still lacking.

## What an honest repricing would actually require

If the argument assembled across this book's chapters is correct even in its more modest, better-evidenced form — not that a dramatic biological breakthrough is imminent, but that the institutions examined throughout this book are already carrying assumptions strained by developments that have already happened, documented in this book's own chapters with real dates, real figures, and real, published sources — then the honest response is not a single dramatic policy change. It is the same unglamorous, continuous discipline this book's chapter on actuarial history found running through the profession since Edmond Halley's original Breslau table: regular, unflinching revision of the underlying assumption, against the best current evidence, rather than treating any single number — a retirement age set in 1935, a pension fund's mortality table last meaningfully revised a decade ago, a national fertility projection based on a trend that has already shifted twice in the years since it was published — as a fixed fact rather than the working approximation it has always actually been.

This book has already found one professional community that has internalized this discipline more thoroughly than any of the political institutions examined elsewhere in these chapters, and it is worth crediting that community explicitly here, near the book's close, rather than only in passing. The Society of Actuaries' continuously updated mortality database, the mortality-improvement scales examined in this book's opening chapter, and the pension risk transfer market's demonstrated willingness to reprice tens of billions of dollars of longevity risk every year, examined in this book's seventh chapter, together represent a genuine, functioning example of the ongoing revision this section is calling for — proof that the discipline this book is asking political institutions to adopt is not a hypothetical ideal but an existing professional practice, already operating at real scale, just confined so far to the private, commercial side of the mortality discount rather than extending fully to the public, political side where this book has found revision hardest to achieve. The gap this book has spent its chapters documenting is not, in this light, a gap between knowing how to do this well and not knowing; it is a gap between a professional discipline that has already solved the problem for the institutions willing to pay actuaries to practice it continuously, and a set of political institutions that have access to the same underlying data and the same professional expertise, but that have not yet built the same institutionalized habit of continuous, unflinching revision into their own decision-making processes.

That discipline is harder to sustain than it sounds, for reasons this book's chapters on retirement-age history and pronatalist policy spending have already documented in painful, expensive detail: revising a number downward, or raising an age, or acknowledging a demographic trend has moved further than a previous projection assumed, is politically costly in a way that simply continuing to operate on an outdated assumption, for one more budget cycle, one more election, one more year, generally is not — right up until the deferred cost arrives all at once, on a schedule an institution's own trustees have often already published years in advance, as the Social Security Administration's annual trustees report has done for the American retirement system this book examined directly. The mortality discount embedded in nearly every institution this book has traced was never a fixed truth handed down from Edmond Halley's original table. It has always been a working guess, revised when the evidence justified revision and left alone, for far too long in case after case this book has documented, whenever revision was politically inconvenient. The chapters that remain ask what would have to be true for this book's own argument to be wrong, and what a society that took the discipline of honest, continuous revision seriously might actually look like, once the repricing this book has traced finally works its way, unevenly and incompletely, through the institutions built to absorb it.
