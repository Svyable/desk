# The Rapamycin Underground

Rapamycin was discovered in the soil of Easter Island, isolated from a bacterium in a sample collected during a 1964 scientific expedition and eventually developed into an immunosuppressant drug used, since the late 1990s, to prevent organ transplant recipients from rejecting a new kidney or liver. It works by inhibiting a cellular signaling pathway called mTOR, a central regulator of growth and metabolism that shows up, unavoidably, on every serious list of the mechanisms thought to drive biological aging. Studies in mice, going back more than a decade, have found that rapamycin extends lifespan reliably and substantially, one of the most robust and repeatedly reproduced findings in the entire field of aging biology. It has never been approved by any drug regulator, anywhere, as a treatment for aging in humans, and it is not likely to be for years, if ever, given the same regulatory obstacles described earlier in this book's account of the TAME trial.

None of that has stopped a meaningful number of people from taking it anyway. A loose, largely online community of self-experimenters — sometimes calling themselves biohackers, sometimes simply longevity enthusiasts — has spent the past several years obtaining low-dose rapamycin through sympathetic physicians willing to prescribe it off-label, through international pharmacies, or occasionally through channels considerably less regulated than either, dosing themselves on the strength of mouse data, a handful of small human safety studies, and the accumulated, informally shared experience of other people doing the same thing. It is not, by any means, a fringe curiosity confined to a handful of eccentrics. Online communities built around off-label longevity-drug use, biomarker tracking, and comparing notes on dosing protocols have grown into a genuine subculture with its own vocabulary, its own minor celebrities, and its own informal peer-review process conducted largely through blog posts and social media threads rather than journals — a parallel, unregulated track running well ahead of the formal clinical trial system this book has already described as underfunded and slow-moving on its own terms.

The existence of this underground is, in its own right, a useful data point for the argument this book has been building. When a formal institution — in this case, the pharmaceutical regulatory apparatus — moves too slowly relative to the pace at which a motivated, resourced subset of the public believes the underlying science justifies action, that public does not simply wait patiently for the institution to catch up. Some of it goes around the institution entirely, accepting a level of uncertainty and personal risk that a regulator would never sanction, in exchange for the chance to act on evidence the regulator has not yet had the opportunity, or the funding, to fully evaluate. Whether that bet pays off for any individual rapamycin self-experimenter is, as of this writing, genuinely unknown, in the most literal sense: nobody has run the kind of large, controlled, long-term human trial that would actually answer the question, for exactly the funding and regulatory reasons described in earlier chapters, which leaves the entire community's dosing protocols built on mouse data, small safety studies, and shared anecdote rather than the kind of evidence this book otherwise insists on before treating a claim as established.

## A clock, and an argument about what it's actually measuring

If a person wanted evidence, beyond how they feel, that their rapamycin regimen — or their diet, their exercise routine, their sleep schedule, or any other lifestyle intervention — was actually working, the most commercially available answer today is a biological age test: a laboratory analysis, usually run on a blood sample, that promises to measure how "old" a person's cells actually are, as distinct from how many years have passed since their birth. The scientific foundation for this entire commercial category traces back to a single 2013 paper.

Steve Horvath, a biostatistician then at UCLA, published a paper that year in the journal *Genome Biology* analyzing roughly eight thousand tissue samples across fifty-one different healthy cell and tissue types, drawn from eighty-two separate datasets, and identified three hundred and fifty-three specific locations on the genome where a chemical modification called DNA methylation changed in a remarkably consistent, predictable pattern as people aged — consistent enough that a single mathematical formula built from those three hundred and fifty-three sites could estimate a person's chronological age, from a tissue sample alone, with striking accuracy across almost every tissue type in the human body. This became known as the Horvath clock, the first broadly cross-tissue epigenetic age predictor, and it opened an entire subsequent research program: if methylation patterns track age this reliably, might they also reveal when someone's biological age has drifted away from their chronological age — running faster or slower than the calendar, in a way that predicts their actual future health, independent of how many birthdays they've had?

A follow-up development, published in 2019 by researchers including Horvath himself, pushed the idea further. Rather than building a clock trained purely to predict chronological age, the researchers built one called GrimAge, trained instead on a composite of methylation-based surrogates for blood-plasma proteins and a methylation estimate of lifetime smoking exposure, and validated it against real health outcomes in large study populations. The results were striking by the standard of biomarker research: GrimAge predicted time to death with a statistical significance so extreme — a p-value on the order of one in ten to the seventy-fifth power — that it left little doubt the underlying signal was picking up something real about mortality risk, alongside strong predictive power for time to coronary heart disease and time to cancer diagnosis, each outperforming the original, purely age-predicting Horvath clock at forecasting actual health outcomes rather than just calendar age.

This is where the underlying scientific consensus becomes considerably murkier than the marketing built on top of it suggests, and where this chapter's central caution belongs. A genuine and unresolved debate runs through the peer-reviewed literature on what these clocks are actually measuring, mechanistically. One school of thought treats epigenetic clocks as causal biomarkers of biological aging itself — direct molecular readouts of the aging process, capable in principle of showing whether a given intervention is truly slowing that process down. A competing, more cautious school argues the clocks may instead be sophisticated statistical proxies for accumulated physiological burden and known risk factors, repackaged through a DNA-methylation lens, rather than novel causal measurements in their own right — a critique with some empirical teeth: Mendelian randomization studies, a technique that uses genetic variation to test for causal rather than merely correlational relationships, have found that the specific genomic sites existing epigenetic clocks rely on are not particularly enriched for locations that appear to be causally involved in aging-related traits, meaning the sites the clocks use to predict age and mortality risk have not been shown to be driving the aging process, as distinct from merely tracking alongside it. A related and, for anyone actually using these tests to evaluate an intervention, more practically important finding is that first-generation clocks like the original Horvath formula appear to reflect a substantial amount of what researchers describe as stochastic epigenetic noise — essentially, biological randomness rather than a clean signal — and have shown limited sensitivity to interventions, meaning a person who genuinely slowed their own aging through diet, exercise, or a drug might not see that improvement reliably reflected in their test score at all, undermining the exact use case — checking whether an anti-aging intervention is working — that most consumers of these tests are actually paying for.

## What the test you can buy today actually promises

None of this scientific uncertainty has slowed the growth of a commercial biological-age testing industry built directly on top of it. Companies including TruDiagnostic and Elysium Health now sell direct-to-consumer epigenetic age tests, typically requiring a blood or saliva sample mailed to a laboratory, returning a report that gives the customer a single number: their biological age, set against their chronological one, often accompanied by recommendations for how to bring the two numbers closer together. The specific scientific critique of at least one of these commercial offerings — that a linear-regression approach to fitting methylation data against chronological age treats each individual genomic site as an independently, proportionally contributing variable, an approach critics argue fundamentally misrepresents aging's actual nonlinear, cascading biology across interacting immune, metabolic, and epigenetic systems — could not be traced, in the research conducted for this book, to a single, clearly citable peer-reviewed source specifically targeting that company by name, which is itself worth flagging rather than papering over: some of the sharpest criticism circulating about specific commercial biological-age products lives in industry commentary and comparison sites that are not fully independent of the broader wellness and longevity-testing market themselves, making it harder than it should be for an ordinary consumer to find a genuinely disinterested evaluation of what they're actually buying.

What can be said with more confidence, because it rests on the peer-reviewed methodological debate described above rather than on any single company's specific claims, is this: a biological age number, purchased today from a direct-to-consumer laboratory test, sits on genuinely contested scientific ground. It may reflect a real, meaningful signal about a person's accumulated health risk — the GrimAge research, in particular, has real predictive power for actual mortality and disease outcomes in large study populations, which is not nothing. But there is limited, and disputed, prospective clinical evidence that these tests reliably predict an individual customer's future disease onset or death with the precision the marketing implies, and there is a genuine, unresolved scientific question about whether the number moves in a trustworthy way in response to the lifestyle change, drug, or supplement regimen a customer adopts specifically to try to move it — which is, again, usually the entire point of buying the test in the first place.

## A frontier running ahead of its own instruments

The rapamycin underground and the biological-age testing industry are, in an important sense, the same phenomenon viewed from two different angles: a public that has absorbed, correctly, that serious science increasingly treats aging as a process rather than a fixed fact of life, and that has responded, in the absence of approved treatments or validated measurement tools, by building its own informal infrastructure — off-label prescriptions, imported drugs, commercial tests of contested reliability — to act on that belief anyway, years or decades ahead of the formal regulatory and scientific apparatus that would eventually be needed to confirm whether any of it actually works. This is not, in itself, irrational; given the funding and regulatory obstacles described throughout this book, waiting for TAME-style trials to fully resolve every open question about aging interventions could mean waiting considerably longer than many of today's fifty- and sixty-year-old self-experimenters have left to benefit from an answer, and reasonable people can and do decide that acting on incomplete evidence, with their own bodies and their own informed consent, is a bet worth making.

It is, however, exactly the kind of gap this book keeps finding between the frontier of a technology and the institutions meant to validate, regulate, and fairly distribute its benefits. A test that claims to measure how fast a person is aging, sold directly to consumers years before the scientific community has resolved whether the underlying measurement is even causally meaningful, is a small, personal-scale version of the same mismatch this book has already found in TAME's decade-long funding shortfall and in the ICD-11's contested aging classification: real, serious science, moving forward unevenly, faster in some places than others, with the institutions meant to certify what's actually proven trailing well behind both the enthusiasm of the field's advocates and the appetite of a public increasingly willing to act before the certification arrives.
