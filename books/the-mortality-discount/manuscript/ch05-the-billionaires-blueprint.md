# The Billionaire's Blueprint

Bryan Johnson spends roughly two million dollars a year trying to reverse his own aging. The figure, by his own account, pays for a team of more than thirty physicians and medical professionals, upward of a hundred daily supplements, a sub-two-thousand-calorie vegan diet eaten entirely before eleven in the morning, an 8:30 p.m. bedtime enforced with the same discipline as everything else in the protocol, and a volume of daily biomarker testing that would be difficult for most hospitals to justify for a single patient. Johnson made his fortune selling the payments company Braintree, which owned Venmo, to PayPal for eight hundred million dollars in 2013; his current net worth is estimated, loosely, somewhere in the low hundreds of millions. He calls his regimen Project Blueprint, and in 2025 it became the subject of a Netflix documentary with a title that states its ambition without much hedging: *Don't Die: The Man Who Wants to Live Forever.*

In 2023, Johnson conducted what he described as the world's first multigenerational plasma exchange: he had roughly a liter of his own blood drawn, separated out the plasma, and infused with plasma from his then-seventeen-year-old son, while separately giving his own plasma to his seventy-year-old father, all under the supervision of his medical team. It was, whatever else it was, a vivid literalization of an old folk idea about young blood and vitality that has surfaced in various forms for centuries and that a small, real body of mouse research had recently given new scientific texture to. By January 2025, after six rounds of the procedure using young donor plasma, Johnson stated publicly that he had observed no benefits, and discontinued the practice, switching instead to a different method — total plasma exchange, without a young donor — that he judged more promising. This is worth pausing on, because it is a genuinely useful data point buried inside a story that could easily be told as pure spectacle: a well-funded, well-documented, closely monitored self-experiment in one of the more headline-grabbing interventions associated with the modern longevity movement, whose own subject publicly declared it hadn't worked and walked away from it. Hype outrunning evidence is not a phenomenon happening only to credulous outsiders. It happened, by his own account, to the person spending more money and more personal attention on this project than almost anyone alive.

Medical criticism of Johnson's broader project has been direct. An analysis published by the American Council on Science and Health in early 2025 made three points worth stating plainly, because they apply well beyond Johnson himself to much of the territory this book covers. First, the evidentiary status of Blueprint is, at best, a case study — an experiment with a sample size of one, uncontrolled, run by the same person who is marketing its results, which places it at the lowest tier of medical evidence regardless of how many biomarkers get tracked along the way. Second, the protocol is not remotely replicable as public health guidance: it requires a personal medical team, access to experimental procedures, and a budget that exceeds most families' lifetime earnings, which makes the question of whether it "works" for Bryan Johnson almost beside the point for anyone trying to think about longevity as a broadly shared social good rather than an individual purchase. Third — and this is where the criticism got personal rather than methodological — at least one liver specialist, in public medical-community commentary, characterized Johnson's claims as outright fraudulent rather than merely unproven.

Whatever the right word for it is, Bryan Johnson is not really the subject of this chapter. He is the most visible, most extreme individual expression of a much larger and better-funded institutional phenomenon: a wave of venture capital, much of it supplied or personally backed by some of the wealthiest people in technology, betting that the biology of aging is now tractable enough to justify billions of dollars in speculative investment, years or decades before any of the resulting therapies could plausibly reach a doctor's office.

## Three billion dollars, before a single patient

In January 2022, a new biotechnology company called Altos Labs began operations having already raised, in its opening funding round, roughly three billion dollars — a sum widely reported at the time as the largest amount of money ever assembled to launch a single biotech startup, before it had produced a drug, run a clinical trial, or in most public accountings, even settled on a lead therapeutic candidate. Its scientific bet, described across extensive technology and business press coverage at the time, centers on partial cellular reprogramming: the idea, discussed in detail in the chapter ahead on that specific science, that cells can be pushed backward toward a more youthful molecular state without erasing their identity entirely. Shinya Yamanaka, the scientist whose original discovery makes the whole field possible, is reported to serve as a scientific advisor to the company. The company's most commonly cited backers in press coverage include Jeff Bezos and the technology investor Yuri Milner, though it is worth being honest about the limits of what can be confirmed here: the exact three-billion-dollar figure and the full roster of investors recur consistently across major outlets like MIT Technology Review, but trace back to reporting rather than to a fully itemized company disclosure, and a reader who wants investor-by-investor certainty should look for Altos Labs' own primary materials rather than taking any single secondary account as final.

Altos was not the first major technology fortune to bet on aging research, only the largest and most recent. Google — later restructured under its parent company Alphabet — founded a longevity-focused subsidiary called Calico, short for the California Life Company, in September 2013, led by Arthur Levinson, the former chief executive of Genentech. Larry Page's public letter announcing the company described its mission, without much embellishment, as health, well-being, and longevity. Calico partnered with the pharmaceutical company AbbVie the following year in a fifty-fifty collaboration that began with each company committing two hundred and fifty million dollars, with an option to expand to a combined one and a half billion, and which press coverage later described as growing to a total alliance value of roughly three and a half billion dollars as the partnership continued. More than a decade into that arrangement, no headline Calico-originated anti-aging drug has reached the market, and the company has remained notably quiet, by industry standards, about what exactly its substantial and sustained investment has produced. That silence is itself a data point worth taking seriously, in a field where the loudest announcements tend to come from companies still years away from clinical results: a decade of well-funded, well-staffed research at one of the most resourced longevity ventures in the world has not yet yielded a public breakthrough, which is either evidence that this is extraordinarily hard even with nearly unlimited resources, or evidence that the work is progressing more quietly than the field's press cycle generally allows, and probably some of both.

A newer entrant, Retro Biosciences, drew attention in March 2023 for a different reason: OpenAI's chief executive, Sam Altman, personally supplied its entire seed round, reported at one hundred and eighty million dollars — an unusually large sum for a single individual to commit to one early-stage biotech company, and among the largest personal investments ever made in the longevity field specifically. The company's stated mission is to add ten healthy years to human lifespan through a combination of in vivo gene therapy, cell replacement, and partial reprogramming. By January 2025, Retro was reported to be raising a billion-dollar Series A round, with Altman again participating; by May 2026, its valuation was reported at roughly one point eight billion dollars. Its most clinically advanced program, as of the most recent reporting available, is not a rejuvenation therapy at all — it is a pill targeting the clearance of protein aggregates in Alzheimer's disease patients, a real and serious medical target in its own right, but a considerable distance from the company's headline mission of reversing aging directly. The gap between Retro's stated ambition and its actual clinical-stage reality is not a criticism unique to Retro. It is close to the standard pattern across this entire funding wave: enormous, well-publicized capital commitments to a mission framed around reversing or radically slowing human aging, and clinical pipelines that, when examined closely, are mostly still working on more conventional, narrower disease targets, with the more ambitious aging-specific therapies remaining years behind the funding that has already been raised in their name.

## A field-wide accounting

Individual company stories, however dramatic, can obscure the scale of what has actually been happening across the field as a whole. Industry tracking from the trade publication Longevity.Technology reported that total financing across the longevity sector reached roughly eight and a half billion dollars across three hundred and twenty-five deals in 2024, up more than two hundred percent from about three point eight billion dollars the year before — even as the number of individual deals fell slightly, from three hundred and thirty-one to three hundred and twenty-five, meaning the average deal size grew substantially even as investors made somewhat more selective bets. Venture capital specifically accounted for more than five billion dollars of the 2024 total, and later-stage venture funding made up close to a third of all financing, a sign of a field maturing past its earliest, most speculative funding rounds into companies further along in development, even if still years from an approved product in most cases.

It is worth flagging plainly that this figure comes from an industry trade outlet whose business model depends on longevity investment continuing to grow, rather than from an independent financial-data provider like PitchBook or Crunchbase — the kind of caveat this book tries to apply consistently rather than selectively, since the same caution would apply equally to a skeptical industry's own self-reported numbers. Treated as directionally indicative rather than audited to the dollar, the figure nonetheless tells a clear and useful story: longevity biotech went, in the space of about two years, from a niche category mostly associated with a handful of billionaire-backed moonshots to a genuine, multi-billion-dollar-a-year asset class that institutional venture capital now allocates to as a matter of course, not merely as a curiosity attached to a famous founder's personal interests.

## What three billion dollars actually buys

The uncomfortable question sitting underneath all of these numbers, and the one this chapter keeps circling back to, is what this scale of capital has actually purchased so far, set against what it has been marketed as purchasing. It has purchased, unambiguously, an enormous amount of serious scientific talent: Altos Labs assembled a roster of prominent academic researchers, including figures directly connected to the original discoveries this field depends on, at compensation levels that a university research budget could never match. It has purchased years of sustained, well-resourced laboratory work at Calico, results largely undisclosed. It has purchased, at Retro Biosciences, a genuinely serious Alzheimer's drug candidate, alongside a stated mission of reversing aging that remains, for now, a stated mission rather than a demonstrated result. And across the field as a whole, according to the trade data available, it has purchased a rapidly maturing, increasingly institutional funding ecosystem that treats aging biology as an investable category the way earlier decades treated oncology or gene therapy.

What none of this money has yet purchased, as of this book's writing, is an approved therapy that reverses or meaningfully slows human aging in the way the founding pitches of these companies describe. That absence does not mean the money has been wasted, any more than the enormous capital poured into unprofitable internet companies in the late 1990s meant the internet itself was a mirage — a comparison the book's opening chapter deliberately set up, and one this field increasingly resembles in its financing pattern if not yet in its outcomes. It means the honest current state of the field is earlier-stage, slower, and less certain than the scale of its funding alone would suggest to an outside observer, and that the gap between the money already committed and the results already delivered is, itself, a kind of open position — a bet, made by some of the wealthiest and most influential people in technology, that the science will eventually catch up to the capital, rather than the more familiar and more common outcome in speculative science funding, where the capital simply arrives a decade or two ahead of an achievable result and much of it never gets the return its backers expected.

The chapters immediately ahead look at the specific science this money is chasing — the biological mechanisms these companies are actually betting on, and the mixed, genuinely uneven track record those mechanisms have produced so far in real trials, including some expensive, well-documented failures that the industry's funding totals tend to make easy to overlook.
