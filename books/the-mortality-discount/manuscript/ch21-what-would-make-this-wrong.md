# What Would Make This Wrong

A book arguing that a set of institutions is quietly mispriced owes its reader a clear account of what evidence would prove that argument wrong, rather than simply piling up confirming examples until the case feels settled. This chapter is that account, gathered from the counter-evidence this book has already preserved, chapter by chapter, rather than introduced for the first time here as a rhetorical concession.

## If the science simply doesn't work

The single largest way this book's argument could fail is the most straightforward: if the biotechnology examined across its middle chapters continues, over the coming decade, to produce mostly failures rather than breakthroughs, at roughly the pace it has produced them so far. This is not a hypothetical risk invented for balance. It is the single best-documented pattern in this book's own research. Unity Biotechnology's lead senolytic drug failed its pivotal osteoarthritis trial outright, and five subsequent years of trials testing the dasatinib-and-quercetin senolytic combination across diabetic kidney disease, osteoporosis, and Alzheimer's disease have produced a consistent pattern of small, mixed, often statistically underpowered results, nothing resembling the breakthrough the field's funding totals imply is imminent. Partial cellular reprogramming, the scientific bet behind Altos Labs' three-billion-dollar opening round, has not yet cleared the basic safety problem — controlling the cancer risk inherent in the underlying mechanism — that stands between its most promising mouse results and a single published human trial. The TAME trial, designed specifically to establish a regulatory pathway for treating aging as a target, remains, a decade after its conception, without the funding it needs to run at full scale. If this pattern simply continues — more expensive failures, more underwhelming trial readouts, no genuine breakthrough clearing human trials at scale over the next decade — then the biological half of this book's thesis weakens substantially, and the institutions this book has argued are underpricing a coming shift in healthy human lifespan would, in that scenario, turn out to have been roughly right to price the science as speculative and unproven, exactly as several of the more skeptical sources examined throughout this book have argued they should.

The skeptical case has its own credentialed champion, and this book has tried to preserve his argument fully rather than merely gesturing at it. S. Jay Olshansky's 2024 research in *Nature Aging*, examined in this book's second chapter, found life expectancy gains among the world's longest-lived populations decelerating sharply since 1990, and predicted, on that basis, that maximum population life expectancy would likely stabilize in the high eighties absent a genuine breakthrough in the biology of aging itself — a continuation of a skeptical position Olshansky has held, and defended against considerable pushback, since the early 1990s. If Olshansky's plateau thesis holds up against the 2025 rebuttal that directly challenged it, and if the pattern of biotech failure just described continues alongside it, the two developments together would constitute close to a direct refutation of this book's biological argument: not merely unproven science, but active evidence of a genuine, durable ceiling that no amount of venture capital has yet found a way around.

## What would count as the science actually working

It is worth being equally precise about the opposite side of this falsification test, because a fair account of what would prove this book's argument wrong requires an equally clear account of what would prove its biological half genuinely right, rather than leaving that side vaguer and more easily dismissed. A single successful, well-powered, placebo-controlled human trial demonstrating that a specific intervention — a senolytic drug, a partial-reprogramming protocol, a metformin-style geroprotector tested through a completed TAME trial — meaningfully delays a real, hard clinical endpoint such as cardiovascular disease, cancer diagnosis, or all-cause mortality, replicated independently by a second research group rather than resting on a single company's own reported results, would constitute exactly the kind of evidence this book's biology chapters have found consistently absent so far. Such a result would not, on its own, prove that biological aging has been solved or that dramatic life extension is imminent; the reprogramming and senolytic chapters of this book have been careful to note that even a genuine, replicated success in one specific intervention and one specific disease target would still leave open how broadly that success generalizes to other diseases, other populations, and other mechanisms among the twelve hallmarks of aging. But it would represent a qualitatively different kind of evidence than anything catalogued across this book's chapters on Unity Biotechnology's failed trial, the TAME trial's funding gap, and Altos Labs' still-unresolved safety problem — a first, concrete proof of principle that the geroscience hypothesis examined in this book's third chapter can actually deliver a clinically meaningful result in humans, not merely in mice or in small, underpowered pilot studies. A reader tracking this book's argument forward should treat the announcement of results meeting this bar, from any of the specific programs examined across this book's biology chapters, as the single most consequential piece of evidence that could emerge to strengthen the biological half of this book's thesis, just as this chapter's preceding section treats continued failure as the clearest evidence that could weaken it.

## If the demographic story reverses

The demographic half of this book's argument has its own, separate falsification path, and recent data already shows some genuine movement in the more optimistic direction, which this book's own chapters have tried to represent honestly rather than minimize. South Korea's fertility rate rose for a second consecutive year to 0.8 in 2025, alongside the largest single-year jump in marriages the country has recorded since 1970. If that trend continues rather than reverting to decline once the underlying post-pandemic marriage rebound this book's ninth chapter described works its way through the data, and if similar modest reversals emerge in Japan and China over the coming years, the demographic collapse this book has traced in detail would prove to have been less durable, and less severe, than its most alarmed observers — Nicholas Eberstadt's projections of a South Korea approaching three deaths for every birth by 2050, chief among them — currently suggest. This book has already preserved a serious, credentialed voice arguing close to this position: the political demographer Jennifer Sciubba's explicit public argument that framing global demographic change as a crisis, rather than as a more manageable challenge in care-infrastructure and labor policy, is itself a category error, and that the population trajectories examined across this book's chapters are, in her professional judgment, no reason to panic. If Sciubba's reading proves closer to correct than Eberstadt's more alarmed projections, and if the pronatalist policy failures this book documented in South Korea and Hungary turn out to be early, unrepresentative data points rather than a durable pattern — perhaps because a different policy design, not yet tried at the scale South Korea's or Hungary's programs were, proves more effective — the institutional strain this book has argued is coming would arrive later, and in milder form, than its chapters have suggested.

The United Nations' own population projections offer a built-in reason for humility on this entire question, one this book's ninth chapter already flagged directly: the UN's own peak-population estimate has moved earlier and lower with each successive revision cycle, from no in-century peak at all in its 2019 projections to a peak around 2084 in its most recent 2024 revision, while a separate, credible academic projection from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation modeled an even earlier and lower peak still, a full billion and a half people below the UN's own 2100 estimate. A body of evidence this uncertain, from institutions this credentialed, disagreeing with each other by a margin this large, is not a sound foundation for the kind of confident, decades-out demographic claim a less careful book might have been tempted to make, and any reader encountering a future revision that moves substantially in the other direction — toward slower decline, or a later, higher peak — should weigh it as genuine evidence against this book's demographic argument, not dismiss it as noise around an otherwise settled trend.

## If the institutions turn out to be more adaptive than this book gave them credit for

The strongest institutional counter-evidence in this book's entire argument does not come from a skeptic outside the actuarial profession. It comes from the profession's own history and its own current practice, documented in this book's first chapter: actuaries are not, and never have been, naive people who assume mortality holds still. The Society of Actuaries maintains a continuously updated mortality database spanning thousands of tables across different populations, products, and eras, publishes recurring studies comparing observed mortality against standard tables, and maintains formal mortality-improvement scales specifically designed to project future changes in death rates rather than assume the current table will hold forever. The profession has already absorbed, without institutional collapse, mortality changes across the twentieth century considerably larger than anything documented in this book's own chapters: the near-total elimination of infectious disease as a major cause of death, a near-doubling of life expectancy at birth in the wealthiest countries, and periodic shocks — the 1918 influenza pandemic, two world wars, COVID-19 — that moved mortality tables by years within a single reporting period.

A specific, near-term signal would meaningfully strengthen this counter-argument if it materialized: a major national retirement system enacting a substantial, evidence-driven reform well ahead of its own published crisis point, rather than only after a depletion date has arrived or a fiscal emergency has already forced the issue, the way the 1983 U.S. Social Security reform examined in this book's eighth chapter required an imminent, months-away funding shortfall before Congress acted. Such a reform — enacted, for instance, a full decade or more before a comparable system's own trust-fund depletion date, rather than in the crisis atmosphere the 1983 reform required, or forced through only over sustained mass protest the way France's 2023 reform was — would be genuine evidence that at least some political systems have internalized the actuarial profession's own discipline of continuous, forward-looking revision, rather than the reactive, crisis-driven pattern this book's institutional chapters have found dominating the historical record so far. No such reform has occurred in any of the countries examined in detail across this book's chapters as of this writing, which is itself part of why this book's central argument leans toward institutional under-adaptation rather than adequate foresight; a reader should treat the arrival of such a reform, in any wealthy country, as a meaningful update toward this section's more optimistic counter-argument.

If this book's central claim — that current institutions are underpricing an unusual, compound shift arriving from two directions at once — turns out to be wrong, the likeliest reason is not that the underlying biological or demographic developments this book has documented were themselves overstated, but that the institutional correction mechanism this book's chapters on pension risk transfer and actuarial history already found operating at real scale — tens of billions of dollars a year moving through the pension buyout and buy-in markets alone, insurers and pension funds revising their internal assumptions continuously rather than only once a generation the way a national retirement age tends to — proves more than adequate to absorb whatever gradual repricing the coming decades actually require, the same way it absorbed the twentieth century's much larger mortality shifts without the kind of institutional crisis this book's chapters on Social Security's depletion projections and France's pension protests have described. This book has documented real, serious friction in that adaptive process — the years-long fight over France's two-year retirement-age increase, the American political system's inaction in the face of Social Security's own published depletion date — but friction and eventual failure are not the same thing, and a fair reading of the actuarial profession's own track record, preserved directly in this book's opening chapter, requires taking seriously the possibility that the friction resolves in time, the way it mostly has before.

## If the equity argument turns out to be backward

One further falsification path is worth adding to the three examined above, distinct from the purely biological, demographic, and institutional-adaptation tests already covered: this book's fourteenth chapter argued that any longevity gains that do materialize are likely to widen, rather than narrow, the already substantial income-linked gap in life expectancy documented in the Chetty study, based on the pattern this book found across Bryan Johnson's expensive personal protocol, Altos Labs' patent-focused venture funding, and the GLP-1 drug class's persistent insurance-coverage and pricing disputes. That argument rests on a specific, testable prediction about how new longevity interventions diffuse through a population over time, and this book's own second chapter preserved real counter-evidence against it: the Preston curve's demonstrated historical pattern, in which twentieth-century medical technology did eventually diffuse from the wealthiest early adopters to become cheap and broadly available worldwide, letting later-developing countries reach a given life expectancy at a far lower income threshold than the pioneering countries needed decades earlier. If the current wave of longevity biotechnology follows a Preston-curve-style diffusion path more closely than this book's fourteenth chapter anticipated — if patents expire, manufacturing costs fall, and public health systems in a widening set of countries adopt whatever does prove effective on a timeline of a decade or two rather than remaining permanently concentrated among the wealthiest patients — the distributional warning at the center of that chapter would prove overstated, and a reader should weigh continued, accelerating price declines and broadening insurance coverage for whatever longevity interventions do reach approval as genuine evidence against this book's equity argument specifically, distinct from the broader biological and demographic arguments examined elsewhere in this chapter.

## A test this book itself has already partly failed, in one narrow place

Intellectual honesty about falsification requires acknowledging, directly, at least one place where this book's own research already turned up evidence complicating its argument more than a first draft of this chapter might have been tempted to smooth over. This book's tenth chapter documented South Korea's fertility rate rising for a second consecutive year, to 0.8 in 2025, alongside the largest single-year jump in marriages the country has recorded since 1970 — a genuine, real, positive data point for exactly the kind of demographic recovery this book's central argument treats as unlikely to arrive at meaningful scale. This book's own analysis attributed that rebound primarily to a temporary, post-pandemic marriage catch-up rather than a durable reversal, a reading this chapter's earlier section preserved and stands behind based on the structural, cohort-size argument examined in this book's ninth chapter. But a fair-minded reader should notice that this book's argument survived that specific piece of contrary evidence through interpretation and context rather than through the data simply confirming the book's thesis outright, and should hold the same standard of scrutiny toward every other piece of evidence this book has presented as confirming its case, rather than extending more generous interpretive latitude to confirming evidence than to disconfirming evidence. This is precisely the discipline good forecasting requires, and precisely the discipline this book has asked the actuarial profession, pension funds, and governments to apply to their own mortality assumptions throughout — a standard this book's own argument should be held to as rigorously as the institutions it examines.

## Holding the falsification tests and the argument together

None of the counter-evidence gathered in this chapter, taken individually, fully undoes the case this book has built. The biotech failures documented across its middle chapters coexist with a regulatory and scientific reclassification of aging that, however partial and contested, is real and moving in one direction rather than reversing. The demographic rebounds in South Korea's most recent data coexist with a fertility rate still far below replacement level, propped up by a marriage rebound most serious demographers studying it expect to prove temporary. The actuarial profession's genuine, well-documented adaptive capacity coexists with a Social Security system whose own trustees have published a specific depletion date without Congress moving to prevent it, and with a French government that needed to bypass a parliamentary vote to secure a two-year retirement-age increase far smaller than its own demographic exposure would eventually seem to require.

What this chapter is meant to establish is not that the counter-evidence loses. It is that a reader taking this book's argument seriously should watch specifically for the developments catalogued here — a continued absence of any successful late-stage human trial in longevity biotech over the next several years, a genuine, sustained fertility recovery in East Asia beyond the current marriage-driven bounce, a UN or IHME population revision moving meaningfully toward slower decline, a Social Security reform enacted well ahead of the 2033 depletion date — as the specific, falsifiable signals that would indicate this book's central argument was wrong, or at least premature, rather than treating the argument as a settled prediction immune to the kind of evidence that could, in fact, unsettle it.
