# The Shrinking Century

In 2023, South Korea recorded a total fertility rate of 0.72 children per woman — the lowest ever documented for any country on Earth, in a nation whose population would need a rate of roughly 2.1 simply to hold steady from one generation to the next without immigration. To put the number in a shape that is easier to hold in the mind: if the pattern of 2023 continued indefinitely and unchanged, each successive generation of South Koreans would be, in round terms, about a third the size of the one before it. A country of fifty-two million people, run forward two generations on that trajectory, becomes a country of roughly six million. No developed economy in recorded history has sustained a fertility rate this low for this long, which means South Korea is not merely an extreme data point on an existing chart. It is, at the moment of this writing, the leading edge of a demographic trajectory that much of the rest of East Asia, and a meaningful share of Europe, appears to be following at a slight delay.

The rate has ticked up modestly since — to 0.75 in 2024 and 0.8 in 2025, per data from Statistics Korea, alongside a striking fourteen-point-nine-percent jump in the number of marriages recorded in 2024, the largest single-year increase since national marriage records began in 1970. It would be a mistake to read that recovery as evidence the crisis has passed, for two related reasons that demographers who study the trend take seriously. First, the rebound is widely attributed to a post-pandemic marriage rebound — couples who delayed weddings and childbearing during COVID-19 restrictions catching up on postponed plans — rather than a durable shift in how many children South Korean couples ultimately intend to have over their lifetimes; a Financial Times-cited South Korean sociologist described the pattern, more bluntly, as a likely post-pandemic blip that could reverse if the broader economy weakens. Second, and more structurally, the cohort of women currently in their early-to-mid thirties, whose marriages and births are driving the recent uptick, is itself considerably smaller than the generations before it, a direct consequence of the very fertility decline this chapter is describing, which places a hard mathematical ceiling on how large any near-term rebound can realistically be, almost regardless of how strongly individual attitudes toward marriage and childbearing shift.

Nicholas Eberstadt, a political economist at the American Enterprise Institute who has written extensively on East Asia's demographic trajectory, has projected that if current trends continue, South Korea could see roughly three deaths for every birth by 2050, a median age approaching sixty, more than forty percent of the population classified as senior citizens, and something close to one in six residents over the age of eighty. His own assessment of the policy tools available to reverse the trend is unambiguous and worth quoting directly for its bluntness: he has stated plainly that he does not believe population policy can effectively raise South Korean fertility levels in any appreciable way. That is one demographer's judgment, not a settled consensus, and this chapter will return later to serious researchers who read the underlying situation quite differently. But it is a judgment from someone who has spent a career studying exactly this question, and it sets the stakes for the rest of this chapter's tour through the region.

## Japan, hollowing from the edges inward

Japan's version of the same story has been unfolding for longer, and its most recent chapter arrived with unusual statistical force. The country's 2025 national census, conducted every five years by Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, recorded a total population of 123,049,524 — a decline of roughly three point zero nine million people, or about two and a half percent, since the 2020 census. The pace of decline itself accelerated sharply between those two counts: the 2020 census had recorded a comparatively modest zero-point-seven-percent drop from the count five years before it, meaning the rate of population loss more than tripled in a single five-year interval. Separate annual estimates, distinct from the quinquennial census, put Japan in its sixteenth consecutive year of year-over-year population decline as of the most recent reporting available, establishing the trend as a long, structural feature of the country's demography rather than a recent or temporary development.

The decline is not distributed evenly across the country, and the unevenness is, in some ways, the more important part of the story for understanding what a shrinking population actually does to the places living through it. The 2025 census found that thirty point one percent of Japan's total population now lives in the greater Tokyo metropolitan area — crossing thirty percent for the first time in the country's history — meaning the national population loss is being absorbed disproportionately by the countryside while the capital region holds its population comparatively steady. Rural and mountainous Japan has a specific term for what this looks like at the level of an individual village: a *genkai shūraku*, literally a "limit hamlet" or marginal settlement, a concept coined by the sociologist Akira Ōno in 1991 and defined, in the scholarship that has grown up around it since, as a settlement where more than half the residents are sixty-five or older, placing the community at genuine risk of losing the basic functions — road maintenance, funeral rites, local governance — that keep a small settlement viable at all, with eventual depopulation to zero a realistic long-run outcome for the most extreme cases.

The most visible individual example of what this looks like on the ground is a hamlet called Nagoro, in the Iya Valley of Tokushima Prefecture on the island of Shikoku. A resident named Tsukimi Ayano returned to her hometown in the early 2000s after decades living in Osaka, and began making life-sized scarecrows — first for practical use around the farm, and then, starting shortly afterward, as deliberate likenesses of specific neighbors and relatives who had died or moved away, so that, in her own description of the practice, she could go on "speaking" to them the way she once had. She has since made more than four hundred scarecrows, with roughly three hundred and fifty currently placed around the hamlet — seated at the old school desks, tending fields, waiting at the bus stop — against a human population that has fallen to approximately twenty-seven residents, the youngest of whom is over fifty, down from more than three hundred people during Ayano's own childhood. Nagoro has become, somewhat against its residents' original intentions, an internationally covered tourist attraction and a recurring visual shorthand in international press coverage for rural Japan's demographic hollowing — a village that now has, by a considerable margin, more painted burlap figures than living people, standing in for neighbors who no longer exist to occupy the actual buildings around them.

## China's reversal, and a caution about blaming the obvious culprit

China's demographic trajectory carries a different texture, because it arrived not through decades of gradual decline like Japan's, but through a sharp, well-documented inflection point that most of the world's major news organizations covered simultaneously. In January 2023, China's National Bureau of Statistics reported that the country's population had fallen by eight hundred and fifty thousand people in 2022, to one point four one one eight billion — the first annual population decline China had recorded since 1961, during the famine years of the Great Leap Forward, and a result that arrived roughly a decade earlier than most official Chinese population projections had anticipated only a few years before. The 2022 birth rate fell to a record low of six point seven seven births per thousand people, with deaths exceeding births for the first time in decades; by January 2026, the National Bureau of Statistics was reporting fewer than eight million total births for 2025, the lowest figure in China since 1949, and an estimated fertility rate of roughly 0.93 — below the level Japan itself has recorded in recent years, in a country whose population is roughly ten times Japan's size.

It is tempting, and it is also too simple, to explain China's collapse in fertility primarily by pointing to the one-child policy that governed reproductive choices from 1980 until its formal repeal in 2015 and 2016. A careful look at the longer time series complicates that story in a way this book's discipline about counter-evidence requires taking seriously: China's fertility rate had already been falling substantially before the one-child policy took effect in 1980, driven by rising education, urbanization, and income that were producing similar declines across much of East Asia during the same decades, with or without coercive state family-planning policy. And the decline has continued, largely uninterrupted, through the one-child policy's replacement first with a two-child policy in 2016 and then a three-child policy in 2021 — official reversals that removed the legal restriction on family size entirely without meaningfully reversing the underlying trend, evidence that whatever is now driving China's ultra-low fertility runs considerably deeper than the specific legal restriction the one-child policy imposed. Beijing introduced a national subsidy of thirty-six hundred yuan per child for three years starting in 2025; contemporary policy analysis has generally been skeptical that a subsidy of this size will meaningfully move a fertility rate shaped by structural forces — chief among them, in most analyses, housing costs in major cities that have risen far faster than income — rather than by the legal availability of a second or third child, which has not been the binding constraint for most Chinese families for close to a decade.

By 2025, China counted roughly three hundred and twenty-three million people aged sixty or older, about twenty-three percent of its total population, with that share projected to reach close to thirty-nine percent by 2050 — a demographic transition compressed into a far shorter span, and occurring at a considerably lower level of national income per capita, than the equivalent transitions in Japan, South Korea, or the countries of Western Europe, a combination that carries real implications for how well-prepared China's healthcare and pension systems will be to absorb it, examined more directly in a later chapter on state policy responses.

## What the whole world's numbers actually show

Zoom out from any single country and the global picture, drawn from the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs Population Division's 2024 revision of its World Population Prospects, complicates a purely alarmist reading of the East Asian story without contradicting it. The UN's medium-variant projection has global population growing from roughly eight point two billion in 2024 to a peak of about ten point three billion around 2084, before declining gradually to approximately ten point two billion by century's end — with roughly an eighty percent probability, in the UN's own estimation, that the peak arrives sometime within this century at all. That headline number, though, obscures the more interesting fact this book's earlier chapter on the UN's own revision history already flagged: the peak keeps arriving earlier and lower with each successive revision. The 2019 round of projections still showed continuous growth all the way to 2100, with no peak in sight; the 2022 revision was the first to project an in-century peak at all, at roughly ten point four billion around 2086; the 2024 revision pulled that peak forward again, to roughly ten point three billion around 2084. An independent academic projection from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, published in *The Lancet* in 2020, modeled an even earlier and lower peak still — around nine point seven three billion by 2064, declining to roughly eight point seven nine billion by 2100 — a gap of roughly a billion and a half people from the UN's own 2100 estimate, produced by a credible, independent academic institute using different modeling assumptions about the pace of continued gains in female education and contraceptive access worldwide. That kind of disagreement between two serious, well-resourced institutions, on a question this consequential, is itself a useful corrective against treating any single population projection as a fixed and certain fact rather than the current best estimate of a number that keeps moving.

What is not in serious dispute, across any of these competing projections, is the underlying regional divergence driving the global total in the first place. Sub-Saharan Africa's population is projected to grow by roughly two-thirds between 2024 and 2050 and, on the UN's own numbers, to become the world's most populous region by the end of the century; nearly every country in the world still recording a fertility rate of four births per woman or higher, as of the most recent data, is in sub-Saharan Africa, with the Democratic Republic of the Congo alone projected to grow by roughly ninety-three percent by 2050. Europe and North America's combined population, by contrast, is projected to remain roughly flat through 2050, while East Asia and parts of Eastern Europe dominate the list of the world's fastest-shrinking countries and regions. This is not, in other words, a single global story of population collapse. It is a sharp bifurcation: a set of countries, concentrated in East Asia and parts of Europe, facing the specific institutional strain this book is centrally concerned with — aging, shrinking populations whose pension systems, labor markets, and care infrastructure were built for a very different demographic shape — arriving at roughly the same historical moment as a separate set of countries, concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia, still managing the entirely different set of pressures that come with rapid population growth. Treating either half of that story as the whole global picture, as popular coverage of "the population crisis" or "the population bomb" tends to do depending on which decade's anxiety is fashionable, misses the more precise and more interesting reality: two demographic worlds, moving in opposite directions, on the same planet, at the same time.

The chapters immediately ahead look more closely at what that bifurcation actually does to the institutions and the individual lives caught inside it — starting with the places where the arithmetic has already run furthest ahead of the policy response built to manage it.
