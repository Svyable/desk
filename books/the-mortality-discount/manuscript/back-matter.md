# Back Matter

## Epilogue

Edmond Halley's table, examined in this book's opening chapter, was never perfect. It was useful for a long time anyway, because a profession grew up around it that kept checking the table against the world and revising it when the world disagreed. That is the smallest, most portable thing this book has to offer a reader who has made it this far: not a prediction about how long you, specifically, are going to live, and not a confident date for when any of the institutions examined in these chapters will finally catch up to the reality this book has tried to document. Just the same discipline Halley's successors have practiced, imperfectly, for three centuries — hold the number loosely, watch for the evidence that would change it, and notice when an institution you depend on has stopped doing the same.

## Notes and Sources

This manuscript was researched in three passes, organized around the biology and medicine of aging, global demographic change, and the actuarial, financial, and policy institutions that price human lifespan. Chapter-level research notes and source citations live alongside the manuscript in this book's `research/` directory, including the source ledger for the opening two chapters contributed during the same drafting pass.

The sourcing hierarchy follows the same discipline this book asks of the institutions it examines: primary sources — peer-reviewed papers, government and regulatory documents, company filings and press releases — are preferred wherever a claim can be traced to one directly. Contemporaneous financial and science journalism is used where the event itself, such as a single day's market reaction, is the subject. Where two credible sources reported materially different figures for the same fact — the TAME trial's funding gap, South Korea's cumulative pronatalist spending, Altos Labs' exact backer list — this book states the discrepancy explicitly rather than silently picking one number to sound more certain than the underlying record supports.

Real counter-evidence is preserved throughout rather than argued away: the Blue Zones controversy is presented with both Newman's challenge and Buettner's rebuttal in view; Olshansky's skepticism about the pace of future life-expectancy gains sits alongside the geroscience research it challenges; South Korea's 2024-2025 fertility uptick is reported honestly even though this book's central argument reads it as likely temporary. A dedicated chapter near the end of this book, "What Would Make This Wrong," gathers these threads explicitly and states what evidence would falsify the book's central argument, in either direction.

## Acknowledgments

This book depends on the people who keep the underlying records this argument is built from: the actuaries who publish mortality-experience studies instead of hiding them, the statistical agencies that keep revising and re-releasing population data even when the revision is unflattering, the researchers who publish a senolytic trial's disappointing results as carefully as its promising ones, and the journalists who tracked a company's stock price on the way down as closely as they tracked it on the way up.

The historical record this book relies on is, as always, incomplete in places no author can fully repair. Where a figure could not be pinned to a single confident number, this book has tried to say so rather than paper over the gap. Any remaining errors of interpretation belong to the author.
