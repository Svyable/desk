# The Ozempic Economy

On September 5, 2023, Novo Nordisk closed the trading day worth roughly four hundred and twenty-eight billion dollars, having just passed LVMH to become the most valuable publicly listed company in Europe. A Danish pharmaceutical firm founded in 1923 to manufacture insulin had, in the space of about two years, become worth more than the conglomerate that owns Louis Vuitton, Moët, and Hennessy combined, on the strength of a drug that had originally been approved for an entirely different purpose than the one now driving its stock price.

Semaglutide was developed and first approved, in December 2017, as a treatment for type 2 diabetes, sold under the brand name Ozempic. It belongs to a class of drugs called GLP-1 receptor agonists, which mimic a hormone the gut releases after eating, signaling the pancreas to release insulin and, crucially for what came next, slowing digestion and suppressing appetite. Patients using it for diabetes management kept reporting, as a side effect, that they were losing a great deal of weight. Novo Nordisk formalized that side effect into a second product: Wegovy, a higher-dose version of the same molecule, approved specifically for chronic weight management in June 2021. What followed was not a gradual product rollout. It was one of the fastest and largest pharmaceutical demand shocks in modern history, driven overwhelmingly by word of mouth, celebrity use, and a genuine, well-documented clinical effect that patients experienced directly on their own bodies rather than something they had to take on faith from a study.

The stock chart tells only the beginning of the story, and the ending, so far, has been considerably less triumphant. By February 2026, financial coverage was describing a very different Novo Nordisk: shares down nearly fifty percent over the course of 2025, and roughly seventy percent below their mid-2024 peak. The company that had briefly been Europe's most valuable had been overtaken in the obesity-drug race by a competitor whose entire pharmaceutical business, until recently, had been a fraction of Novo's size. Eli Lilly's tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro for diabetes and Zepbound for weight loss, had captured roughly sixty percent of the U.S. GLP-1 market by early 2025, helped along by clinical trial data suggesting greater average weight loss than semaglutide, and by the failure of Novo's next-generation candidate, CagriSema, to clearly beat tirzepatide in head-to-head testing. In November 2025, Eli Lilly became the first healthcare company in history to reach a one-trillion-dollar market valuation, on the strength of combined Zepbound and Mounjaro sales that had grown to roughly nineteen billion dollars in the first nine months of that year alone, overtaking Merck's cancer drug Keytruda to become the best-selling pharmaceutical franchise in the world.

This is, in miniature, exactly the pattern the first chapter described as the recurring shape of a genuine technological revolution meeting a market that cannot yet price it correctly: real, substantial, durable value being created, alongside enormous swings in who captures it, on a timeline far more volatile than the underlying medical reality actually changed. GLP-1 drugs did not get meaningfully less effective between mid-2024 and early 2026. What changed was competitive position, trial results, and market expectations — the layer where a genuine breakthrough gets translated, however imperfectly, into a price.

## A diabetes drug becomes a longevity drug, by accident

It is worth being precise about the order events actually happened in, because the popular version of this story tends to compress it into something cleaner than the record supports. GLP-1 drugs were not conceived, designed, or first approved as longevity medicine. They were developed to manage blood sugar in diabetics. Weight loss was a secondary effect, discovered largely through patient experience rather than the original trial design, that turned out to be commercially and medically enormous enough to justify an entirely new drug and a new indication. Only after obesity treatment had already made these drugs into some of the most valuable pharmaceutical products in history did evidence begin to accumulate that they might do something more directly relevant to the subject of this book: not just help people lose weight, but reduce their risk of dying from the diseases most strongly associated with excess weight and metabolic dysfunction.

The strongest evidence for that broader claim comes from a trial called SELECT, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in November 2023. It was a large, rigorously designed study — a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial run at more than eight hundred sites in forty-one countries, enrolling participants forty-five and older who had a body mass index of twenty-seven or higher and pre-existing cardiovascular disease, but who were not diabetic. This last detail matters: the trial was specifically designed to separate the drug's cardiovascular benefit from its effect on diabetes, since a drug that merely manages blood sugar better would be expected to reduce cardiovascular risk in diabetics for reasons that have nothing to do with weight or metabolic health more broadly. Over an average follow-up of nearly forty months, participants taking semaglutide at 2.4 milligrams experienced a twenty percent reduction in the combined rate of cardiovascular death, non-fatal heart attack, and non-fatal stroke compared to placebo, alongside an average weight reduction of just over ten percent by the end of the study period. A twenty percent reduction in hard cardiovascular outcomes, in a population that did not have diabetes, is a genuinely significant result — the kind of finding that moves a drug class from "helps you lose weight" to "measurably reduces your risk of dying of the most common cause of death in the developed world," which is about as close to a plausible longevity claim as a pharmaceutical trial is likely to produce without directly targeting the biology of aging itself.

The trial's less-quoted numbers are worth keeping in view alongside the headline result, because they are a small, concrete instance of this book's recurring caution against one-sided enthusiasm. Adverse events serious enough to cause participants to permanently stop taking the drug occurred in 16.6 percent of the semaglutide group, versus 8.2 percent on placebo — roughly double the discontinuation rate. A drug that measurably reduces cardiovascular death is not the same thing as a drug with no real cost to the people taking it, and a fifth of the treatment group in the pivotal trial found the tradeoff bad enough to stop.

## Knock-on effects, measured

Part of what makes the GLP-1 story unusually good material for a book about institutional repricing is how quickly and measurably its effects have spread beyond the pharmaceutical industry itself, into adjacent businesses that had built their own economics around a set of assumptions about how many people would need their services.

Bariatric surgery is the clearest case. A study published in *JAMA Surgery* in October 2024, covered by Harvard's T.H. Chan School of Public Health and the health-news outlet STAT, found that GLP-1 prescriptions rose roughly 140 percent between 2022 and 2024, while bariatric surgery volume fell by about a third over the same period. Figures from accredited surgical centers showed the annual procedure count dropping from 217,387 in 2023 to 177,297 in 2024 — a decline of more than twenty percent in a single year, in a surgical specialty that had spent decades building capacity around a fairly stable demand curve for severe obesity intervention. It is not yet possible to say with certainty how much of that decline is a direct, causal substitution effect of patients choosing a drug over an operation, since other factors — changing insurance coverage, shifting post-pandemic patterns in elective surgery generally — plausibly contribute as well. But the direction and the scale of the shift are exactly the kind of thing an entire allied medical specialty needs to plan capacity, training pipelines, and reimbursement models around, on a timeline of a few years rather than the decades those systems were designed to absorb change over.

The insurance and entitlement side of the story is still unresolved, and it is worth understanding why, because it is a live, ongoing example of an institution running directly into a repricing problem this book keeps returning to. Federal law currently bars the U.S. Medicare program from covering GLP-1 drugs for weight loss alone; coverage is permitted only when the drug is prescribed for an already-approved indication such as diabetes or, following the SELECT trial's results, cardiovascular risk reduction. An April 2025 proposal from the Trump administration to expand Medicare and Medicaid coverage of GLP-1s specifically for obesity was not finalized. A subsequent negotiated arrangement between the federal government and both Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk — sometimes referred to as the BALANCE model — offers discounted pricing for these drugs through Medicare Part D, without resolving the more fundamental question of whether an entitlement program designed around treating diagnosed disease should pay for a drug primarily to prevent obesity-related disease from developing in the first place. One government cost projection estimated that Medicare could spend as much as sixty-six billion dollars over a decade if broad weight-loss coverage were adopted. That is the kind of number that makes a fiscal question out of what looks, from a purely medical standpoint, like an obviously good idea: a drug that measurably reduces cardiovascular death is, by most ordinary reasoning, worth paying for. Whether the specific institution built to pay for elderly Americans' healthcare was ever designed to absorb a preventive drug at that annual cost, across a population as large as everyone who might benefit from it, is a genuinely open, unresolved policy fight — a live instance, playing out in real time as this book is being written, of an entitlement system built on one set of assumptions colliding with a medical breakthrough that its rules were never written to anticipate.

## A trillion-dollar market, and the discipline that should come with one

Analysts covering the sector have projected the global obesity-drug market could reach roughly one hundred billion dollars in annual sales by 2030, a scale that invites comparison with the largest and most durable pharmaceutical franchises in history. That scale is also precisely why this chapter, consistent with the rest of the book, resists treating the GLP-1 story as settled or purely triumphant.

A single drug class reaching a trillion dollars of combined market value for its manufacturer, as Eli Lilly did in November 2025, concentrates an enormous amount of financial expectation into a category of medicine that is still, in a meaningful sense, young — SELECT's results are from 2023, Zepbound's obesity approval is from November 2023, and the field is already crowded with next-generation competitors: oral GLP-1 formulations that could displace the injectable versions currently on the market, triple-hormone agonists designed to improve on tirzepatide's mechanism the way tirzepatide improved on semaglutide's, and an unknown number of therapies still in earlier-stage trials at companies betting they can take share from two now-enormous incumbents. Novo Nordisk's roughly seventy percent decline from its 2024 peak is itself the clearest evidence available that this market is nowhere near finished repricing itself: a company that briefly looked like the unambiguous, permanent winner of the most important pharmaceutical story of the decade lost most of that lead to a single competitor in about eighteen months, on the strength of trial results and product formulation differences that were, on the scale of the underlying medical breakthrough, comparatively minor.

That volatility is the chapter's real subject, more than any single drug or company. GLP-1 drugs are the clearest existing case of a medicine with a real, trial-documented effect on the diseases that kill people — cardiovascular disease chief among them — reaching the market at a scale large enough to move national accounts, reshape an entire surgical specialty's caseload, and force a decades-old entitlement program to confront a coverage question it was never designed to answer cleanly. It is, in other words, the first genuinely large-scale test case for nearly every argument this book makes about the mortality discount, arriving years ahead of the more speculative science covered in the chapters that follow — not because anyone set out to build a longevity drug, but because a diabetes medication turned out, almost by accident, to be one anyway.
