# Half-Life

In November 2004, the European Investment Bank announced what its structurer, BNP Paribas, called the world's first traded longevity bond: a twenty-five-year, five-hundred-and-forty-million-pound instrument, its annual payout tied directly to how many people in a reference group of English and Welsh men, aged sixty-five in 2003, remained alive each year. The idea, elegant on paper, was to let pension funds do something they had never been able to do cleanly before: buy protection against the specific financial risk that their members would live longer than expected, the same way a company can buy insurance against a fire or a currency swing. If the reference population lived longer than the bond's issuers had projected, the bond would pay out more, offsetting the extra pension payments the buying fund would owe its own, similarly long-lived members. It was reinsured through the Bermuda-based reinsurer PartnerRe and was, by any reasonable description, a serious, well-engineered piece of financial infrastructure, built by some of the same people who had spent careers thinking rigorously about exactly the problem this book is concerned with: what happens to an institution that has priced a promise against an assumption about how long people will live, when that assumption turns out to be wrong.

It failed. Investor demand fell short of what the deal needed to launch, and by late 2005 the bond had been formally withdrawn for redesign, never to return to market in its original form. A subsequent, detailed academic postmortem by the actuaries David Blake, Andrew Cairns, and Kevin Dowd — published in the *British Actuarial Journal* in 2006 and still treated as the definitive technical account of what went wrong — identified several compounding problems. The bond hedged only a narrow reference population, English and Welsh men who turned sixty-five in 2003, that did not closely match the actual membership of most pension schemes considering buying it, creating what actuaries call basis risk: the protection you're buying doesn't quite match the risk you're actually carrying, which makes the hedge less useful and harder to value. The deal required investors to commit an unusually large amount of capital relative to the specific slice of risk being transferred, which crowded out their capacity to use the same capital hedging other exposures. And PartnerRe, the reinsurer actually absorbing the underlying longevity risk, was unwilling to provide cover beyond the bond's five-hundred-and-forty-million-pound size, capping how large the deal could ever scale even if demand had otherwise been stronger.

The failure of the EIB bond is worth dwelling on for longer than a single market footnote deserves, because it captures something true about the entire history of institutions trying to price and trade longevity risk directly: the problem is not that nobody has understood the risk. Actuaries have understood, with real technical sophistication, that longevity risk is real, quantifiable, and hedgeable in principle for at least as long as this book's opening chapter's story about Edmond Halley and his Breslau life table. The problem has been building financial instruments precise enough, liquid enough, and large enough to actually transfer that risk at the scale modern pension systems require, without the basis risk, capital intensity, and thin reinsurance capacity that sank the first serious attempt to do it in a fully public, tradable form.

## Two ways to hedge the same risk, and why one of them won

It is worth being concrete about what basis risk actually meant for a pension fund considering the EIB deal, because the abstraction can make an otherwise intuitive problem sound more technical than it is. Imagine a pension scheme covering retired schoolteachers, a workforce that is disproportionately female, university-educated, and drawn from a specific set of regional labor markets — a population whose actual mortality experience might differ meaningfully, for reasons having nothing to do with random chance, from the EIB bond's reference population of English and Welsh men who happened to turn sixty-five in 2003. If teachers, as a group, tend to live somewhat longer or somewhat shorter than that reference population, a bond payout indexed to the wrong group's survival will not track the pension fund's actual liability correctly: the fund could pay for protection against a specific flavor of longevity risk and still find itself under-hedged, or over-hedged, against the risk it actually carries, once the two populations' mortality experience diverges over the following twenty-five years. A hedge that does not reliably track the thing it is meant to hedge is, in an important financial sense, not a very good hedge, however elegant its underlying construction — and building a single, standardized reference index broad and representative enough to serve every pension scheme's differently composed membership, without becoming so generic that it stops tracking any specific scheme's real risk well, turned out to be a harder engineering problem than the deal's original architects had fully solved in 2004.

The private, bilateral buy-in and buyout market examined later in this chapter solved the same underlying problem through a comparatively unglamorous but more effective route: rather than building one standardized public instrument and asking every buyer to accept its basis risk, each transaction is negotiated individually, with the insurer pricing the specific mortality experience of the specific pension scheme's specific membership, using detailed, plan-level data about the actual people involved — their ages, their occupations, their historical health patterns, sometimes even individual medical underwriting for large schemes — rather than a single national reference population standing in for all of them. This is, in effect, a return to something closer to Edmond Halley's original insight from this book's opening chapter, just at drastically larger and more sophisticated scale: build the table from the actual population you are pricing, rather than borrowing someone else's population and hoping it transfers cleanly. The tradeoff is that this bespoke, deal-by-deal approach sacrifices exactly the liquidity and standardization that would have made a public, tradable longevity bond market genuinely valuable — every transaction has to be separately negotiated and separately priced, with none of the ease of simply buying and selling a standardized security on an open exchange, which is part of why the market that actually grew, described below, looks nothing like the liquid public market Blake, Cairns, and Dowd's original academic vision anticipated, even as it moved vastly larger sums of money.

## The market that grew anyway, quietly, without the bond

What is genuinely striking, twenty years on, is that longevity risk transfer did not die with the EIB bond. It simply stopped trying to be a public, tradable security and became, instead, a large, opaque, insurer-to-insurer wholesale market — less elegant than Blake, Cairns, and Dowd's academic vision of a liquid longevity bond market, but enormously larger in practice than that original 2004 experiment ever reached.

In the United Kingdom, pension schemes transfer longevity risk to insurers through what the industry calls buy-in and buyout transactions: a pension fund pays an insurer a lump sum, and the insurer takes on the obligation to pay the scheme's members' pensions for as long as they live, effectively selling the fund an enormous, custom-built annuity covering its entire membership at once. According to year-end data compiled by the actuarial consultancy Lane Clark & Peacock, UK buy-in and buyout volumes reached forty-seven point eight billion pounds in 2024 — the second-highest year on record — spread across two hundred and ninety-eight individual transactions, up more than thirty percent from two hundred and twenty-seven deals the year before. More than half of that annual total, thirty-two and a half billion pounds across over a hundred and sixty deals, closed in the second half of 2024 alone, suggesting a market still accelerating rather than plateauing. Trade press coverage projected 2025 would set a new transaction-count record, around three hundred and fifty deals, even as total pound volume for the first half of the year came in at its lowest level since 2021 — a genuine complication worth preserving rather than smoothing over, since it shows the market maturing toward a larger number of smaller transactions rather than simply growing uniformly on every metric at once; more than eighty-five percent of first-half 2025 transactions, by number, were valued under one hundred million pounds.

The American version of the same market has grown on a comparable scale. According to LIMRA, the life insurance industry's own research association, total U.S. single-premium pension risk transfer sales reached fifty-one point eight billion dollars in 2024, up fourteen percent from the year before and within one percent of the record set in 2022, spread across a record seven hundred and ninety-four contracts. Buy-out premium alone — the version where an insurer takes full, permanent responsibility for a pension plan's obligations, rather than merely reinsuring part of the risk — rose sixteen percent to forty-eight point one billion dollars across a record seven hundred and eighty-four contracts, and fourteen separate insurance carriers each closed at least one deal worth more than a billion dollars, the highest number of billion-dollar-plus single carriers ever recorded in a single year. Momentum accelerated further into the following year: LIMRA reported that new single-premium pension risk transfer sales in the fourth quarter of 2025 alone jumped a hundred and thirty-two percent year over year to twenty-eight billion dollars, with cumulative in-force buy-out assets on insurers' books reaching three hundred and twenty-six billion dollars, up ten percent from the prior year.

Put those two markets together and the picture is unambiguous: tens of billions of dollars and pounds of longevity risk are now changing hands every single year, quietly, transaction by transaction, between corporate pension sponsors eager to remove an uncertain, balance-sheet-distorting liability and insurers willing to absorb it — usually in exchange for a price that assumes the insurer's own actuaries have a better handle on future mortality trends than the pension sponsor's did. This is, in effect, the market Blake, Cairns, and Dowd's original longevity bond was trying to create in 2004, achieved twenty years later through a less elegant but evidently more workable mechanism: not a single, standardized, publicly tradable instrument, but thousands of bespoke, privately negotiated deals between institutions that each employ their own actuaries and have their own reasons to believe they're pricing the underlying mortality risk correctly.

## What a small mispricing does at scale

It is worth walking through, in simple illustrative terms, why a seemingly minor mortality misestimate can matter so much financially at the scale this market now operates. Consider a hypothetical pension buyout covering ten thousand retirees, each expected, on the insurer's pricing table, to receive an average pension of twenty thousand dollars a year for an average remaining lifespan the table sets at eighteen years. If the insurer's mortality assumption turns out to be off by even a single year — the retirees, on average, actually live nineteen years rather than eighteen — the insurer owes an additional year of payments across the entire pool: on the numbers just given, roughly two hundred million dollars of unplanned additional liability, before accounting for investment returns on the reserves set aside to cover it, spread across a single transaction that might have looked, at signing, like a routine, adequately reserved deal. Multiply that kind of exposure across the hundreds of individual buyout and buy-in transactions closing every year in the markets described above — the seven hundred and ninety-four single-premium contracts LIMRA recorded in the U.S. in 2024 alone, or the two hundred and ninety-eight UK transactions LCP tracked the same year — and the aggregate sensitivity of the entire pension risk transfer industry to even a modest, economy-wide shift in life expectancy becomes considerably larger than any single transaction's fine print would suggest. This is precisely why insurers active in this market maintain large teams of actuaries dedicated specifically to monitoring emerging mortality trends, medical developments, and demographic data in close to real time, rather than simply pricing a deal once against a static table and moving on: the cost of being wrong compounds with the size of the book of business, and the pension risk transfer market's rapid recent growth has made that book very large indeed.

## Somebody is still holding the bag

That last clause is the one worth sitting with, because a pension risk transfer market this large does not eliminate longevity risk from the financial system. It relocates it, concentrating an enormous and growing share of it onto the balance sheets of a comparatively small number of life insurers, each of whom is making the same fundamental bet this entire book is interested in: that their internal mortality assumptions, however sophisticated, are accurate enough to price tens of billions of dollars of decades-long liability correctly, today, based on data that is necessarily backward-looking.

This is not a hypothetical risk invented for the sake of this book's argument; it is the ordinary, acknowledged business risk that every actuary pricing a longevity-linked product understands explicitly, even if the public rarely hears it discussed in those terms. An insurer that takes on a pension buyout is, functionally, betting that the members of that pension scheme will not live meaningfully longer than the insurer's models predict, because every additional year of unexpected survival across a large enough pool of policyholders is a year of benefit payments the insurer's pricing did not account for. If the biotechnology developments discussed elsewhere in this book — GLP-1 drugs' demonstrated reduction in cardiovascular mortality, or a future therapy that more directly targets the biology of aging — meaningfully shift population-level mortality in a direction faster or larger than current actuarial tables assume, the institutions now holding hundreds of billions of dollars of longevity risk on their books, across the UK and US markets alone, are the ones who would feel it first and most directly, in a way ordinary depositors or taxpayers mostly would not notice until much later, if a genuine mispricing forced a reckoning.

The life settlements market, a smaller and considerably murkier corner of the same broad category, illustrates what can go wrong when mortality estimates used to price a longevity-linked financial product turn out to be badly off — in either direction. In this market, investors buy existing life insurance policies directly from living policyholders, typically elderly or terminally ill people willing to sell their policy for a lump sum larger than its cash surrender value but smaller than its eventual death benefit, with the investor then paying ongoing premiums and collecting the payout when the original policyholder dies. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission brought fraud charges in 2012 against a major life settlements firm, Life Partners Holdings, along with its chief executive and two other senior officers, alleging the company had systematically and materially underestimated the life expectancies used to price transactions with investors — understating them, the SEC alleged, by a margin of at least eight years in some cases — while relying on a single reviewing physician, with no actuarial training, who assessed cases in a matter of minutes and, according to contemporaneous reporting cited in later coverage, was reportedly not paid unless his life-expectancy estimates came in short enough to make the investment look attractive. It is worth stating the ending plainly, because it complicates the story rather than resolving it neatly in either direction: a federal jury ultimately found that Life Partners had not committed fraud and that its officers had not engaged in the insider trading the SEC separately alleged. The underlying controversy over how the company's life-expectancy estimates were produced remains real and well-documented regardless of the trial's outcome, but the case did not end in a clean regulatory victory establishing fraud as a matter of law, and a fair account of it needs to hold both facts at once.

A newer, still-unresolved dispute in the same market involves the private equity firm Apollo Global Management, accused in a 2024 lawsuit of running what the suit calls an illegal human-life-wagering scheme through a roughly twenty-billion-dollar portfolio of policies acquired from strangers to the insured — a legally fraught category of arrangement, since most jurisdictions require an "insurable interest," some legitimate financial or familial stake in a person's continued life, before someone can profit from a policy on that person's death. Apollo has publicly and firmly denied the allegations, describing them as flatly wrong and stating that the fund in question only acquired existing pools of already-issued insurance-linked securities rather than originating the underlying policies itself, before winding the fund down in 2019. A Delaware court has issued at least one preliminary ruling favorable to the plaintiffs in the underlying dispute, but the matter remains contested and unresolved as of this writing, and it should be described as an allegation, not an established fact, until it is.

## What half a century of hedging longevity risk has actually taught the industry

Read together, the story this chapter tells is not one of an industry that failed to notice or take seriously the risk this book is centrally about. It is closer to the opposite: a specialized profession, actuarial science, that has spent the better part of the last two decades building increasingly large, increasingly sophisticated machinery specifically to transfer and manage longevity risk at scale — and that machinery, having failed in its first, most elegant public form, quietly rebuilt itself as a private, bilateral market that now moves tens of billions of dollars a year without most people outside the pension and insurance industries ever hearing about it.

That machinery's success at moving risk around should not be confused with having solved the underlying pricing problem. Every buy-in, every buyout, every reinsurance treaty in this now-enormous market still depends on the same fundamental input this book's opening chapter identified in Edmond Halley's original life table: a best-current-guess number for how long people will live, dressed up in enough actuarial rigor to look settled, waiting for the world to eventually prove it right or wrong. The scale has grown by orders of magnitude since 2004. The exposure to being wrong has grown right along with it.
