# Chapter 8 research — retirement age history and Social Security's finances

Checked September 10, 2026.

## Claim corrections that should govern the next prose pass

### 1. Bismarck did not choose 65, but that does not make 65 non-actuarial

The familiar Bismarck story is wrong in the narrow historical sense: Germany's 1889 old-age insurance system initially used age 70, and the threshold was lowered to 65 only in 1916, long after Bismarck left office and after his death.

But the stronger manuscript inference — that 65 therefore was "not a carefully calculated actuarial threshold at all" — goes too far for the U.S. case. The Social Security Administration's own historical account says the Committee on Economic Security chose 65 pragmatically because it was already common in state pension systems and the 1934 Railroad Retirement System, then checked the choice against actuarial studies. Those studies showed that a system using 65 could be made self-sustaining with comparatively modest payroll taxation.

Source: Social Security Administration, "Age 65 Retirement" historical FAQ: https://www.ssa.gov/history/age65.html

**Permissible inference:** retirement ages are policy choices constrained by actuarial arithmetic, labor-market practice, distributional concerns, and political feasibility. Their contingency does not make them arbitrary in the sense of being uncalculated.

**Counterevidence to preserve:** a retirement age can be historically contingent and still be actuarially defensible at the moment it is chosen. The more interesting problem is how slowly the political number can adjust after the population, labor market, or fiscal assumptions move.

### 2. The current Social Security countdown is now Q4 2032 for OASI

The manuscript's 2025 figures are stale. The 2026 Social Security Trustees Report, released June 9, 2026, projects:

- OASI reserves depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032, one quarter earlier than the 2025 projection;
- 78 percent of scheduled OASI benefits payable from continuing income at depletion;
- combined OASI + DI reserves depleted in the third quarter of 2034, unchanged from the 2025 combined-fund projection;
- 83 percent of combined scheduled benefits payable at that point;
- DI reserves remaining positive through the 75-year projection period.

Primary sources:

- SSA press release, June 9, 2026: https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/press/releases/2026-06-09.html
- Trustees Report summary: https://www.ssa.gov/oact/TRSUM/
- Actuarial publications index: https://www.ssa.gov/OACT/pubs.html

**Important legal/analytic boundary:** OASI and DI are legally separate trust funds. A combined OASDI depletion date is a useful summary measure, but it assumes a legal combination or reallocation that current law does not itself provide automatically.

### 3. Do not say the depletion date moves "almost entirely" with demographics

The 2026 Trustees Report directly gives a stronger and more nuanced decomposition. The 75-year OASDI actuarial balance worsened by 0.60 percentage points versus the 2025 report. The report attributes the change approximately as follows:

- demographic data and assumptions: -0.44 percentage points;
- legislation/regulation: -0.16;
- change in valuation period: -0.07;
- methods/programmatic data: -0.05;
- economic data and assumptions: +0.10;
- disability data and assumptions: +0.02.

Demography is the largest single driver in this year's deterioration, but not the only material one. The chapter should treat Social Security solvency as an interaction among fertility, mortality, immigration, covered earnings, wage growth, employment, legislation, benefit rules, and valuation assumptions rather than as a nearly pure longevity/fertility meter.

Source: 2026 Trustees Report summary, Table 11: https://www.ssa.gov/oact/TRSUM/

**Counterargument:** this weakens any attempt to use the trust-fund date as a direct proxy for "the mortality discount." Social Security is unusually transparent, but it is still a fiscal model containing many moving inputs.

### 4. France is now an even better example of political reversibility

The manuscript currently ends the French case with the 2023 reform raising the legal minimum retirement age from 62 toward 64. That is no longer the whole story.

France's 2026 Social Security financing law partially suspended the 2023 timetable for affected cohorts. Article 105 of the December 30, 2025 law revised the age schedule, including a legal age of 62 years 9 months for people born from January 1, 1963 through March 31, 1965; 63 for the remainder of the 1965 cohort; 63 years 3 months for 1966; 63 years 6 months for 1967; 63 years 9 months for 1968; and 64 for people born from January 1, 1969. A May 7, 2026 implementing decree explicitly describes these changes as taking account of the suspension of the 2023 pension reform and applies relevant provisions to pensions taking effect from September 1, 2026.

Primary sources:

- Légifrance, 2026 Social Security financing law, Article 105: https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/article_jo/JORFARTI000053227007
- Légifrance, Decree 2026-345 of May 7, 2026: https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000054047736

**Permissible inference:** France is not merely a story about how politically costly it is to raise a retirement age. It is now a cleaner example of retirement age as a negotiated promise that can advance, stall, and partially reverse even after legislation has been enacted.

**Counterargument:** this does not prove governments cannot reform retirement systems ahead of crisis. It shows that implementation itself remains politically contingent and that "the retirement age" is often a cohort schedule rather than one immutable national number.

## Strongest counterargument to Chapter 8's current thesis

The strongest rebuttal is not that retirement ages never calcify. It is that a single retirement age is an intentionally crude coordination device. Governments need a legible eligibility rule even though people differ dramatically in health, occupation, disability, wealth, expected lifespan, and ability to keep working. Making the age update mechanically with average life expectancy could be regressive if longevity gains accrue disproportionately to healthier and wealthier groups while workers in physically demanding jobs see smaller gains in healthy working life.

That suggests a stronger chapter thesis than "sixty-five was a guess": **retirement ages are political compression algorithms for heterogeneous lives.** The challenge is not simply to keep one number synchronized with national life expectancy. It is to decide which biological, fiscal, labor-market, and distributional changes should move eligibility rules, for whom, and on what timetable.

## Recommended manuscript changes

A future prose pass should:

1. keep the Bismarck myth correction, but remove the inference that the U.S. age 65 lacked actuarial support;
2. replace the 2025 Trustees figures with the June 9, 2026 figures;
3. remove the claim that the depletion date moves almost entirely with demographic assumptions and use the Trustees' own decomposition instead;
4. update France through the 2026 partial suspension/revised cohort schedule;
5. make distributional heterogeneity the chapter's principal counterargument to mechanically indexing retirement age to average longevity.

The revised chapter should therefore move from **"the number was arbitrary"** toward **"the number is necessarily political, but the evidence underneath it keeps moving."**
