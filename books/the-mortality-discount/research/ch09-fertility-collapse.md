# Chapter 9 research — the global fertility collapse

Covers South Korea's fertility rate (0.72 in 2023, 0.75 in 2024, 0.80 in 2025) and Eberstadt's 2050 projections; Japan's 2025 census preliminary counts (123.05 million, -3.097 million/-2.5% since 2020, 30.1% in the Tokyo metropolitan area) and the genkai shūraku concept (Ōno 1991) plus Nagoro; China's 2022 first population decline since 1961 and official 2025 population data (7.92 million births, 11.31 million deaths, 323.38 million people age 60+, 23.0% of the population), alongside the Our World in Data nuance that fertility decline predates and outlasted the one-child policy; and the UN World Population Prospects 2024 revision (peak ~10.3B in the mid-2080s, with an 80% probability of an in-century peak) versus the IHME/Lancet 2020 independent projection (peak ~9.73B around 2064).

Primary-source corrections checked 2026-09-02: Japan's Statistics Bureau describes the May 29, 2026 release as **preliminary counts** from the October 2025 Population Census, so manuscript language should not present those figures as final census totals. China's National Bureau of Statistics reports 7.92 million births in 2025 and a year-end population of 1,404.89 million; it does not itself report the chapter's ~0.93 fertility-rate estimate, so that estimate should remain clearly attributed to an external estimate or be omitted. South Korea's 2025 total fertility rate of 0.80 is now supported by the government's 2025 birth-statistics release; the 2024 marriage increase was 14.8%, not 14.9%.

Counterevidence: South Korea's fertility rate has now risen for two consecutive years, and marriages increased again in 2025. That does not establish a durable reversal, but it weakens any framing that treats the 2023 low as an uninterrupted trajectory. Likewise, the UN's 2024 projection still has global population growing for roughly another six decades; the book's institutional argument is regional and age-structural, not a claim of imminent global depopulation.

Falsification note: weakens if South Korea's fertility rebound continues for several more years and broadens across cohorts rather than reflecting timing/composition effects, if China's fertility stabilizes materially above current estimates, or if future UN revisions move toward a later/higher rather than earlier/lower global peak. The chapter should treat projection revisions as evidence of uncertainty, not as proof that every future revision must move in the same direction.
