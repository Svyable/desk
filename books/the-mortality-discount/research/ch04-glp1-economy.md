# Chapter 4 research — GLP-1 drugs and the Ozempic economy

Traces semaglutide/tirzepatide's regulatory timeline, the Novo Nordisk/Eli Lilly valuation swing, the SELECT cardiovascular-outcomes trial, the bariatric-surgery volume decline, and the unresolved Medicare/employer coverage fight.

Primary-source anchors strengthened on 2026-09-02:

- FDA, March 8, 2024: Wegovy (semaglutide) received a new indication to reduce cardiovascular death, heart attack, and stroke in adults with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity. FDA reported major adverse cardiovascular events in 6.5% of Wegovy participants versus 8.0% on placebo in the supporting randomized trial. This is an outcome indication, not merely a weight-loss claim.
- FDA, December 20, 2024: Zepbound (tirzepatide) became the first medication approved to treat moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. The approval rested on two randomized trials totaling 469 adults without type 2 diabetes and broadens the institutional consequences of incretin drugs beyond weight management alone.
- Novo Nordisk Annual Report 2025: the company reported 31% obesity-portfolio sales growth at constant exchange rates in 2025, 3.6 million people reached with obesity medicines, and Wegovy availability in 52 countries by year-end. These are issuer-reported commercial figures and should be attributed as such rather than treated as independent market measurement.

Editorial implication: the chapter is strongest when it treats GLP-1 medicines as a case of category migration. A product first understood primarily through diabetes and weight loss acquired cardiovascular and sleep-apnea indications, forcing insurers, employers, surgical specialties, drug manufacturers, and public programs to reconsider which budget and benefit category bears the cost. That institutional repricing is better supported than a simple claim that one company or molecule permanently "won" the market.

Counterevidence / limits:

- SELECT enrolled people with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease; its result is not evidence that semaglutide produces the same cardiovascular benefit in every person using it for weight loss.
- Zepbound's sleep-apnea approval applies to adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA and was studied alongside reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity. It does not imply that pharmacotherapy replaces PAP or surgery for every patient.
- Company annual reports are primary for the company's own sales and reach but interested sources for competitive interpretation.
- Market-cap and market-share snapshots remain time-sensitive and should be dated. They are evidence of repricing, not proof of durable dominance.

Falsification note: the chapter's "institutions racing to catch up" argument weakens if coverage rules, manufacturing capacity, and benefit design converge quickly and durably around the expanded indications, and if the observed market revaluation proves to be a short-lived competitive swing rather than a persistent institutional adjustment. It also weakens if later randomized evidence fails to reproduce important non-weight endpoints outside the specific populations in which they were demonstrated.

## SELECT endpoint audit — 2026-09-14

A fresh primary-source check identified a material wording boundary in the current chapter. SELECT's prespecified primary endpoint was a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke. It occurred in 569 of 8,803 semaglutide participants (6.5%) and 701 of 8,801 placebo participants (8.0%), hazard ratio 0.80 (95% CI 0.72–0.90; P<0.001). The chapter can confidently say that semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events in this secondary-prevention population.

The cardiovascular-death component did not independently cross its prespecified significance threshold: 223 participants (2.5%) receiving semaglutide died from cardiovascular causes versus 262 (3.0%) receiving placebo, hazard ratio 0.85 (95% CI 0.71–1.01; P=0.07). Because cardiovascular death was the first confirmatory secondary endpoint in the hierarchical testing sequence, the original SELECT paper did not treat the later confirmatory endpoints as independently confirmed once that comparison missed significance. A later mortality analysis reported fewer all-cause deaths with semaglutide (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.93), but the cardiovascular-death estimate itself remained HR 0.85 with a confidence interval crossing 1.0. That later analysis is useful evidence, not permission to rewrite the original trial as having separately proved a cardiovascular-mortality benefit.

Primary / authoritative sources checked:

- Lincoff et al., *New England Journal of Medicine* (2023), “Semaglutide and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Obesity without Diabetes”: https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2307563
- U.S. Food and Drug Administration, March 8, 2024 approval announcement: https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-treatment-reduce-risk-serious-heart-problems-specifically-adults-obesity-or
- Deanfield et al., *Journal of the American College of Cardiology* (2024), SELECT mortality analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39217559/

### Manuscript boundary

The current chapter uses variants of “measurably reduces cardiovascular death” more than once. That wording is stronger than the trial supports. The safer formulation is **“measurably reduces major cardiovascular events”**, or, where the endpoint itself matters, **“reduced the composite risk of cardiovascular death, nonfatal heart attack, or nonfatal stroke.”** The population should remain explicit: adults with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity, without diabetes in SELECT.

There is a real counterargument to overcorrecting this passage. FDA's approved indication itself says Wegovy reduces the risk of cardiovascular death, heart attack, and stroke, and the agency relied on the statistically significant composite outcome. It would therefore also be misleading to imply that cardiovascular benefit is speculative or merely a weight-loss surrogate. The distinction is narrower: the regulatory indication and composite trial result are strong; the cardiovascular-death component alone was not independently statistically significant in SELECT.

This distinction improves the book's larger argument rather than weakening it. The chapter is about institutional repricing under genuine medical change. A drug does not need a separately significant mortality component to force insurers, manufacturers, employers, surgical specialties, and public programs to reconsider their assumptions. Keeping the endpoint exact prevents a legitimate cardiovascular result from being inflated into a cleaner “longevity drug” story than the evidence currently earns.

Falsification / update condition: if later adequately powered randomized evidence, a prespecified pooled analysis, or another robust confirmatory trial establishes a cardiovascular-death reduction in a comparable population, the manuscript can strengthen the mortality wording. Until then, distinguish the composite cardiovascular benefit from proof of an individual cardiovascular-mortality endpoint.
