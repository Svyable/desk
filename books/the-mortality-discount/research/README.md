# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*The Mortality Discount* asks what happens when a slow-moving actuarial assumption — how long a person is likely to live, and how healthy those extra years will be — starts moving on two different, partly contradictory timelines at once.

The strong version of the argument — that a "longevity breakthrough" is imminent and every institution built on mortality tables is about to be blindsided — is too clean to be true and does not survive contact with the evidence: most longevity biotech promises of the last fifteen years have failed, stalled, or not yet been tested in humans at all. The version worth testing is narrower:

> Institutions that price human lifespan — insurers, pension funds, national retirement systems, employers, and families — were built around the assumption that life expectancy moves slowly, predictably, and roughly in one direction. Two forces are now straining that assumption independently: a biotechnology industry deliberately targeting aging as a process rather than treating individual diseases, and a fertility collapse that is shrinking the next generation faster than most demographic models expected. Neither force has to fully deliver on its most dramatic promises to make the old assumption wrong; it only has to be wrong about the pace, the distribution, or the direction to force a repricing.

The book should be able to survive either outcome. If longevity science mostly fails to deliver, and fertility rates stabilize, the argument should still explain why institutions built on old assumptions were already strained. If a genuine biological breakthrough arrives, or the demographic trend reverses, the book should be able to say so without having overclaimed in either direction.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Historical pace of life-expectancy gains:** How much of the twentieth century's life-expectancy gain came from public health and infrastructure (sanitation, vaccination, antibiotics, income) rather than from treating aging itself, and is that pace continuing, slowing, or reversing in specific populations?
2. **Regulatory and scientific reclassification:** Is aging actually being treated as a directly targetable condition by regulators and major trial infrastructure (the TAME trial, ICD-11's aging-related extension code), or is this reclassification narrower and slower than popular coverage suggests?
3. **The GLP-1 case study:** How much of the "longevity economy" story is really being carried by a class of drugs developed for diabetes and obesity, and what does their real, dated economic impact on pharmaceutical companies and adjacent industries look like?
4. **The failure record:** What is the real track record of venture-funded longevity biotech (senolytics, reprogramming, biological age tests), including the failures, not just the raises and the press releases?
5. **Demographic reversal:** How severe, and how well-documented, is the fertility collapse in South Korea, Japan, and China, and what has actually happened when governments spent large sums trying to reverse it?
6. **Actuarial and pension exposure:** How have insurers and pension funds priced longevity risk historically, where has that pricing failed (the 2004 longevity bond, mortality-table revisions), and how large is the current market for transferring that risk?
7. **Retirement-age political economy:** How arbitrary were the founding retirement ages of major pension systems relative to the life expectancy of their time, and how are governments adjusting (or failing to adjust) those ages now?
8. **Distributional effects:** How much does life expectancy already vary by income within rich countries, and what does that imply about who actually captures the gains from any future longevity science?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter should look for evidence against the central argument.

- Life expectancy gains in several rich countries, including the United States, have stalled or reversed in the past decade for reasons (opioid deaths, COVID-19, metabolic disease) that have nothing to do with a slow-moving repricing story.
- The most visible longevity biotech promises — senolytics, reprogramming therapies, biological age tests sold direct to consumers — have a poor clinical track record so far, and some of the most heavily funded companies and drug candidates have failed in trials.
- Blue Zones and other extreme-longevity claims that motivate public enthusiasm for anti-aging science have been directly challenged by research suggesting many "supercentenarian" clusters reflect bad record-keeping and pension fraud rather than real biology.
- Mainstream biodemographers have argued that life expectancy gains are subject to biological limits and are already "faltering" in some high-income countries, which cuts against a narrative of accelerating gains.
- Pronatalist government spending in South Korea and elsewhere has been large and has not reversed fertility decline, which is evidence that the demographic trend may be less responsive to policy than institutions assume.
- Pension and insurance systems have absorbed large historical mortality-table revisions before without institutional collapse, which is evidence for adaptive capacity rather than fragility.
- UN population projections have themselves been revised substantially over time, which is a reason for humility about any single demographic forecast used in this book.

The manuscript should distinguish these possibilities rather than forcing every fact into a single "everything is about to change" narrative.

## Evidence lanes

### I. The history of pricing a life

Actuarial science from John Graunt's 1662 Bills of Mortality and Edmond Halley's 1693 life table through the founding of modern pension and insurance systems. Distinguish reconstructed historical estimates from directly measured modern statistics.

### II. Twentieth-century life expectancy gains

Track how much of the twentieth century's roughly thirty-year gain in life expectancy came from public health versus direct intervention in aging, and where that gain has stalled or reversed in specific populations.

### III. The regulatory and scientific reclassification of aging

The hallmarks-of-aging framework, the TAME trial and its funding history, and the WHO ICD-11 aging-related extension code — treated as a real but limited and still-contested reclassification, not a settled scientific consensus.

### IV. The longevity biotech record, failures included

GLP-1 drugs and their real, dated economic effects; the venture-funded reprogramming and senolytics field (Altos Labs, Calico, Retro Biosciences, Unity Biotechnology's UBX0101 failure); biological age clocks and what they can and cannot show; the Blue Zones controversy.

### V. Demographic collapse

South Korea, Japan, and China's fertility and aging trajectories; UN World Population Prospects; the real, documented failure of large pronatalist spending programs to reverse fertility decline; migration as a partial, politically contested offset.

### VI. Institutions built on the old numbers

Pension funds, life insurers, the pension risk transfer and life settlement markets, Social Security's trust fund projections, the history and political economy of retirement ages, and the distributional question of who actually captures any longevity gains.

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary sources: peer-reviewed papers, government and regulatory documents (WHO, FDA, SSA, national statistics offices, UN DESA), company filings and press releases, and contemporaneous, well-established financial and scientific journalism.
- Record the vintage of every dataset. Life expectancy, fertility, market-cap, and pension figures are snapshots; note the date and do not treat them as permanent facts.
- Separate announced/funded research programs from clinical results, and clinical results in animals from results in humans.
- Preserve counter-evidence and known controversies (Blue Zones, Olshansky's skepticism, biotech failures) rather than resolving them in the thesis's favor.
- Never quote a scientist, executive, or public figure without verifying the original source.
- For every major claim, keep a note on what would make it false.
- Every source used in the manuscript has a corresponding fragment in `research/sources/` with a real, checkable URL.
