# Chapter 10 research — pronatalist policy and migration

Covers South Korea's cumulative pronatalist spending (kept as an explicit $210–270B range rather than a single figure because published tallies differ), Hungary's pronatalist policy suite and its rise/fall in total fertility, Germany and Japan as contrasting migration cases, OECD/IMF dependency-ratio data, and Japan's Silver Human Resource Centers.

Primary/current evidence checked 2026-09-02 adds two important corrections to the political and migration framing. Germany's Federal Returning Officer records the AfD at 20.8% of second votes in the final February 23, 2025 Bundestag result; use that official result rather than a polling source. In Japan, end-2025 Immigration Services Agency figures show 4,125,395 foreign residents, up 9.5% year over year and above four million for the first time, while the Specified Skilled Worker category rose sharply. The cleanest interpretation is therefore not that Japan has chosen "no immigration" but that it is expanding labor migration through bounded status categories while remaining politically reluctant to describe itself as an immigration country.

The 2025 Japanese upper-house result is useful counterevidence to a simple technocratic story: Sanseitō won 14 contested seats on a "Japanese First" campaign as the ruling coalition lost its majority. That shows the political constraint on migration is real, but it does not by itself establish that anti-immigration sentiment is the dominant cause of the election result or that foreign-resident growth has stopped. Keep those two facts in tension rather than converting them into a single causal claim.

Counterevidence: pronatalist policy can affect timing, marriage, labor-force participation, and the marginal cost of children even when it does not restore replacement fertility. Migration can materially soften labor shortages without fully offsetting population aging, and Japan's rapidly rising foreign-resident population is evidence that policy adaptation is already occurring. Germany's and Japan's election results should be used as constraints on policy space, not as proof that electorates categorically reject migration.

Falsification note: weakens if sustained fertility gains in Hungary or South Korea can be causally tied to specific policy packages at plausible cost; if Japan's foreign-resident and foreign-worker populations continue rising fast enough to offset a large share of labor-force decline without producing the institutional strain described in the chapter; or if later elections show migration becoming materially less salient. The chapter should avoid treating a single spending total, election, or headline fertility rate as dispositive.
