# Chapter 2 research — the life-expectancy curve

## Purpose

Chapter 2, “The Line That Wouldn't Bend,” should explain how a century of mortality improvement trained institutions to expect a broadly favorable slope while also showing why life expectancy is not a simple measure of biological aging.

The chapter should distinguish life expectancy at birth from life expectancy at older ages. Improvements in infant and child survival can move life expectancy at birth substantially without implying an equivalent extension of old age. Conversely, a change in mortality among older adults can materially affect pension and annuity liabilities even when the headline life-expectancy-at-birth number moves less dramatically.

## Current U.S. anchor

CDC/NCHS final 2024 mortality data, released January 29, 2026, report U.S. life expectancy at birth of 79.0 years, up 0.6 year from 78.4 in 2023. Life expectancy at age 65 was 19.7 additional years in 2024, up 0.2 year from 2023. The same release states that the 2024 increase at birth was driven largely by lower mortality from unintentional injuries, COVID-19, heart disease, cancer, and homicide.

This is a useful current anchor because it demonstrates how a headline life-expectancy change can reflect several cause-specific mortality movements at once rather than a single underlying “aging” mechanism.

The chapter should not describe 79.0 as a permanent or universal U.S. lifespan. It is a period life-expectancy estimate from 2024 mortality rates. It does not predict that a baby born in 2024 will literally live 79.0 years, and it differs across sex, race and ethnicity, geography, socioeconomic status, and other dimensions.

## Historical interpretation discipline

The book's premise is not that twentieth-century gains came mostly from anti-aging interventions. The opposite is the important baseline. Improvements in sanitation, vaccination, infectious-disease control, maternal and infant survival, cardiovascular treatment, smoking reduction in some populations, workplace safety, and other public-health and medical changes altered mortality at different ages.

A future chapter can quantify the historical decomposition more deeply, but Chapter 2 should avoid the synthetic shorthand that “medicine added thirty years.” The causal history is distributed across public health, infrastructure, behavior, clinical medicine, income, education, and technology.

## Counterevidence and discontinuity

Recent U.S. history is particularly useful because the line did bend. Life expectancy fell during the COVID-19 pandemic and had also been pressured before the pandemic by drug overdoses and other causes. The 2024 recovery to 79.0 does not erase that volatility; it shows that mortality can deteriorate and rebound over relatively short periods.

This cuts against any chapter that treats longevity as a smooth secular ascent. It also cuts against the opposite error of treating one bad period as a permanent reversal.

The overdose story itself now supplies an additional warning against freezing a fast-moving trend in prose. Final NCHS data show 79,384 U.S. resident drug-overdose deaths in 2024, with the age-adjusted overdose death rate falling 26.2% from 2023 to 2024. Provisional NCHS data released May 13, 2026 estimate 69,973 overdose deaths during 2025, almost 14% below the provisional 2024 estimate; CDC's July 2026 overdose page reports a further 12.1% year-over-year decline in the preliminary count for the 12 months ending February 2026. These provisional figures can still be revised and should be labeled as such.

This materially qualifies any sentence saying overdose mortality simply “remained stubbornly elevated through the recovery.” It was historically elevated in 2023, but a large subsequent decline is now part of the record. The better argument is not that overdose mortality stayed high; it is that cause-specific mortality can reverse sharply in either direction, making the aggregate life-expectancy line a poor proxy for a single biological process.

## Actuarial relevance

For long-duration liabilities, age-specific mortality matters more than a single headline average. Pension and annuity exposure is especially sensitive to mortality at older ages, while life-insurance books can have different exposure depending on issue age, underwriting class, duration, and product structure.

Chapter 2 should therefore use the public headline number as an entry point, then show the reader why actuaries work with full age-specific tables rather than a single life-expectancy statistic.

## Falsification note

The chapter's implied argument would be weakened if recent mortality volatility proves temporary and long-term age-specific improvement returns to a stable, easily forecastable range across the populations that dominate pension and insurance liabilities. It would also be weakened if future biomedical gains remain too small, too late, or too narrowly distributed to change population mortality assumptions materially.

The chapter should preserve a second counterpoint: the fact that the twentieth century's mortality regime changed enormously without destroying actuarial institutions is evidence for adaptive capacity, not only for fragility.
