# Chapter 11 research — off-label rapamycin and epigenetic clocks

Chapter 11 needs to hold two different evidence problems apart. Rapamycin has unusually strong lifespan evidence in model organisms and a clear molecular target in mTOR, but the human longevity claim remains unproven. Epigenetic clocks can predict chronological age or mortality risk, but prediction is not the same thing as demonstrating that moving a clock score changes aging itself.

## Human rapamycin evidence, updated through September 2, 2026

The strongest recent evidence makes the chapter more interesting because it is mixed rather than uniformly favorable.

The PEARL randomized controlled trial, published in *Aging* in April 2025, tested low-dose intermittent rapamycin for one year in adults interested in healthy aging. It is useful as direct human evidence because it moved beyond short safety studies, but it did not establish that rapamycin extends human lifespan or broadly slows aging. The study reported acceptable overall safety and selected healthspan signals, while its authors and subsequent reviews still characterize the evidence base as insufficient to establish rapamycin as a proven anti-aging therapy in healthy adults. The trial was also closely connected to AgelessRx, a company that offers longevity services, so conflicts and study design belong in any interpretation rather than being treated as footnotes.

A 2025 review of clinical evidence for off-label rapamycin in healthy adults reached the narrower conclusion the chapter should preserve: animal data are compelling, but human studies have not yet established rapamycin or its analogues as proven therapies that delay aging in healthy older adults. This is a useful counterweight to self-experimentation culture because it does not argue that rapamycin is implausible; it argues that the evidentiary threshold has not been crossed.

The 2026 RAPA-EX-01 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial provides harder counterevidence. Forty sedentary adults aged 65–85 received either 6 mg of sirolimus once weekly or placebo for 13 weeks while following the same exercise program. Rapamycin did not improve the primary functional outcome. Sensitivity analyses suggested it may have modestly attenuated short-term exercise gains. Seventeen participants in each arm reported at least one adverse event, but the rapamycin arm had more total events (99 versus 63), including one pneumonia judged possibly drug-related. This is a small exploratory trial and cannot settle the longevity question, but it directly undercuts the easy assumption that a regimen with strong animal rationale should produce visible near-term functional benefits in healthy older humans.

A separate 2025 open-label phase 1 trial in ten people with mild cognitive impairment or early dementia found rapamycin was not detectable in cerebrospinal fluid after eight weeks at 1 mg/day and reported increases in several Alzheimer’s and inflammatory biomarkers. That population is not a proxy for healthy aging, and the study was too small and uncontrolled for efficacy conclusions. Its value here is narrower: biologic plausibility in animals does not guarantee the expected target engagement or downstream biomarker pattern in humans.

FDA labeling also provides an important boundary. Sirolimus is an approved drug for transplant-related indications, not for slowing normal human aging. Off-label prescribing is legally distinct from regulatory evidence that an anti-aging indication is effective. The chapter should not let the drug’s FDA-approved status imply that the longevity use itself has been validated.

## Epigenetic clocks

Horvath’s 2013 cross-tissue clock used 353 CpG sites to predict chronological age across human tissues. GrimAge, introduced in 2019, was trained differently: it combines methylation-based surrogates for plasma proteins and smoking exposure to predict mortality and healthspan-related outcomes. That makes GrimAge more prognostic, but it also makes interpretation harder. A biomarker can forecast risk without being a causal lever on the process it predicts.

The methodological critique remains central. Mendelian-randomization and related work has not shown that the CpG sites that make clocks predictive are generally causal drivers of aging. First-generation clocks can therefore be useful measurement devices while remaining poor intervention targets. A treatment that lowers a commercial “biological age” score has not, by that fact alone, been shown to extend life or prevent disease.

Direct-to-consumer biological-age tests should be described as a commercial translation of an unsettled measurement science. Company claims require source-by-source verification; the chapter should avoid treating a single clock score as a clinical endpoint or equating movement in that score with rejuvenation.

## Editorial implications

The strongest version of the chapter is not “rapamycin does not work.” It is that a plausible mechanism, extraordinary mouse evidence, a legal off-label market, and an expanding measurement industry can coexist for years before the human outcome evidence resolves. The existence of mixed randomized human data makes that tension more concrete.

The chapter should distinguish three levels of claim whenever possible: target engagement, movement in a biomarker or intermediate phenotype, and an outcome people actually care about such as disability, disease incidence, or survival. Conflating those levels is the mechanism by which an experimental geroscience result becomes a consumer anti-aging promise.

## Falsification note

The chapter’s skepticism would weaken materially if a sufficiently large, well-controlled human trial showed that a tolerable rapamycin regimen reduced major age-related morbidity, disability, or mortality in generally healthy older adults, with replication and a benefit-risk profile suitable for long-term use. It would also weaken if validated interventions that reproducibly move a specific epigenetic clock were shown to cause corresponding improvements in hard clinical outcomes rather than merely correlate with them.

The contrary evidence should also be allowed to accumulate. Repeated randomized trials showing no clinically meaningful benefit, unacceptable infection or metabolic risk, or poor correspondence between clock movement and health outcomes would strengthen the chapter’s case that the market is pricing mechanistic promise faster than medicine can validate it.
