# Chapter 1 research — actuarial foundations and mortality-table adaptation

## Claims supported

Chapter 1 needs to establish two things at once: mortality tables are foundational infrastructure for long-duration financial promises, and actuarial practice is already built around revision rather than the assumption that mortality never changes.

The historical spine is conservative. The Society of Actuaries' own history traces the profession's European foundations through probability theory, Edmond Halley's 1693 mortality table, James Dodson's level-premium work, the Society for Equitable Assurances founded in 1762, and Richard Price's 1771 work on life contingencies. The Institute and Faculty of Actuaries likewise describes the Equitable as the first insurance office to operate on scientific lines using actuarial mathematics and mortality analysis, and credits Price with shaping the actuarial role around reviewing mortality experience and valuing whether future claims could be met.

The chapter therefore treats the life table not as a one-time discovery but as an operating technology: observed mortality is converted into age-specific probabilities, then used to price or reserve cash flows contingent on death or survival.

## Modern adaptation evidence

The counterevidence to any claim that actuaries assume a fixed mortality regime is strong and should remain explicit.

The Society of Actuaries publishes recurring mortality-experience studies comparing actual deaths with expected deaths under standard tables. Its MORT database provides electronic access to thousands of published mortality and other rate tables. It also publishes mortality-improvement scales used alongside valuation tables. These practices show that modern actuarial work expects mortality assumptions to drift and incorporates experience review and prospective improvement.

The book's stronger claim must therefore be narrower: institutional stress arises when the mechanism producing future mortality changes in ways that are difficult to extrapolate, when revisions are politically or contractually slow, or when different institutions have asymmetric exposure to longer survival.

## Financial direction of longevity risk

The chapter distinguishes life-insurance exposure from annuity/pension exposure. In broad terms, lower mortality can delay life-insurance claims while increasing the duration of annuity and pension payments. This is directional rather than universal; product guarantees, investment returns, lapses, underwriting mix, reserve standards, and capital requirements also matter. The prose avoids converting the direction into a universal profit claim.

## Falsification note

Evidence against the book's thesis would include:

- mortality improvements continuing within ranges already handled comfortably by existing table-update and capital processes;
- longevity biotechnology failing to produce meaningful human mortality effects at population scale;
- fertility stabilizing enough that old-age dependency projections improve materially;
- insurers, pension systems, and governments repricing quickly enough that no persistent mismatch accumulates.

Chapter 1 explicitly preserves the strongest current countercase: actuarial institutions have already absorbed enormous twentieth-century changes in mortality and possess mature mechanisms for revising assumptions. The existence of longevity risk is not new. The empirical question is whether coming changes are larger, less forecastable, more unevenly distributed, or slower to transmit through institutions than the profession's existing correction machinery can comfortably absorb.
