# Chapter 16 — Mosquitoes Released to Stop Disease

The twentieth-century Army released mosquitoes because it wanted to know whether insects could help deliver disease.

The twenty-first century releases mosquitoes because public-health programs want to stop it.

That reversal is one of the most beautiful facts in the story.

*Wolbachia* is a naturally occurring intracellular bacterium found in many insects. Scientists introduced particular *Wolbachia* strains into *Aedes aegypti* populations because the symbiont can reduce the mosquito's ability to transmit dengue and other viruses. In the randomized controlled trial in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, deployments were associated with substantial reductions in virologically confirmed dengue and hospitalizations.

WHO has treated the intervention as promising while maintaining a formal evidence and guideline process. In June 2026, WHO announced an updated review of *Wolbachia* population replacement, noting that evidence had expanded and that final recommendations were anticipated after guideline-development work.

The historical parallel must be handled carefully.

Both military entomological research and *Wolbachia* programs involve rearing and releasing mosquitoes.

That does not make them morally or functionally equivalent.

Purpose matters.

Pathogen effect matters.

Consent and governance matter.

Monitoring matters.

The point of comparing them is almost the opposite of conspiracy. It shows how little can be inferred from the mere fact of release.

A photograph of technicians holding mosquito containers does not tell you whether you are looking at warfare, ecological research or disease prevention.

Context is evidence.
