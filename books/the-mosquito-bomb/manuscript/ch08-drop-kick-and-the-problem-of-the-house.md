# Chapter 8 — Drop Kick and the Problem of the House

Operation Drop Kick and related Savannah trials in 1956 pushed the question inward.

Not merely: can mosquitoes survive release?

Can they get into where people live?

Later Chemical Corps summaries describe releases of uninfected female mosquitoes in a residential area and efforts to estimate house entry and biting. Operation May Day is associated with tests of urban dispersal and recovery using traps.

A house is a bad military target for a mosquito because it is also a complicated ecosystem.

Openings vary. Screens vary. Occupants move. Airflow changes. Human odors compete. People swat.

The point sounds comic until one remembers why the Army cared.

A vector weapon does not end at area coverage. It has to produce contact.

In the 1950s, that contact problem could be investigated empirically with traps, recapture, bite reports and spatial sampling. But translating those measurements into disease outcomes would require additional assumptions about infection, vector competence, pathogen development, dose, immunity, medical response and onward transmission.

The number of assumptions multiplies faster than the confidence.

This is a useful general rule for classified technology claims. A successful subsystem test is often retold as successful system capability. Sometimes that inference is warranted. Sometimes the missing interfaces are the whole difficulty.

In entomological warfare, the interfaces are alive.
