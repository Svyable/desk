# Chapter 2 — Plague Fleas Over China

Any history of insect warfare that begins with American mosquito tests risks becoming morally upside down.

The first obligation is to look at the case in which biological warfare was not a feasibility study.

Imperial Japan's biological-warfare program, associated most notoriously with Unit 731, conducted lethal human experimentation and field operations in China. Historical and medical reviews describe attacks involving pathogens, contaminated materials and plague-bearing fleas. The precise casualty totals remain debated because wartime records are incomplete, later testimony varies, and epidemic disease does not leave a neat signature of intent. The existence of the program and its atrocities are not similarly in doubt.

A 2014 review in *Clinical Microbiology and Infection* summarizes evidence that Japanese forces used plague-infected fleas, contaminated food and water, and other biological methods in China. This is evidence class one for the book: documented biological warfare, not an allegation generated decades later.

The flea mattered because plague has an ecology. The bacterium *Yersinia pestis* circulates among rodents and fleas. That ecological fact existed before anyone weaponized it. Weaponization meant trying to force a natural transmission relationship into a military schedule.

Here is where the history gets uglier than the phrase biological weapon usually allows. A shell explodes and stops being a shell. A biological release may continue as an event in living systems. Infection can move through hosts not selected by the attacker. An outbreak may be amplified by local conditions. The weapon can blur into an epidemic.

That lack of sharp boundaries is sometimes described as an advantage. It is also a strategic defect and a forensic nightmare.

A battlefield commander usually wants effects that can be timed, mapped and integrated with movement. Vector-borne disease offers delay and uncertainty. Incubation time separates release from illness. Ecology separates the place of release from the place of consequence. Medical reporting may lag further. If the attacking force later enters the area, the same biological process can threaten it.

Accounts of Japanese biological attacks include episodes in which Japanese troops themselves suffered disease. Even where specific numbers are uncertain, the underlying systems problem is obvious: a living transmission chain is indifferent to uniforms.

This is the first recurring law of the mosquito bomb, although here the carrier was often a flea.

**The more autonomy you borrow from ecology, the more control you surrender to it.**

The Japanese program also created a second legacy: information. At the end of the war, U.S. occupation authorities became interested in the data produced by Japanese biological-warfare research. American officials made decisions that protected important figures from prosecution in exchange for information. Those decisions have been documented by historians and U.S. government investigations.

The moral failure is not peripheral to the technical history. It shaped the archive itself.

If perpetrators are protected because their data are deemed valuable, then the resulting record is contaminated in two senses. Ethically, the knowledge has been extracted through crimes. Epistemically, officials now have incentives to overstate its uniqueness and usefulness because the bargain needs justification.

This matters later when claims arise that Japanese expertise flowed directly into American wartime biological operations in Korea. There was postwar American access to Japanese personnel and data. That is documented. It does not follow that every later allegation involving insects and disease is therefore true.

Secrecy creates bridges that conspiracy can later drive across.

The bridge may be real.

The destination still has to be proved.
