# Chapter 11 — Ecology Refuses Orders

There is a reason modern vector-control programs obsess over local ecology.

Mosquitoes are not interchangeable syringes with wings.

Species differ in feeding behavior, habitat, resting preference, flight, seasonality and competence for particular pathogens. Even within a species, populations can differ. Temperature affects development. Rainfall can expand or destroy breeding habitat. Housing changes exposure. Insecticide resistance changes control.

None of this is secret.

It is ordinary public-health entomology.

That is precisely why it is devastating to the fantasy of perfect entomological warfare.

The attacker's system model has to include variables the defender and the weather can alter faster than doctrine can.

There is also a time problem. Some vector-borne pathogens require development inside the insect before the vector can transmit efficiently. If that period is long relative to vector survival under field conditions, the apparent delivery advantage collapses. If the pathogen kills or disables its carrier too quickly, the system can sabotage itself. If transmission requires specific feeding patterns, generic area coverage is a poor proxy for effect.

This is why the strongest claim available from the U.S. field tests remains bounded: they demonstrated aspects of vector handling, dissemination and behavior.

They did not abolish epidemiology.

Ecology is not noise around the weapon.

Ecology is the weapon's operating system.
