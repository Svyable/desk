# Chapter 3 — The Data Nobody Should Have Wanted

After a war, captured knowledge acquires a strange innocence in bureaucratic language.

It becomes material.

Intelligence.

Data.

The words make no distinction between a radar manual taken from an airfield and observations produced by infecting prisoners.

American authorities after World War II sought information from Japanese biological-warfare personnel, including Shiro Ishii and colleagues associated with Unit 731. The bargain has been examined in U.S. government records and historical scholarship: information was obtained while major perpetrators escaped the prosecutions their conduct warranted.

There are two temptations when telling this story.

The first is to sanitize it as intelligence collection. That fails morally.

The second is to make the immunity bargain prove every later claim about American biological warfare. That fails logically.

The useful question is narrower: what happens to a national research program when it absorbs knowledge from a defeated adversary whose work was both criminal and technically relevant?

One answer is that the line between foreign-materiel exploitation and scientific inheritance becomes difficult to see. The United States did not need Japan to invent germ theory, mosquito biology or aerosol science. American biological-warfare work had already begun during the war. But Japanese experience could still be treated as a data set about things U.S. planners had not done themselves and, one hopes, would not reproduce on human subjects.

This produces the darkest form of technological transfer: learning from an experiment that should never have existed.

It also creates mythology. Once people learn that officials secretly protected war criminals to obtain biological-warfare information, later denials become harder to believe even when the denial is true. Institutional trust is not a philosophical abstraction. It is a technical variable in future attribution.

This is particularly important in the Korean War chapter. The Chinese and North Korean allegations of American biological warfare drew persuasive force from facts that were already compromising: the United States had protected Japanese biological-warfare figures; it had an active biological-warfare research program; it was interested in vector delivery; and much of the work was secret.

Those facts create prior plausibility.

They do not create event proof.

An intelligence historian should be comfortable holding both thoughts at once. A state can have lied before and still tell the truth today. A state can possess a capability and not use it. A state can deny an allegation for self-serving reasons and nevertheless be correct.

The immunity bargain damaged more than justice. It damaged the future information environment.

That is one of the book's central themes: secrecy does not merely hide evidence. It changes the evidentiary ecology in which later claims are judged.

When an institution spends credibility, the debt does not vanish when the file is declassified.
