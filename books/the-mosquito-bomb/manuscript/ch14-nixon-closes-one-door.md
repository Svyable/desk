# Chapter 14 — Nixon Closes One Door

On November 25, 1969, President Richard Nixon announced that the United States would renounce the use of lethal biological agents and weapons and confine biological research to defensive measures such as immunization and safety.

The decision did not erase the preceding program.

It changed its legal and strategic direction.

The 1972 Biological Weapons Convention then prohibited the development, production, acquisition, transfer and stockpiling of biological and toxin weapons, entering into force in 1975. The treaty is short, powerful and famously difficult to verify compared with regimes built around large, distinctive hardware.

Entomological warfare exposes why.

Much of the underlying science is dual-use. Mosquito rearing can serve vaccine research, vector ecology, sterile-insect programs or disease control. Pathogen-vector interactions are legitimate public-health questions. Dispersal ecology can matter for controlling invasive species or evaluating environmental risk.

Intent and system context matter.

That makes governance harder than banning a uniquely military machine.

The BWC does not become meaningless because biology is dual-use. It becomes more dependent on transparency, norms, institutional practice and the ability to distinguish peaceful purpose from prohibited weaponization.

Modern BWC working groups continue to wrestle with scientific and technological change. In 2026, UN processes are still discussing ways to strengthen the Convention.

The mosquito bomb did not disappear because insects stopped being capable of carrying disease.

It disappeared from legitimate military arsenals because states built a norm that certain uses of biology are beyond the line.
