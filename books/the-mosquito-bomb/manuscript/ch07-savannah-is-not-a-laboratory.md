# Chapter 7 — Savannah Is Not a Laboratory

A proving ground can be closed.

A city cannot.

The entomological-warfare tests around Savannah, Georgia, are often retold in a compressed and inflammatory form: the Army released mosquitoes on Black neighborhoods. Some accounts are careful; others mix operations, dates and locations or imply that disease-bearing insects were used when the documented U.S. tests employed uninfected mosquitoes.

The correction should not become an acquittal.

Uninfected does not mean ethically trivial.

The Army wanted to know how mosquitoes behaved around real human habitation. That meant houses, residents, windows, screens, yards and bodies became part of the measurement environment. Later Chemical Corps summaries described residential-area tests and estimates of how many mosquitoes entered houses and bit people.

The historical record on exactly what residents were told, who consented, and how participation was structured deserves more archival reconstruction than most popular summaries provide. This manuscript therefore refuses to state a stronger consent claim than the available source chain supports.

What can be said is simpler.

A military experiment that depends on uninvolved civilians being present has crossed a line a range test has not.

That line becomes especially charged in Savannah because American medical and military research existed inside a larger history of racial inequality, segregated institutions and abuses of Black communities. It is possible to acknowledge that context without converting every uncertain detail into a proved atrocity.

Precision is not exoneration.

Precision is how the indictment survives contact with evidence.

The technical reason the Army wanted inhabited terrain is revealing. Buildings change vector behavior. Screens matter. Human density matters. Indoor resting and biting patterns matter. A mosquito system designed on a clean map encounters architecture immediately.

The city fights back without knowing it is fighting.

Walls, fans, clothing, weather, insecticides, habits and chance all enter the system.

This is ecology at human scale.
