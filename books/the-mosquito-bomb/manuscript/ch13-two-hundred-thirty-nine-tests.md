# Chapter 13 — Two Hundred Thirty-Nine Tests

In March 1977 Americans learned a number that changed the emotional scale of the story: 239.

The Army disclosed that it had conducted 239 open-air biological-warfare-related tests between 1949 and 1969, including releases of simulant organisms in public places. Contemporary reporting in *The Washington Post* described tests in cities, transportation hubs and military sites. Later government reviews, including GAO work on Project 112 and SHAD, documented how broad the Cold War testing culture had been.

The mosquito experiments sit inside that larger culture.

This is important because isolated code names encourage folklore. A single operation sounds like a rogue event. A program of hundreds of open-air tests reveals an institution trying systematically to answer vulnerability and dissemination questions.

It also reveals how badly risk assumptions can age.

Organisms once treated as harmless simulants were later understood to carry more risk for some populations than planners had assumed. The historical lesson is not that every release caused disease. It is that safety judgments are provisional, and secrecy delays external correction.

A classified test program has an epistemic asymmetry.

The institution gets the data.

The exposed public gets the uncertainty.

When the program is later revealed, every unexplained illness can be pulled into the secrecy field, whether connected or not. Again, trust becomes a technical variable.

Secrecy protects operational information in the short term.

It can destroy attribution capacity in the long term.
