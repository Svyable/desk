# Chapter 15 — How to Prove an Insect Attack

Suppose an unusual cluster of insects appears near an outbreak.

What would count as evidence of attack?

Not the insects alone.

The forensic problem requires layers: species identification, population genetics where useful, pathogen typing, spatial and temporal epidemiology, environmental plausibility, chain of custody, witness records, intelligence context, and exclusion of ordinary ecological explanations.

No single layer automatically wins.

A genetically unusual mosquito could result from a legitimate control program. A familiar mosquito carrying an unusual pathogen might reflect travel or natural introduction. An outbreak near a military facility can be coincidence. A classified program can establish capability but not attribution.

The strongest case would be convergent and adversarially testable.

The weakest case would begin with a conclusion and recruit every insect afterward.

This is where the Korean War history becomes practical. Evidence supplied by a belligerent and never independently controlled is vulnerable. So is evidence collected after intense publicity, when observers know what they are expected to find.

Good attribution therefore needs negative controls and boring alternatives.

Where else was the species present?

Was the pathogen already circulating?

Did weather explain the dispersal?

Do genetic signatures support a source population?

Were samples blinded across laboratories?

Can an independent team reconstruct custody?

The goal is not skepticism for sport.

The goal is a claim strong enough to survive the fact that biological events are naturally messy and politically useful.
