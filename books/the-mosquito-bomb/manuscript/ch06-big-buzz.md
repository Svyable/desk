# Chapter 6 — Big Buzz

The mosquito version came next.

Operation Big Buzz in 1955 tested *Aedes aegypti*, the mosquito associated with yellow fever and now notorious as a vector for dengue, Zika and chikungunya. The mosquitoes released in the U.S. test were uninfected. The objectives, as summarized in later Army material, included demonstrating that large numbers could be produced, handled, dispersed and still seek blood meals.

The phrase blood meal is scientific and obscene in this context.

In vector biology it is ordinary language. In a weapons program it becomes a performance metric.

Public accounts of the test describe aerial and ground releases in Georgia and recovery of mosquitoes after dispersal. The important fact is not the exact number in any single secondary retelling. It is the architecture of the test: the Army was trying to turn an animal's ordinary behavior into terminal delivery.

A missile receives guidance commands.

The mosquito receives carbon dioxide, heat, odor and opportunity.

That difference is the source of both the idea's attraction and its weakness.

If the insect can locate a host by itself, the attacker does not need to place every infectious unit on a person. But the attacker also cannot decide which person is bitten, how many times, at what interval, or whether the vector survives long enough for a pathogen's required internal development.

Big Buzz therefore proved less than its name implies and more than a skeptic might want to admit.

It did not prove an epidemic weapon.

It did prove that military engineers could integrate mass-reared mosquitoes into a dispersal experiment and recover living, host-seeking insects afterward.

That is enough to deserve serious historical attention.

It is not enough to deserve mythology.
