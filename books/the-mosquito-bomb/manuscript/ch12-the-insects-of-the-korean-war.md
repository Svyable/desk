# Chapter 12 — The Insects of the Korean War

In 1952, insects became evidence.

China and North Korea accused the United States of conducting biological warfare during the Korean War, including the aerial release of infected insects and other materials. The allegations became an international propaganda crisis. Joseph Needham chaired an International Scientific Commission that produced an enormous report supporting the charges.

The case is still worth studying because it contains almost every epistemic hazard in this book.

The United States had a biological-warfare program.

It had protected Japanese biological-warfare personnel after World War II.

It was interested in vector delivery.

Its program was secret.

Those facts make the allegation imaginable.

They do not prove the alleged events.

Later Soviet and Chinese-source evidence changed the balance substantially. Materials published and discussed by the Wilson Center's Cold War International History Project include post-Stalin Soviet documents describing fabrication and a memoir by Wu Zhili, who had led the Chinese People's Volunteer Army Health Division, calling the biological-warfare episode a false alarm and describing how evidence was manufactured. Milton Leitenberg has argued from these sources that the allegations were false.

There remain historians who have defended versions of the accusation, and the original Needham Commission archive is real. The appropriate response is not to pretend there was never a dispute. It is to compare provenance.

An insect presented by a belligerent government is not self-authenticating.

A commission allowed to inspect only evidence supplied by one side has a chain-of-custody problem.

A captured airman's confession has coercion problems.

A secret American capability program creates motive for suspicion but not proof of use.

Later adversary documents admitting fabrication are not magical either; they need provenance and context. In this case, the accumulating archival record has made the fabrication interpretation substantially stronger.

The Korean War episode teaches a principle that matters today for UAPs, cyberattacks, directed-energy allegations and covert biological events alike:

**Capability evidence and event evidence are different evidence.**

Confusing them is how a real secret program can become proof of an event that did not happen.
