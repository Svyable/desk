# Chapter 10 — The Fantasy of a Self-Delivering Weapon

The fantasy behind entomological warfare is elegant.

Do not build the last mile.

Recruit it.

A mosquito already knows how to find exposed human skin. A flea already knows how to find a mammalian host. If an organism can do the terminal work, the weapon seems to become cheaper, quieter and harder to attribute.

This is the same fantasy that appears whenever engineers outsource complexity to a self-organizing system. Let the swarm route itself. Let the market allocate. Let the ecosystem propagate. Let the model infer.

Sometimes the outsourcing is extraordinary.

Sometimes the hidden control problem simply moves somewhere less visible.

For a living vector, the hidden control problem includes life history. A pathogen and a vector must be biologically compatible. The vector's survival must overlap the time needed for transmission. Environmental conditions affect both. Human hosts may have immunity or protection. Public-health response changes the landscape after the first cases appear.

A self-delivering weapon is therefore not self-delivering.

It is environment-delivered.

That distinction should make planners nervous.

It should also make historians cautious when reading cost-effectiveness calculations in old military studies. A spreadsheet can assign values to deaths, coverage and cost. The arithmetic may be correct inside the assumptions. The assumptions may be doing almost all the work.

One of the failures of Cold War strategic thinking was not that analysts used numbers.

It was that numbers made uncertain biology look obedient.
