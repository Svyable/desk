# The Mosquito Bomb

## When War Learned to Recruit Ecology

### Author's note

There are subjects that become less believable when described accurately.

In the 1950s the United States Army bred mosquitoes and fleas, put them into devices meant to survive dispersal, released uninfected insects in field tests, and tried to learn what happened next. This is documented history. It is not necessary to add infected American civilians, secret present-day swarms, or omnipotent bioweapons to make it strange.

The discipline of this book is to resist that temptation.

A military document proves that a program existed. It does not prove that every idea in the program worked. A successful release of uninfected mosquitoes proves that mosquitoes can survive a particular release. It does not prove that a pathogen-vector-host system would produce the casualties imagined on a planning slide. A witness proves that someone remembers an event. A declassified page can still be wrong. A classified stamp never certified truth.

The book also refuses the opposite shortcut. The fact that biological systems are messy does not mean militaries did not study them seriously. The fact that contemporary scientists release modified or symbiont-carrying mosquitoes to suppress disease does not erase the history of military vector research. Similar hardware can serve opposite moral purposes.

Four labels recur in the notes: **documented**, **credible but incomplete**, **disputed**, and **speculative**. They are not decorations. They are load-bearing.

This is a history of what happens when a weapon system is alive enough to disobey.
