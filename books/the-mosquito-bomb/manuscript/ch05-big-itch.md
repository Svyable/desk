# Chapter 5 — Big Itch

The name sounds like soldiers naming something they did not want to explain to civilians.

Operation Big Itch, conducted at Dugway Proving Ground in 1954, tested whether fleas could survive aerial dissemination and acquire hosts afterward. Public summaries of the declassified record describe uninfected tropical rat fleas and experimental munitions. At least one delivery-device failure allowed fleas to escape inside an aircraft and bite crew members.

That small incident contains the entire strategic problem in miniature.

The weapon bit the people carrying it.

Nothing catastrophic happened because the test insects were uninfected. But the event exposes a category error that recurs in biological-warfare imagination: treating a living carrier like inert payload.

A conventional submunition that remains inside an aircraft is a malfunction.

A flea that escapes is a malfunction with legs.

The test nevertheless demonstrated that fleas could survive aspects of aerial release and find hosts. In the narrow engineering sense, this was success. The military question was whether an arthropod carrier could endure the delivery chain.

The broader operational question was uglier. A flea-borne weapon would depend on the biology of the flea, the pathogen, the local host community, climate, defensive measures and time. The Army could evaluate pieces. It could not make ecology sign a performance specification.

This is where historical accounts can become accidentally promotional. Describing successful survival, dispersal or host acquisition can sound like a recipe for efficacy. It is not. A systems claim requires all the steps and their uncertainties.

The ethical boundary is also important. Big Itch was conducted on a proving ground, not an urban neighborhood. That difference matters. It does not erase the purpose of the work, but it separates a military range trial from later tests in inhabited places.

The evidence classes should remain visible even in narrative.

**Documented:** the Army tested uninfected fleas as potential vectors and evaluated delivery.

**Credible inference:** the tests informed assessments of entomological-warfare feasibility.

**Not established by Big Itch:** real-world casualty production by infected fleas under combat conditions.

The last sentence is less exciting than the first two.

It is also the one that keeps history from becoming advertising.
