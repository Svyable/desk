# 01 — The Machine Said Yes

Mars ate the food.

That is not what happened, exactly. It is close enough to explain why the argument never died.

In 1976 a small laboratory sitting on another planet took a sample of Martian soil, gave it nutrients tagged with radioactive carbon, and waited to see whether radioactive gas appeared above the sample. The logic was almost offensively legible. If something in the dirt metabolized the food, carbon would move. The instrument would count it.

The counter on Mars moved.

Then it moved at the other landing site too.

There are discoveries that arrive wearing a tuxedo. A clean spectral line. A particle at the predicted mass. A photograph with the thing in the middle. Viking gave astrobiology something more irritating: the experiment designed to ask the biological question behaved in a way its investigators regarded as compatible with biology, while the mission as a whole refused to give that answer permission to become a discovery.

This is where the story is usually ruined.

One version says NASA found life and covered it up. Another says Viking found reactive dirt, everyone serious understood this, and one stubborn principal investigator spent the rest of his life refusing to accept defeat. Both versions are too efficient. They remove the part worth looking at.

The machine really did something interesting.

The biological explanation really was not established.

And one of the reasons the biological explanation lost standing was that another Viking instrument appeared not to find the organic material that life should leave behind.

That last fact aged badly.

Not disastrously. Not enough to turn the first experiment into a microbial census. Badly enough that the old argument deserves to be reopened with the labels still attached.

## The experiment with the dangerous name

It was called Labeled Release, LR. Gilbert Levin was its principal investigator; Patricia Straat worked with him on the experiment and its interpretation. The name is beautifully literal. Label the nutrient with radioactive carbon. Release it into a soil sample. Watch for labeled gas.

At both Viking landing sites, adding the nutrient produced radioactive gas. The responses were similar enough that Levin and Straat could write in *Science* in 1976 that the available facts did not yet permit a conclusion about life. That sentence matters because later retellings tend to recruit the early paper for one side or the other. It was neither a discovery announcement nor a dismissal. It was an unresolved result with teeth.

The heat controls made the teeth sharper.

Material heated hard enough to sterilize terrestrial microorganisms lost the active response. Lower heat reduced it. That is the sort of behavior a biologist likes because dead things do not eat. It is also not uniquely biological. Heat changes chemistry. It destroys compounds, rearranges surfaces, drives off volatiles, alters oxidants. A sterilization-shaped result is not the same thing as proof of sterilization.

That distinction is the whole book in miniature.

A result can look biological without biology being the only mechanism capable of producing it.

The correct response is not to call it life. The correct response is to find the chemistry that can impersonate life and make it reproduce the whole pattern, including the controls.

People tried.

Hydrogen peroxide became one candidate. Superoxides and other reactive surface chemistry entered the discussion. Levin and Straat themselves tested nonbiological explanations. A 1981 paper reported that peroxide on certain Mars-analog soils could reproduce important kinetic and thermal features of the LR response. Then came the annoying clause: the peroxide concentration required seemed to demand stability or production rates the authors regarded as implausibly high for Mars. Their conclusion was not that peroxide had failed and therefore Mars was alive. It was narrower: biology had not been ruled out.

That is a much less marketable sentence. It is also the sentence science keeps producing when the universe refuses to sign the paperwork.

## The second machine

Viking carried a gas chromatograph–mass spectrometer. Its job included looking for organic compounds in heated Martian material. The mission did not obtain the expected organic inventory. Two simple chlorinated hydrocarbons appeared, but contamination was considered a plausible explanation.

This mattered enormously.

Imagine the evidentiary geometry from inside 1976. One experiment says something in the soil reacts quickly when fed. Another says the soil does not appear to contain the organic carbon expected in a living system. Mars is cold, dry, irradiated and hostile. The parsimonious move is toward reactive geochemistry.

That move was not stupid. It may still be right.

But it contained a hidden dependency: **the no-organics result had to mean what scientists thought it meant.**

Three decades later, Mars supplied a new reagent to the argument.

Phoenix found perchlorate in Martian soil.

Perchlorate is not a magic biological rescue. It is chemistry. That is why it matters. When Mars-like soil containing organic carbon is mixed with magnesium perchlorate and heated, the heating process can destroy much of the organic material and produce chlorinated compounds. In 2010 Rafael Navarro-González and colleagues explicitly reanalyzed the Viking result in this light. Their experiments and model suggested that Viking's heating protocol could have oxidized organic material and generated chloromethane and dichloromethane—the same classes of chlorinated molecules that had once been treated as contamination.

Klaus Biemann, a leader of the original Viking molecular-analysis work, pushed back. The reinterpretation depended on assumptions, including perchlorate being present at the Viking sites, and he disputed whether the proposed chemistry justified rewriting the old result.

Good.

This is what a live scientific argument looks like when nobody gets to skip a step.

Then Curiosity complicated the old negative further. At Cumberland in Gale Crater, its Sample Analysis at Mars instrument produced the first definitive detection of Martian organic chemicals in surface material. NASA's own explanation includes the possibility that perchlorate reacting during heating created the chlorinated molecules from Martian organic precursors.

Read that slowly.

The modern result does **not** say Viking detected life.

It says the proposition *Mars has no detectable surface organics, therefore the LR response is unlikely to be biological* no longer has the clean force it once had. Mars does contain indigenous organic material. Heating oxychlorine-bearing Martian samples can transform what an instrument sees. The old negative control was not simply wrong; its meaning became conditional on chemistry learned later.

This is more interesting than vindication.

Vindication is a courtroom word. Science rarely gives it cleanly. What happened instead is that one piece of the structure holding down an anomalous result became weaker after the structure had already been built.

## The disappearing veto

Call it the disappearing veto.

Experiment A produces a result compatible with extraordinary hypothesis H.

Experiment B produces a result that appears inconsistent with H.

The field sensibly downgrades H.

Years later, new knowledge shows that B was less diagnostic than believed.

What happens to A?

Nothing automatic.

This is the trap. Removing evidence against a hypothesis is not the same thing as producing evidence for it. If a prosecutor's witness is shown to be unreliable, the defendant is not thereby proven innocent. The case changes because the likelihoods change, not because reality runs backward.

Viking is a perfect machine for thinking about this because every temptation is available at once.

The believer can say: *See? They found organics after all.*

The skeptic can say: *Perchlorate gives us weird chemistry; case closed.*

Neither statement earns the ending.

Perchlorate helps explain why thermal organic detection can fail or produce misleading chlorinated products. It does not, by itself, reproduce every feature of the Labeled Release flight data under the conditions at both Viking sites. Conversely, later confirmation of Martian organics removes one historical objection to biology but does not identify the agent that produced the LR gas.

The active agent remains unidentified at the level that matters.

That sentence should bother everyone equally.

## We have not repeated the question

The strangest fact may be methodological rather than Martian.

We have landed increasingly sophisticated laboratories on Mars. We have drilled. Heated. Sniffed. Imaged. Measured radiation. Watched methane arguments rise and collapse and rise again. We have become much better at asking whether ancient Mars was habitable.

We have not simply rerun Viking's metabolic question with a modern instrument designed to discriminate biological metabolism from the reactive chemistry now known to exist there.

There are good reasons. Planetary protection. Instrument mass. Mission priorities. The scientific preference for biosignatures that can be cross-validated through multiple chemical and geological channels. The recognition that terrestrial metabolism is not a universal template for alien life.

Still: it is remarkable.

The only time humanity put purpose-built metabolic life-detection experiments on Mars, one of them produced the result that has caused an argument for half a century. Our response was not to build a better version of that experiment and send it back.

We changed the question.

Maybe that was wisdom. Maybe the original question was underdetermined by design. Maybe a nutrient soup dropped onto oxidizing Martian dirt was always going to create a chemistry experiment wearing a biology costume.

But there is a difference between learning that a question was badly posed and deciding, after an ambiguous answer, not to ask it again.

That difference is where this book lives.

## Four boxes

Before going further, put the evidence into boxes.

**Documented:** the LR flight response occurred at both Viking sites. Heat treatment altered or eliminated the response. The original investigators and other scientists considered biological and nonbiological explanations. Viking's molecular-analysis instrument did not establish an indigenous organic inventory. Phoenix later found perchlorate. Laboratory work showed perchlorate can complicate thermal organic analysis. Curiosity later established indigenous Martian organics.

**Credible but incomplete:** particular chemical mechanisms can reproduce subsets of LR behavior; retrospective reconstructions can change the weight assigned to Viking's molecular negative; institutional judgments can be reconstructed from publications and mission histories but not every conversation that produced consensus survives.

**Disputed:** LR detected extant Martian microorganisms. A particular oxidant fully explains LR. NASA knew life had been found and suppressed the conclusion.

**Speculative:** Viking may be a general model for scientific results whose interpretation depends on a second measurement that later becomes less diagnostic. Consensus can be rational when formed and still inherit a hidden expiration date from one of its premises.

That final idea is mine. It is not a NASA finding. It can fail.

If a nonbiological mechanism is demonstrated that reproduces the LR response, its kinetics, its repeat behavior, its thermal controls and its behavior under relevant Martian conditions without special pleading, the biological case contracts dramatically.

If preserved samples or a modern repeat experiment reveal metabolism with independent molecular signatures, the case moves the other way.

Until then, the most accurate sentence may also be the least satisfying one.

In 1976, on Mars, the machine said yes.

We still do not know what answered.
