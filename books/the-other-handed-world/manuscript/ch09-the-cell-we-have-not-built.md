# The Cell We Have Not Built

The easiest way to misunderstand mirror-life risk is to imagine a finished bacterium because the headlines did.

There is no known self-replicating mirror bacterium.

That absence is not a technical footnote. It defines the evidence. Every claim about mirror-bacterium ecology, pathogenicity, environmental persistence or evolutionary behavior is prospective. The science can be rigorous while remaining model-based, but the verbs have to stay honest.

What exists are pieces: mirror peptides and proteins, mirror nucleic acids, mirror polymerases, mirror replication and transcription systems, and increasingly capable synthetic methods. What does not exist is an autonomous cell whose core informational and protein machinery is consistently inverted and which can acquire resources, grow and reproduce without continuous external reconstruction.

The gap is enormous because cells are integrated systems. A mirror genome would need compatible replication. Gene expression would need mirror transcription. Translation would require a mirror or otherwise stereochemically compatible apparatus. Metabolic enzymes would need mirrored substrates or pathways. Transporters would need the correct molecular geometry. Cell-envelope synthesis, energy coupling and repair would all have to coexist.

This integration problem creates uncertainty about timelines. The 2024 *Science* commentary described mirror life as likely at least a decade away and requiring substantial investment and advances. Such forecasts are not laws. Synthetic biology sometimes moves faster when a bottleneck yields and slower when integration reveals hidden dependencies.

The responsible posture is therefore neither imminence nor complacency.

The technical report behind the warning tried to enumerate barriers and possible routes around them without claiming that completion was guaranteed. That is exactly the kind of document a mature field should produce before a capability exists: identify what would have to become true, which advances would matter, and which safety assumptions fail if those advances occur.

There is a historical reason scientists often resist this mode. Predictions of transformative biology have a poor calibration record. Artificial life, minimal cells, de novo genomes and fully programmable organisms have repeatedly taken longer and behaved less cleanly than forecasts suggested. Biology punishes modular thinking. Components that work separately can interfere when combined.

Mirror life may prove another example. Perhaps translation remains prohibitively difficult. Perhaps mirror metabolism creates dependencies that make environmental survival weak. Perhaps ecological interactions turn out less asymmetric than current models fear. Those possibilities belong in the book because uncertainty cuts both ways.

But absence of a finished organism also means there is still a choice.

Once a self-replicating lineage exists, knowledge cannot be recalled in the same sense a grant can be canceled. Physical samples can be destroyed, but methods, sequence designs, trained personnel and tacit knowledge may persist. The first successful construction would therefore change governance even if no release occurred.

This is what makes the unfinished cell scientifically interesting. Most frontier technologies invite acceleration because feasibility is the desired result. Mirror life creates a rare case where feasibility itself may be a policy-relevant hazard state.

The cell we have not built is not evidence of safety.

It is evidence that the decision is still upstream.
