# Chemistry Chooses

The origin of homochirality attracts theories because it offers a rare scientific luxury: the final state is obvious and the path is missing.

Every known organism inherits the same broad stereochemical convention. That is stronger than many traits we confidently assign to deep ancestry. Cells disagree about oxygen, sunlight, temperature, salt, cell walls and metabolism. They do not disagree about which amino-acid hand belongs in ordinary proteins.

A universal feature can arise in two ways that are easy to confuse. It may be physically necessary. Or it may be historically locked in.

Mirror biochemistry makes the second possibility difficult to ignore. Synthetic D-proteins fold into mirror structures. Mirror nucleic acids can store information. Chiral-inverted polymerases can operate on chiral-inverted templates. Nothing in these demonstrations says an autonomous mirror cell is easy. They do say that several central biochemical operations are not restricted in principle to Earth's chosen orientation.

So why does life need a single orientation at all?

Because mixed handedness creates geometry problems everywhere. Protein secondary structures depend on repeated stereochemical relationships. Enzyme active sites are three-dimensional. Nucleic-acid helices inherit sugar chirality. Translation couples a nucleic-acid information system to a protein-building system with its own stereochemical standard. A partly mixed system is not just diverse; it can be internally frustrated.

Origin-of-life chemistry therefore has two problems: symmetry breaking and symmetry amplification. First, something creates or selects a small difference. Then a network turns that difference into a nearly universal convention.

The literature offers mechanisms in both categories. Circularly polarized radiation can create enantiomer-specific photochemistry. Mineral surfaces can bind one hand more strongly than another. Mechanical and fluid processes can bias crystallization. Autocatalytic reaction networks can magnify tiny initial differences. Eutectic behavior can concentrate one enantiomer in solution. Amino-acid excesses can influence sugar chemistry and vice versa under particular laboratory conditions.

The existence of many mechanisms is not evidence that one of them happened on the early Earth. It is evidence that symmetry breaking is chemically possible without invoking biology to explain biology. The historical reconstruction remains difficult because prebiotic environments were heterogeneous and the relevant transition likely involved multiple coupled molecular systems.

This is where public explanations often become too smooth. They present homochirality as a solved consequence of meteorites, polarized light, or a famous autocatalytic reaction. Each mechanism demonstrates a route, not the route. The actual origin may have involved weak biases amplified repeatedly across different stages.

Mirror life matters because it offers a counterfactual with teeth. Imagine an early pond, vent, ice film or mineral surface in which the opposite enantiomer happened to gain the first durable advantage. If the chemistry can support equivalent mirror molecular machinery, then an alternate biosphere could plausibly emerge with reversed handedness while preserving many of the same abstract functions.

That does not mean evolution would replay identically. Chirality changes interactions with any asymmetric environment, including other chiral molecules delivered from space. But the broad claim survives: life may need a hand without needing our hand.

The difference changes how we think about necessity in biology. The genetic code is nearly universal but contains historical contingency. Cellular membranes are universal in function but not chemically identical across domains. Chirality may be the deepest example of a frozen standard—arbitrary at the beginning, indispensable after adoption.

Engineers understand this kind of thing. A railway gauge can be contingent and still become enormously expensive to change. A voltage standard can be historically arbitrary and physically consequential once infrastructure accumulates around it. Biology's gauge happens to be molecular.

Unlike railways, however, a second gauge could be built from scratch.

That is what synthetic mirror biology proposes in pieces: not converting existing life, but constructing a parallel molecular standard that ordinary enzymes often cannot service. The property sounds like isolation. For therapeutics and information storage, isolation is useful.

For an organism, isolation may become ecological power.

The same contingency that makes mirror life conceivable is the reason natural life may be poorly prepared to control it.
