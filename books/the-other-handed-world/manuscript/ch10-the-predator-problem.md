# The Predator Problem

Bacteria do not live alone, even when the laboratory tube makes them look solitary.

In soil, water, guts and biofilms, bacterial populations are eaten, infected, poisoned, starved and competed with constantly. Bacteriophages kill bacteria at enormous scale. Protists graze on them. Other bacteria deploy antibiotics and contact-dependent weapons. Nutrients run out. Hosts detect microbial patterns and mount defenses.

Ecology is a machine for preventing one replicator from receiving the whole planet for free.

The mirror-life risk assessment asks what happens if many of those controls depend on stereochemical recognition.

Phages are especially revealing. Infection begins with molecular contact between viral structures and bacterial receptors. A fully mirrored cell surface could present the wrong geometry for ordinary phages. Even if some physical interactions survived, downstream viral enzymes and replication machinery would confront mirrored substrates. Existing phage populations would not automatically be equipped for that biochemical world.

Protist predation may also depend on molecular recognition and digestibility. Engulfment alone is not enough; prey must be processed. Mirror proteins and other macromolecules can resist ordinary enzymes. A predator that swallows a cell it cannot efficiently digest has not solved the ecological problem.

These are mechanistic reasons for concern, not measurements of mirror ecosystems. No one has seeded a pond with mirror bacteria and watched the food web collapse. The absence of that experiment is the point.

The risk argument is strongest when it does not claim perfect invulnerability. A mirror organism would still face temperature, osmotic stress, starvation, ultraviolet light, physical removal, toxic chemicals and competition for resources. It would not float outside ecology. Many nutrients in nature are themselves chiral, which could constrain metabolism unless the organism had routes to achiral resources or compatible feedstocks.

That counterweight matters. Mirror bacteria are sometimes described as able to eat the world while nothing can eat them. That is rhetoric, not a demonstrated ecological model. Resource competition alone can be severe. Natural organisms may produce achiral toxins. Evolution could generate indirect controls. Environmental conditions might make a mirror lineage fragile.

The troubling question is narrower: would a new lineage enter ecosystems with unusually few adapted biological enemies?

Invasion biology says enemy release can matter. Organisms introduced to new regions sometimes expand because parasites and predators that controlled them in their native environment are absent. Mirror life would be stranger: not foreign geography but foreign stereochemistry.

There is no ancestral mirror ecosystem from which specialized predators could follow.

That is the asymmetry the 2024 authors considered potentially unprecedented. Natural invasive species still share a biochemical language with their new environment. Their predators may be missing, but the environment's enzymes, immune systems and molecular interactions remain broadly compatible. A mirror lineage could combine ecological novelty with biochemical mismatch.

Evolution might eventually close the gap. Natural organisms could evolve mechanisms to exploit mirror prey or its metabolites. Mirror organisms could evolve toward ordinary substrates. But evolutionary rescue takes generations and requires viable intermediate steps. A fast-spreading lineage does not owe the ecosystem time.

The predator problem therefore turns mirror life from a laboratory biosafety question into a planetary ecology question.

Containment plans usually assume that outside the vessel, nature becomes hostile.

What if nature mostly fails to recognize the intruder as food?
