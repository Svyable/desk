# DNA Nobody Can Read

Information storage usually fails because somebody can read it too easily or because nature destroys the medium.

Mirror DNA offers the opposite inconvenience.

Natural DNA uses D-sugars in its backbone. Its mirror form, L-DNA, can in principle encode the same sequences because sequence information and molecular handedness are separable. The letters remain letters. The molecular machine required to read them changes.

This distinction became concrete in 2021 when researchers synthesized a high-fidelity mirror-image Pfu DNA polymerase and used it to assemble kilobase-scale L-DNA. One demonstration encoded a passage associated with Pasteur's old speculation about a mirrored biological world. The joke was technically exact: information about the mirror world stored in a molecular script ordinary biology could not conveniently read.

The group described applications in bioorthogonal information storage and chiral steganography. A mixed library could contain normal DNA readable by normal polymerases and mirror DNA requiring mirrored molecular machinery. To an ordinary biological reader, part of the archive is effectively dark.

This is not encryption in the cryptographic sense. The sequence is not mathematically protected from an adversary who can build the appropriate chemistry. It is protection by molecular incompatibility. The lock is stereochemical.

That property matters for mirror-life risk because nucleic-acid degradation is one of nature's cleanup systems. Cells and environments are full of nucleases evolved to cut ordinary DNA and RNA. Mirror nucleic acids can resist many such enzymes. In medicine, that can be useful. In a replicating organism, resistance to ordinary molecular turnover could alter persistence.

Again, a molecule is not an ecosystem. L-DNA left in soil does not divide. It cannot manufacture a membrane or forage for carbon. The risk analysis starts only when multiple orthogonal components become integrated into autonomous biology.

The technology nevertheless reveals how much of life's interoperability is accidental. We often speak of DNA as a universal language. Sequence-level conservation justifies the phrase within known life. But the universality has a hidden prerequisite: a compatible stereochemical reader.

A hypothetical mirror organism could carry genes that look structurally alien to natural enzymes while encoding familiar biochemical functions in a mirrored molecular system. Sequence alone would not save us if the sample-preparation and amplification tools failed to engage the substrate.

This becomes an astrobiology problem as well as a synthetic-biology one. Life-detection instruments frequently target molecules or reactions inherited from terrestrial biochemistry. If extraterrestrial life used reversed chirality, some Earth-designed enzymes and affinity reagents could miss it even if the underlying chemistry were otherwise astonishingly familiar.

That does not make mirror aliens likely. There is no evidence that an extraterrestrial biosphere uses L-nucleic acids or D-proteins. The point is methodological: a detector built around Earth's molecular fit contains assumptions about life's handedness whether the instrument team states them or not.

The best life-detection systems therefore use multiple layers. Measure molecular distributions. Look for persistent enantiomeric excess. Examine isotopic fractionation. Search for repeating polymers. Image structures. Test chemical disequilibrium. Avoid making one terrestrial enzyme the sole gatekeeper of alien biology.

Mirror DNA sharpens the same lesson for laboratories on Earth. Orthogonality is not invisibility in an absolute sense. It is invisibility to a particular interface.

We can design another interface.

The danger is assuming nature already did.
