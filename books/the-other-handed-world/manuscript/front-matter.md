# The Other-Handed World

## Mirror Life, Biological Chirality, and the Boundary We May Decide Not to Cross

**Sven Hardy Benson**

Every known cell on Earth has made the same old choice so consistently that we stopped calling it a choice.

Proteins are built almost entirely from left-handed amino acids. DNA and RNA use sugars of the opposite handedness. The arrangement is not metaphorical. Molecular shapes that contain the same atoms can exist as non-superimposable mirror images, like hands. Biology cares because enzymes, receptors, antibodies, ribosomes and proteases meet one another in three dimensions. A left glove is not a defective right glove. It is a different object.

For most of modern biology, this asymmetry belonged to the origin-of-life shelf: one of those profound facts everyone could state and nobody could fully explain. Then chemistry began to trespass. Researchers synthesized mirror proteins. A mirror polymerase copied mirror DNA. Later work pushed mirror genetic systems toward greater fidelity and larger molecules. None of this is mirror life. No mirror bacterium exists in the public scientific record. But the road stopped being philosophical.

In December 2024, thirty-eight scientists published a warning in *Science*. Several had spent years treating mirror biology as an intellectually attractive destination. They now argued that a self-replicating mirror organism could create unusually severe biological and ecological risks because much of ordinary life recognizes other life through chiral molecular fit. Natural proteases may not digest mirror proteins well. Natural nucleases may not cut mirror nucleic acids. Phages adapted to normal bacteria may fail to infect a mirror cell. Immune recognition could be badly compromised. The same property that makes mirror peptides useful as durable drugs could become a problem if persistence acquired metabolism and replication.

That reversal is the book.

The subject is not a monster bacterium waiting in a freezer. The technical report behind the 2024 warning says the capability is not imminent and would require major advances. The United Nations Scientific Advisory Board went further in March 2026, arguing for proactive global red lines before feasibility arrives. The extraordinary fact is procedural: a scientific community is being asked to decide whether a line of research should stop before the decisive experiment exists.

That is unusual enough to deserve patience. Science usually learns by making the thing, measuring it, discovering the failure mode, and revising the theory. Mirror life asks whether the first complete demonstration could itself consume too much option value. It forces a distinction between mirror molecules, which already have legitimate medical and technical uses, and self-sustaining mirror organisms, which may belong to a different risk category.

The book therefore moves backward before moving forward. It starts with Pasteur and molecular asymmetry, then follows meteorites, prebiotic chemistry and the unresolved origin of homochirality. Only after the reader understands why all known life is one-handed do we enter the synthetic mirror: D-proteins, L-DNA, mirror polymerases, bioorthogonal storage, drug discovery and the still-unbuilt cell. The final third asks what ordinary immune ecology assumes about chirality, how those assumptions might fail, what is known versus modeled, and what responsible abstention would actually mean.

The evidence posture is strict. Mirror biomolecules and mirror enzymatic functions are documented. Universal terrestrial homochirality is documented. Meteoritic enantiomeric excesses are real but do not by themselves explain life's handedness. The hazards of a complete mirror bacterium are prospective scientific assessments, not observed outbreak data. Claims of inevitable extinction are not established. Claims that containment would certainly fail are not established. The strongest case is stranger because it lives before certainty: enough molecular asymmetry is known that several mechanisms of biological control plausibly weaken at the same time.

We are used to asking whether alien life would recognize us.

Mirror biology asks a narrower question first.

Would our biology recognize it?
