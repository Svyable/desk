# The Mirror Central Dogma

Molecular biology's central dogma is usually drawn as arrows: DNA to RNA to protein. Arrows conceal hardware.

DNA does not copy itself because the letters wish to continue. Polymerases bind, select nucleotides, catalyze bonds and proofread. RNA does not become protein by symbolic interpretation. Ribosomes, transfer RNAs, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and accessory factors execute a physical translation system. Every step has stereochemistry.

Mirror biology becomes difficult exactly where the diagram looks simplest.

Researchers have demonstrated mirror genetic replication and transcription with chemically synthesized mirror polymerases. They have produced large D-proteins and increasingly sophisticated L-nucleic-acid systems. The remaining leap toward autonomous mirror life requires something much closer to a mirrored translation economy.

Translation is a bottleneck because ribosomes are enormous molecular assemblies containing both RNA and protein. Their manufacture in cells depends on other ribosomes. This is one of biology's bootstrapping problems even in the natural handed world: the machinery that makes proteins contains proteins and RNA whose production already assumes the machinery exists.

A complete mirror cell would need to escape that circularity by construction. The public literature discusses several conceptual routes, but the technical 2024 mirror-bacteria assessment is careful about the scale of the challenge. This is not an organism one synthesizes by flipping a setting on a DNA printer.

That difficulty is part of why the warning came early. If complete mirror life were already routine, governance would be late. If it were physically impossible, governance would be unnecessary. The current state is awkward: key components are scientifically credible, progress is visible, integration remains formidable, and the eventual risk could be unusually asymmetric.

The central dogma also explains why partial mirror systems are useful without being existential. A mirror peptide drug enters ordinary biology as a chemically persistent object. It does not possess mirror ribosomes to make more of itself. An L-DNA archive can store information but cannot recruit natural polymerases to proliferate. Orthogonality is bounded by dependence.

Autonomy removes that dependence.

This suggests a cleaner safety distinction than the phrase *mirror biology* provides. The relevant threshold is not mirroredness alone. It is mirroredness plus self-replication plus access to environmental resources. A D-protein therapeutic can be valuable. A mirror polymerase can be a tool. A mirror protocell unable to sustain itself may be experimentally useful under appropriate controls. A robust free-living mirror bacterium would occupy a different governance category because it could generate descendants outside the laboratory.

The distinction matters because broad bans can become intellectually lazy. The 2024 authors did not argue that all mirror molecules should stop. Several are leaders in mirror-biochemistry research and emphasized legitimate applications. Their concern centered on constructing self-replicating mirror organisms.

That is a more defensible red line because it follows mechanism.

It also leaves difficult intermediate cases. What about a mirror system dependent on a synthetic nutrient absent from nature? What if mutations could alter the dependency? What if only some macromolecular classes were mirrored? What if the organism could replicate inside a bioreactor but not outside it? These are real governance questions, but they should not be answered by inventing confidence where experiments do not yet exist.

The central dogma teaches restraint in both directions.

We should not pretend the arrows are already mirrored end to end.

We should not assume they never will be.
