# The Other-Handed World

The mirror world already exists in fragments.

A D-peptide survives where a natural peptide is quickly degraded. An L-DNA molecule stores information that ordinary polymerases cannot conveniently read. A mirror polymerase copies a mirror template. A meteorite carries small chiral biases that may be older than terrestrial life. A UN scientific body asks governments to discuss red lines around an organism nobody has yet made.

The fragments do not add up to a bacterium.

That missing object is the most important fact in the book.

Scientific history usually celebrates the removal of the word *impossible*. Powered flight. Nuclear fission. Organ transplantation. Synthetic genomes. Technologies become morally legible after they become physically real. Mirror life has arrived at an earlier stage, where enough component science works that feasibility no longer sounds absurd, while enough biology is understood that completion itself may be the experiment we should decline.

The case remains conditional. A mirror organism would not be magic. It would need energy, nutrients, water, tolerable temperature and a viable metabolism. It could be killed by sufficiently harsh physical and chemical means. Some immune responses might still detect tissue damage or non-chiral features. Ecology might constrain it more strongly than current worst-case models assume.

The reason for restraint is not certainty of catastrophe.

It is that the ordinary safety net around microbes is biological, and biology is chiral.

Proteases, nucleases, phages, receptors, antibodies and predators participate in a world standardized around one molecular hand. Mirror biomolecules are useful precisely because parts of that world fail to engage them. Turning that property into a self-replicating lineage could convert orthogonality from engineering convenience into ecological asymmetry.

No single paper proves the outcome. The evidence arrives sideways: meteoritic chemistry says asymmetry can precede life; synthetic chemistry says the opposite hand can perform central molecular functions; drug development says mirror molecules can evade biological degradation; immunology says recognition depends on molecular shape; ecology says enemy release can matter; governance says some decisions are easier before capability than after it.

That collision is enough for a book and not enough for a prophecy.

The origin-of-life question remains beautiful because we still do not know why our biosphere chose this hand. The synthetic-biology question remains beautiful because building mirror molecular machinery reveals how much of life's architecture is contingent. Neither beauty obliges us to build every possible organism.

There is a mature form of curiosity that includes boundaries.

Pasteur wanted to understand the asymmetry of life. A century and a half later, we can make enough of the mirror to understand his question better than he could. We may also be approaching the point where the final proof would tell us less than the decision to seek it costs.

The other-handed world is no longer pure fantasy.

For now, it is still mostly chemistry.

That may be exactly where we should keep it.
