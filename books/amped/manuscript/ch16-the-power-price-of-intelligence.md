# The Power Price of Intelligence

A data center can pay every bill the utility sends it and still make electricity more expensive for everyone else.

That sentence sounds like an accusation.

It is actually a distinction.

There are two ways a giant new customer can raise another customer's cost.

The first is cost shifting.

The utility builds a substation, transmission line, generation resource, or other equipment primarily to serve the new load and then allocates too little of that cost to the customer that caused it. Existing households and businesses pick up the difference through rates.

The second is system repricing.

The new customer pays its assigned share correctly, but its demand increases the need for scarce generation, transmission, fuel, land, equipment, or capacity. The marginal resource becomes more expensive. Wholesale prices rise. Capacity prices rise. Transmission expansion accelerates. Everybody pays more because the system itself has become more expensive to operate and expand.

The two mechanisms are politically easy to confuse.

They require different remedies.

Cost shifting can often be attacked through tariff design.

System repricing cannot be legislated away without moving the cost somewhere else.

Virginia has become the clearest American laboratory for the first problem.

By 2025, Dominion Energy Virginia's territory contained enough hyperscale demand that the State Corporation Commission no longer wanted the largest customers pooled automatically into the same rate structure used for ordinary commercial and industrial load. The Commission approved a new GS-5 class for customers meeting high capacity and usage thresholds, including large data centers. The class is scheduled to take effect in 2027.

The logic is straightforward.

If a customer is large enough to cause unusual infrastructure investment, unusual forecast risk, and unusual system obligations, its rate can be designed around those characteristics rather than averaging them across a broader class.

The Commission added other protections.

Large customers can be required to remain on service long enough to match the assets built for them. They can face minimum payments tied to contracted demand even if actual usage falls. They can provide collateral behind portions of those obligations. The point is to keep a project cancellation or a sharp reduction in load from leaving everyone else with a forty-year electrical asset and no matching revenue stream.

This is infrastructure underwriting disguised as a utility tariff.

A utility does not care whether a hyperscaler has a famous name.

It cares whether the cash flows supporting the substation survive after the ribbon cutting.

A fourteen-year service obligation is therefore not mainly about punishing a company that changes its mind.

It is about matching two clocks.

The technology company's clock is short.

The grid asset's clock is long.

A server fleet can be replaced in years.

A transmission line can remain in service for generations.

The rate contract is the bridge between them.

Minimum charges serve the same purpose.

Suppose a utility builds to serve a customer that contracts for five hundred megawatts.

The campus later uses only two hundred fifty.

If the customer simply pays for actual demand while the utility planned around five hundred, the unused capacity does not vanish from the utility's revenue requirement. Regulators must decide who absorbs it.

A minimum bill tells the customer that reserving capacity has value even when it is not fully used.

Airlines understand this.

A passenger who buys a seat and does not show up cannot expect the airline to recover the fixed cost from another traveler after departure.

Electric capacity is more complicated, because spare transmission can sometimes serve others, but the principle remains.

Reservation is an economic act.

The most aggressive protection is collateral.

A utility can require the customer to stand behind part of its future minimum charges with a guarantee or other financial security. This makes speculative load more expensive to put into the forecast. A developer with uncertain financing or an uncommitted tenant has to decide whether preserving electrical capacity is worth tying up capital.

That is exactly the signal a congested grid needs.

The tariff starts filtering fantasy.

This is why debates about data-center rates should not begin with whether technology companies are rich.

Ability to pay is relevant politically.

Cost causation is the more durable principle.

A good tariff asks which costs the customer causes, which costs it avoids, which costs are shared, and which risks its behavior introduces.

The answer can produce a high bill or a low one depending on location and service design.

A flexible data center built beside abundant generation may impose less peak-system cost than a smaller but fully firm facility in a constrained pocket.

A campus that funds its own substation and accepts curtailment may create fewer public obligations than one asking for immediate firm service.

A customer that arrives where transmission already has spare capacity can improve utilization of sunk assets.

Scale is not guilt.

Scale is materiality.

This brings the analysis to the harder mechanism: system repricing.

Virginia's own legislative review made the point carefully. Its commissioned analysis found that existing rate structures at the time were generally allocating current utility costs to the customers responsible for them, including data centers. Yet the review still estimated that rapid data-center growth could raise generation and transmission costs for typical households substantially over time.

Both findings can be true.

The data center can pay its current cost of service.

Its presence can still make the next increment of the system more expensive.

Imagine a town with ten houses sharing one well.

An eleventh house arrives and pays its fair share of the existing pump.

If the added demand requires drilling a deeper well, the cost of water can rise for all eleven houses even though nobody subsidized the newcomer.

The data-center problem is the grid version at industrial scale.

The marginal plant matters.

The marginal transmission project matters.

The marginal transformer matters.

The marginal gas molecule during a winter emergency matters.

Electricity markets price scarcity at the margin because somebody has to build or operate the resource that clears the last unit of demand.

When a cluster of enormous loads pushes the system toward that margin, average cost can rise even under perfect accounting.

This is where slogans about forcing data centers to pay their own way become incomplete.

They should pay their own way.

That does not guarantee everybody else's bill stays flat.

The public policy question becomes whether the broader price effect is worth the broader economic benefit.

That is an industrial-policy question, not merely a utility-accounting question.

A new aluminum smelter, steel mill, semiconductor fab, or automobile plant can also increase local electricity demand and trigger grid investment. Communities accept those costs when they believe the investment creates enough jobs, income, strategic capability, tax base, or downstream industry.

Data centers complicate the bargain because the local job count can be modest while the national economic value of the computation can be enormous.

The benefit and the power bill may occur in different places.

An AI training campus in Virginia can produce services sold globally.

The company's profits may accrue to shareholders everywhere.

The local county may collect tax revenue.

The regional power market may absorb the load.

A household in the same utility territory may face a higher system cost without owning a direct share of the software value produced by the servers.

This does not make the project unjust.

It makes incidence hard to see.

Economists use incidence to describe who ultimately bears a cost regardless of who writes the first check.

The concept belongs in the data-center debate.

A utility can bill the hyperscaler for new transmission.

The hyperscaler can pass some of the cost into cloud prices.

Its enterprise customers can pass the cost into products.

Shareholders can absorb lower margins.

Workers can absorb lower compensation growth.

The county can offset household taxes with data-center revenue.

The state can impose an electricity-consumption tax and recycle the proceeds.

The federal government can subsidize generation or transmission and move cost to taxpayers.

The final economic burden spreads through several systems.

There is no single line item called the power price of intelligence.

That is why transparency matters.

A serious policy should report at least four things separately.

What infrastructure is directly attributable to the large customer?

What portion of shared-system expansion is driven by aggregate load growth?

What market-price effect is expected as demand rises?

What public revenue or economic benefit is associated with the load?

Without this separation, every side can cherry-pick one ledger.

The industry can point to enormous utility payments and say it is covering its costs.

Consumer advocates can point to rising bills and say households are subsidizing data centers.

Both can be describing real numbers generated by different mechanisms.

The argument becomes productive only after the ledgers are reconciled.

Capacity markets make the distinction especially vivid.

In regions like PJM, the system procures enough dependable capacity to meet forecast peaks plus a reserve margin. If forecast demand grows rapidly, the market may need more capacity even before every new data center is operating. Higher auction prices can signal that supply is not keeping pace with expected load.

Those prices are paid across a broad customer base according to market and retail arrangements.

A particular data center may pay a large capacity bill too.

Its presence can still contribute to the scarcity that raises the capacity price for everyone.

This is not a billing error.

It is the market working.

The policy remedy is more supply, less firm demand, better forecasting, or some combination.

Trying to solve a scarcity price solely through cost allocation is like rearranging restaurant checks when the kitchen is out of food.

The quantity problem remains.

Transmission has a similar structure.

A line built solely for one campus can be assigned largely or entirely to that customer depending on jurisdiction and tariff.

A regional upgrade triggered by several loads and broader reliability needs can benefit many customers. Allocating its cost entirely to one project may be unfair and can discourage efficient development. Socializing all of it can shift too much risk to existing ratepayers.

The correct answer depends on use and benefit, not political convenience.

FERC's 2026 large-load proceedings were important partly because the old tariffs were not written for the new mix of scale, speed, co-location, and flexible service. The Commission directed the regional grid operators under its jurisdiction to justify or reform how large loads obtain and pay for transmission service. The national debate had moved beyond whether data centers were a local utility issue.

They had become a federal transmission-rate issue.

This is the normal consequence of scale.

Once a customer can alter regional planning, its tariff stops being merely retail.

The fight over cost allocation will intensify because the sums are large and the counterfactual is uncertain.

Every infrastructure project has a story about what would have happened without the load.

Would the line have been built anyway for reliability?

Would the generator have been needed for manufacturing growth?

Would electrification have required the substation later?

Would the upgrade lower congestion enough to benefit existing customers?

Would the data center's flexibility reduce the need for another project?

Cost causation is rarely a perfect fingerprint.

This creates room for politics.

Technology companies will argue that new infrastructure creates broad public benefits.

Consumer groups will argue that existing customers did not ask for a trillion-dollar compute buildout.

Utilities will prefer investments they can recover through stable rates.

Regulators will try to distinguish useful expansion from speculative overbuild.

Governors will count investment announcements.

Local officials will count tax receipts.

Households will count dollars on the monthly bill.

Each is measuring the same boom at a different boundary.

The rate design can improve the bargain even when it cannot erase the conflict.

Long-term contracts protect against stranded assets.

Minimum bills protect against underutilization.

Collateral protects against project failure.

Separate rate classes improve cost visibility.

Flexible service can lower the amount of firm capacity the system must build.

Customer-funded infrastructure can assign project-specific costs more directly.

Exit fees can protect remaining customers if a large load leaves utility supply after assets are committed.

Dynamic prices can encourage the customer to consume when the system has room.

None is exotic.

The novelty is applying them at hyperscale.

The design should also reward behavior that lowers shared costs.

A tariff focused only on protecting against downside can miss the opportunity described in the previous chapters. A data center with dependable curtailment, onsite storage, geographic workload mobility, or phased energization can reduce its system obligation. If the rate ignores those benefits, the customer has less reason to provide them.

Cost causation runs both directions.

If flexibility avoids a transmission upgrade, the customer should capture part of the value.

If a battery reduces peak capacity requirements, the rate should recognize the reduction.

If the campus locates where the system has spare headroom, the price should communicate that advantage.

A sophisticated tariff becomes a map of grid scarcity.

This is better than a political map.

The cheapest electricity for the data center should increasingly be where the system can serve the load cheaply.

That sounds obvious.

Economic-development incentives often work against it.

A state may offer tax breaks to attract a campus into a region where electrical infrastructure is constrained. The public then subsidizes the project through taxes while ratepayers fund the grid expansion. Each policy can look rational in isolation. Together they can amount to paying a company to create a scarcity problem.

This is why utility regulators and economic-development agencies need to talk.

The state should know the full public balance sheet before celebrating the private capital number.

Virginia's policy evolution again provides a preview.

The state built a dominant data-center market partly with favorable tax treatment. It later strengthened utility protections around large-load cost recovery. In 2026 it added a temporary electricity-consumption tax on certain data centers. These moves can look contradictory only if the industry is treated as one static policy object.

It is not.

A nascent cluster and a mature cluster deserve different policy.

Attracting investment and governing dependence are different stages.

The rest of the country is entering those stages at different speeds.

Some states still see a data center mainly as a trophy investment.

Others see it as a grid-planning emergency.

Some see tax revenue.

Others see water, transmission, and neighborhood conflict.

Some have spare generation and welcome the load.

Others are already competing with retirements, electrification, and manufacturing for limited capacity.

There should not be one national retail rate for data centers.

There should be common principles.

Pay for attributable infrastructure.

Secure long-lived obligations against short-lived commercial decisions.

Do not socialize speculative capacity reservations.

Recognize real flexibility.

Expose location and timing scarcity in price where practical.

Protect existing customers from abandonment risk.

Do not promise that fair allocation will prevent all systemwide price increases.

The last principle is politically unpleasant.

It is also necessary.

If the United States chooses to build a far larger electricity system to support AI, advanced manufacturing, electrification, and population growth, some infrastructure cost will appear somewhere.

The country can decide who pays.

It cannot decide that steel, copper, transformers, turbines, substations, rights of way, fuel, labor, and capital are free.

The best outcome is not zero cost.

It is useful cost.

Build assets that serve more than one boom.

Use customer commitments to reduce stranded risk.

Let flexible demand avoid unnecessary peak investment.

Expand supply where scarcity is real.

Create transmission with benefits that survive one customer.

Use the extraordinary credit quality of large technology buyers to finance infrastructure that strengthens the wider grid when the bargain is sound.

This is the deeper meaning of the power price of intelligence.

Artificial intelligence may make computation more valuable.

That value is now bidding for electricity against every other use of the system.

The grid translates the bid into physical work.

Somebody builds the line.

Somebody finances the plant.

Somebody reserves the transformer.

Somebody bears the forecast risk.

Somebody pays when the project changes.

A fair tariff can make those somebodies visible.

It cannot make them disappear.

There is one more reason incidence matters: the benefits are also passed through.

A data center that pays a large utility bill can increase utility revenue and spread fixed system costs across more sales. A county that receives equipment-tax revenue can lower other local taxes or fund services without raising them. A long-term corporate power contract can support a generator that would otherwise retire. New transmission can lower congestion after it is built. A strengthened substation can make later industrial development cheaper.

The same project can therefore raise one cost and lower another.

A household may pay more for generation while paying less in local property tax. A manufacturer may face a higher capacity price but gain access to transmission that did not exist before. A state may give up sales-tax revenue to attract the campus and later collect income, property, or consumption-related revenue through other channels. The public ledger has several columns.

This does not justify netting everything into one heroic estimate of “economic impact.” Those studies can become exercises in assumptions. It does mean that a credible consumer-protection analysis should track **offsetting channels** rather than stopping at the electric bill.

The cleanest way to do that is to separate transfer from resource cost.

A tax paid by the data center and used to lower another tax is largely a transfer within the public system. A new turbine, transformer, transmission line, or fuel purchase uses real resources. A higher wholesale price can transfer income from consumers to generators in the short run while also signaling that new supply is needed. A tax incentive can transfer value to the developer without changing the physical cost of the substation at all.

These are different economic objects.

Confusing them makes every argument sound larger than it is.

The publication version of the data-center debate should therefore resist one seductive number: “the amount households pay for AI.” There is no honest single figure without defining geography, time horizon, counterfactual, tax effects, market structure, and which infrastructure would have been built anyway.

The better discipline is to show the mechanism.

Which bill rose?

Which asset caused it?

Which customer commitment reduced it?

Which public revenue offset it?

Which scarcity remained after all transfers were counted?

That framework does not promise a politically comfortable answer.

It produces an answer that can survive contact with the next rate case.
