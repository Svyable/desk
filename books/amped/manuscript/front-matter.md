# Amped: How Power-Hungry Data Centers Are Revolutionizing the American Grid

**Sven Hardy Benson**

---

For most of my life, electricity was something America could afford not to think about.

You flipped the switch. The room changed.

The machinery behind that small miracle was enormous, but the customer did not need to understand it. Somewhere a turbine turned, a reactor split atoms, a dam released water, a gas plant came up, a wind farm caught a front, a solar field followed the sun, a transformer stepped voltage down, and a web of wires balanced supply and demand quickly enough that the clock on the microwave kept blinking the right numbers.

The grid was a civilization-sized machine disguised as a wall socket.

Data centers are making the disguise harder to maintain.

A modern hyperscale campus can ask for hundreds of megawatts. Several campuses in one utility territory can ask for gigawatts. The request no longer looks like another office building joining the local distribution system. It can look like a new city arriving without houses, a new aluminum smelter arriving without furnaces, or a power plant appearing on the wrong side of the meter.

The startling part is not simply the amount of electricity.

America has always had large industrial loads. Steel mills, refineries, chemical plants, aluminum smelters, automobile factories, paper mills, mines, semiconductor fabs, and military installations have all forced utilities to build around customers whose appetite for power would look absurd on a residential bill. Industrialization and electrification grew together. There is nothing historically unprecedented about a company saying it needs a lot of power.

What is different is the combination.

The data center is physically fixed and economically mobile. Its concrete, substations, chillers, cooling towers, backup systems, network links, and server halls are attached to a place. The service it produces can cross a continent in milliseconds. The customer may have enough cash to finance energy projects that utilities once would have built through the slow accumulation of rate base. Its business can justify a construction schedule measured in months while the transmission line it needs may take years. Its product is computation, which means at least some work can sometimes move through time or across geography in ways a blast furnace cannot. Yet much of that same computation now supports services that customers expect every second of every day.

The result is a new kind of industrial customer.

And industrial customers change grids.

That sentence is the center of this book.

The common story about artificial intelligence and electricity runs in one direction. AI gets bigger. Models require more accelerators. Accelerators consume more electricity. Data centers require more cooling. Utilities face more demand. The grid strains.

There is truth in that story, but it leaves the grid passive. It imagines an old electrical system receiving a new technological burden.

The more interesting story runs both ways.

Data-center demand is already changing the rules for connecting large loads. It is changing how utilities forecast demand. It is changing how regulators think about minimum bills, contract terms, collateral, and the possibility that ordinary customers could be left paying for infrastructure built for a campus that never arrives. It is reopening questions about nuclear plants that had been closed for economic reasons. It is strengthening the commercial case for new gas generation in places where power is needed faster than other resources can be built. It is making batteries valuable not only as grid storage but as pieces of a reliability stack inside the building. It is reviving arguments about private wires, co-location, backup service, and what a customer should pay when it sits beside a generator but still depends on the wider network.

It is also changing the map.

Northern Virginia became one of the great data-center clusters because fiber, customers, land, tax policy, and electricity created reinforcing advantages. Once the cluster became large enough, those advantages began encountering their own physical limits. Transmission corridors mattered. Substation sites mattered. Generator retirements elsewhere in the regional grid mattered. The electrical appetite of buildings in Loudoun and neighboring counties began affecting planning decisions far beyond the property line.

Texas is confronting a different version. ERCOT has had to rethink how it studies giant new customers because the flood of proposed loads became too large to treat as a sequence of isolated requests. In 2026, Texas approved a batch process for qualifying loads of seventy-five megawatts and above, studying them together so planners could see the system rather than only the next applicant.

Virginia regulators have taken another route. They created a separate large-load rate class for customers such as hyperscale data centers and added long contract obligations, minimum charges, and collateral provisions intended to reduce the chance that households and ordinary businesses inherit the cost of a failed forecast.

Federal regulators have been pulled in too. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission spent 2025 and 2026 wrestling with co-located loads in PJM and then with large-load interconnection rules across the regional transmission organizations under its jurisdiction. Questions that once sounded specialized—what transmission service is a co-located customer actually taking, which upgrades does it cause, how should flexible service be priced, who pays when a giant load disappears—became national questions because the size of the customer changed the stakes.

This is not a book about whether AI is worth the electricity.

That question is too large, too moralized, and too dependent on what artificial intelligence becomes. A medical discovery and a synthetic advertisement can run on similar hardware. A useful coding agent and an infinite stream of generated junk can draw from the same power system. Electricity does not know whether the computation using it is wise.

The grid has a narrower job.

It has to serve demand reliably enough, quickly enough, and at a price society can live with.

That turns the AI electricity question into an infrastructure question.

How should a grid respond when a customer says it needs five hundred megawatts in three years?

Should a utility build generation before the customer is certain to arrive?

Should the customer pay for transmission that may later benefit everyone?

Should a data center receive cheaper or faster service if it agrees to curtail during system emergencies?

If a company funds the restart of a nuclear plant, is it merely buying electricity, or is it changing the generation fleet available to the regional grid?

If a campus places turbines behind the meter, has it solved its grid problem or only rearranged the point where reliability risk enters?

If computational workloads can move from Virginia to Texas for several hours, is that a demand-response resource or a product-design feature that utilities should never assume will be available?

If an announced campus disappears after a substation and transmission line are under construction, who owns the mistake?

These are the questions hidden inside the phrase power-hungry data center.

They are not engineering questions alone.

They are questions about contracts, institutions, timing, and who carries risk.

Electric grids are full of physical laws and human bargains. Voltage obeys physics. Cost allocation does not. Frequency can be measured. Fairness cannot. A transmission line either has thermal capacity or it does not, but deciding which customers should pay for that line depends on a theory of causation, benefit, obligation, and time.

The data-center boom is arriving precisely where those theories are already under pressure.

For roughly two decades, American electricity demand grew slowly. Utilities became accustomed to an era in which efficiency gains, structural economic change, and modest population growth made national demand comparatively flat. At the same time, the generation fleet was changing. Coal plants retired. Wind and solar expanded. Gas became central. Nuclear plants that could operate reliably sometimes struggled economically in competitive markets. Generator interconnection queues filled with projects that took longer to study and build. Transmission planning became a national argument because the system needed more wires even before the data-center wave fully appeared.

Then load growth returned.

The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated that U.S. data centers used about 176 terawatt-hours in 2023. Its 2024 report placed data centers at about 4.4 percent of national electricity consumption that year and offered a wide range for 2028. A 2026 update pushed the horizon to 2030 and produced a midpoint estimate near twelve percent of U.S. electricity, with a range wide enough to remind any honest planner that the future is not a meter reading.

Those numbers are large.

The uncertainty is just as important.

A forecast can create infrastructure before the load exists.

That is one of the peculiar powers of demand.

If a utility believes a new region of data centers will require several gigawatts, it cannot wait until the servers switch on to begin planning. Transmission takes time. Turbines take time. Transformers take time. Nuclear takes more time. Even projects with short construction schedules can sit behind interconnection studies, permitting, equipment orders, fuel arrangements, financing, and local opposition.

The grid therefore has to act on claims about the future.

Data-center developers understand this. So do utilities. A company that wants optionality may ask about power in several places. A utility that wants to be ready may include projects in its load forecast before every commercial decision is final. A grid operator that over-discounts proposed loads may be caught short if they arrive. One that accepts every request at face value may order infrastructure for a boom that exists partly on spreadsheets.

This is not fraud. It is planning under competitive uncertainty.

But when the numbers are large enough, uncertainty becomes expensive.

The American grid is learning to make customers prove that they mean it.

Longer contracts. Minimum bills. Deposits. Collateral. Milestones. Site control. Construction commitments. Financial security. More rigorous forecasting. Batch studies. Special tariffs. Interruptible-service options. These mechanisms may sound like utility bureaucracy. They are actually attempts to answer one question: how much of the future should the rest of the system build on your promise?

The data center is forcing that question because it can be both enormous and uncertain.

It may also force a second question that is more hopeful.

What if a customer with enormous demand is not merely a burden on the grid but a financing event for the grid?

An industrial customer that can sign a long contract changes the economics of generation. A customer willing to pay for a new substation changes the timing of local infrastructure. A customer that can curtail under defined conditions may reduce the amount of firm capacity needed to serve it. A company willing to back a nuclear restart, geothermal project, storage fleet, or new transmission arrangement can help turn a project from theoretical to financeable.

This is where the title comes from.

America is not merely plugging data centers into the grid.

The data centers are amping the grid.

They are increasing the load, certainly. They are also increasing the pressure on every institution that decides how electrical infrastructure is built. Pressure can break a system. It can also force a system to change.

The outcome is not predetermined.

A badly designed bargain could leave households paying for private ambition, communities trading scarce land and water for surprisingly few permanent jobs, and utilities building gas plants or transmission projects that outlive the demand that justified them. A boom in artificial intelligence could become a boom in stranded electrical assets.

A better bargain could do something else.

It could make the largest new loads carry more of their own forecasting risk. It could create service tiers that reward genuine flexibility. It could turn long-term corporate demand into bankable support for generation. It could accelerate transmission needed by more than one customer. It could give grid planners better information about what is actually coming. It could use the extraordinary urgency of the compute industry to repair electrical bottlenecks that the rest of the economy had tolerated for years.

The difference will not come from slogans about innovation or ratepayers.

It will come from contract design and physical reality.

That is why this book spends so much time in places that do not look glamorous: commission orders, load forecasts, interconnection studies, substations, capacity markets, transmission plans, backup systems, transformer yards, gas pipelines, nuclear licenses, and utility tariffs.

The future of artificial intelligence may be written in software.

The future of artificial intelligence in America is also being negotiated in megawatts.

And once those megawatts are committed, the wires remember.
