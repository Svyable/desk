# The Queue on the Other Side

For years, the American electricity industry worried about the queue on the supply side.

Developers wanted to build solar farms, wind projects, gas plants, batteries, and other generation. They applied to connect to the transmission system. Grid operators studied what would happen if the projects came online. A study might conclude that the proposed generator required transmission upgrades before it could connect reliably. The developer then had to decide whether the project still worked economically.

As applications multiplied, the queue became a problem of its own.

There were more proposed projects than the study process had been designed to handle. Many projects would never be built. Yet every speculative request could consume engineering time and alter the assumptions used to study the projects behind it. Developers sometimes entered queues early to preserve an option. Grid operators struggled to distinguish the serious from the provisional until costly work had already been done.

By the mid-2020s, the lesson was familiar.

A queue full of projects is not the same thing as a fleet of power plants.

Then the same problem appeared on the other side of the meter.

Data centers, factories, cryptocurrency facilities, hydrogen projects, and other large customers began asking for quantities of electricity big enough to require transmission-level study. The old assumption that load could be forecast statistically at the utility level was no longer enough. A single project could alter a zone's needs. Dozens of proposed projects could distort the forecast if treated as certain.

The grid had acquired a load queue.

The phrase sounds bureaucratic.

The change is fundamental.

Electric systems historically treated generators and customers differently because the two sides played different roles. A generator seeking interconnection was a discrete project whose power injections had to be studied. Load grew more diffusely. Utilities forecast customer demand, and regional planners used those forecasts to ensure enough generation and transmission would exist.

A hyperscale data center breaks the scale assumption behind that division.

It is still a customer.

But electrically, it can arrive with the consequence of a power plant.

A five-hundred-megawatt generator can change power flows when it injects electricity into the network. A five-hundred-megawatt data center can change power flows when it withdraws electricity. In both cases, the network has to be studied under normal conditions and contingencies. The direction of the energy is opposite. The planning burden can be comparable.

This is why FERC's large-load proceedings matter beyond the narrow world of transmission tariffs.

In June 2026, the commission opened targeted proceedings involving the six regional transmission organizations and independent system operators under its jurisdiction, asking them to justify how their tariffs address the interconnection of large loads. The move followed a year of argument over data-center co-location in PJM and reflected a broader realization: the country had no uniformly adequate framework for integrating enormous new customers into a transmission system already struggling with supply-side interconnection.

The threshold FERC used in the related rulemaking discussion was not one megawatt or even ten.

It was generally twenty megawatts and above.

That sounds small beside the biggest hyperscale proposals, and that is precisely the point. The regulatory problem begins well below the scale of the most dramatic campuses. A twenty-megawatt customer can already be consequential in the wrong place. A cluster of them can become a transmission issue quickly.

The old customer-generator distinction remains legally important.

The planning distinction is getting thinner.

Consider what happens when a developer asks a utility for four hundred megawatts.

The utility first has to know where the load will connect. That identifies the relevant substation and transmission facilities. Engineers then consider whether existing equipment can carry the additional demand under expected operating conditions. They consider contingencies: what if a line trips, a transformer fails, or generation elsewhere is unavailable? If the load causes a reliability violation, the system needs an upgrade or an operating restriction.

That is only the technical study.

The commercial study is harder.

Is four hundred megawatts the load on the first day of operation?

The fifth year?

The maximum eventual buildout?

Will the customer use three hundred most of the time and reserve the rest for future expansion?

Will it accept curtailment?

Does it have a signed tenant?

Has it acquired the land?

Has it ordered equipment?

Is it asking another utility for the same four hundred megawatts because management has not chosen the final region?

Every answer changes the meaning of the request.

The physics cannot tell whether a project is commercially serious.

The interconnection process therefore has to become partly a credit process.

This is an unusual institutional shift.

Grid engineers are trained to ask what happens if the load exists.

The boom requires the system to ask how likely the load is to exist.

That probability is not a power-flow variable. It comes from contracts, deposits, site control, financing, customer identity, construction milestones, and developer behavior.

The electric grid is being forced to learn commercial due diligence.

Texas made the problem visible at extraordinary scale.

By late 2025, ERCOT reported a large-load request pipeline measured in hundreds of gigawatts, with data centers accounting for most of the requested capacity. The headline total was larger than the system could plausibly energize on the implied schedule.

That did not mean the requests were fake.

It meant the queue was describing option value as much as future load.

A developer who thinks power will be scarce has an incentive to enter the process early. A company considering several Texas sites may want to understand each one's electrical feasibility before making a final decision. A speculative land developer may see an approved power path as something that makes the property more valuable to a future data-center tenant. A phased campus may request the power needed for full buildout even though the later phases depend on demand that cannot yet be known.

These are rational behaviors at the project level.

Collectively, they can destroy the informational value of the queue.

If every developer reserves capacity cheaply, nobody knows how much capacity is actually reserved.

The problem resembles airline passengers holding refundable bookings on several flights, except the airline would have to build a new runway because enough of them clicked reserve.

The grid needs a cancellation penalty before the runway order is placed.

ERCOT's response was to change the study process.

In 2026, Texas regulators approved a first batch process for qualifying large loads of seventy-five megawatts or more. Rather than study each giant request as though it arrived in isolation, ERCOT could examine a group together and model their combined effect on the system.

The batch matters because network effects are nonlinear.

Two nearby loads may create a constraint neither creates alone. Several projects can shift the limiting contingency from one transmission path to another. An upgrade that solves the first project may be inadequate if three more arrive in the same region. Studying projects one at a time can produce a sequence of answers that is technically correct for each assumption set and collectively wrong for the system that eventually emerges.

Batching lets the planner see the cluster before it is built.

That is not the same as solving the forecast problem.

A batch full of uncertain projects remains uncertain.

The process therefore depends on qualification. Texas can ask which projects are mature enough to enter the batch. Financial commitments and project milestones can separate serious requests from early options. The details will evolve as regulators learn what developers do in response.

Every rule creates a new strategy.

A deposit large enough to deter speculation may also disadvantage smaller developers. A strict site-control requirement may encourage premature land acquisition. A high bar for queue entry may reduce clutter but cause developers to delay seeking studies until projects are more advanced, compressing the remaining development schedule.

There is no frictionless mechanism.

The goal is not to make the queue small.

It is to make the queue informative.

That distinction should guide national reform.

Supply-side interconnection queues taught a painful lesson about first-come, first-served planning. A project could enter early, wait years, trigger costly studies, then withdraw. The withdrawal could change the network assumptions for everyone behind it, forcing restudies. Regional operators have moved toward cluster studies, stronger financial readiness requirements, and other reforms precisely because speculative optionality created system costs.

Large-load interconnection is young enough to learn faster.

The symmetry is almost perfect.

A speculative generator can reserve engineering attention and apparent transmission opportunity on the supply side.

A speculative data center can reserve apparent demand and infrastructure obligation on the load side.

Both can cause planners to model a future that may not exist.

Both can delay serious projects.

Both can create cascading restudies when they withdraw.

Both can impose costs on parties that made firmer commitments.

The cure begins with the same principle: options should have prices.

If a developer wants the grid to treat a project as real, the developer should eventually put real resources behind the claim.

That can take several forms.

A deposit can finance study work and create a cost to withdrawal.

Collateral can protect the utility if a project cancels after infrastructure is committed.

Minimum billing can ensure a completed facility pays something close to the capacity reserved for it even if utilization is lower than forecast.

Construction milestones can release capacity if the developer stops progressing.

Phased commitments can match infrastructure to a realistic ramp rather than the ultimate master plan.

None of these mechanisms proves demand.

They improve the signal.

This is a deeper role for utility tariffs than most people associate with electricity pricing.

A price can ration scarce capacity.

A contract can reveal information.

A queue rule can change behavior before any power is delivered.

The data-center boom is turning market design into grid planning.

The federal role is complicated because retail electric service is still heavily shaped by state regulation, while FERC has jurisdiction over interstate transmission and wholesale power in much of the country. A large load can therefore sit at the boundary of jurisdictions.

A state-regulated utility may sign the retail customer.

The regional transmission organization may have to study the transmission impact.

FERC may govern the tariff under which transmission service or upgrades are provided.

A state commission may decide whether the utility can recover costs from other customers.

The customer sees one project.

The law sees layers.

Co-located data centers made the boundary especially hard to ignore.

If a facility sits next to a power plant, perhaps it appears to avoid the transmission system. But if the generator and load remain synchronized with the grid, the surrounding network can still matter during outages, maintenance, startup, excess generation, or other conditions. The customer may reduce net withdrawals while still relying on transmission as insurance.

That creates a queue question with a philosophical edge.

What does it mean to be connected?

The old answer was physical.

The emerging answer is service-based.

A customer should pay for the grid services it actually uses or causes the system to reserve, whether the average meter reading is large or small. That sounds straightforward until planners try to quantify backup reliance, contingency effects, and future operational behavior.

The large-load queue is therefore not merely a list of connection requests.

It is the place where the electric system is being forced to define the product it sells.

For a household, the product is simple enough to hide. The customer pays for electricity and perhaps a few standardized charges. Reliability is bundled into service.

For a hyperscale load, the bundle can be unbundled conceptually.

How much firm capacity?

How much transmission reservation?

What curtailment rights?

What backup service?

What ramp schedule?

What power-quality requirements?

What restoration priority?

What notice before interruption?

What facilities are dedicated?

What facilities are shared?

The answers can become contract terms.

This is how a grid becomes more sophisticated under pressure.

The old system did not need to price every dimension separately for most customers. Standard service was easier. But once one customer can alter the regional buildout, bundling everything into one ordinary rate can hide risks large enough to matter.

The temptation is to solve the problem by giving data centers their own private electricity systems.

Bring your own power. Build behind the meter. Co-locate with generation. Use microgrids. Install batteries. Reduce the public obligation.

These strategies can be useful.

They do not eliminate the queue so much as change the question asked inside it.

A campus with onsite generation may still want a transmission connection for backup. A customer that can self-supply during emergencies may deserve a different service class. A data center willing to disconnect under system stress can be studied as a conditional load rather than a fully firm one. A behind-the-meter generator that cannot export has different impacts from one that participates in the market.

The future queue may therefore contain service types, not merely megawatts.

That would be a meaningful innovation.

Today, a large-load request often starts with a number: five hundred megawatts.

The better question is five hundred megawatts of what obligation?

Firm at every hour?

Interruptible under rare emergencies?

Ramped over seven years?

Supported by onsite batteries?

Partly self-supplied?

Geographically movable?

Able to pause training workloads but not inference?

The same nominal size can create different planning needs.

The data center's software nature makes those distinctions technically plausible.

The grid has to make them contractually dependable.

That is harder than it sounds because the customer itself may not know its future operating profile. AI workloads are evolving. Hardware efficiency changes. A campus designed today may host workloads that do not yet exist. Operators may promise flexibility based on batch training and later discover that inference demand dominates. Commercial teams may be more willing to curtail than engineering teams. Reliability managers may refuse interruptions that looked harmless in a planning model.

A good tariff has to survive internal corporate disagreement years after it is signed.

The grid cannot rely on goodwill.

This is why telemetry and testing will matter.

If a customer receives credit for being flexible, the system should be able to observe whether it responds when called. If a project receives faster connection because it accepts curtailment, the consequences of nonperformance should be clear. If a load is assumed to ramp gradually, the utility needs notice before acceleration.

The queue becomes an operating relationship.

That is a significant departure from treating interconnection as a one-time engineering gate.

The relationship will probably become more dynamic because data centers themselves are dynamic. A facility can add halls in phases. Hardware can be replaced on shorter cycles than electrical infrastructure. Workloads can shift. Power density can rise. A campus that began as one hundred megawatts can ask for three hundred later without moving an inch.

Connection is not an event.

It is a trajectory.

The planning process needs to see the trajectory early enough that the network can keep up without reserving the entire imagined future on day one.

This is where the best lesson from supply-side queues may be the least glamorous one.

Milestones matter.

A project should earn its place in the future by becoming progressively more real.

Land acquired.

Permits advanced.

Equipment ordered.

Contracts signed.

Construction started.

Financial security posted.

Load energized.

Each milestone can justify a stronger system commitment.

This is infrastructure planning as staged belief.

The idea is almost embarrassingly simple. It is also exactly what a boom needs.

Booms create more claims on the future than the future can fulfill.

Queues are where those claims become visible.

America spent a decade learning that lesson from generators waiting to connect.

Now the customers are lining up too.

The grid's next challenge is not deciding which side deserves priority.

It is building a process in which neither side can turn cheap paperwork into an expensive obligation for everyone behind it.

There is another reason the load queue matters: it changes the value of **truthful withdrawal**.

In a poorly designed queue, a developer has an incentive to remain until the last possible moment. Leaving early can mean surrendering a scarce position. The project may keep an option alive even after its probability of construction has fallen, because the private cost of waiting is small while the private value of preserving the slot is large. From the grid's perspective, that stale project contaminates the forecast and the studies behind it.

A better process rewards early truth.

That can sound backwards. Why reward a company for canceling? Because the grid is not trying to maximize the number of names in a queue. It is trying to maximize the accuracy of the future it is preparing to serve. A clean withdrawal before major equipment is ordered can save more money than a punitive fee collected after an unnecessary substation is built.

This suggests that readiness requirements should be paired with **off-ramps**. A developer that learns its project no longer works should be able to surrender capacity cleanly, with consequences proportional to costs already caused rather than penalties so severe that the rational move is to hide bad news. The system wants commitment from viable projects and candor from failing ones.

That is a subtle design problem because the queue also creates strategic behavior among competitors. A developer may care where rival projects sit. A landowner may value a site's apparent access to power. A customer may fear that releasing capacity means it cannot return later. Transparency that improves system planning can expose commercially sensitive information.

The answer cannot be to make the process opaque again. It is to define the information the system actually needs. Identity of the accountable customer. Requested ramp. Financial readiness. Site control. Milestones. Flexibility commitments. Withdrawal status. Enough to plan the network without turning the queue into a public map of every corporate strategy.

This is where load integration begins to resemble capital-market infrastructure. The valuable output is not merely a ranked list. It is a continuously updated estimate of which claims on scarce capacity deserve to influence real investment.

That estimate will never be perfect.

It can be much better than a pile of forms.

If the data-center boom leaves behind a demand-side interconnection process that prices optionality, rewards truthful updates, studies clusters rather than fantasies of isolation, and makes service obligations explicit, the reform will outlast the current generation of AI hardware.

Hydrogen plants, semiconductor fabs, advanced manufacturing, electric fleets, carbon-removal facilities, and industrial electrification will all benefit from a grid that knows how to ask a giant new customer a question more precise than: how many megawatts do you want?

The durable question is:

How much of the future are you prepared to stand behind?
