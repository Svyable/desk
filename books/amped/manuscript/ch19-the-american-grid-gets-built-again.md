# The American Grid Gets Built Again

A transmission line is easiest to appreciate after it exists.

Before construction, it is a route through somebody's land.

A permit.

A hearing.

A cost-allocation dispute.

A queue of objections.

A steel order.

A transformer schedule.

A decade of forecasts arguing about a need that has not happened yet.

After construction, the line becomes background.

Power moves.

Prices change.

A generator hundreds of miles away becomes useful to a city that barely knows the generator exists. A factory connects. A power plant retires without causing the same reliability problem it would have caused before. A wind farm, gas plant, nuclear unit, battery, hydro system, or solar field gains access to more customers. One region can help another during stress.

The line disappears into the system because successful infrastructure becomes ordinary.

The data-center boom is making the missing lines visible again.

For roughly two decades, many parts of the United States became accustomed to slow electricity-demand growth. Energy efficiency improved. Heavy industry changed. Population moved unevenly. The grid still needed investment, but planners were not universally preparing for a sudden wave of enormous new industrial loads.

Then the forecasts changed.

Data centers arrived alongside new manufacturing, building electrification, electric vehicles in some regions, and other large loads. The Department of Energy's 2026 draft National Transmission Needs Study described a country facing substantial present and future transmission constraints under a new era of demand growth. FERC's long-term transmission rule, Order No. 1920 and its rehearing orders, had already required regional planners to look farther ahead, evaluate multiple scenarios over a long horizon, and consider transmission needs that ordinary near-term planning could miss.

The timing is important.

The country did not invent long-term transmission planning because of AI.

AI made the consequences of not doing it harder to ignore.

A grid designed around incremental demand growth can respond badly to lumpy demand.

A ten-megawatt load increase can often be absorbed.

A five-hundred-megawatt campus can change the network.

Several campuses arriving in one corridor can force a new transmission architecture.

The same is true on the supply side. A new generator hundreds of miles from load may be inexpensive to build but economically useless if transmission cannot deliver its output. A region can appear short of generation while another has power trapped behind congestion.

Transmission converts local abundance into regional capacity.

This is why the grid buildout caused by data centers may outlast the data centers themselves.

A dedicated substation can be stranded.

A well-planned regional line usually has more options.

It can serve future customers.

It can reduce congestion.

It can increase access to a larger generation fleet.

It can support reliability during outages.

It can make new generation financeable in places where the resource is good but the grid is weak.

It can permit old equipment to be replaced at higher capacity.

The durable infrastructure thesis of the AI boom therefore depends on what gets built.

Not all megawatts are equal.

Not all wires are equal either.

A country can spend an enormous amount on grid infrastructure and still end up with an inefficient system if each project is built narrowly around the last emergency.

The opportunity is to use the demand shock as a reason to build a more capable network rather than merely a larger collection of radial extensions to private campuses.

This sounds easy in a book.

It is difficult in a tariff.

Utilities recover costs from customers under rules that require some connection between spending and benefit. A regional line may create diffuse benefits across states, utilities, generators, and future customers. The customer that needs the line first may not be the customer that benefits most over forty years. States can disagree about whether their residents should pay for a project that helps another state meet policy goals. Existing utilities can prefer local projects over regional alternatives. Generators and customers can argue over whose behavior created the need.

Transmission is a public-goods problem wearing private ownership.

FERC's Order No. 1920 was an attempt to improve the planning side of that problem. It required transmission providers subject to the rule to conduct long-term regional planning over a twenty-year horizon, consider multiple plausible future scenarios, evaluate a broader set of benefits, and establish processes for cost allocation with an expanded role for states.

The rule did not magically produce lines.

Planning is not permitting.

Cost allocation is not siting.

A scenario is not a steel tower.

But institutional sequence matters.

The first step toward building a line before a crisis is admitting that future needs belong in present planning.

Data centers strengthen the case because their growth is both large and uncertain.

A planner cannot simply extrapolate yesterday's load.

The region needs scenarios.

High data-center growth.

Lower data-center growth.

More manufacturing.

Different generator retirements.

Different fuel prices.

Different weather extremes.

Different technology costs.

The right transmission portfolio should perform reasonably across several of them.

This is where the word robust belongs.

A robust grid plan is not the plan optimized for one perfect forecast.

It is the plan that remains useful when the forecast is wrong in ordinary ways.

That is especially important in the AI era because the range of plausible load outcomes is wide.

If artificial-intelligence demand grows as quickly as the most aggressive projections imply, the system needs a great deal more infrastructure.

If efficiency improves rapidly, projects are cancelled, or computing economics change, some forecast load will disappear.

A line that connects regions, unlocks generation, lowers congestion, and improves resilience may still earn its keep.

A line whose only purpose was to serve one speculative campus may not.

This is not an argument to reject customer-specific infrastructure.

Some facilities require it.

It is an argument to distinguish private extensions from public network expansion and finance them accordingly.

The data-center customer should generally bear the risk of assets useful mainly to that customer.

The broader rate base can reasonably support infrastructure with demonstrated broader benefits.

That sounds like common sense.

The difficult work is demonstrating the broader benefit before the future arrives.

This is why modeling matters.

Transmission planners simulate contingencies, power flows, congestion, generation changes, and load growth. The models are imperfect. They are still better than pretending that a line's value is only the first project that triggers it.

A new interstate highway is not evaluated only by the first warehouse built beside the exit.

A transmission network should not be either.

The network quality of the grid is one of America's underappreciated industrial advantages.

A generator does not need to sit beside every customer.

A factory does not need to own its own power plant.

A city can survive the outage of one unit because a larger system shares resources.

A region can import during local scarcity.

These benefits arise from interconnection.

Data-center proposals to build private generation or co-locate behind existing plants are rational responses to grid scarcity.

They also remind the country what is lost if the public network becomes too weak.

A world in which every giant customer builds its own electrical island can be fast for the customer and inefficient for the society.

Generation is duplicated.

Backup is duplicated.

Fuel infrastructure is duplicated.

Diversity benefits shrink.

Smaller customers cannot bargain for the same arrangements.

The grid becomes a residual service for everyone without the capital to leave.

That is not an attractive end state.

The alternative is a public network strong enough that private self-supply remains an option rather than a necessity.

This is why the AI load shock could produce a grid renaissance.

The demand is creating customers willing to pay for speed, reliability, and scale.

Those customers can support investment that was harder to justify during years of flat demand. Utilities can spread fixed costs over more electricity sales. New generation can find buyers. Transmission upgrades can become economic sooner. Suppliers can expand transformer and switchgear production. Workers can justify training for a market with a longer project pipeline.

Growth can make infrastructure easier to finance.

It can also overwhelm the supply chain.

The American grid is not built from abstract capital.

It needs physical components.

Large power transformers.

Distribution transformers.

Circuit breakers.

Steel structures.

Conductors.

Insulators.

Protection systems.

Power electronics.

Specialized vehicles.

Engineers.

Lineworkers.

Construction crews.

Manufacturing slots.

A demand boom reaches each of these markets.

Lead times become part of reliability.

A utility can receive regulatory approval and still wait for equipment.

This is why the grid buildout is also a manufacturing story.

DOE's electricity programs have increasingly emphasized domestic supply chains for critical grid components because dependence on limited suppliers can turn infrastructure plans into paper. A transformer shortage is not only a utility procurement problem if every data center, subdivision, factory, and storm recovery project needs the same equipment.

The constraint becomes national.

This gives the data-center boom a second-order benefit if policy handles it well.

Steady demand for grid equipment can support expansion of domestic manufacturing capacity that later improves resilience across the system.

The first data-center substation can help pay for the factory that builds transformers for the next hurricane recovery.

That spillover is real only if capacity actually expands.

Scarcity without investment simply produces higher prices and longer waits.

The same is true of skilled labor.

A boom can create a durable workforce or a bidding war over a fixed one.

Training is the difference.

Utilities, contractors, unions, technical colleges, equipment manufacturers, and technology companies all have incentives to expand the pipeline of electrical workers if the demand outlook is credible. The work is not glamorous in the way artificial intelligence is glamorous.

It is more durable.

The grid cannot be automated into existence.

Another source of capacity is hiding inside the existing network.

New transmission does not always require a new corridor.

Existing lines can sometimes be reconductored with higher-capacity conductors. Grid-enhancing technologies can improve awareness of actual thermal limits, reroute flows, or increase utilization of existing rights of way. Old equipment scheduled for replacement can sometimes be right-sized so the replacement carries more power. Substation upgrades can relieve bottlenecks that appear much larger when described only as regional megawatts.

These options matter because siting is often harder than engineering.

The cheapest new corridor on paper can be the slowest project in reality.

An upgrade using an existing corridor may cost more per mile and still deliver value sooner.

Speed to power therefore should not mean only speed to generation.

It should mean speed to usable network capacity.

The data-center customer is unusually motivated to pay for that distinction.

A technology company may happily fund an advanced conductor or substation upgrade if it accelerates energization by two years. The utility gains infrastructure. The broader system may gain headroom. The customer gains time.

This is the kind of bargain that can make the boom additive rather than extractive.

The public should receive an asset with continuing value.

The customer should receive a service worth paying for.

There is a limit to how much can be solved through optimization of old wires.

Eventually, a growing electricity system needs more corridors.

This is where America's infrastructure process becomes slowest.

A major transmission line can cross multiple states, federal lands, private properties, tribal interests, environmental jurisdictions, utility territories, and political communities. The benefits may be regional while the visible burden is local. A landowner sees the tower. A distant customer sees a lower electricity price.

That mismatch has delayed infrastructure for generations.

Data centers do not repeal it.

They can change the political coalition.

A transmission project once described mainly as enabling distant generation can now also be described as enabling domestic manufacturing, AI investment, tax base, reliability, and general economic growth. Supporters from technology and industrial development can join utilities and power producers.

The coalition becomes broader.

Opposition can broaden too.

Residents may reasonably ask why their land should host a corridor built partly to serve server campuses in another county. States may object to paying for projects driven by other states' development choices. Consumer advocates may resist socializing infrastructure for companies with enormous balance sheets.

The answer cannot be that growth is good.

The answer has to be a credible benefit-sharing mechanism.

If the line benefits a wide region, demonstrate the benefits.

If one customer drives most of the need, require that customer to contribute accordingly.

If host communities bear unusual burdens, compensate or mitigate them transparently where law permits.

If the project creates optionality for future development, include that value in planning rather than pretending every future user is unknowable.

Infrastructure legitimacy is built from distribution as much as engineering.

This is why the cancellation of three proposed federal transmission-corridor designations in 2026 should not be read as evidence that the need for transmission disappeared. DOE released a draft national study describing significant need and later chose not to advance those particular corridor designations after public feedback, changed program conditions, and other considerations.

Need and route are different questions.

The country can need more grid and reject a given tool, map, or process.

A mature buildout has to survive that distinction.

The fastest infrastructure plan is useless if it collapses politically.

The most consensual plan is useless if it arrives after the shortage.

The art is building consent before emergency removes the choices.

Data-center demand can help because the need is becoming tangible earlier.

A county can see the campus proposal.

A utility can see the load letter.

A grid operator can see the queue.

A regulator can see the forecast.

This is better than discovering scarcity only when prices spike or reliability margins disappear.

The boom is an early warning.

The country can use it to build ahead.

That is the optimistic case for Amped.

Artificial intelligence becomes the customer that wakes up a stagnant infrastructure system.

The grid expands.

Transmission planning improves.

Large-load tariffs become more precise.

Flexible demand becomes a real resource.

Transformer manufacturing grows.

Nuclear plants gain new commercial pathways.

Batteries scale.

New gas generation provides near-term firmness where needed.

Renewables gain transmission access.

Regions with spare power attract industry.

Old industrial sites acquire new value because they already possess grid connections.

The public network gets stronger because private demand finally makes expansion urgent.

The pessimistic case is just as plausible.

The country builds bespoke assets for forecasts that do not materialize.

Existing customers inherit costs.

Transmission remains too slow, so companies construct isolated power systems.

Gas and generator orders crowd out other needs.

Communities rebel against projects after incentives are promised.

Utilities reserve capacity for duplicate requests.

The grid becomes more expensive without becoming much more capable.

The difference is planning quality.

The boom itself does not guarantee renewal.

It creates the financing and political conditions in which renewal becomes possible.

The country still has to choose the right assets.

This is why the question at the end of every major data-center infrastructure decision should be broader than the customer.

What will this investment be good for if the customer changes?

A substation can serve another load.

A regional line can serve another generator.

A battery can respond to another peak.

A trained workforce can build another project.

A transformer factory can supply another utility.

A better forecasting process can govern another industrial boom.

Those are durable returns.

The servers inside today's campus will eventually be obsolete.

The best grid built around them should not be.

That is the standard.

If America spends the 2020s and 2030s rebuilding its electricity system because machines became power hungry, the achievement will not be that every forecast came true.

It will be that the physical network became useful for futures nobody forecast correctly.

Good infrastructure does that.

It outlives the story that paid for it.
