# The Grid Becomes Industrial Policy

For most of the internet era, the United States could pretend that digital policy and energy policy were separate subjects.

The pretense is ending.

A government can subsidize semiconductor fabrication, restrict exports of advanced chips, fund artificial-intelligence research, encourage domestic cloud infrastructure, and declare leadership in computation a national priority.

Then the servers ask for electricity.

At that moment the policy leaves the press release and enters the power system.

The grid becomes industrial policy whether anyone uses the phrase or not.

This is not because electricity regulators suddenly want to choose technology winners.

It is because access to power has become one of the inputs determining which industries can scale, where they can locate, and how quickly they can deploy.

A company that cannot secure processors has a compute problem.

A company that owns the processors but cannot energize the building also has a compute problem.

The bottleneck has moved from silicon into infrastructure.

That movement changes government.

In 2025 and 2026, federal energy institutions began treating large loads as a national grid category rather than a collection of unusual utility customers. FERC opened proceedings around co-located data centers in PJM. The Department of Energy directed the Commission to consider broader reforms for connecting large loads to interstate transmission. In June 2026, FERC issued separate show-cause orders to the six regional grid operators under its jurisdiction, requiring them to justify or reform rules governing how major new loads obtain and pay for transmission service.

The language around those actions was revealing.

The problem was framed simultaneously as reliability, affordability, manufacturing, national security, and competition in artificial intelligence.

Those objectives do not always point in the same direction.

That is why the grid is becoming a difficult place to conduct industrial policy.

Speed favors the new customer.

Cost protection favors the existing customer.

Reliability favors conservative planning.

Competition favors open access.

National-security arguments can favor domestic capacity even when it is expensive.

Local politics can resist the transmission needed to achieve national objectives.

Environmental policy can favor some generation paths over others.

A regulator cannot maximize all of these at once.

The old utility bargain was easier because the objective looked narrower.

Provide reliable electricity at just and reasonable rates.

Even that mission has always required judgment, but the customer mix changed relatively slowly. A regulator could evaluate a utility investment around forecasts of household growth, commercial demand, industrial development, retirement of old equipment, and reliability standards.

The data-center boom compresses the political calendar.

A hyperscaler can announce a multibillion-dollar campus and ask for power on a schedule shorter than the time required to build major transmission. A governor wants the project. The utility wants the load. The county wants the tax base. The federal government may view the compute as strategically important. Existing ratepayers do not want their bills to become an industrial subsidy.

The grid operator receives all those preferences as one technical request.

Can the system serve the load?

This is why the phrase speed to power has become so important.

It sounds like a construction objective.

It is actually an allocation objective.

If power is scarce in the near term, getting one project connected faster can mean delaying another, accepting more risk, using an expensive temporary resource, reserving transmission headroom, or building generation that would not otherwise be chosen.

Speed has a cost.

Industrial policy decides whether the cost is worth paying and who should pay it.

The United States has conducted energy-intensive industrial policy before.

Hydroelectric dams supported aluminum production and regional development.

The Tennessee Valley Authority linked electricity, flood control, fertilizer, navigation, and economic modernization. Federal power marketing administrations shaped industrial geography across the West. Military facilities and national laboratories received infrastructure because the government considered the mission important enough to justify it.

Cheap power has always been an industrial location policy.

Data centers make the relationship visible again after decades in which electricity demand growth was slow enough to fade into the background.

The new version is less centralized.

No federal planner decides where every AI campus should go.

States compete.

Utilities compete.

Regional grid operators apply different market structures.

Local governments control zoning and tax treatment.

Technology companies compare power prices, fiber, water, land, tax incentives, permitting, weather, latency, and political support.

The result is industrial policy by auction among jurisdictions.

This can be productive.

Competition forces states to identify their comparative advantages. Regions with abundant generation, strong transmission, industrial land, and responsive utilities can attract investment. A distressed area with existing electrical infrastructure may find a new use for assets built around an old factory or power plant. Data-center demand can make generation projects financeable and support new transmission that later serves other users.

It can also be wasteful.

A state can subsidize a project whose electricity needs force expensive public investment. Another state can respond with a larger tax incentive. Utilities can compete for load by offering terms that transfer too much risk to existing customers. Local officials can count the capital announcement while ignoring the regional cost of serving it.

The winner of the site-selection contest can become the loser of the infrastructure bargain.

This is why industrial policy needs a full-cost ledger.

The relevant number is not the dollar value of the data-center campus.

It is the value of the economic activity minus the public costs and risks required to support it.

Some of those costs are obvious.

Tax exemptions.

Road improvements.

Local grants.

Utility extensions.

Some are harder.

Higher wholesale electricity prices.

Transmission projects socialized over a region.

Capacity procurement accelerated by the load forecast.

Land-use conflict around new generation.

Fuel infrastructure.

The opportunity cost of scarce grid capacity that could have served another customer.

These are not arguments against the project.

They are the missing columns in the industrial-policy spreadsheet.

The opportunity-cost column will matter more as electricity becomes scarce.

If a region has enough firm capacity for only one additional five-hundred-megawatt customer before a major upgrade, which customer should receive it?

The grid operator should apply neutral service rules.

The state may care deeply about the answer.

A semiconductor fab can employ thousands and support a strategic supply chain.

A data center can contribute enormous tax base and enable AI services of national importance.

An electrified steel plant can cut fuel dependence and anchor manufacturing.

A hydrogen facility can support chemical or transport markets.

A battery factory can create industrial employment and energy-storage capacity.

Every project can tell a national-interest story.

Electricity scarcity turns those stories into competition.

A pure price mechanism offers one answer.

Let whoever values the electricity most pay the most.

This has strong efficiency logic. A high willingness to pay signals that the electricity creates high private value. Scarcity prices encourage users to locate elsewhere, become flexible, bring generation, or invest in efficiency.

Private willingness to pay is not identical to public value.

A defense facility may have national-security value not captured in its electricity bid. A critical-material plant may support resilience beyond its profits. A data center may create enormous consumer surplus outside the state that hosts it. A factory may have political value because it employs thousands.

Once government adjusts the price to reflect these external benefits, however, the process becomes industrial policy explicitly.

That can be justified.

It should be admitted.

Hidden industrial policy is worse because it makes accountability impossible.

A discounted rate for a favored project is a subsidy if other customers pay the difference.

A tax exemption is a subsidy even when it does not appear on the utility bill.

A federally financed transmission line can be a public investment with broad benefits, but it is still a public allocation of capital.

Transparency does not make the policy bad.

It makes the bargain discussable.

The federal role is especially awkward because electricity remains divided among state, regional, and federal authority.

States regulate retail utilities and much generation investment.

Localities govern land use.

FERC regulates interstate transmission and wholesale electricity in much of the country.

Regional transmission organizations operate markets and planning processes where they exist.

DOE studies needs, funds or finances certain infrastructure, manages federal power assets, and can use statutory tools related to transmission corridors and emergency reliability.

A hyperscale campus can touch all of them.

This fragmentation is often treated as a defect.

It also prevents one national industrial strategy from automatically overriding every local interest.

The tension became visible in transmission policy in 2026. DOE's draft National Transmission Needs Study described substantial need for additional transmission associated with data centers, manufacturing, other large loads, and economic growth. Yet later that summer, the Department decided not to proceed with three proposed National Interest Electric Transmission Corridors that had advanced under an earlier process, citing a combination of public concerns, changed program conditions, lack of broad support, and other factors.

The two actions were not necessarily contradictory.

A government can believe more transmission is needed and reject particular corridor designations.

That distinction is fundamental to infrastructure politics.

Everyone can agree that the grid should expand in the abstract.

The line still has to cross a place.

Industrial policy becomes land policy at the right of way.

This is where technology companies may discover that money cannot compress every timeline.

A hyperscaler can fund a substation quickly.

It cannot unilaterally approve a transmission corridor through several counties.

It can finance a generation project.

It cannot manufacture a federal or state permit out of capital alone.

It can buy transformers early.

It cannot instantly train the crews needed to build thousands of miles of grid infrastructure.

The bottleneck shifts from finance into consent and physical capacity.

That is a mature-infrastructure problem.

The United States is exceptionally good at financing valuable technology once expected returns become large enough.

It is less consistently good at coordinating long-lived infrastructure across jurisdictions.

Data centers expose the difference because the customer is rich enough that capital is not always the limiting factor.

A company can offer to pay.

The system can still be unable to deliver on schedule.

This changes the role of the technology buyer.

The hyperscaler begins acting like an infrastructure developer.

It signs long-term power-purchase agreements.

It supports nuclear restarts or new nuclear development.

It contracts for renewable generation.

It invests in batteries.

It negotiates dedicated substations.

It studies onsite gas or other generation.

It evaluates private-wire and co-location arrangements.

It hires energy traders and regulatory teams.

The company does not become a utility legally.

It acquires utility-like capabilities economically.

This creates a new form of private industrial policy.

The largest technology firms can choose which power technologies receive bankable demand.

A twenty-year contract from a highly rated customer can change the financing prospects of a generation project. A commitment to buy output from an existing nuclear plant can increase the value of that asset. A willingness to pay a premium for firm clean electricity can pull capital toward technologies that struggle in ordinary commodity markets.

The compute buyer starts shaping the generation mix.

This is not philanthropy.

The company wants dependable power, price visibility, emissions performance, and speed.

The public consequence can be large anyway.

Private procurement can accelerate technologies that later serve the broader grid.

It can also divert scarce output from ordinary customers or create bespoke arrangements whose backup costs remain socialized.

The same bargain can contain both outcomes.

Regulators therefore need to distinguish additionality from appropriation.

Did the data-center contract cause a new resource to be built or an old one to remain available?

Or did it merely redirect an existing resource toward one customer while leaving the public system to replace the lost capacity?

The answer determines whether the private deal expands the grid or rearranges claims on it.

This is one reason co-location around existing power plants became so controversial.

The technology company sees direct access to generation.

The generator sees a valuable customer.

The grid sees a resource that may previously have served the wholesale market.

Existing consumers care whether they still receive backup obligations without the same energy supply.

The public policy cannot be resolved by declaring the arrangement private.

Electric reliability follows electrons and contingencies, not labels.

Industrial policy also has a geography problem.

If the federal government wants more domestic AI capacity, it should care whether that capacity clusters in regions already short of transmission or spreads toward places with energy headroom. The market will perform some of this allocation through price and connection timing. State incentives can overpower the signal by making a constrained region artificially attractive.

A smarter policy would align incentives with system capability.

Encourage large loads where generation and transmission can grow efficiently.

Reward flexibility.

Use brownfield industrial sites with existing grid access where practical.

Coordinate tax incentives with utility planning.

Avoid promising subsidies before the electrical path is credible.

Make the customer bear the financial risk of speculative infrastructure reserved on its behalf.

These principles sound technical.

They are industrial strategy.

The country is deciding where the next layer of computation lives.

Power is one of the deciding variables.

That will influence regional wealth.

A place with abundant energy, available land, and fast interconnection can attract data centers and the tax base that comes with them. The new demand can then justify additional generation and transmission, strengthening the region further. Another place with constrained infrastructure can lose projects, which reduces the private incentive to expand the grid.

Success can compound.

So can scarcity.

This feedback loop is familiar from ports, railroads, highways, and broadband.

Infrastructure creates economic geography.

Economic geography creates demand for more infrastructure.

Data centers are reactivating that loop around electricity.

This is why the grid may become one of the most important industrial institutions of the AI era.

Not because a transmission operator understands machine learning.

Because the operator controls access to a scarce enabling input.

The same fact should make policymakers cautious.

The AI boom may not unfold as forecast.

Industrial policy built on one technology narrative can leave expensive assets behind if the narrative changes. A grid expanded for data centers can still be valuable if the lines, substations, and generation serve a broader economy. A bespoke asset stranded beside a never-completed campus is harder to defend.

The best industrial policy therefore leaves options.

Build multipurpose infrastructure where possible.

Use long-term customer commitments where specialization is unavoidable.

Avoid treating every announced gigawatt as inevitable.

Prefer investments that remain useful under several futures.

This is not timid planning.

It is what makes ambitious planning survivable.

The United States may decide that leading in artificial intelligence is important enough to justify building far more electrical infrastructure than current demand alone would support.

That can be a coherent national strategy.

The honest version says what it is.

The country is converting public and private capital into a larger power system because it believes computation will create enough future value to justify it.

That is a bet.

Every industrial revolution contains one.

The grid is where this bet becomes concrete.

Literally.

Steel towers.

Copper conductors.

Gas turbines.

Reactors.

Solar fields.

Battery containers.

Transformers.

Substations.

Rights of way.

The artificial-intelligence economy may be described in software.

Its industrial policy is being poured into foundations.
