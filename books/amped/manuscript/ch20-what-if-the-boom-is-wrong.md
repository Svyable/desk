# What If the Boom Is Wrong?

The most dangerous data center is the one that never opens.

A completed campus at least pays an electric bill.

A cancelled campus can leave behind a substation, a transmission upgrade, a generation contract, a transformer order, a capacity forecast, debt service, and a utility revenue requirement built around a customer that vanished before the first server was installed.

This is the countercase the AI power boom has to survive.

Not whether artificial intelligence is useful.

Not whether data centers will consume more electricity than they did a decade ago.

Not whether the United States needs a stronger grid.

The harder question is whether today's specific forecasts of hyperscale electricity demand are accurate enough to justify the specific assets being built around them.

Infrastructure magnifies forecast errors because it lasts.

A technology company can change a deployment plan in a quarter.

A utility can take years to plan and build a major project.

A transmission line may operate for half a century or longer.

A gas plant can outlive several generations of computing architecture.

The economic problem is not merely getting the forecast wrong.

It is getting the forecast wrong with an irreversible asset.

This is why load forecasting has become one of the most consequential technical functions in the AI economy.

The forecast decides how much generation a region procures.

It influences transmission plans.

It informs utility integrated-resource plans.

It affects equipment orders.

It can influence market prices years before the load fully appears.

A spreadsheet of future data centers can move real capital in the present.

That creates incentives worth examining.

A technology company has reason to preserve multiple location options.

A developer has reason to advertise ambitious phases because scale can attract tenants, capital, and political support.

A local government has reason to celebrate the largest plausible investment number.

A utility has reason to take a large customer's request seriously and may prefer growth because more investment can expand its rate base under conventional regulation.

A grid operator has reason to plan conservatively enough that the lights stay on if demand materializes.

None of these actors has to fabricate anything for the aggregate forecast to become too high.

Option value can create exaggeration without deception.

This is what makes duplicate and immature load requests so difficult.

One underlying expansion plan can appear in several utility territories while the customer searches for the fastest path to power. A developer can reserve land in multiple regions. Several phases of a campus can appear as future load even though later phases depend on the success of earlier ones. A customer can ask for a maximum electrical envelope that it may never use continuously.

The grid adds the requests.

The company chooses among them.

The difference can be billions of dollars of infrastructure.

PJM's evolving forecasting process provides a useful example of institutions learning in public.

The region's 2025 discussions highlighted enormous projected growth from data centers, with potential additions reaching tens of gigawatts over only several years. By the 2026 long-term forecast, PJM had tightened the treatment of large-load adjustment requests. Near-term additions needed firmer evidence of commitment. Less mature projects farther out could be treated as non-firm and derated for uncertainty. PJM also published accuracy materials and commissioned outside review of its data-center forecasting methodology.

The result was not a declaration that the boom was fake.

It was a recognition that a queue needs probabilities.

This is one of the most important institutional improvements in the entire data-center story.

Future load should not enter the plan as a binary variable.

A project is not either imaginary or certain.

It can be zoned but unfinanced.

Contracted but waiting on power.

Power-ready but waiting on servers.

Partially energized.

Committed for one phase and speculative for three more.

Backed by a named customer.

Backed by a developer hoping to find one.

Each state contains different information.

A mature forecast should use it.

This sounds obvious until reliability enters the room.

Underforecasting has a frightening failure mode.

The load arrives and the system does not have enough capacity.

Prices spike.

Connection dates slip.

Emergency procedures become more common.

Reliability margins shrink.

Political leaders ask why planners failed to see an announced industrial boom.

Overforecasting has a quieter failure mode.

The system builds assets that are not fully needed.

Customers pay the fixed costs over decades.

Because the lights stay on, the mistake can look like prudence.

The political system therefore tends to punish underforecasting faster than overforecasting.

That asymmetry can bias planning upward.

The solution is not to become reckless on reliability.

It is to move uncertainty into contracts.

If a large customer wants the grid to treat its future demand as firm enough to drive construction, the customer should make a corresponding commitment.

Minimum bills.

Collateral.

Construction milestones.

Deposits.

Long-term service agreements.

Phased capacity reservations.

Take-or-pay obligations.

These are not simply financial protections.

They improve the forecast.

Money reveals belief.

A company willing to sign a long-duration obligation around a five-hundred-megawatt request is providing stronger evidence than a company willing only to submit a form.

The commitment does not guarantee the project succeeds.

It changes who pays if it does not.

That is the essential risk-allocation move.

Forecast risk belongs closest to the party with the best information and the greatest ability to control the outcome.

The utility knows the grid.

The developer knows the project.

The hyperscaler knows its compute plan.

The regulator should resist arrangements in which the developer keeps the upside of an option while ordinary customers absorb the downside of infrastructure built for it.

The same principle applies to generation contracts.

A technology company may sign a long-term agreement that supports a nuclear plant, gas project, renewable facility, or other resource. If the contract is financially durable, the project can be built around a real revenue stream rather than a vague forecast. The buyer may later route different workloads or change hardware, but the contractual payment survives.

This is a much stronger foundation for infrastructure.

The grid does not need to know whether a particular model architecture remains fashionable.

It needs to know who owes the money.

The countercase becomes more interesting when efficiency enters.

Artificial-intelligence hardware and software are not static loads.

Accelerators improve.

Models change.

Quantization, sparsity, better algorithms, specialized chips, cooling improvements, higher utilization, and changes in model architecture can reduce the energy required for a unit of useful computation.

Historically, computing efficiency has improved dramatically.

That fact does not automatically reduce total electricity use.

Cheaper computation can create more demand.

This is the rebound effect at the center of the AI power debate.

If the cost of an inference falls by a factor of ten, customers may ask for one hundred times more inferences.

If training becomes more efficient, developers may train larger models or run more experiments.

Efficiency can lower energy per task while raising total energy.

Both can happen at once.

The forecast therefore depends on an economic variable no utility planner controls.

How much demand will cheaper intelligence create?

That is not an electrical engineering question.

It is a market-formation question.

The answer could be enormous.

It could also be less than the most aggressive capital plans imply.

Consumers may not pay enough for every AI service to justify the compute behind it. Enterprise adoption may be slower. Models may commoditize. Competition may drive inference prices down faster than usage rises. Some training workloads may become less valuable as techniques improve. Companies may build fewer frontier-scale models than expected. Hardware utilization may improve enough that installed capacity produces much more output than planners assumed.

A technologically successful AI industry can therefore disappoint an electricity forecast.

This is the subtle countercase.

AI does not have to fail for power demand to underperform.

The same thing has happened in other infrastructure booms.

Telecommunications demand grew explosively while some fiber investors lost fortunes because too much capacity was built ahead of revenue. Railroads transformed economies while many railroad securities failed. The internet exceeded early expectations while individual data-center and network projects still went bankrupt.

Technology adoption and infrastructure return are different variables.

A grid planner should remember this because utility assets do not receive venture-style upside.

A transmission line does not own equity in the AI application that becomes a trillion-dollar business.

It receives regulated or contracted payments.

If the load disappears, the line cannot recover the lost upside somewhere else unless the network has broader uses.

This is why multipurpose infrastructure is such an important hedge.

Build the regional line that remains valuable under several growth patterns.

Use existing rights of way where upgrades can unlock multiple customers.

Prefer substations that can support an industrial district rather than one fenced campus where practical.

Design generation and transmission portfolios around a range of load outcomes.

Make bespoke assets customer-backed.

Infrastructure can be ambitious without being fragile.

The question of what happens if the boom is wrong should also influence timing.

The grid does not have to build the final system on day one.

A data-center campus is usually developed in phases.

Service can be phased too.

Connect the first hall.

Require additional commitment before the next transmission stage.

Use temporary flexible service while a permanent upgrade is built.

Increase firm capacity as actual load demonstrates itself.

This approach trades some construction efficiency for option value.

The system avoids making every long-lived investment against the maximum announced campus size before demand exists.

The customer may dislike the uncertainty.

That is precisely the point.

If certainty has value, it can be purchased through stronger commitment.

One of the worst policy responses would be to treat skepticism about forecasts as hostility to AI.

Forecast discipline is pro-build.

Credible demand makes infrastructure easier to finance and defend.

A system cluttered with duplicate requests slows serious projects because every project has to be studied against phantom competitors. Ratepayer backlash grows when customers believe utilities are building speculative assets for rich technology companies. Communities become suspicious of infrastructure claims that keep changing.

Cleaning the forecast accelerates what is real.

Texas's Batch Zero and PJM's firmer large-load adjustment methods are different expressions of the same idea.

The queue needs quality, not just quantity.

This is likely to become a national standard.

FERC's 2026 large-load proceedings put the issue in front of every major regional operator under its jurisdiction. Different markets will choose different mechanisms, but the questions will repeat.

What qualifies as a serious request?

How are duplicate requests identified?

How should projects be derated by maturity?

When should a customer fund studies?

What financial security is required?

When does a forecast become a service obligation?

What happens if the customer misses milestones?

How much of a speculative project should enter resource-adequacy planning?

These questions are not glamorous.

They may save more money than any breakthrough power technology.

The cheapest stranded asset is the one never ordered.

There is another possibility the countercase should preserve.

The boom could be wrong in location rather than size.

National data-center electricity use can rise dramatically while individual regions underperform their forecasts. Companies can shift new campuses toward states with cheaper power, faster permitting, better generation access, or more favorable tax treatment. Training workloads can move more easily than latency-sensitive services. New fiber routes can change the geography. Onsite generation can make previously weak locations viable.

A national forecast can be right while a utility forecast is wrong.

This is especially dangerous because utility assets are local.

A transmission line in Virginia does not automatically move to Texas when a hyperscaler changes its plan.

Geographic mobility therefore belongs in load-risk analysis.

The more movable the workload, the stronger the customer commitment should be before highly specialized local assets are socialized.

The same applies to technological mobility.

A company can shift from one accelerator vendor to another.

It can improve server density.

It can redesign cooling.

It can consolidate facilities.

It can buy another cloud provider's capacity rather than build its own.

The electrical plan must survive business-model change.

This is why the strongest version of Amped does not require today's gigawatt projections to be correct.

The argument is not that every announced data center will open.

It is that the wave of requests is already forcing the grid to learn skills it needed anyway.

Better load forecasting.

Stronger customer commitments.

More precise tariffs.

Flexible service.

Batch studies.

Long-term transmission planning.

Supply-chain investment.

More explicit allocation of stranded-cost risk.

These institutions remain valuable if the final demand is lower.

This is the book's own falsification test.

If data-center load growth slows sharply and the institutional changes prove useless for other large loads, the revolution was narrower than argued here.

If new tariffs merely protect utilities while increasing costs, the bargain failed.

If flexible compute proves operationally unavailable when the grid needs it, data centers are less grid-like than the thesis suggests.

If transmission built for the boom becomes stranded rather than broadly useful, the infrastructure dividend was overstated.

If the entire large-load wave turns out to be a short-lived speculative artifact, this book will look like a document from inside a mania.

That possibility should remain alive on the page.

A serious argument is not weakened by describing what would make it false.

It becomes measurable.

The stronger counterevidence today is not that demand has stopped growing.

It has not.

The stronger evidence is that institutions closest to the forecasts are already reducing confidence in the most immature parts of the queue. PJM's 2026 forecast was lower in the near term than its prior forecast in part because large-load assumptions became more disciplined. ERCOT moved to readiness and commitment criteria. Virginia adopted contracts and minimum-charge protections against underuse and departure.

The boom is teaching its own participants not to believe every boom number.

That is healthy.

The outcome the country should fear is not a downward revision.

It is a downward revision after irreversible public costs have already been committed without protection.

Uncertainty is manageable before construction.

After construction it becomes debt service.

This is why the final grid of the AI era should be built around two convictions that appear to conflict.

The first is that demand may be enormous.

The second is that the forecast may be wrong.

Good infrastructure planning can hold both ideas at once.

Prepare for growth.

Charge for certainty.

Stage what can be staged.

Build broad network value where possible.

Put specialized risk on the specialized customer.

Update forecasts as evidence changes.

Do not confuse caution with delay.

Do not confuse optimism with inevitability.

The electric system has always been a machine for making promises about a future nobody can see.

Data centers have made the promises larger.

They should also make the evidence better.
