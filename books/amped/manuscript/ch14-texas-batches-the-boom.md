# Texas Batches the Boom

Texas did not run out of ambition.

It ran out of a planning method designed for the size of the requests arriving at its grid.

That distinction matters.

For years, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas could evaluate new industrial customers largely one at a time. A refinery had a site. A factory had an expected load. A new mine, compressor station, semiconductor plant, or chemical complex could be studied against the transmission system serving the area. The utility and ERCOT could ask what had to be reinforced, what equipment had to be added, and when the customer could be energized.

The process assumed something about scarcity.

It assumed that the difficult question was whether one project could be served.

The data-center boom changed the question.

By 2026, ERCOT was confronting enough requests for very large loads that studying them individually could produce a misleading answer. A transmission path that looked adequate for the first campus might be claimed by the second. A substation plan that worked for one developer could fail once three more projects appeared nearby. The same limited headroom could be promised repeatedly in studies completed at different moments.

A queue was forming on the demand side of the grid.

Texas responded with a revealing institutional invention.

It created a batch.

In June 2026, the Public Utility Commission of Texas approved what ERCOT called Batch Zero, a transitional process for qualifying large-load projects of at least seventy-five megawatts. Instead of evaluating each mature request as though the others were background noise, ERCOT would study a group of projects together. The purpose was not to slow development for its own sake. It was to see the system-wide collision before contracts and construction made the collision expensive.

The move sounds procedural.

It is actually a change in how a growth economy understands scarcity.

A batch says that the projects are not independent.

That is obvious physically. It is less obvious commercially.

Each developer experiences its project as one deal. The land has been optioned. The customer has a deployment plan. Financing assumes a date. A utility account team may be working intensely to make the connection happen. Every company can tell itself a reasonable story in which its own request should be studied promptly and judged on its own merits.

The grid does not experience the projects separately.

It sees simultaneous withdrawal.

That is the insight Batch Zero makes explicit.

ERCOT had already defined a large-load interconnection process for facilities at or above seventy-five megawatts at a single site. Batch Zero went further by grouping eligible projects that had reached specified levels of maturity and commitment. ERCOT could then study the combined impact, identify transmission constraints across the system, decide where available capacity actually existed, and determine which upgrades would be required if several projects moved forward together.

The process also introduced a useful word into the data-center power debate.

Commitment.

A forecast is not a commitment.

A land purchase is not a commitment.

A press release is not a commitment.

An interconnection request is not necessarily a commitment either.

The American power system has become crowded with future load that is real enough to influence planning but uncertain enough to disappear. A developer can submit in multiple territories. A hyperscaler can compare several regions. A utility can receive requests that represent overlapping versions of the same underlying expansion plan. The project may be waiting on a customer contract, transformer availability, financing, a zoning decision, water, tax treatment, or a generation arrangement.

If planners treat every possibility as inevitable, they can build too much.

If they ignore possibilities until every uncertainty is resolved, they can build too late.

The large-load problem lives between those errors.

Batching is one way to make that uncertainty visible.

It forces planners to ask not only which projects want power but which projects have advanced far enough that the system should reserve scarce capacity around them.

ERCOT's Batch Zero documentation required forms, attestations, information about commissioning, transmission-provider participation, and other evidence tied to eligibility. Certain projects could seek treatment as provisional controllable-load resources. Others could be studied with generation in arrangements designed to limit withdrawals from the public system.

The details will change as the rules mature.

The principle is more durable.

A serious place in the queue has to cost something.

Not necessarily a punitive fee.

Something.

Collateral.

Engineering work.

A contract.

A demonstrated site.

A deadline.

A minimum payment.

An obligation to curtail.

A forfeiture if the project walks away after causing others to plan around it.

Without such signals, a queue becomes a collection of options held at public expense.

This is already familiar on the generation side of the grid. Developers learned that requesting interconnection was cheap relative to the value of preserving an option. Queues filled with projects that were economically plausible but far from certain. Transmission providers were forced to study enormous volumes of proposed generation, often sequentially, while project changes required restudies. FERC eventually moved the generator process toward cluster studies and stronger readiness requirements because the old queue had become a barrier to actual construction.

The same economics can appear in reverse.

A prospective data-center customer wants to preserve the option to build where power becomes available first.

The rational strategy for the company may be to explore several sites.

The rational strategy for every utility may be to take the inquiry seriously because the load is valuable.

The collective result can be fictitious certainty.

Everyone holds an option.

The grid reads the options as demand.

This is how a commercial search process can become a reliability forecast.

The first job of the new large-load institutions is to keep those categories separate.

Texas is a particularly revealing place for the experiment because ERCOT's market has long rewarded speed.

The state built an enormous quantity of wind generation while other regions argued about whether variable renewables could scale. It then added solar at extraordinary speed. Batteries followed. Gas generation, transmission expansion, market-rule changes, and demand response have repeatedly adapted around a grid with rapid population growth and unusually dynamic investment.

The system is not famous for telling industrial developers to come back in a decade.

That is why Batch Zero matters.

It is not the reflex of a slow bureaucracy.

It is the response of a fast market discovering that speed itself needs coordination.

The data-center request arrives with an unusual clock.

A technology company may value months more than a conventional utility values years. Computing hardware depreciates quickly. A generation of accelerators can lose relative economic value before a transmission project clears permitting and construction. AI competition turns deployment timing into corporate strategy. The company wants power before the utility's normal infrastructure cycle can provide it.

That creates a temptation.

Make an exception.

Use temporary generation.

Create an interim service agreement.

Let the facility energize partially.

Connect the campus behind an existing plant.

Treat some load as interruptible.

Build a substation first and solve the rest later.

Each option can be sensible.

At scale, exceptions become the architecture.

Texas is trying to turn the improvised architecture into rules.

A batch is one layer.

Controllable load is another.

Withdrawal-limited arrangements are another.

Net-metering rules around large loads and existing generation are another.

Ride-through requirements for large electronic loads are another.

The state is effectively inventing a new industrial interconnection regime while the customers are already waiting at the fence.

This is regulation under load.

The phrase is literal.

It also explains why some of the rules look provisional.

No jurisdiction has decades of operating history with multi-hundred-megawatt AI campuses that can change consumption through software, pair with onsite generation, and negotiate service across several utility territories. The planners are building the vocabulary while the market is building the facilities.

That creates risk of overreaction.

A seventy-five-megawatt threshold is administratively useful. It is not a law of physics. A sixty-megawatt project can matter greatly on a weak part of the system. A two-hundred-megawatt project can be comparatively easy to serve near abundant generation and strong transmission. A campus may energize in phases. Several separate customers can aggregate into a local constraint even if each falls below the formal threshold.

Rules built around nameplate size can become crude if the underlying system condition is ignored.

The batch therefore cannot replace engineering.

It organizes engineering.

That distinction is essential.

The purpose is not to allocate power politically among favored companies. It is to make the physical consequences of simultaneous projects legible before private commitments outrun the network.

This also changes the meaning of first come, first served.

The phrase sounds fair because it treats a queue like a line at a counter.

But electric capacity is not one uniform seat.

A project in West Texas and a project near Dallas do not compete for every same asset. Two projects connecting to the same constrained transmission corridor may compete intensely. A flexible project and a fully firm project impose different obligations. A project paired with generation may still rely on public backup in a way that differs from a genuinely withdrawal-limited facility.

Chronology alone cannot capture those differences.

The batch lets the grid ask a better question.

Which combination of projects can the system support, under which service conditions, with which upgrades?

This is closer to portfolio construction than queuing.

The political problem begins immediately.

If ten large projects want to connect and the system can reliably serve only six without major upgrades, somebody must wait, reduce its request, accept non-firm service, bring generation, move, or pay for expansion.

The scarcity already exists.

The process merely reveals it.

Revealing scarcity creates losers.

That is why transparent criteria matter so much.

A utility that privately decides which data center gets power can create suspicion that commercial relationships are driving reliability decisions. A regulator that allocates capacity through opaque exceptions can turn electricity access into an industrial patronage system. A pure auction can favor the richest firms even when another project brings more employment or strategic value. A political allocation process can sacrifice grid efficiency for local headlines.

There is no neutral answer once demand exceeds near-term capacity.

There can be neutral rules about the electrical problem.

Readiness.

Impact.

Cost responsibility.

Firmness.

Curtailment capability.

Location.

Timing.

Those criteria keep the grid focused on the service it is actually providing.

States may still choose industrial policy around the edges. They can offer tax incentives. They can prioritize manufacturing. They can favor projects in distressed regions. They can condition subsidies on jobs or infrastructure. Those are legitimate political choices when made openly.

They should not be smuggled into transmission studies.

The grid engineer should not have to decide whether one AI company is socially more deserving than another.

The engineer should be able to say what each connection does to the system.

Batch Zero is an attempt to protect that boundary.

It also makes another truth unavoidable.

Data-center development is becoming transmission planning.

A county zoning board may approve a campus because the site is industrially appropriate. A utility may welcome the customer. A technology company may announce billions of dollars of investment. But the ultimate scale of the project is constrained by a network stretching far beyond the property line.

The project is local in land use and regional in electricity.

This creates an asymmetry in benefits and costs.

The county may collect tax revenue.

The utility territory may collect sales.

Construction workers may come from the region.

The transmission upgrade may be paid through a much wider rate base depending on the rules.

Generation prices may rise across the market.

The reliability consequences can extend across ERCOT.

Batching exposes those shared consequences before the local deal is mistaken for a local matter.

Texas has another reason to care about timing.

Its grid is electrically distinct from the large synchronized interconnections covering most of the rest of the continental United States. That independence has regulatory and operational advantages the state values, but it limits the degree to which Texas can simply import massive amounts of power from neighboring systems when internal demand surges.

The state has interties.

It does not have an unlimited extension cord.

New demand therefore has to be matched substantially by resources and wires inside the ERCOT system.

This makes the large-load boom unusually honest.

A company can bring money.

It cannot bring imaginary electrons.

That is why one of the most interesting features of the Texas rules is the effort to distinguish ordinary load from load that can help solve its own problem.

A provisional controllable-load resource can be evaluated differently because it offers the system something in return: the possibility of reducing withdrawal under defined conditions. A withdrawal-limited arrangement paired with generation can be studied around a cap on how much it takes from the public grid.

The customer is no longer merely asking for a connection.

It is negotiating a shape.

This is where Batch Zero connects to the deeper argument of this book.

The American grid is beginning to sell customized obligations.

The old industrial bargain was simple enough to summarize: build the plant, connect the customer, supply the load.

The emerging bargain has dimensions.

How much is firm?

How much can be interrupted?

How fast can the load change?

Is onsite generation operating in parallel?

How much grid withdrawal is guaranteed?

What happens during a contingency?

How quickly will the campus ramp from its first energized hall to full capacity?

What evidence shows the later phases will actually arrive?

A request for five hundred megawatts is becoming the beginning of the conversation rather than the answer.

That is progress.

It is also administratively expensive.

Every custom service creates measurement, enforcement, and planning obligations. Grid operators cannot negotiate bespoke constitutional arrangements with thousands of customers. The successful rules will therefore need standard products.

Firm service.

Interruptible service.

Controllable load.

Limited withdrawal.

Phased energization.

Customer-backed upgrades.

Each can have defined rights and obligations.

The goal is not to make the grid simple again.

The goal is to make complexity repeatable.

Texas is unusually good at turning messy market evolution into standardized products once the economic value becomes clear. Energy-only wholesale pricing, scarcity mechanisms, ancillary services, congestion rights, and battery participation all developed through repeated rounds of rules, mistakes, and adaptation.

Large load will likely follow the same path.

Batch Zero is not the final system.

The name admits that.

Zero comes before one.

The process is a bridge from a world in which giant loads were exceptional to one in which they are a planning category.

That future category will probably be broader than data centers.

Hydrogen production can be enormous and flexible.

Direct-air-capture facilities can consume large amounts of power with some scheduling freedom.

Synthetic fuels, electrified industrial heat, semiconductor fabs, battery factories, and large charging depots can all arrive at scales that matter to regional planning. Some will be more interruptible than others. Some will bring generation. Some will have local economic benefits far larger than their permanent headcount suggests.

The institutions created for AI may become the standard industrial connection rules of the next electricity era.

That is one reason to get them right.

The other is more immediate.

A queue can hide scarcity for only so long.

Developers interpret silence as possibility.

Utilities interpret requests as opportunity.

Communities interpret announcements as investment.

Markets interpret infrastructure plans as future demand.

Eventually, the transmission system has to say which promises can coexist.

Texas decided it wanted to hear those promises together.

That is what the batch does.

It takes a thousand private schedules and forces them onto one electrical clock.

The result may frustrate companies that thought they were next.

It may also keep Texas from building around a future that exists only in spreadsheets.

The difference between those outcomes is not bureaucracy.

It is information.

The grid cannot manufacture capacity by believing every request.

It cannot build fast enough by distrusting all of them.

So it has started asking the market to prove which projects are serious, which loads can bend, and which pieces of the boom can fit on the same wires.

The most important Texas innovation in the data-center race may therefore be neither a power plant nor a server farm.

It may be the decision to stop pretending that a line is enough when the problem is a crowd.
