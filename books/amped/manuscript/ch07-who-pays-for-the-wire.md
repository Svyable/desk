# Who Pays for the Wire?

A transmission line can have one cause and a million beneficiaries.

That sentence is why cost allocation is one of the hardest parts of the data-center boom.

Imagine a utility territory where existing customers are served reliably but with little room for new growth. A data-center developer arrives and asks for five hundred megawatts. Engineers conclude that the local system cannot serve the load without a new high-voltage line and a major substation expansion.

The intuitive answer is simple.

The data center caused the upgrade.

Make the data center pay.

Now let the line operate for thirty years.

The new transmission reduces congestion for existing customers. It gives the system another path during outages. It allows a new solar project to deliver power into the region. A factory arrives five years later because capacity is available. Ten years later, an older line is rebuilt and the new corridor carries more of the system during construction. The data center still uses the network, but the line has become part of the region's electrical skeleton.

Who paid for what?

The question has no purely engineering answer.

Power-flow studies can identify which constraints appear when load changes. They can estimate which projects solve those constraints. They can model benefits. They cannot decide what fairness means over the life of an asset.

That is the work of regulation.

Electric rates are often described as prices, but they are also accounting systems for shared infrastructure. A household bill may contain charges associated with generation, transmission, distribution, fuel, riders, taxes, public programs, and other costs according to the local regulatory structure. The bill is the final page of a much larger argument about who caused costs, who benefits from assets, and how quickly investments should be recovered.

Data centers are forcing that argument into the open because their scale makes ordinary averaging uncomfortable.

When a utility serves millions of small customers, many individual differences disappear into rate classes. One homeowner uses more power. Another uses less. A restaurant has a different load shape from a hardware store. Regulators group customers with similar service characteristics and recover costs through standardized rates.

A five-hundred-megawatt customer is harder to average away.

Its decisions can move the denominator.

This is why Virginia's decision to create a separate rate class for large loads was more than a local tariff revision. It was an institutional admission that the existing customer categories no longer described the economics of service well enough.

The Virginia State Corporation Commission approved a new large-load class for qualifying customers and paired it with protections unusual enough to reveal the perceived risk: long minimum service obligations, minimum charges tied to contracted demand, and collateral requirements under specified conditions.

The details turn the abstract question of cost shifting into a contract.

A long service obligation says: if the utility is building around your demand, you cannot treat the connection like a one-year lease.

A minimum demand charge says: if the grid has reserved capacity for you, you owe something even when you do not use every reserved megawatt.

Collateral says: if your promise is what protects other customers, the promise should have financial substance.

These are not punishments for using electricity.

They are prices for causing the system to prepare.

That distinction is essential because a large data center can be a valuable utility customer. High utilization can generate substantial revenue. Stable demand can improve the use of existing infrastructure. A creditworthy buyer can support generation investment. The existence of a special rate class should not imply that data centers are inherently undesirable.

The issue is asymmetry.

The utility can spend first.

The customer can change its mind later.

Ordinary customers cannot easily leave the service territory if the resulting cost lands in rates.

A good tariff narrows that asymmetry.

Georgia reached a similar conclusion through its own regulatory system. Its commission approved rules giving utilities stronger tools to protect existing customers when serving very large loads, including longer contract terms, minimum billing, and recovery of project-specific or upstream infrastructure costs. The architecture differs from Virginia's, but the emerging principle is recognizable.

Large customers should not receive a free option on the grid.

The word option is useful because it explains what is really scarce.

Suppose a utility has five hundred megawatts of feasible expansion headroom before a major transmission project becomes necessary. A developer asks the utility to hold four hundred megawatts for a campus expected in three years. If the utility treats that request as firm, other projects may be told that capacity is unavailable or that they must fund the next upgrade.

The first developer has received something valuable even before consuming electricity.

It has reserved the cheap part of the system.

If the reservation costs almost nothing, developers will rationally ask for more capacity and earlier capacity than they are certain to need. That behavior produces bad information and shifts infrastructure risk toward the utility.

Charge appropriately for reservation and the request becomes more credible.

The idea is familiar in other networks.

A pipeline customer can reserve capacity. A telecommunications carrier can buy dedicated bandwidth. A company can lease warehouse space it expects to use later. Capacity has value before throughput occurs because somebody else cannot count on using the same capacity freely.

Electricity complicates the analogy because network capacity is not a stack of identical lockers. The ability to serve one more megawatt depends on location, time, contingencies, generation patterns, and the rest of the system. Reserving capacity at one substation can have system effects that are difficult to isolate.

But the economic truth survives the complexity.

A large promise can consume planning headroom.

The data-center tariff is becoming the mechanism for pricing that promise.

This changes the familiar debate over electricity rates.

Public arguments often focus on whether data centers pay a lower or higher cents-per-kilowatt-hour rate than households. That comparison can be misleading because large industrial customers typically have different cost structures and load profiles. A utility does not spend the same amount to meter, bill, and serve one three-hundred-megawatt customer as it does to serve hundreds of thousands of small accounts consuming the same annual energy. High-voltage service can avoid parts of the distribution network. High load factor can make infrastructure more fully utilized.

A lower average energy price does not prove subsidy.

A low minimum commitment can.

The real cost-shift question is whether the rate recovers the costs the customer causes and assigns cancellation risk appropriately.

That requires looking beyond the energy charge.

Dedicated facilities are the easiest category. If a substation, feeder, or transformer is built solely for one campus and has little value without it, assigning much or all of that cost to the customer is straightforward.

Shared upstream upgrades are harder.

A transmission line may be triggered by the customer but benefit everyone. A generation project may be built because of aggregate load growth that includes several data centers plus residential and industrial demand. A reliability upgrade can solve an immediate data-center constraint while also addressing an older weakness the system had tolerated.

The phrase beneficiary pays sounds fair until every party produces a different map of benefits.

The phrase cost causer pays sounds fair until time turns the cause into a shared asset.

Regulators live between those slogans.

A useful way to think about the problem is to separate three layers.

The first is customer-specific infrastructure. If the asset has little purpose without the customer, the customer should generally carry most of the risk.

The second is acceleration. The system may have needed an asset eventually, but the data center causes it to be built sooner. The relevant cost is not necessarily the entire asset. It can be the financial cost of advancing the investment and the risk that future users do not materialize as expected.

The third is system expansion. A project can create broad capacity or reliability benefits large enough that spreading some cost is reasonable even if one customer made the need urgent.

Real cases blur the layers.

That is why transparent analysis matters more than one ideological rule.

The data-center boom also complicates the traditional utility concept of obligation to serve.

Regulated utilities are generally expected to provide reliable service to customers within their territories under approved terms. The obligation is foundational: monopoly franchise comes with public duties.

But what does obligation to serve mean when one customer can ask for the equivalent of a small city's load on a schedule the existing system cannot meet?

The utility cannot manufacture transformers by regulatory principle.

It cannot violate reliability standards to honor an economic-development announcement.

It cannot make a transmission line appear faster because the customer is important.

The obligation has to coexist with feasibility.

This is one reason phased service is becoming more valuable.

A utility may be able to provide one hundred megawatts now, another hundred after a substation expansion, and the remainder after transmission work. The campus can match construction to the electrical ramp. That is less satisfying than a single date and a single number, but it converts a binary connection problem into a sequence.

The tariff can follow the sequence too.

A customer's minimum obligation can rise as capacity is actually reserved or made available. Collateral can be tied to specific construction commitments. Milestones can release the utility from holding capacity indefinitely. The result is closer to staged project finance than to ordinary retail electricity service.

This may become the standard relationship between utilities and hyperscale customers.

The policy challenge is preventing custom negotiation from becoming opacity.

Large customers have bargaining power.

A governor wants the project. A utility wants load. A county wants tax revenue. A developer can compare several states. The temptation is to treat the contract as a confidential economic-development matter and reveal only the final rate.

But the public has an interest in the risk structure when regulated assets and captive customers are involved.

Confidentiality around commercially sensitive details can be legitimate. It should not become a way to hide who pays if the forecast fails.

At minimum, regulators should be able to explain the principle.

Does the customer cover dedicated facilities?

Does it guarantee a minimum revenue stream?

Does it post security against cancellation?

Can costs shift to other rate classes?

How are shared upgrades treated?

What happens at contract expiration if the asset has decades of life remaining?

The final question is especially important.

A fourteen-year minimum service obligation sounds long in the technology industry.

It is short in transmission.

A line can operate for generations. A substation can remain useful for decades. The customer commitment may cover the risky early years when debt service and underutilization matter most, but it does not match the full physical life of the asset.

That is reasonable if the asset has durable system value afterward.

It is dangerous if the asset is highly specialized.

The mismatch again returns to transferability.

Good grid investments should survive the first customer's business plan.

This principle can guide regulators toward projects that create option value for the wider economy. A new high-voltage corridor that unlocks several future sites may be safer than an enormous bespoke extension into a location with few alternative uses. A substation designed with expansion pathways may be more resilient than one optimized narrowly around a single generation of equipment. Transmission that connects new supply and new demand can remain useful across scenarios.

The data-center boom can therefore improve grid planning if regulators resist the urge to treat every project as an isolated customer accommodation.

The largest loads are big enough to justify system thinking.

This is one reason FERC's long-term transmission planning reforms matter in the background. Order No. 1920 requires regional planners to look farther ahead and consider a broader set of benefits and scenarios than traditional short-term approaches often captured. The rule was not written only for data centers. Large-load growth makes its logic more urgent.

If a region expects substantial demand growth, planning one transmission upgrade at a time around each immediate customer can produce a patchwork that is more expensive than a broader buildout.

But broad planning creates another cost-allocation fight.

Future beneficiaries do not yet have meters.

Existing customers can be asked to fund infrastructure for anticipated growth.

The data-center customer may argue that it should not pay for a regional line sized beyond its own needs.

Regulators have to decide how much present certainty is enough to justify future capacity.

We are back to the forecast.

The wire and the forecast cannot be separated.

A line justified by one highly credible customer is a different investment from a line justified by ten speculative projects. A customer willing to sign a long obligation can make a broader project more defensible. Several committed customers can transform a regional plan from anticipatory overbuild into prudent preparation.

This is demand as underwriting.

The idea should not be romanticized.

A technology company is not a public utility because it signs a contract. Its goal is to secure compute capacity, not to optimize the regional grid. It will choose terms that serve its own shareholders. A utility and regulator still have to protect customers who did not negotiate the deal.

But the buyer's credit can be used deliberately rather than accidentally.

That is the opportunity.

The old model often asked ratepayers to finance infrastructure and hoped new load arrived to use it.

The emerging model can ask the largest new load to guarantee enough of the investment that the public system is not making a one-sided bet.

This is a more mature form of economic development.

Instead of offering the cheapest possible power and celebrating the capital announcement, a state can compete on the quality of its infrastructure bargain.

Can it provide a credible path to power?

Can it build useful shared assets?

Can it protect existing customers from abandonment risk?

Can it reward flexibility?

Can it make the customer pay for dedicated facilities without charging twice for infrastructure that later benefits everyone?

These questions are less photogenic than a ribbon cutting.

They determine whether the ribbon cutting was expensive.

The answer to who pays for the wire will never be one sentence because the wire is never only one thing.

It is a physical asset.

It is a reliability resource.

It is an option on future growth.

It is a political object crossing land.

It is a line on a regulated balance sheet.

It is sometimes the reason a project can exist at all.

The data-center boom is forcing America to price all of those meanings at once.

That may be uncomfortable.

It is healthier than pretending electricity infrastructure is free because the customer can afford the power bill.

The kilowatt-hour is only what passes through the wire.

The expensive part is deciding who had to believe in the wire before it was there.
