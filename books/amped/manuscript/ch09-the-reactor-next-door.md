# The Reactor Next Door

A nuclear reactor can spend years being too expensive for the market and then become exactly what a data center wants.

The reactor has not changed.

The customer has.

That reversal is one of the most revealing developments in the American data-center boom because it shows how new demand can change the value of old supply.

Nuclear power has several characteristics that look unusually attractive to large computing customers. Existing reactors can produce large quantities of electricity around the clock. Their operating emissions are low. Their sites already contain electrical infrastructure, grid connections, security systems, trained workforces, and a regulatory history that would be extraordinarily difficult to reproduce from scratch.

Those advantages do not make nuclear electricity automatically cheap.

For years, several U.S. reactors struggled economically in power markets where natural gas was inexpensive, electricity-demand growth was slow, and subsidized or low-marginal-cost resources put pressure on wholesale prices. Some plants closed even though they remained physically capable of generating. Others required state policies, market reforms, or long-term arrangements to remain economic.

Then the price of certainty changed.

A hyperscale customer does not necessarily want the cheapest spot-market megawatt-hour.

It wants a credible path to enormous amounts of power over many years.

That is a different product.

The distinction helps explain Constellation Energy's plan to restart the former Three Mile Island Unit 1 in Pennsylvania as the Crane Clean Energy Center, backed by a twenty-year power purchase agreement with Microsoft. The reactor had shut in 2019 for economic reasons, not because it was the unit involved in the 1979 accident. The proposed restart, announced in 2024, requires regulatory approvals, engineering work, staffing, and other steps before electricity can flow again.

The agreement is not proof that every closed nuclear plant should reopen.

It proves something narrower and more consequential.

A creditworthy data-center buyer can make an existing power asset financeable under conditions where the ordinary market did not.

That is demand acting as industrial policy without a statute bearing the name.

The buyer is not subsidizing nuclear power out of civic generosity.

It needs electricity.

The generator is not reviving a plant because artificial intelligence is culturally important.

It has a customer.

The public consequence emerges from a private contract.

This is what makes the data-center boom so powerful as a grid force. It can attach corporate balance sheets to assets whose value extends beyond the customer.

If the reactor restarts, it is physically connected to a regional system. The electrons cannot be painted Microsoft blue. Commercial arrangements can assign attributes and financial rights, but the plant's operation changes the supply available to the grid as a whole. Other generators respond. Transmission flows change. Capacity conditions change. The regional resource mix changes.

A private purchase can therefore create a public electrical fact.

That is one version of revolutionizing the grid.

There is another version that looks more speculative.

Technology companies have become increasingly interested in advanced nuclear reactors, including small modular designs. Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and other companies have announced agreements, investments, or procurement strategies connected to nuclear energy. The motivation is understandable: AI demand needs firm power, clean-energy goals constrain fossil alternatives, and a new class of reactors promises the possibility of modular deployment.

Promises are not power plants.

That distinction has to remain visible.

Most advanced reactor concepts are not yet operating at commercial scale in the United States. Development timelines, licensing, supply chains, financing, construction performance, fuel availability, and public acceptance remain material uncertainties. A data-center contract can improve the commercial case. It cannot repeal nuclear engineering.

The same urgency that makes hyperscalers valuable customers can also make new nuclear a poor match for the first wave of demand.

A campus seeking power in three years cannot wait for a technology that may require longer to license and construct. That does not make the technology irrelevant. It changes which wave of demand it can serve.

Existing nuclear is a near-term asset.

Nuclear restarts are a medium-term possibility.

New large reactors and advanced designs are longer-term bets unless development accelerates materially.

The clocks matter more than the slogans.

This is where the reactor next door becomes an ideal symbol for the whole book.

The data center wants density.

Nuclear power provides density.

The data center wants round-the-clock operation.

Nuclear plants are designed for sustained output.

The data center wants long-lived certainty about electricity availability.

A reactor can operate for decades.

The data center wants lower-carbon supply.

Nuclear provides it during operation.

The apparent fit is so strong that it can hide the mismatches.

Nuclear plants are large and inflexible capital projects.

Data-center hardware changes quickly.

Nuclear licensing is deliberate.

Compute deployment is impatient.

A reactor cannot easily follow a data-center company's decision to move the next campus to another state.

The customer must therefore make a commitment long enough to justify the asset.

This is why long power-purchase agreements matter.

The PPA aligns time horizons.

A generator receives confidence that revenue will exist over enough years to support investment. The customer receives a defined path to power and often the environmental attributes associated with the generation. The project can be financed around the contract rather than around volatile short-term electricity prices alone.

The arrangement is not unique to nuclear. Renewable projects have been financed through corporate PPAs for years. What is different is the use of such contracting to support firm, capital-intensive generation and the scale of demand behind it.

The data center is becoming an anchor tenant for power plants.

That analogy is useful.

A real-estate developer can finance a building more easily when a creditworthy tenant signs a long lease. The tenant does not own the whole neighborhood. Its commitment makes the building credible to lenders.

A hyperscaler can play a similar role in energy.

The power plant may serve a broader market, but the long-term buyer changes the bankability of the investment.

This creates a potentially productive division of risk.

The technology company bears more of the demand risk because it signs the purchase commitment.

The generator bears construction and operating risk according to the contract.

The public grid receives additional supply without necessarily placing the entire investment into a regulated utility's rate base.

The arrangement can still affect other customers through markets, transmission, and system planning, but the central commercial bet is not automatically socialized.

This is one reason the corporate balance sheet matters so much in the grid transition.

America has spent years debating how to finance new electrical infrastructure. Large technology customers arrive with a reason to finance it now.

That urgency can be valuable if the asset is useful.

The phrase useful deserves emphasis.

A nuclear plant restarted for one data-center buyer can still be a durable regional resource. A bespoke generator isolated deep inside a campus may have less wider value. The public interest is strongest when private demand helps finance infrastructure that remains valuable across customers and scenarios.

Nuclear often scores well on durability.

It scores less well on speed.

This tradeoff is pushing the industry toward existing assets first.

Keeping an existing plant online avoids the need to replace its output. Uprates can sometimes increase capacity at existing sites. Life-extension investments can preserve decades of future generation. Restarts can recover value from sites where much of the expensive infrastructure already exists.

Each option is project-specific.

A closed reactor may have deteriorated equipment, lost staff, altered supply chains, expired contracts, or licensing work that makes restart uneconomic. Some plants were shut for reasons that cannot be solved by a better electricity buyer. A company should not assume that every retired reactor is a stranded data-center asset waiting to be claimed.

The larger idea is that scarcity changes what assets are worth examining.

When power was abundant and demand growth slow, an economically marginal nuclear plant could look like an aging cost problem.

When firm, low-carbon electricity becomes scarce, the same plant can look like a strategic resource.

The data-center boom reprices the fleet.

This repricing can extend beyond nuclear.

Hydroelectric resources become more valuable. Gas plants with dependable fuel access become more valuable. Transmission into regions with spare generation becomes more valuable. Batteries capable of supporting peak conditions become more valuable. Sites with retired power plants become more valuable because they may retain grid connections, land, and infrastructure.

The reactor next door is part of a broader phenomenon: electrical brownfields becoming digital industrial assets.

A power plant site is already wired for scale.

That can be more valuable than pristine land.

This reverses ordinary technology geography. Data centers were often discussed as greenfield real estate projects seeking tax-friendly land and fiber. In an era of electrical scarcity, old industrial sites can become attractive precisely because the grid already knows they exist.

The new economy goes looking for the old economy's electrical skeleton.

That is an American-grid story worth following because it could accelerate reuse rather than expansion at the margins.

Reusing an existing transmission interconnection can avoid some of the work required for a wholly new site. Using existing industrial land can reduce greenfield pressure. Restarting a generator can be faster than building a new one.

None of these advantages is automatic, and repurposing can create its own technical and regulatory studies. But the search for power can turn legacy infrastructure into an asset rather than a liability.

This is one reason the data-center boom may strengthen the grid even if the first public reaction is to fear scarcity.

Scarcity reveals value.

A transmission corridor ignored during slow load growth becomes strategic.

A nuclear site becomes financeable.

A region with spare generation gains industrial leverage.

An interconnection point becomes a development asset.

Capital follows the revealed constraint.

The danger is that scarcity also rewards the fastest solution rather than the best long-lived solution.

If a company needs power before a nuclear project can possibly arrive, it may contract for gas. If utilities need firm capacity quickly, they may extend the life of fossil plants scheduled to retire. If transmission takes too long, onsite generation can become the path of least resistance.

The climate outcome of the data-center boom will therefore depend partly on whether clean firm resources can be made to operate on an industrial clock without compromising safety or reliability.

Nuclear is the hardest test.

Speeding a paperwork process that is duplicative can be valuable.

Speeding a safety process by pretending the underlying engineering risk has disappeared is not.

The distinction sounds obvious in principle and becomes contentious in practice because every participant has a different view of which delay is essential.

The technology industry is used to iteration.

Software can ship, observe, patch, and improve.

Nuclear plants do not get to run that experiment in the same way.

The cost of certain errors is too high. Physical systems with long-lived failure consequences demand more assurance before operation.

Data-center urgency cannot turn every infrastructure sector into software.

The better contribution may be financial rather than procedural.

A long-term buyer can make it easier to fund the engineering, manufacturing, workforce, and licensing capacity needed to build responsibly. Stable demand can support suppliers. Repeat customers can create learning. A pipeline of projects can justify investment in specialized manufacturing that one isolated reactor would not.

This is how industrial scale can eventually improve speed without simply cutting corners.

The same thing happened in other technologies. Repetition creates supply chains. Standardization reduces bespoke engineering. Skilled labor develops around a predictable pipeline. Financing becomes easier when lenders understand the asset class.

Data-center demand could create that repetition if the technology actually performs.

The condition matters.

Advanced nuclear advocates have promised modularity before. The industry has a history of cost overruns, schedule problems, canceled projects, and designs that looked better in planning than in construction. A bestseller about the grid should not solve those problems with enthusiasm.

The evidence has to come from projects delivered.

That is why existing and restarted reactors are so valuable analytically. They show what happens when the customer changes without requiring a speculative technology breakthrough.

The Crane project is a contract around an old plant.

The Susquehanna data-center relationship is a commercial and regulatory experiment around an operating plant.

Together they reveal the new bargaining power of load.

Nuclear operators have something the hyperscalers need.

Hyperscalers have something nuclear operators need.

One side has megawatts.

The other has credit.

The grid sits between them.

That last part is important because the power does not exist outside the system simply because two companies sign an agreement. Transmission, market rules, reliability planning, and backup obligations still matter. A nuclear plant can be financially anchored by one customer and operationally integrated with millions of others.

This is why private contracting can strengthen public infrastructure when the boundaries are designed well.

The customer gets certainty.

The generator gets revenue certainty.

The grid gets a durable resource.

Other customers should not be forced to subsidize the private bargain, but they can benefit from the physical asset's continued operation.

This is the ideal case.

There will be worse cases.

A company can lock up scarce generation in a way that raises prices for others. A co-location structure can shift transmission costs. A long-term contract can become expensive if technology or market prices change. A nuclear project can fail to restart after money is committed. New construction can arrive late enough that the data-center demand has already moved elsewhere.

The contract does not eliminate risk.

It decides who faces it first.

That is the recurring theme of the grid revolution.

Data centers are forcing infrastructure institutions to make risk visible because the old assumption of passive demand no longer works.

The nuclear story makes this unusually concrete.

For decades, policy arguments asked whether governments should subsidize nuclear plants, whether capacity markets valued reliability correctly, whether carbon-free attributes deserved payment, and whether old plants could compete against gas and renewables.

A hyperscaler asks a more direct question.

Can you sell me the power for twenty years?

The answer can change the plant.

That is what happens when demand becomes strategic.

The reactor next door is not merely a source of electricity for the data center.

The data center may be the reason the reactor exists.

And once the reactor exists, it belongs to the physical future of the grid long after the server generation that justified it has been replaced.

The most valuable nuclear option may therefore be neither “build a new reactor” nor “do nothing.” It may be preserving and upgrading **option-rich nuclear sites** while demand becomes clearer.

An operating plant can pursue life-extension investment. A site can study uprates. A retired site can preserve enough of its workforce, records, grid connection, security boundary, and physical infrastructure that a restart remains possible rather than becoming prohibitively difficult. None of this guarantees a future project. It keeps the option from decaying unnecessarily.

That distinction matters because nuclear optionality disappears unevenly. A shuttered server hall can be repurposed quickly. A nuclear workforce that disperses, specialized equipment that is removed, licenses that lapse, and supply relationships that vanish can make a later restart dramatically harder. The option has maintenance costs even when no one has decided to exercise it.

Data-center demand changes the value of paying those maintenance costs.

A utility or plant owner that once saw only an aging asset can now ask whether the site has strategic value as one of a limited number of places where hundreds of megawatts of firm low-carbon generation can plausibly exist on a known grid connection. That does not justify keeping every uneconomic plant alive indefinitely. It just means the abandonment decision should account for the value of a future buyer whose demand profile did not exist when the plant first became marginal.

The same logic applies to the grid interconnection around the plant. A retired generation site often sits at a point where the transmission system was designed to move a large quantity of power. Reusing that node for a restart, a new generator, storage, or even a nearby data center can be easier than creating an equivalent connection in a greenfield location.

The asset is not only the reactor.

It is the **electrical address**.

This is one reason the data-center boom may produce a map of reuse alongside a map of new construction. Old coal sites, retired industrial facilities, nuclear stations, and other electrically important locations can become valuable because their grid relationships already exist. The fastest path to new digital infrastructure may run through places built for the old industrial economy.

That is a better kind of continuity than nostalgia. The point is not to preserve every legacy asset because it is old. It is to recognize which parts of the old system are expensive to recreate and still useful under a new demand regime.

Nuclear makes that principle impossible to ignore because so much value is embedded in the site before a single new megawatt is produced.

The data center changes the demand case.

The existing site changes the supply clock.

The best projects will be the ones where those two facts meet without pretending either side has eliminated the other’s risk.
