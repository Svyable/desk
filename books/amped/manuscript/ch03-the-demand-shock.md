# The Demand Shock

A forecast is not a power plant.

It cannot light a room, spin a motor, cool a server, or keep a transmission line inside its thermal limit. Yet a forecast can cause all of those things to be built.

That is why the numbers surrounding data-center electricity demand deserve more care than they usually receive.

The headline numbers are enormous.

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated that U.S. data centers consumed roughly 176 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2023, about 4.4 percent of national electricity use. Its 2024 report offered a broad range for 2028 rather than pretending that one number could settle a fast-moving market. A 2026 update extended the analysis to 2030 and placed the midpoint near 11.8 percent of U.S. electricity consumption, with a range from roughly 9.5 to 15.3 percent.

A range that wide is not an analytical embarrassment.

It is the story.

America is trying to plan an electric system while one of its fastest-growing categories of demand remains unusually difficult to forecast.

The error bars are large because the underlying industry is moving quickly. New generations of accelerators change energy per unit of computation. Model architectures change. Training and inference economics change. Demand for AI services changes. Companies announce campuses whose later phases may depend on customer growth that has not happened yet. Efficiency improves. More efficient computation can reduce electricity required for a fixed task while lower cost creates more tasks worth running.

Then geography multiplies the uncertainty.

A national forecast can be approximately right and still leave individual regions disastrously wrong.

This is because electricity is not a national bathtub.

A data center in Virginia cannot be served merely by the existence of spare generation somewhere in Arizona. Power moves through networks with physical limits. Transmission constraints divide the country into practical regions. Fuel systems matter. Weather matters. Local generation matters. The hour of demand matters. A region can face an acute capacity problem even while the national energy balance looks manageable.

The data-center boom is therefore two phenomena at once.

It is an energy story measured in terawatt-hours.

It is a capacity story measured in megawatts and gigawatts at particular locations.

Public debate often slides between the two.

Someone cites the annual electricity consumption of data centers to argue that the grid can absorb the demand because the number is only a fraction of national generation. Someone else cites a queue of proposed projects totaling tens or hundreds of gigawatts to argue that the system is facing an impossible wall of load.

Both can be misleading.

Annual national consumption smooths away local bottlenecks.

Project queues can count possibilities as if they were operating facilities.

The planner has to live between them.

The return of demand growth is visible beyond the data-center category. The U.S. Energy Information Administration reported in 2026 that U.S. electricity demand grew at an annual average rate of about 1.7 percent from 2020 through 2025, compared with roughly 0.1 percent between 2005 and 2019. Data centers are one important driver, alongside manufacturing and other loads.

The difference between those two growth rates is more consequential than it looks.

A grid built during an era of nearly flat demand accumulates habits.

Generation reserve margins are planned around slow changes. Transmission proposals can be judged against forecasts that move gradually. Utilities can retire plants without expecting a sudden offsetting wave of load. Equipment procurement can be organized around incremental expansion. Regulators can treat unexpectedly large demand growth as an outlier rather than the central case.

Then the outlier becomes the plan.

NERC's 2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment, published as the load-growth story was accelerating, projected enormous increases in peak demand over the following decade and identified data centers as a major source of growth in several regions. The exact ten-year number will change as forecasts are revised. The institutional significance is already clear: reliability organizations that spent years worrying about resource transition are now worrying about resource transition and rapid load growth at the same time.

Those are not the same problem added together.

They interact.

A coal plant retirement matters differently if demand is flat than if a cluster of five-hundred-megawatt campuses is arriving. A transmission line delayed by three years matters differently if the surrounding region has spare capacity than if load is climbing quickly. A generator interconnection queue matters differently when new supply is optional than when new customers are waiting on it.

The data-center demand shock is arriving while the supply side is already changing.

That makes timing central.

If every additional unit of demand could be matched instantly by the cheapest new generation, the problem would be easier. Electricity markets would signal scarcity, developers would build supply, and the system would expand.

But new supply is not one commodity with one construction time.

A solar project can be physically built quickly once it has land, equipment, permits, interconnection, and a commercial path. Batteries can be deployed quickly in some settings but do not create energy; they shift it through time and depend on charging supply. Gas generation is dispatchable but depends on turbine availability, fuel infrastructure, permits, and future emissions economics. Nuclear power can offer long-lived, high-capacity-factor generation but is slow and institutionally demanding to build new. Existing reactors can sometimes be preserved or restarted faster than new ones can be constructed, but a restart still requires engineering, licensing, staffing, fuel, financing, and system integration.

Transmission is another clock entirely.

Berkeley Lab's 2026 update on generator interconnection queues found thousands of projects seeking connection and development timelines that had become long enough to turn the queue itself into an infrastructure bottleneck. The report also documented a fact that should discipline every discussion of proposed capacity: only a minority of requested projects historically reach commercial operation.

This is the supply-side mirror of the large-load problem.

A queue is not a fleet.

A request is not a customer.

Both sides of the grid are filling with proposals that may never become physical.

The demand shock therefore creates a strange planning problem. The country can simultaneously have too many proposed power projects and too little power available where a customer wants it.

That sounds contradictory only if electricity is treated as fungible across time and geography.

It is not.

A proposed solar farm in one queue does not solve a transmission constraint in another region. A battery awaiting interconnection does not meet an around-the-clock load until it has charging energy and the system can support its duty cycle. A gas plant can be dispatchable yet useless to a specific project if it cannot be built before the customer's deadline. A nuclear project can be valuable over sixty years and irrelevant to a site that wants five hundred megawatts in thirty months.

The boom is making schedule part of resource adequacy.

That is one reason natural gas keeps returning to the conversation even when technology companies announce aggressive clean-energy goals.

Gas plants can offer firm, dispatchable power and can often be planned on shorter timelines than new nuclear. In regions with existing pipeline capacity and a familiar permitting environment, they can appear to be the bridge between the speed of compute demand and the slower development of other resources.

The climate consequence is obvious enough that it should not be softened.

If data-center demand causes additional fossil generation that otherwise would not have operated or been built, the emissions are part of the load's system effect even if a company separately buys renewable energy or certificates. Matching annual consumption with clean-energy purchases is not the same thing as ensuring that the marginal electricity serving every hour is carbon-free.

The opposite overstatement should also be avoided.

It is too simple to say that AI means gas.

Renewables, storage, transmission, existing nuclear, nuclear restarts, geothermal, demand flexibility, and efficiency can all contribute. The resource mix will differ by region and by how quickly demand materializes. A company with a long enough horizon can contract for projects that would not otherwise be built. A data center willing to accept flexible service may reduce the amount of firm generation required. A region with abundant existing clean power can absorb some growth without adding fossil generation at the same rate.

The useful question is marginal and temporal.

What has to run or be built because this load exists at this place at this hour?

That question is much harder than asking whether a company bought enough renewable megawatt-hours over a year.

It is also where the grid revolution becomes visible.

The International Energy Agency's 2025 work on energy and AI projected that data centers could account for a large share of U.S. electricity-demand growth through 2030. The global numbers attracted attention, but the more important American implication is that a single industrial category could shape decisions about generation and transmission across several major power markets.

This is not because data centers are the largest use of electricity in absolute terms.

It is because new investment is driven by change at the margin.

A residential base load that has existed for decades does not suddenly justify a new substation. A new five-hundred-megawatt campus can.

A system can be mostly old demand and still be transformed by new demand.

The distinction is similar to housing. A city can contain a million existing homes, but a relatively small number of new households can determine where new subdivisions, roads, schools, and water lines are built. Marginal growth directs marginal capital.

Data centers are becoming an unusually powerful marginal buyer of electrical infrastructure.

Their influence becomes even larger when they cluster.

A single campus can often be planned around.

A cluster changes the system's geometry.

Northern Virginia is the clearest American example. The region's data-center industry grew from a combination of network connectivity, proximity to important customers, land, tax policy, skilled firms, and existing electrical infrastructure. Each new facility benefited from the ecosystem created by the earlier ones. That is how industrial clusters work.

Then the cluster became electrically consequential enough to create its own constraints.

PJM and Dominion began planning around levels of data-center demand that would once have been extraordinary for an entire metropolitan load category. By 2026, PJM filings described data-center growth in Virginia as a major driver of transmission investment and load-forecast uncertainty. The system was no longer simply accommodating buildings one at a time. It was reconfiguring around a regional industry.

Texas shows the other extreme: not an established cluster hitting mature constraints, but a flood of requests large enough to overwhelm ordinary study logic.

ERCOT's large-load pipeline grew to a number so large that repeating it without context would be irresponsible. The requests represented possibilities, not customers certain to energize. Many were data centers, but other industrial projects were present as well. The important fact is not the headline total. It is that ERCOT and Texas regulators concluded the requests needed to be studied in batches because one-at-a-time analysis no longer captured the system effects.

Planning procedure became evidence of scale.

The grid does not change its interconnection process because journalists are excited about AI.

It changes because the old process no longer produces reliable answers.

This is why the demand shock should be understood institutionally rather than rhetorically.

A boom becomes real before every project is built when the institutions responsible for reliability begin changing their rules around it.

Virginia created a separate large-load customer class.

Texas created a batch-study process.

FERC opened national proceedings around large-load interconnection.

PJM revised its forecast methodology and co-location rules.

These are not forecasts of AI capability.

They are responses to electrical requests already large enough to affect public infrastructure planning.

The strongest counterargument is that the industry may be over-forecasting itself.

That possibility deserves more than one skeptical paragraph because it could determine whether the current buildout becomes productive infrastructure or stranded capital.

Technology industries are good at creating option value. A company may want several possible sites because it does not yet know where future demand will be strongest. A developer may secure land and utility capacity before it has a final tenant because scarce power access itself can make the site valuable. A utility may see the same customer through multiple project proposals. A regional planner may receive forecasts from utilities that each have incentives to be ready rather than short.

Add those possibilities together and the paper future can become larger than the physical future.

PJM's recent forecast revisions show why the problem cannot be solved by simply trusting the first number. The grid operator tightened its treatment of large loads and adjusted near-term forecasts as better information became available. That did not make the long-run load-growth issue disappear. It demonstrated that forecasting discipline changes the shape of the problem.

A lower forecast can be good news and still imply enormous growth.

This nuance tends to disappear in public arguments.

Critics cite canceled or delayed projects as proof that the boom is imaginary.

Industry advocates cite every announced campus as if it were a binding physical obligation.

Grid planners cannot afford either posture.

They have to assign probabilities.

Probability is uncomfortable in infrastructure because construction is discrete. A transmission line is not thirty percent built because planners think a data center has a thirty percent chance of arriving. A utility eventually has to order the transformer. A developer eventually has to pour concrete. A regulator eventually has to approve cost recovery.

The solution is not to make the forecast certain.

It is to make the customer participate in the uncertainty.

This is the logic behind minimum bills, collateral requirements, financial security, phased service, and milestone-based capacity commitments. The more credible the customer's commitment becomes, the more rational it is for the grid to build around the request. The less credible it is, the more the customer should bear the cost of reserving an option.

The price of uncertainty should not automatically land on everyone else's bill.

This is the principle that turns a data-center rate case into a planning innovation.

It also creates a feedback loop.

Better contracts produce better forecasts.

If reserving five hundred megawatts requires real financial commitment, developers will think harder before requesting it. If capacity can be held cheaply with little consequence, speculative requests will proliferate. The tariff can therefore improve information quality before it collects a dollar of electricity revenue.

That is a subtle but powerful function of price.

The grid is using money to ask whether the future is serious.

The same idea applies to supply.

Generator interconnection reform increasingly tries to make projects demonstrate readiness through deposits, site control, milestones, and other requirements. A queue full of speculative power plants is not much more useful than a queue full of speculative data centers. Both consume study resources and distort the picture of what is coming.

The demand shock is exposing that symmetry.

America needs better ways to distinguish projects from possibilities on both sides of the meter.

This may turn out to be one of the most valuable institutional consequences of the boom.

The country does not merely need more infrastructure.

It needs better evidence before committing infrastructure.

That is why the wide forecast ranges should remain visible rather than being collapsed into one dramatic number.

If data centers consume closer to the low end of the 2030 range, the investment challenge is still large but materially different from the high end. If efficiency accelerates, fewer megawatts may be required for a given level of computing. If AI usage expands faster than expected, efficiency gains may be overwhelmed. If inference becomes more distributed, geographic load patterns may change. If specialized chips become much more efficient, some campuses may grow more slowly. If new applications make computation cheap enough to saturate every business process, demand could surprise upward again.

Nobody knows which path dominates.

That is not a reason to stop building.

It is a reason to build institutions that survive forecast error.

A resilient tariff can protect other customers if one project disappears.

A transmission project serving several uses can remain valuable if one data-center forecast is wrong.

A flexible-service contract can reduce capacity obligations if the customer can actually curtail.

A modular generation plan can add resources in stages rather than betting everything on one scenario.

A regional forecast can be updated as projects pass concrete milestones.

The best response to uncertainty is optionality with accountability.

This is the opposite of the way boom narratives usually work.

Booms want one number.

They want a hockey stick. A trillion dollars. A gigawatt campus. A deadline. A shortage. A winner.

Grids do not have the luxury of narrative compression.

They have to work after the headline is wrong.

That is why the demand shock should not be measured only by how many terawatt-hours data centers eventually consume.

Its larger significance is that it is forcing the American power system to relearn how to plan for growth under uncertainty.

For twenty years, slow demand allowed the country to postpone that lesson.

Now the lesson has arrived in server racks.

There is one further consequence of treating uncertainty as a contractual problem rather than an embarrassment in the forecast. It changes the cadence of planning. Instead of asking a utility commission to approve a forty-year asset against one heroic demand curve, planners can tie successive commitments to evidence that arrives over time: executed service agreements, deposits that become nonrefundable, land and equipment orders, construction milestones, energization dates, and actual meter data from earlier phases of a campus. Each step converts a little uncertainty into evidence.

That does not eliminate the need to build ahead. A transformer ordered only after every server is installed is already late. Transmission cannot be permitted one month before a load arrives. But the sequence can be designed so that irreversible public commitments grow as the customer's own irreversible commitments grow. The developer still gets a path to power. The utility still plans ahead. The people already on the system are less likely to finance an option somebody else can abandon for free.

This is a more mature response to a boom than either disbelief or surrender. It accepts that the load may be real without treating every announced megawatt as destiny. The forecast becomes a living ledger of commitments rather than a single number printed once and defended forever.

The grid was built to balance electricity in real time.

The data-center era is forcing it to balance belief in the future too.
