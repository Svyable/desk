# The New Company Town

The server does not vote in the county election.

The transformer does not enroll a child in school.

The cooling tower does not eat lunch downtown.

This is why the local economics of a data center can feel strange even when the tax revenue is enormous.

A manufacturing plant traditionally announces itself through people. Shift changes fill roads. Workers buy houses. Suppliers open nearby. A town can see the payroll moving through restaurants, dealerships, schools, churches, medical practices, warehouses, machine shops, and municipal budgets. The industrial facility is an employer before it is an electrical load.

A hyperscale data center reverses the visibility.

Construction can be immense. The permanent workforce can be modest. The tax base can grow far faster than the population. The facility can consume the electrical output of a power plant while employing fewer people than a large hotel.

This is not evidence that the project has no economic value.

It means the value arrives through a different channel.

Virginia's experience makes the distinction unusually clear. A 2024 review by the state's Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission found that data centers contributed substantial economic activity, but that much of the employment and labor-income effect came during construction. Industry representatives told the commission that a typical quarter-million-square-foot building might have roughly fifty full-time workers, while peak construction could put around fifteen hundred people on the site. The same study found that local tax collections from data centers could become extremely important: among five Virginia localities with mature markets, data-center revenue ranged from less than one percent to nearly a third of total local revenue.

A new type of company town is forming.

The company does not need to employ most of the town.

It can still underwrite it.

That creates a political relationship different from the one Americans learned around steel mills, mines, auto plants, and military bases.

The old company town concentrated labor dependence.

The new one can concentrate fiscal dependence.

A county that receives a large share of its revenue from server equipment and industrial property can use the money for schools, public safety, parks, debt service, tax relief, road improvements, or other services. Residents may benefit even if they never work for the data-center operator. The local government can become wealthier because a globally distributed digital business placed machines inside its borders.

This is one reason communities compete so aggressively for the projects.

It is also why the backlash can be so fierce once the trade becomes visible.

A resident living near a campus does not experience a line in the county budget.

She experiences land clearing.

Substations.

Transmission corridors.

Backup-generator testing.

Construction traffic.

Water questions.

Low-frequency mechanical noise.

A wall where woods or farmland used to be.

The local bargain is therefore asymmetric in a second way.

The benefits can be widely distributed through public finance.

The burdens can be intensely local.

A county may gain millions in annual revenue while one neighborhood absorbs the visual and acoustic cost. The average resident can plausibly support the industry while the residents beside a proposed campus plausibly oppose that campus. Both can be responding rationally to different parts of the same transaction.

This is where the phrase company town becomes useful again.

Not because the technology company owns the houses.

Because the company's infrastructure begins shaping the town's choices.

A locality with a rapidly growing data-center tax base can lower other taxes or expand services. That makes the revenue politically valuable. Once budgets incorporate it, future development can feel less optional. A zoning dispute is no longer only about one parcel. It can become a debate over school funding, debt capacity, tax rates, and the fiscal model of the jurisdiction.

The industry gains leverage without asking for it explicitly.

Dependence does the work.

This is not unique to data centers. Casino towns, oil regions, university communities, defense-industry clusters, tourist destinations, and port cities all build public finances around concentrated economic engines. The difference is the peculiar combination of data-center characteristics: very high capital intensity, low relative permanent employment, heavy infrastructure demand, and extreme sensitivity to electricity availability.

The tax base can be enormous because the machinery is expensive.

The employment multiplier can be comparatively muted because the product is computation.

The grid footprint can be enormous because the machinery must run continuously.

That combination creates a local development puzzle.

What exactly should a community ask in return?

Jobs are the traditional answer.

For a data center, jobs alone may be the wrong metric.

A locality can bargain for tax revenue, infrastructure upgrades, water commitments, road work, noise protections, landscaping, buffers, workforce training, energy-system contributions, and contractual protections around abandoned phases. The relevant return is not simply payroll.

It is the whole local balance sheet.

This requires unusually competent local government.

Technology companies and data-center developers negotiate these projects repeatedly across many jurisdictions. A county may negotiate only a handful in its history. The developer has lawyers, engineers, site-selection teams, energy specialists, tax experts, and models of long-run ownership cost. A rural county may have a small planning staff and part-time elected officials.

The asymmetry can be enormous.

The remedy is not hostility to development.

It is institutional memory.

States can provide model ordinances, technical assistance, standard impact disclosures, templates for host-community agreements, and guidance on utility coordination. Localities can share experience rather than reinventing the bargain one rezoning at a time. The goal is to let the town understand the project in the same integrated way the developer does.

Electricity should be part of that understanding from the beginning.

A data-center campus is not merely a land-use decision.

It is an electrical-development decision whose consequences may extend beyond the locality. A county can approve zoning for a facility that the regional grid cannot serve on the requested schedule. The approval creates political expectations anyway. Developers announce investment. Land values move. Tax forecasts appear. Residents organize for or against the project. Then the utility explains that major transmission upgrades are required.

By that point, the wires look like someone else's obstacle.

They are actually part of the project.

This is one reason local planning processes are likely to become more power-aware.

A mature data-center jurisdiction may begin asking for evidence of a credible energy path before treating a development proposal as fully actionable. That does not mean the county should perform a transmission study. It means officials should understand whether the electrical assumptions are speculative, contingent, or contracted.

A two-gigawatt campus described without its power plan is like a port proposal described without water access.

The missing infrastructure is not a detail.

The land itself can become a grid asset.

This is another inversion.

For many industrial projects, the first question is whether the site is near workers, customers, raw materials, roads, rail, or ports. For hyperscale data centers, those factors matter, but access to high-capacity transmission and substations can dominate. A parcel near an existing electrical corridor can become more valuable than a prettier parcel closer to population.

The grid begins shaping real-estate geography.

Communities that once treated transmission lines as unpleasant background infrastructure can discover that the lines are the reason investment arrives.

That can change local attitudes toward expansion.

Not always in favor.

A locality may want the tax base but resist additional transmission corridors crossing residential or agricultural land. The data center may sit in one jurisdiction while the line passes through another that receives little direct revenue. Regional infrastructure distributes the physical footprint beyond the jurisdiction receiving the fiscal benefit.

This creates a classic infrastructure fairness problem.

Who hosts the wire?

Who receives the tax base?

Who pays the rate?

Who gets the jobs?

Who hears the generators?

The answers rarely line up neatly.

Water creates a similar mismatch, though the popular debate often simplifies it.

Data-center water use varies widely by cooling technology, climate, server density, and facility design. Some campuses can use substantial water. Others use far less. Virginia's legislative review found that many facilities used water at levels comparable to or below large office buildings, while a smaller number used much more. The state as a whole was not facing one uniform data-center water crisis, but some local systems had tighter constraints and less oversight over how limited water should be allocated among competing uses.

The lesson is not that data centers use too much water.

The lesson is that averages are weak planning tools for local infrastructure.

A county should care about the specific campus, the specific cooling system, the specific watershed or aquifer, and the specific drought condition.

The same principle applies to noise.

Most facilities may generate few complaints.

A poorly sited facility can still make a nearby resident miserable.

JLARC found that a substantial minority of Virginia data centers were located near residential areas and that low-frequency mechanical noise had created problems in some places. Ordinary noise ordinances were often designed for short-lived disturbances rather than continuous industrial hum.

The technical fix is straightforward compared with the political one.

Model the sound before construction.

Establish measurable standards.

Require buffers where appropriate.

Treat the facility as industrial infrastructure rather than a mysterious office building full of computers.

The political problem is that the best time to impose those conditions is before the project becomes central to a locality's fiscal plan.

Once dependence arrives, bargaining changes.

This is where tax incentives deserve more scrutiny.

Virginia's sales-tax exemption helped build the largest data-center cluster in the world. The state review estimated the exemption produced very large tax savings for the industry and was used by most qualifying operators. Data-center companies told the commission that such incentives mattered in site selection because they evaluate ownership costs over long horizons.

This is unsurprising.

Server equipment is replaced repeatedly. Exempting it from sales tax can materially change economics.

The harder question is what public objective the incentive serves after the industry has become established.

An incentive created to attract a nascent cluster can become an entitlement once the cluster has network advantages no competitor can easily reproduce. Fiber, customers, workforce, vendors, land familiarity, and electrical infrastructure can make a mature market attractive independent of the original subsidy.

At that point, the state should ask what behavior it is buying.

More construction?

More jobs?

More power demand?

Geographic diversification?

Energy flexibility?

Clean generation?

Local mitigation?

If the answer is simply more of everything, the policy can become self-defeating.

A tax incentive that accelerates projects faster than the grid can serve them may create pressure on the same public infrastructure the subsidy was meant to leverage.

Virginia's 2026 decision to impose a temporary electricity-consumption tax on certain data-center operators shows how quickly the politics can evolve. The state that once focused on attracting the industry was now also developing tools to capture more value from its power consumption.

That does not mean Virginia had turned against data centers.

It means the industry had become large enough to govern.

This is the normal lifecycle of infrastructure.

At first, a community asks how to attract it.

Then how to live with it.

Then how to tax it.

Then how to prevent dependence on it.

The last step is the hardest.

A mature company town needs an exit plan even while the company is thriving.

Data centers can look permanent because the buildings are heavy, the substations are large, and the power contracts are long. The economic product inside the building is unusually fast-moving. Hardware generations turn over. Cooling architecture changes. Network topology changes. A campus can be expanded, repurposed, sold, or eventually left behind.

The utility assets built to serve it may last much longer.

The locality therefore faces a version of stranded-cost risk too.

What happens if a campus does not complete all announced phases?

What happens if server equipment becomes less valuable for tax purposes?

What happens if a major operator consolidates workloads elsewhere?

What happens if a tax regime changes and future campuses move to another state?

What happens if a technology breakthrough reduces the electricity needed for a given amount of computation?

A county budget should not treat a boom as an annuity.

This is difficult politically because fiscal abundance creates immediate choices. The locality can increase recurring spending on the assumption that revenue persists. It can also use windfall revenue for one-time capital projects, debt reduction, reserves, tax stabilization, and infrastructure that remains useful if the industry changes.

The second approach is less exciting.

It is more resilient.

The old company towns learned this after the dominant employer closed.

The new company town can learn it before the closure.

There is another path to resilience: use the infrastructure created for the data center to widen the local economy.

A strengthened grid can support other industry.

Fiber can attract technology services.

Road and substation investments can make industrial land more valuable.

Construction activity can deepen local contractor capability.

Workforce programs can build electrical, mechanical, controls, and maintenance skills useful beyond one employer.

A data center can become an anchor without becoming a monoculture.

This is the best version of the development story.

The facility arrives because the locality has power and land.

Its investment pays for more capability.

That capability attracts other investment.

The town becomes less dependent on the original facility because the first project expanded the platform.

The worst version runs backward.

The locality gives away too much tax revenue to win the project.

Infrastructure spending becomes specialized around the campus.

Transmission corridors create local conflict.

The permanent employment disappoints expectations.

The tax base becomes politically indispensable anyway.

The county starts approving more projects to sustain the revenue model.

Electric demand compounds faster than infrastructure.

The grid constraint becomes the town's constraint.

This is how a successful development strategy can become a treadmill.

Neither outcome is predetermined.

The difference is governance.

Data centers are forcing communities to get better at distinguishing economic development from project acquisition.

Winning the campus is not the same as improving the place.

The relevant test comes years later.

Did the locality capture durable public value?

Did residents bear costs that were understood and mitigated?

Did new infrastructure create options beyond the original customer?

Did tax dependence remain manageable?

Did the town preserve the ability to say no to the next project?

That final question may be the most important.

A community that cannot say no is not negotiating.

It is servicing dependence.

The data-center company is not malicious for taking advantage of a favorable location. The utility is not malicious for building infrastructure to serve a paying customer. The county is not foolish for wanting a stronger tax base.

The problem appears when each participant optimizes its own transaction and nobody owns the combined consequence.

That is the recurring pattern of this book.

The data center turns private computing demand into public infrastructure decisions.

At the local level, it also turns global software economics into zoning, tax, water, road, and neighborhood decisions.

The cloud is supposed to make geography disappear.

The data-center boom is doing the opposite.

It is making specific acres, substations, aquifers, tax codes, transmission corridors, and county budgets matter more.

The servers may serve the whole world.

The hum belongs to somebody's backyard.

The tax payment belongs to somebody's school system.

The line crosses somebody's farm.

The electricity comes from somebody's grid.

That is the new company town.

It is not built around where the workers live.

It is built around where the electrons arrive.
