# Northern Virginia Runs Into Physics

Northern Virginia did not become one of the world's great concentrations of data centers because somebody planned an electrical monument to the internet.

The cluster accumulated.

Networks attracted networks. Customers wanted proximity to connectivity and to one another. Developers learned how to entitle and construct the buildings. Contractors learned the specialized work. Local governments discovered the tax base. Utilities learned the load. Land near fiber and power acquired a premium. Each successful project made the next one easier to imagine.

This is what economic geography looks like while it is working.

Then geography meets electricity.

A digital service can be globally distributed, but a substation is stubbornly local. A server request can cross the country, but electrons still have to reach the rack through actual equipment. A company can choose among regions before construction, but once a giant campus is built, the electrical system around it has to carry the consequences of that choice.

Northern Virginia is where the data-center industry's apparent placelessness ran into the physical map.

By the middle of the 2020s, the region's scale had become large enough that PJM, the regional transmission organization serving Virginia and parts of twelve other states plus the District of Columbia, was no longer dealing with data centers as a collection of unusual buildings. They had become a central planning variable.

In a 2026 filing at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, PJM described the Dominion zone in Virginia as the epicenter of data-center growth and reported metered data-center demand of about 3.6 gigawatts in December 2024. It also described an extraordinary volume of capacity requests submitted to Dominion during 2024—roughly forty thousand megawatts.

Those two numbers should never be placed beside each other without explanation.

The first was measured demand.

The second was requests.

A request can be a serious project, an early-stage project, an option, a duplicate path, a later phase, or something that never energizes. Adding forty gigawatts to a forecast as though every inquiry were a future meter reading would be absurd.

Ignoring the requests would be absurd too.

That tension is Northern Virginia in miniature.

The industry has become too large to dismiss and too fluid to forecast casually.

The electrical consequences are already measured in billions of dollars. PJM's 2026 filing pointed to more than $22 billion of baseline transmission investment across three recent regional planning procurements, overwhelmingly driven by projected data-center growth. Again, the number needs discipline. Those investments are part of a regional transmission plan, not a private extension cord to one building. Transmission projects can solve several reliability problems and can produce system benefits beyond the customer whose growth made the need urgent.

But the scale makes one fact difficult to avoid.

Buildings in Northern Virginia are changing wires far beyond Northern Virginia.

This is how a cluster becomes a grid institution.

The concentration creates advantages first.

A utility serving many large customers can gain experience with their construction schedules, load profiles, service requirements, and expansion patterns. Developers can find land near existing high-voltage infrastructure. The region can support specialized substations, contractors, equipment suppliers, and operating expertise. Network effects on the digital side reinforce infrastructure effects on the electrical side.

Then the same concentration creates fragility.

A transmission corridor has a limit.

A substation has a limit.

A generation zone has a limit.

A set of contingencies has a limit.

The limits are not always encountered during normal operation. Power systems are planned to survive failures. A line may have apparent capacity on an ordinary afternoon but become unacceptable if another line is lost. A transformer can carry normal load and still be inadequate under a contingency. A region can have enough annual generation and still lack capacity during a stressed hour.

This is why developers can look at a landscape covered in wires and still be told that power is scarce.

The scarcity exists in the network state, not the photograph.

Northern Virginia makes that distinction politically visible because the customers are so large and the growth so concentrated. A new transmission line is hard to hide. New rights-of-way cross communities that may receive little direct benefit from the data center creating the need. Substations occupy land. High-voltage corridors affect development. Generation built elsewhere may burn fuel because server racks here are drawing power around the clock.

The costs become regional while many of the immediate benefits are local or corporate.

This is where the simple economic-development story begins to fracture.

A county can receive tax revenue from a data center.

A homeowner fifty miles away can see a transmission project.

A utility customer in another part of the state can worry about rates.

A generator hundreds of miles away can receive higher market revenue because load has increased.

A neighboring jurisdiction can lose a project because the first county consumed available electrical capacity.

The data center exists on one tax parcel and several balance sheets.

That separation is common in infrastructure, but the density of the Northern Virginia cluster makes it unusually sharp.

It also complicates the meaning of local opposition.

A community resisting a new transmission corridor can be described as blocking infrastructure needed for economic growth. The community can also reasonably ask why it should host the visible burden of a project located elsewhere. A utility can argue that the line improves regional reliability. That may be true. It does not make the siting consequences evenly distributed.

Infrastructure is collective in service and local in impact.

The grid has always contained that contradiction.

Data centers magnify it because the new load often employs fewer permanent workers per megawatt than older industrial facilities. A giant factory could transform a town's labor market. A giant data center transforms its tax base and infrastructure more than its daily street traffic. The value can still be substantial, especially during construction and through equipment and tax revenue, but the political bargain feels different.

The community can see a building consuming industrial quantities of electricity without seeing an equally industrial number of people walking through the gate.

That visual imbalance is becoming part of the politics.

It can also obscure the real economic product.

The data center's employment footprint is only one measure of its value. The facility may support cloud services, artificial-intelligence training, enterprise software, scientific computation, network services, and digital products used by people far beyond the county. Measuring its value only by onsite jobs would be like measuring a port only by dockworkers while ignoring the commerce passing through it.

But the opposite mistake is equally easy.

A globally valuable service does not automatically entitle its infrastructure to local subsidy.

The local bargain still has to stand on its own.

Northern Virginia is beginning to teach the rest of the country how hard that bargain becomes after the cluster is mature.

Early projects often arrive when spare capacity exists. The first developer uses infrastructure built for a broader system. The cost of connection can be modest relative to the later projects. Each increment of growth consumes some of the headroom inherited from earlier planning.

Eventually the next megawatt is expensive.

That is a basic infrastructure pattern.

The hundredth house in a subdivision may fit on an existing road. The ten-thousandth requires another interchange. The first industrial customer may use spare water capacity. The next one requires a new treatment plant. A network can absorb growth until it reaches the threshold where a large, lumpy investment becomes necessary.

Data centers are discovering the lumpy side of electricity.

Transmission does not expand one megawatt at a time.

Neither do large substations or generation plants.

This creates a difficult fairness problem around the triggering customer.

Suppose a new data center is the project that causes a transmission upgrade to become necessary. Should that customer pay for the entire project?

The intuitive answer is yes: but for the data center, the line would not be needed now.

Then suppose the new line also improves reliability for existing customers, unlocks new generation, supports later industrial development, and has a useful life far beyond the first customer's contract. Charging the entire cost to one customer may understate the broader benefit.

Now reverse the problem.

Suppose the costs are spread widely because the line is a regional asset. The data center cancels after construction begins. Existing customers are left paying for capacity built around a demand forecast that failed.

There is no universal rule that makes both problems disappear.

The answer has to come from cost allocation, customer commitments, and the degree to which the asset is genuinely shared.

This is why the Northern Virginia story cannot be solved by saying data centers should pay their own way.

What is their own way?

The dedicated substation is easy to identify.

The regional transmission reinforcement is not.

The generation capacity needed during the system peak may be partly attributable to the load, but the same plant can serve everyone. The fuel system behind that plant may serve households and factories too. A new line can reduce congestion for existing customers even if a data center caused it to be built sooner.

The concept of causation becomes temporal.

Who caused an asset that the system would eventually have needed anyway?

The data center may be responsible for moving the investment forward by ten years, not for the entire existence of the asset.

That distinction will matter more as utilities around the country encounter similar clusters.

Northern Virginia is not merely a cautionary tale about concentration.

It is a laboratory for timing.

The region shows what happens when the location value of a cluster grows faster than the surrounding infrastructure can be expanded.

At first, companies may accept delays because the cluster's advantages are so strong. Then the delay itself becomes part of site selection. New projects look elsewhere. Later phases migrate. Companies explore onsite generation. Utilities develop new rate classes. Regulators ask for stronger financial commitments. The cluster begins exporting demand growth to other regions because its own success raised the marginal cost of staying.

This is a classic industrial response to scarcity.

The irony is that the product being exported is digital capacity while the constraint causing the migration is physical electricity.

The geography of AI may therefore be written partly by transformer yards.

This is not the map many people expected from cloud computing.

The cloud was marketed as abstraction from place. Customers rented computation without caring which specific server performed it. Infrastructure became a service. A developer in New York could deploy software in Virginia, Oregon, Ireland, or Singapore without owning a building there.

The abstraction was real for the customer.

It was never real for the operator.

Every region required land, substations, fiber, cooling, backup, maintenance, permits, taxes, and power. The cloud hid location from the buyer by making the provider extraordinarily sophisticated about location.

AI is making that hidden geography important again.

The amount of capital required for leading-edge compute means companies cannot treat every region as interchangeable. Power availability can determine how many accelerators can be installed. Network connectivity can determine which workloads make sense. Water and cooling design can constrain sites. Equipment supply can constrain energization. Local regulation can affect construction speed.

The cloud remains abstract at the software interface.

The underlying factory has become more industrial.

Northern Virginia is one of the places where that industrial nature is hardest to ignore.

PJM's role makes the story larger than one state.

The regional grid operator has to preserve reliability across a multi-state system. It plans transmission and operates wholesale markets under federal jurisdiction while the retail customer may be served by a state-regulated utility. This means a data-center load in Virginia can create obligations that cross institutional boundaries before electrons ever cross a state line.

The utility knows the customer.

PJM knows the regional system.

The Virginia State Corporation Commission knows the retail rate and utility obligations.

FERC knows the interstate transmission tariff and wholesale rules.

Local governments know land use.

The customer experiences one power problem.

The system experiences several legal problems stacked together.

This fragmentation is not an accidental bureaucratic annoyance. It reflects real differences in responsibility. A local utility should not decide by itself how a multi-state transmission project is paid for. A federal regulator should not decide a county zoning question. A regional grid operator should not decide whether a local tax incentive is worthwhile.

But the customer's clock does not slow down because authority is distributed rationally.

That is the institutional bottleneck hidden inside the physical bottleneck.

Northern Virginia's experience suggests that the next generation of grid reform has to address both.

Building more transmission matters.

Making the planning process faster matters.

Making load forecasts more credible matters.

Making customers financially responsible for commitments matters.

Giving flexible loads a way to connect without pretending they need full firm service at every hour may matter.

Coordinating the utility and regional forecasts matters.

None of these reforms alone creates spare electrical capacity.

Together they can reduce the amount of capacity wasted by uncertainty.

This is an important distinction.

The cheapest new megawatt is sometimes the megawatt that does not have to be built because the system has better information or more flexibility.

If a data center can accept curtailment during a few critical hours, the grid may not need to build the same amount of firm capacity to support it. If a customer posts collateral, a utility can build with less risk of abandonment. If duplicate requests are removed from a forecast, planners can avoid overbuilding. If transmission is planned for several future uses rather than one immediate problem, the resulting asset may remain valuable even if one project changes.

Better institutions can create effective capacity before new steel arrives.

Northern Virginia needs both.

The physical system still has to expand. The region's data-center base is already real, not merely forecast. Existing facilities draw power every day. New transmission and generation cannot be replaced by a clever tariff.

But a clever tariff can determine who carries the risk of the next expansion.

This is why the Virginia State Corporation Commission's later decision to create a separate class for very large loads should be understood as part of the same story as the transmission buildout. The commission was not regulating software. It was responding to the financial consequences of concentration.

When one customer class becomes large enough to drive major infrastructure, ordinary rate design starts to look like a weak description of the relationship.

The utility is not merely selling kilowatt-hours.

It is reserving future system capacity.

That reservation has value even before the customer uses it.

A hotel charges for a room that cannot be sold to someone else. An airline values a seat differently once capacity is scarce. An electric grid cannot reserve three hundred megawatts in quite the same way because network capacity is shared and stochastic, but the economic intuition survives: holding a large option for one customer can prevent other uses or force investment.

The customer should pay something for the option.

How much is the emerging question.

Northern Virginia reached that question first because its cluster grew first.

Other regions will reach it through different paths.

Texas may confront raw scale and speed. Georgia may confront utility generation plans built around multiple campuses. Pennsylvania may confront nuclear and gas opportunities. The Midwest may attract projects seeking abundant land and generation. Western states may confront water, transmission, and renewable-resource tradeoffs. Each region will tell itself a local story.

The underlying mechanism will be familiar.

A digital industry will discover that geography still exists.

A utility will discover that the customer is too large for ordinary assumptions.

A regulator will discover that the cost of forecast error can no longer be hidden across millions of small bills.

A community will discover that the cloud has a fence line.

Northern Virginia did not fail by becoming a data-center cluster.

The region succeeded so thoroughly that it exposed the infrastructure beneath the abstraction.

That is a more useful lesson than either celebration or warning.

Clusters create productivity because concentration makes some things easier.

They create infrastructure pressure for exactly the same reason.

The same network effect that makes a region valuable to the next data center can make the next megawatt harder to supply.

Economics pulls activity together.

Physics eventually sends the bill.

And once that bill becomes large enough, scarcity starts rewriting the cluster itself.

The next important unit of competition may no longer be the individual site. It may be the **portfolio of regions** a hyperscaler can move among. A company with viable expansion options in Virginia, Texas, the Midwest, and the Mountain West can treat electrical scarcity differently from a developer whose business depends on one metropolitan market. It can shift the next campus before construction begins, even when the servers already operating in Virginia cannot move physically at all.

That distinction matters for policymakers tempted to think every announced project must be won locally. A jurisdiction can offer tax incentives and streamlined permits and still lose the project because a transformer delivery date or transmission constraint dominates the economics. Conversely, a region with modest incentives but credible power delivery can become more attractive as electricity moves from a background input to a gating resource.

This creates a new form of competition among grids. Utilities are not merely competing on cents per kilowatt-hour. They are competing on the credibility of a **power-delivery schedule**. Can the substation be energized when promised? Can generation be added without creating unacceptable reliability risk? Can a customer expand in phases? Can the utility distinguish a real expansion plan from a speculative reservation quickly enough that serious projects are not trapped behind unserious ones?

Those questions turn reliability administration into economic development.

They also create a healthy constraint on subsidy races. A tax abatement can change a spreadsheet. It cannot create thermal capacity on a transmission corridor. Political enthusiasm cannot accelerate a large transformer that has not been ordered. The harder electricity becomes to obtain, the more site selection is forced back toward measurable infrastructure rather than promotional promises.

Northern Virginia therefore offers a second lesson beyond the dangers of concentration. Mature clusters eventually reveal which advantages are truly self-reinforcing and which were borrowed from spare infrastructure. Fiber density can compound. Skilled contractors can compound. Customer proximity can compound. Electrical headroom does not compound by itself. It has to be rebuilt.

That is the moment a successful digital cluster becomes an infrastructure project.

The cloud may still look weightless from the browser.

Its next region is being chosen by steel, rights-of-way, substations, and time.
