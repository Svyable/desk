# The First Machine Constituents

Imagine opening your phone one morning.

Your personal AI has reviewed eighteen pieces of legislation being considered by your city, state, and national government.

It knows your finances, commute, profession, children, neighborhood, environmental preferences, and previous political views.

It summarizes three proposals that materially affect you.

One would raise your property tax.

Another would change regulations affecting your business.

A third would fund a transportation project likely to reduce your commute.

Your agent explains each proposal.

You ask questions.

It finds counterarguments.

Then you say:

“Tell my representatives I support the transportation bill but oppose the tax provision unless the exemption threshold changes.”

Your AI sends the message.

Millions of other agents do the same.

Suddenly democratic participation is no longer constrained by citizens' time.

This sounds empowering.

It also introduces an extraordinary problem.

Who is speaking?

The citizen?

The model?

The company that built the model?

The information sources used by the model?

The political groups trying to influence it?

Democracy has spent centuries deciding who gets a vote.

It may spend the next century deciding what counts as a voice.
