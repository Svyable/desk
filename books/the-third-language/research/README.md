# Research brief — The Third Language

## Book claim

*The Third Language* argues that an important class of communication between humans and other animals is missed when the scientific and popular question is framed only as translation.

The book’s central object is the **relationship-specific communicative system** that can emerge when two unlike animals repeatedly coordinate: a negotiated layer of gaze, movement, timing, distance, sound, touch, tools, routine, expectation, refusal, and repair that neither participant brings complete to the relationship.

The claim is intentionally narrower than “animals have language” and broader than “animals emit signals.” It treats reciprocal adaptation as worthy of study in its own right.

## What would make the thesis wrong or unhelpful

The concept should be weakened or discarded if research showed that apparent relationship-specific communication is generally reducible to one-sided conditioning with no meaningful partner specificity; that familiarity does not improve prediction or coordination under controlled tests; that apparent repair does not depend on receiver response; that animals do not differentiate humans according to response history beyond simple reinforcement conditions; or that individual/relational models add no predictive value beyond species-wide behavior and immediate context.

The thesis should also be narrowed if “third language” proves too broad to distinguish communication from ordinary cue use. The manuscript therefore uses reciprocal consequence, partner sensitivity, repair, and the ability of either participant to alter the other’s next move as stronger indicators than mere correlation.

## Prior-art boundary

The book does **not** claim to be the first work to describe co-created human-animal meaning.

Relevant prior traditions include:

- Thomas Sebeok and zoosemiotics: animal sign systems and cross-species semiotic questions long predate this manuscript.
- Donna Haraway’s companion-species and contact-zone work: human and animal partners train, shape, and become with one another rather than existing as isolated endpoints.
- Barbara Smuts’s writing on interspecies relationship and subjectivity: close human-animal relations can be emergent, improvisational, and co-created.
- Don Kulick’s review of human-animal communication: human-animal communication is an established anthropological topic with a substantial literature.
- Recent interspecies-pragmatics and interaction-analysis work: embodied human-animal interaction is increasingly treated as sequential, multimodal, and jointly produced.
- Animal-computer interaction: interfaces can be designed around animal sensory, motor, welfare, and agency needs rather than only human usability.

The distinct trade-book contribution is to put the **shared relationship-specific code itself** at the center and follow that object across domestic interaction, comparative cognition, animal communication, experimental repair, AI decoding, interface design, and ethics.

The phrase “third language” is therefore a synthesis and a proposed research object, not a priority claim that reciprocal interspecies meaning has never been discussed.

## Factual discipline

The manuscript separates:

1. **Observation** — the measured behavior or signal.
2. **Contingency** — what changes with audience, recipient, context, knowledge, history, or response.
3. **Interpretation** — the semantic, intentional, emotional, or cognitive account proposed to explain the pattern.

Popular shorthand such as “name,” “ask,” “repair,” “argument,” and “language” is used only after the narrower evidence is described, or with qualifiers such as “name-like.”

No claim that an animal “said” an English sentence is treated as scientific fact unless a validated symbolic interface would justify that level of description. Current AI systems are described as pattern-discovery, prediction, generation, classification, or experimental-interface systems according to what they actually do.

## Core evidence lanes by chapter

### Chapter 1 — The Animal Replies

Core lane: great-ape intentional communication and dog-human social cognition. The chapter’s test—recipient response changes the sender’s next behavior—draws on audience attention, response waiting, persistence, and elaboration in ape gesture research, then generalizes cautiously to relationship-specific human-animal interaction.

### Chapter 2 — The Silence Error

Core lane: multimodality and the distinction between signals and cues. Cat slow-blink, name discrimination, owner-voice recognition, and social referencing supply controlled examples showing that a species can respond socially without performing in a dog-like way.

### Chapter 3 — Before Words, Turns

Core lane: interaction sequence, turn-taking, and repair. Great ape research is used because it operationalizes recipient attention and communicative persistence without requiring a human-language interpretation.

### Chapter 4 — The Dog at the Door

Core lane: domestication and human-oriented social cognition. Hare et al. (2002), Miklósi et al. (2003), Nagasawa et al. (2015), and behavioral-synchronization work support the chapter’s claims about dog sensitivity to human cues and reciprocal interaction. The chapter does not claim that all such effects are uniquely caused by domestication; comparative and developmental alternatives remain live.

### Chapter 5 — What the Cat Does With Its Eyes

Core lane: cat-human communication. Saito and colleagues on voice/name discrimination; Humphrey et al. on slow blinking; Merola et al. on social referencing. The chapter deliberately distinguishes reception from compliance.

### Chapter 6 — The Horse Asks for Help

Core lane: human-directed signaling in domesticated ungulates. Ringhofer & Yamamoto’s horse work and Nawroth et al.’s goat study support the narrower claim that humans can be recruited into unsolvable tasks. Physiological synchronization work is treated cautiously and not equated with shared emotion.

### Chapter 7 — The Bird Points

Core lane: raven referential gestures, crow human-face recognition, and parrot symbolic learning. Pika & Bugnyar support the gesture example; Marzluff et al. support long-lasting human-face discrimination; Pepperberg supplies controlled evidence for learned vocal labels and relational concepts in an African grey parrot.

### Chapter 8 — Names in the Water

Core lane: bottlenose dolphin signature whistles and artificial symbol comprehension. King, Janik, and colleagues support the individually distinctive learned-label and copying claims; Herman’s research supports dolphin comprehension of human pointing and structured artificial commands. “Name” is used as a functional analogy, not equivalence to the full human proper-name system.

### Chapter 9 — Names Across the Savannah

Core lane: Pardo et al. (2024) on receiver-specific, name-like elephant calls and McComb et al. (2014) on elephants discriminating human voices by socially relevant categories. The 2025 author correction to the Pardo paper should remain attached to this evidence lane.

### Chapter 10 — The Whale Alphabet Is Not a Dictionary

Core lane: Sharma et al. (2024) on contextual and combinatorial structure in sperm-whale codas. The manuscript explicitly rejects the inference that discovery of rhythm, tempo, rubato, and ornamentation is equivalent to semantic translation.

### Chapter 11 — The Dance Is Listening

Core lane: honeybee waggle-dance information, social learning, and audience effects. Dong et al. (2023) support social calibration by experienced dancers; 2026 work on audience effects supports the chapter’s key example that receiver-side conditions can alter signal precision.

### Chapter 12 — The Bat Argument

Core lane: Prat, Taub & Yovel on Egyptian fruit-bat vocalizations containing information associated with emitter, addressee, social context, and outcome. The annotated corpus is important because it links ordinary social noise to individual interactions rather than relying only on dramatic alarm contexts.

### Chapter 13 — A Message Made of Skin

Core lane: Brown, Garwood & Williamson (2012) on split tactical displays in mourning cuttlefish; reviews and robotic interaction work on electric fish. The chapter’s technological proposal—interfaces optimized for mutual learnability rather than English translation—is a synthesis, not a claim that current systems have achieved it broadly.

### Chapter 14 — The Clever Hans Problem

Core lane: Oskar Pfungst’s Clever Hans investigations and modern experimental-control logic. Button studies are used as the contemporary case requiring blinding, generalization, and alternative-explanation tests.

### Chapter 15 — Buttons Are Not Language

Core lane: Bastos and colleagues’ 2024 controlled studies of soundboard-trained dogs. Evidence supports responses to some button-associated words and nonrandom/nonimitative two-button patterns; it does not establish human-like grammar or stable abstract semantics for all labels.

### Chapter 16 — Repair

Core lane: chimpanzee and orangutan persistence/elaboration after apparent communicative failure. Repair is treated as a stronger indicator of reciprocal organization than isolated signal matching.

### Chapter 17 — No Is a Complete Sentence

Core lane: welfare and cooperative-care logic. The chapter makes an ethical argument from communication rather than claiming an animal behavior is legally equivalent to human consent. Refusal is treated as information with practical weight, not an absolute veto in emergencies.

### Chapter 18 — The Private Dialect

Core lane: synthesis and prior-art boundary. This chapter explicitly names Haraway, Smuts, Kulick, zoosemiotics, interspecies pragmatics, and interaction research so the book does not create a false novelty claim. Its testable predictions are new hypotheses derived from the synthesis.

### Chapter 19 — The Translator’s Mistake

Core lane: DolphinGemma, Project CETI, Earth Species Project, and peer-reviewed animal-communication machine-learning work. First-party project sources describe systems; peer-reviewed papers carry behavioral claims. The chapter distinguishes next-signal prediction and generative plausibility from semantic translation.

### Chapter 20 — The Conversation We Owe the Living World

Core lane: synthesis. No new scientific mechanism is introduced. Ethical implications follow from the earlier evidence and remain framed as arguments rather than discoveries.

## Falsification tests for the “private dialect” hypothesis

A strong research program could test at least six claims:

1. **Familiarity advantage:** familiar human partners predict the next behavior or resolving outcome of a known animal better than unfamiliar novices and, for relationship-local signals, unfamiliar experts.
2. **Compression:** familiar pairs achieve recurrent outcomes with lower signal intensity, shorter latency, or fewer repair turns than newly paired participants.
3. **Partner specificity:** the same animal uses measurably different signaling strategies with humans who have different response histories.
4. **Transfer failure and repair:** replacing the familiar human increases clarification, escalation, or modality switching for local signals while species-general signals transfer more readily.
5. **Model hierarchy:** species + individual + relationship-history models outperform species-only models on prospective prediction of interaction outcomes without relying on environmental leakage.
6. **Responsiveness effect:** when low-intensity animal signals reliably alter human behavior, escalation becomes less necessary in matched contexts over time.

Any of these could fail. The book’s thesis should become narrower if they do.

## Claims to avoid in publicity

Do not say:

- “Sven Hardy Benson discovered the first language between humans and animals.”
- “Nobody has ever written about co-created interspecies communication.”
- “AI can now translate whales/dolphins/elephants.”
- “Dogs using buttons have language.”
- “Elephants literally use human-style names.”
- “Animals can consent exactly as humans do.”

Safer positioning:

- “A new way to think about the communication that forms **between** species rather than inside either one.”
- “The book asks whether the relationship itself develops a private code.”
- “AI may become most useful not by giving animals a human voice, but by helping humans notice, test, and respond to reciprocal meaning.”
