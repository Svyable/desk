# Before Words, Turns

Conversation has a shape before it has a vocabulary.

One participant does something. Another participant has an opportunity to respond. The first participant behaves as if the response mattered. Sometimes the response satisfies whatever was underway. Sometimes it does not. Sometimes the first participant repeats, waits, exaggerates, changes channels, leaves, or tries again.

This sequence is so basic that it can hide beneath the glamour of words.

If I ask you a question and immediately walk away before you can answer, the sentence was grammatically complete and socially defective. If I say your name but never check whether you heard me, I have produced an address without completing the interaction it was meant to begin. If you misunderstand me and I continue as though you understood, our vocabulary has not saved us.

Turn structure is part of meaning.

Humans are exquisitely sensitive to it. We notice interruptions measured in fractions of a second. A pause that is ordinary in one culture can feel uncomfortable in another. We overlap, yield, hold the floor, display attention, signal completion, repair trouble, and anticipate when a turn is ending. Much of this happens faster than conscious grammatical planning could explain.

Before children become fluent speakers, they enter versions of this rhythm. Caregivers respond to cries, movements, gaze, facial expression, and early vocalization. Infants learn that some acts produce socially contingent effects. Researchers studying early human interaction have long treated this back-and-forth not as decorative pre-language but as part of the developmental ground from which language emerges.

That fact should make us cautious about demanding words from other animals before granting them communicative sophistication.

Turns are not uniquely human.

Researchers have described turn-taking structures across a range of species, though the mechanisms and functions differ. Songbirds alternate calls. Primates exchange vocalizations and gestures. Marmosets are especially striking because vocal exchanges between individuals can show orderly timing over distances that prevent constant visual contact. Great ape gestural communication often includes waiting for a response. Dolphins can exchange signature whistles. Social mammals coordinate contact calls. Even when these systems are far from human conversation, the fact that timing is organized around another participant changes what the signal is.

The receiver is not scenery.

This becomes especially clear in great apes.

Decades of gesture research with chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas, and orangutans have produced large repertoires of observed gestures and arguments about how intentional they are, how much is innate, how meanings vary, and what should count as evidence. One of the most useful contributions of this work is methodological. Researchers do not have to infer intention from a gesture’s appearance alone. They can examine how the signaler behaves around the recipient.

Was the recipient attending?

Did the signaler orient toward a particular individual?

Was there a pause after signaling?

Did the signaler persist when the apparent goal was not achieved?

Did the signal change when the recipient seemed not to understand?

Did signaling stop after a particular social outcome occurred?

None of these questions requires mind reading. Together they reveal a behavior organized as communication rather than discharge.

A movement becomes more interesting when its next moment depends on somebody else.

One study of wild chimpanzee gestures examined persistence and elaboration after unsuccessful communication. Another body of work with orangutans found patterns consistent with different responses to partial versus complete misunderstanding: individuals might repeat or elaborate when a recipient appeared partly responsive and switch tactics when the interaction failed more completely. The precise interpretation should remain cautious. But the general phenomenon matters. Communication failure does not always end the sequence. It can become information.

That is repair.

We will return to repair later because it may be one of the strongest windows into communication across species. For now, notice what repair requires at minimum: the sender’s next behavior has to be sensitive to what the receiver just did.

A signal followed by a fixed second signal regardless of outcome is one thing.

A signal followed by a different strategy when the receiver fails is another.

The distinction also appears in ordinary human-animal life.

A dog may stand by a door. If the human does not notice, the dog may move closer, vocalize, scratch, retrieve the human, or abandon the attempt. A parrot may use one call, wait, and escalate. A horse may orient toward a person, then toward an inaccessible resource, then return attention to the person. A cat may try the bedroom door, then the sleeping human, then an object that reliably makes noise when moved.

Anecdotes cannot tell us which cognitive mechanism produced each sequence. But they can suggest where experiments should look.

The valuable unit is not the isolated act.

It is act, response, consequence.

That three-part view sounds simple. In practice it rearranges the field.

Suppose we record a hundred thousand animal vocalizations and discover ten recurring acoustic clusters. The obvious next task is to infer what each cluster “means.” Researchers might correlate them with feeding, travel, aggression, affiliation, identity, age, sex, social rank, predator presence, or environmental conditions.

Now imagine instead that each vocalization is embedded in an interaction graph. We know who was nearby, who was oriented toward whom, which individual vocalized, what the likely addressee did in the next second, five seconds, and minute, whether the caller repeated, whether another individual replied, whether the group changed direction, whether the distance between two animals increased, and whether the sequence ended after some apparent outcome.

The problem becomes much richer.

A call that sounds identical across two contexts may do different work because the receivers differ. Two acoustically different calls may turn out to serve similar interactional functions. A vocalization that correlates weakly with any environmental event may correlate strongly with the identity or knowledge state of a social partner.

This is why the rise of biologging and synchronized multimodal data matters so much. The future of animal communication research may depend less on finding more sound than on attaching more world to the sound.

Timing is part of that world.

Every species experiences temporal constraints imposed by bodies and environments. Sound travels. Vision can be blocked. Social groups spread out. An animal has to wait long enough for a recipient to respond but not so long that a relevant opportunity disappears. Underwater communication operates in acoustic environments very different from a forest canopy or a burrow. A fast visual display can contain structure invisible to ordinary human observation until slowed by video. Vibrational communication may move through a substrate our instruments have to reveal.

Turn-taking therefore cannot be reduced to one human-like gap between speakers.

The interesting question is whether timing is contingent on the other participant.

That distinction helps avoid a common error: seeing alternation and calling it conversation. Two frogs can call alternately because each is responding to the acoustic environment through relatively simple mechanisms. That may still be communication, but it does not demonstrate the flexible turn management of a human discussion. Pattern alone is not enough. We want to know how the pattern changes with social context, individual identity, audience, outcome, and disruption.

Disruption is often revealing.

A smooth routine can conceal its rules because nothing goes wrong. Interrupt the sequence and the participants show you what they expected.

Human conversation works this way. If I reach for your hand and you unexpectedly move it away, my next action reveals what I thought was happening. If you answer a question I did not ask, my repair reveals the mismatch. If a familiar greeting receives silence, the silence becomes salient because the routine had a predicted next step.

Animal interaction can be probed similarly, with ethical care.

Researchers can alter the availability of an expected object, the attentional state of a human experimenter, the visibility of a recipient, the knowledge available to a social partner, or whether a familiar response occurs. The animal’s adjustment tells us which variables belonged to the communicative routine.

Dogs have been tested in versions of this logic for decades. Researchers can compare whether a dog steals food more readily when a human’s eyes are closed, whether a dog follows a point or gaze cue, whether it distinguishes a knowledgeable human from an ignorant one in a task, whether it persists differently after an ambiguous cue, or whether human attention changes its signaling.

Individual studies differ in design and result, and strong claims about dogs “reading minds” usually outrun the evidence. But the experimental strategy is powerful: break the expected relationship and watch what the animal notices.

The third language grows through the same process outside the laboratory.

A person gives a cue and receives an unexpected response.

The first temptation is to say the animal is wrong.

Sometimes it is. Training relies on establishing discriminations, and animals make errors. But the mismatch can also expose ambiguity in the human’s behavior. A dog trained to respond to a spoken cue may actually be following a hand motion. A horse may have learned the rider’s weight shift more strongly than the intended verbal instruction. A cat may respond to the routine preceding a word rather than the word itself.

When the usual context changes, the hidden cue becomes visible.

This can be humbling.

Humans imagine that we are the deliberate side of interspecies communication. We say the cue. We set the task. We decide what the animal learned.

Yet our bodies leak information continuously.

We look toward targets before pointing. We tense before an expected response. We shift weight. We reach toward rewards. We change breathing. We alter the leash. We repeat cues more urgently when uncertain. We orient our feet toward the direction we expect. We make microscopic movements at the moment we believe an animal is approaching the correct answer.

Clever Hans made this problem famous because the humans around him did not know they were helping.

The lesson is not that communication is impossible to study. It is that turn-taking includes involuntary turns.

A rigorous experiment controls them.

A real relationship incorporates them.

Those two facts can coexist.

Suppose a dog has learned to follow a nearly invisible shift in its handler’s shoulders during agility training. In a study intended to test whether the dog understands a verbal label, that shoulder movement is a confound. In competition, it may be part of a beautifully refined interspecies signal system.

Context decides whether a cue is contamination or communication.

This is one reason expertise can look like magic from the outside.

An experienced animal handler often appears to do very little. The movements are small because large movements are unnecessary. Timing has been tuned through thousands of repetitions. The animal anticipates patterns. The human anticipates the animal. Correction occurs before an outsider notices deviation.

The system has compressed.

Novices are louder.

They repeat cues, exaggerate movements, add pressure, miss early responses, and then apply stronger signals because the animal “isn’t listening.” The animal may be listening to too many things at once.

Communication can improve when the human becomes quieter.

That does not mean mystical energy. It means signal-to-noise ratio.

If one cue reliably predicts one consequence and the human pauses long enough to observe a response, the animal has a solvable learning problem. If five cues occur together, vary from trial to trial, and continue through the animal’s correct response, the relevant contingency becomes harder to identify.

People often ask how to get animals to listen.

A better question is whether our turns are readable.

Readability is not the same as simplicity. A person can be very consistent while using multiple modalities. A shepherd may combine whistle, body orientation, location, livestock movement, and a dog’s prior training. A rider may communicate through reins, seat, legs, voice, and balance. A wildlife researcher may deliberately minimize direct approach while using placement, routine, or food in carefully controlled ways.

What matters is that the animal can discover structure.

The same is true in the reverse direction.

Humans become better receivers when we learn which variables are stable for the animal. A dog’s bark by itself may be ambiguous. Bark plus location plus body orientation plus time of day may not be. A horse’s head movement may mean little in isolation but a sequence of weight shift, ear orientation, neck tension, and withdrawal may reliably precede something the handler should notice. A cat’s meow can be interpreted partly through who is present, where the cat is, and which household routine has just been interrupted.

The third language is often context-heavy because neither species shares the other’s symbolic system deeply enough to strip context away.

Human language performs a remarkable trick: it allows us to refer beyond the immediate environment. We can say *There was a fox near the river yesterday* while sitting in a locked office miles away. Some animal communication systems also transmit information about absent things or distant locations in ways that challenge simple here-and-now accounts; the honeybee waggle dance is the classic example.

But much interspecies communication remains anchored to shared circumstances.

The leash is present.

The door is closed.

The food is inaccessible.

The path is blocked.

The stranger is approaching.

The body hurts here.

The game should continue.

The touch should stop.

A translator obsessed with nouns may miss that the central problem is coordination around a present situation.

This is not lesser communication.

A surgical team coordinating during an operation is highly contextual too. So are firefighters, dancers, musicians, athletes, and people carrying the sofa down the stairs. Shared tasks reduce the need to state everything explicitly because the environment already holds part of the meaning.

Animals and humans often communicate best when they share a task.

This helps explain why working relationships can produce extraordinary interspecies coordination without resembling language lessons. Herding dogs, detection dogs, search-and-rescue teams, guide dogs, livestock handlers, falconers, riders, and animal-care professionals develop repertoires around consequential joint action.

The work gives signals a reason to become precise.

If an ambiguous cue sends a dog to the wrong side of livestock, the error is visible. If a rider’s signal conflicts with balance, the horse’s movement reveals it. If a detection dog’s change of behavior is missed, the handler loses information. The environment provides feedback to both participants.

Shared work creates external truth.

Household relationships have fewer objective scoreboards, which is why projection can flourish there. If someone believes a cat is “being spiteful,” almost any inconvenient behavior can be interpreted as evidence. If someone believes a dog “feels guilty” because of a particular posture, the person may overlook research suggesting that the familiar “guilty look” is strongly influenced by the human’s behavior and expectation rather than reliable knowledge of a past transgression.

The interaction still matters. The interpretation may not.

A disciplined third-language approach therefore separates three layers.

First: what happened physically?

Second: what contingency can be demonstrated?

Third: what mental-state interpretation is justified?

The physical layer might be: the dog lowered its body, turned its head, and avoided direct gaze as the owner approached and spoke sharply.

The contingency layer might be: this posture occurs more often in response to scolding, including when the dog did not perform the forbidden act.

The mental-state layer—*the dog knows it did wrong and feels guilty*—requires more evidence.

Keeping the layers separate does not make the interaction less meaningful. It makes us less likely to reward ourselves for a story the animal did not tell.

This discipline will matter increasingly as machines begin generating interpretations for us.

A model can put fluent language around uncertain patterns with alarming ease. Feed it a sequence of animal sounds and enough context, and it can produce a plausible sentence. The sentence may feel more explanatory than a probability distribution or cluster label. That fluency is exactly why caution is needed.

An animal does not become better understood because a machine has become better at sounding sure.

The strongest systems will expose uncertainty and preserve interactional structure. They will say, in effect: this pattern occurs disproportionately when these individuals are separated; it is often followed by approach from a particular partner; the signaler tends to repeat when no approach occurs; acoustic variation predicts identity; the function remains uncertain.

That description may be less satisfying than *Come here, Mara.*

It is scientifically richer because it tells us what to test next.

A good science of animal communication should make reply possible.

Once we can identify a candidate pattern, we can play it back, vary it, alter context, observe receivers, and learn whether our hypothesis survives contact with the animal. Playback experiments have long done versions of this. Modern generative tools may eventually allow far more precise and dynamic interaction, but the principle remains old: do not merely classify what the animal says. See what happens when the animal hears you say it back.

Then pay attention to the next turn.

The dangerous fantasy is that one successful playback completes the translation.

The more interesting reality is that the animal may answer differently.

That difference is where conversation starts.
