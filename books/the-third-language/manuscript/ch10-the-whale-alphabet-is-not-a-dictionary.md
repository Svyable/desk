# The Whale Alphabet Is Not a Dictionary

Sperm whales click.

The sentence is true in roughly the same way that “humans move air through their throats” is true. It identifies a mechanism and hides a civilization of structure.

Sperm whales use powerful echolocation clicks to navigate and hunt in the deep ocean. They also exchange patterned sequences of clicks known as codas in social communication. Some coda patterns vary across social groups and have been associated with what researchers call vocal clans—large cultural groupings whose members share aspects of coda repertoire and social identity.

For decades, researchers recorded these sequences, described coda types, compared repertoires, and tried to understand how social learning and culture organize whale communication.

Then the datasets grew.

In 2024, researchers working with Project CETI published an analysis in *Nature Communications* examining thousands of codas from sperm whales in the eastern Caribbean. Using computational methods, they described a richer combinatorial structure than a simple inventory of fixed coda types. They identified dimensions they called rhythm, tempo, rubato, and ornamentation—features that could vary systematically across sequences and context.

The phrase that traveled furthest was “sperm whale phonetic alphabet.”

It is a wonderful phrase.

It is also a trap.

An alphabet is not a dictionary.

Structure is not meaning.

The fact that a communicative system can be decomposed into reusable components does not tell us what those components do socially, any more than discovering the phonemes of an unknown human language would tell us what its sentences mean.

This distinction may become the single most important discipline in AI-assisted animal communication.

Pattern discovery is accelerating faster than semantic understanding.

Machine learning excels at finding regularities across large datasets. Give a model thousands of codas aligned with caller identity, timing, behavioral context, social relationship, movement, and sequence, and it can detect statistical organization that human analysts may miss. It can cluster, classify, predict, compress, and generate. With enough multimodal data, it can reveal which acoustic features covary with which social variables.

All of that can be revolutionary without producing one trustworthy English sentence.

The pressure to produce the sentence will be enormous.

Humans understand progress through translation benchmarks. We want to move from “these clicks have internal structure” to “this means *come with me*.” A sentence is legible to funders, journalists, documentary viewers, product designers, and the public. It turns statistical discovery into apparent access to another mind.

But fluent translation can arrive before grounded meaning if we allow the machine to fill gaps with plausibility.

A model that has learned every human text ever written knows how to make animal communication sound human.

That is not the same as knowing what the animal means.

Imagine an archaeologist finds an unknown script on thousands of tablets. A computational system discovers recurring symbols, positional constraints, common sequences, apparent names, and patterns correlated with storage rooms, burial sites, and trade goods. The system can generate new strings that look statistically authentic.

Would we call it translation?

Not yet.

We would celebrate the structure and keep looking for anchors.

Animal communication deserves the same patience.

The difficulty is greater because there may be no Rosetta Stone. There may be no human language aligned line by line with whale codas. Meaning has to be inferred from behavior, social context, experimental response, ecological consequence, and perhaps physiological state.

The animal must become part of the validation loop.

Project CETI is important partly because its ambition extends beyond passive audio. The project has worked toward dense, synchronized observation of sperm whale social life using hydrophones, tags, robotics, computer vision, and other tools. The goal is not simply more clicks. It is clicks attached to whales doing things.

That attachment is everything.

Who produced the coda?

Who was nearby?

Were whales diving, resting, socializing, caring for calves, traveling, approaching, separating, or changing formation?

What vocalization came before?

What came after?

Did another whale answer?

Did the original signaler alter behavior after the answer?

Were particular combinations more likely with certain partners or social contexts?

The questions sound tedious next to the phrase “whale translator.”

They are how translation becomes possible without fiction.

Sperm whales make context especially demanding because humans inhabit their world badly.

A whale can dive to depths where ordinary human observation disappears. Social lives unfold underwater across three dimensions. Sound propagates over distance. Individuals may separate from visible companions. A coda can occur in an acoustic scene containing calls from multiple animals. The same group can have relationships built across years or decades that a short field season captures only partially.

Humans want meaning to sit inside the waveform.

The whale may have put part of it in the relationship.

This is the strongest reason to resist dictionary thinking.

Suppose a particular coda sequence is statistically associated with reunion. It would be tempting to label it *hello*.

But perhaps it functions only between particular individuals. Perhaps it indicates identity. Perhaps it coordinates spacing. Perhaps it occurs because reunion changes arousal. Perhaps it recruits contact rather than marking contact. Perhaps its acoustic form varies with the receiver. Perhaps the sequence is part of a longer exchange whose function disappears when extracted.

“Hello” would not necessarily be wrong.

It would be underdetermined.

Human language itself should make us cautious. The phrase *What are you doing?* can be curiosity, accusation, greeting, flirtation, alarm, disbelief, or a literal request for information. The words do not change. Prosody, relationship, location, preceding events, and expected response do.

A text transcript throws away some meaning even among humans who share the language.

Why would we expect a whale transcript to be easier?

The third-language approach suggests a different target.

Do not begin by asking what human sentence best replaces the coda.

Ask what distinctions the whales themselves appear to preserve.

Which acoustic changes produce different responses?

Which changes do not matter?

Which dimensions carry identity?

Which carry social context?

Which combinations predict different next actions?

Which variations are copied, learned, or culturally shared?

Where does a receiver’s response alter the sender’s next coda?

A “phonetic alphabet” becomes useful when it lets researchers manipulate these dimensions carefully.

If rubato, ornamentation, rhythm, and tempo are meaningful components, scientists can eventually test them in playback rather than treating an entire coda as one indivisible object. Change one feature while holding others constant. Observe whether whales discriminate. Change the social context. Observe whether the effect changes.

The research program begins to resemble experimental linguistics without assuming the system is language in the human sense.

This is a more exciting future than automatic subtitles because it lets whales define relevance.

Humans are notoriously poor at knowing which features of another species’ signal matter. We divide sounds according to what our ears notice. A model can reveal dimensions outside those intuitions. Controlled playback can then ask the animal whether the discovered dimension has communicative force.

The machine proposes an alphabet.

The whale decides which letters count.

This structure keeps artificial intelligence in the right role.

AI can become a microscope for pattern rather than a ventriloquist for meaning.

A microscope expands perception. It does not write dialogue for the cell.

The distinction matters because generative models create social pressure to anthropomorphize. A system that outputs “Whale 7 is asking Whale 3 to babysit” will be more emotionally compelling than one that outputs a distribution over call types and social contexts. The first will spread on social media. The second may be the scientifically responsible result.

Once a fluent animal voice exists, people will build relationships with the voice rather than the evidence.

This can happen even if the voice carries disclaimers.

We know how quickly humans attribute personality to responsive systems. A chatbot that speaks in first person invites assumptions about inner state even when users know it is a model. An “elephant translator” or “dog translator” will amplify the effect because the animal body is actually present. The machine’s sentence will seem to reveal what the eyes cannot.

The risk is epistemic laundering.

Uncertain behavioral evidence enters a model.

A fluent sentence comes out.

The uncertainty disappears in grammar.

The machine has converted ambiguity into confidence without adding knowledge.

Sperm whales are a perfect species for resisting this because their signals are so obviously embedded in a world we do not share.

We cannot follow them unaided to depth.

We do not naturally hear every relevant feature as they do.

We do not belong to their social clans.

We did not grow up learning coda patterns from relatives and associates.

We lack the cultural history a whale brings to every exchange.

Any system that speaks confidently on their behalf should therefore be treated with suspicion proportional to its fluency.

This does not make the CETI ambition foolish.

It makes it profound.

A serious attempt to understand another species’ communication may teach us as much about the limits of translation as about the signals themselves.

Human linguistics has long wrestled with meaning as a problem that cannot be reduced to word substitution. Field linguists do not simply segment sounds and assign English labels. They learn contexts, test contrasts, work with speakers, distinguish grammatical categories, revise hypotheses, and confront concepts that do not map neatly across languages.

Animal communication removes the cooperating human consultant.

Or rather, it replaces the consultant with behavior.

The whale cannot explain our mistake in English.

It can fail to respond as predicted.

That failure must become precious.

A model trained to maximize apparent translation quality may treat anomalies as noise. A scientist should treat them as doors.

Why did this whale not respond?

Why did the same coda produce different behavior here?

Why did a receiver answer only when one associate was present?

Why does a pattern cluster by clan rather than immediate context?

Why does a sequence change during reunion but not separation?

What looks like model error may reveal the variable humans forgot.

Relationships are variables humans often forget.

A social network is not background metadata. It can determine what a signal does.

Suppose two whales have a long affiliative history. A coda between them may function differently from the same acoustic pattern exchanged by weak associates. Human communication is saturated with such effects. Sarcasm works among friends who share expectations. A command sounds different from a subordinate and a superior. A nickname can be affectionate or insulting depending on the speaker. Silence means different things after conflict and after comfort.

A universal animal dictionary would flatten these relational dimensions.

The third language expects them.

This is why the concept can apply even before humans communicate actively with whales. Every animal social pair may have a history-specific layer on top of species-wide signals. Humans entering the system become another possible relationship, though one with enormous asymmetries and limited sensory competence.

Long-term field researchers already build such relationships carefully.

Wild animals learn research boats, vehicles, voices, equipment, and routines. Researchers learn individual animals, associations, movement patterns, and tolerances. Ethical science tries not to make those relationships distort the behavior under study more than necessary, but the animals are not passive scenery. They can recognize and adapt to human presence.

The observer enters the observed world.

Technology can either hide or acknowledge this.

An autonomous underwater robot following whales may appear more neutral than a boat, but it is still an object in the animals’ environment. If whales notice it, its behavior matters. A tag changes the body carrying it. Playback introduces sound. Drones create acoustic and visual stimuli. Every measurement device becomes potentially communicative when an animal can perceive and respond to it.

There is no view from nowhere.

The best science minimizes, measures, and reports its own footprint.

The same principle should guide future conversational systems.

If we synthesize a coda and a whale approaches, we have not simply decoded meaning. We have changed the whale’s environment.

If repeated playback conditions whales to research devices, we have created a new learned relationship.

If the response weakens over time, is that habituation, changed meaning, social context, or evidence that our original translation was wrong?

Once humans speak into the channel, the channel changes.

This is the central paradox of the third language.

We want to understand communication as it exists without us.

To test our understanding, we often have to participate.

Participation can create new meaning.

The solution is not to avoid interaction. It is to recognize interaction as an intervention and design accordingly.

Humans have already done this successfully in other areas of animal cognition. Playback experiments, robotic conspecifics, artificial signals, and trained interfaces can reveal what animals perceive and how they decide. The challenge is scaling this work without turning the ocean into a laboratory full of synthetic chatter.

Restraint will become a technical capability.

A good system should know when evidence is too weak to generate.

It should separate “structurally plausible” from “functionally validated.”

It should track which individuals and contexts support a hypothesis.

It should expose the difference between a model’s confidence and biological certainty.

It should preserve raw context so researchers can revisit interpretations.

It should make falsification easier than publicity.

These are unglamorous design requirements.

They may determine whether animal communication AI becomes science or entertainment.

The public deserves awe, but awe does not require overclaiming.

Sperm whales already give us enough.

They are deep-diving, long-lived social mammals whose communicative clicks carry structured variation, whose social groups maintain cultural repertoires, and whose lives unfold in an acoustic environment humans can access only with technological assistance. A 2024 analysis could detect combinatorial dimensions that decades of human categorization had not captured fully.

That is astonishing.

We do not improve the astonishment by pretending the clicks have already become sentences.

The mystery is not an embarrassment waiting for a product release.

It is evidence of the size of the world.

One day we may understand parts of sperm whale communication well enough to produce translations that deserve the word. If so, those translations will have been earned through patient alignment among signal, context, relationship, experiment, and response.

And even then, something may remain untranslated because it belongs to the whales’ history with one another.

That is not failure.

Every human language contains words whose full meaning depends on having lived inside the culture that uses them. Every friendship contains references no dictionary can explain. Every family contains tones that defeat transcription.

Why should another species be simpler?

The whale alphabet, if that is what we eventually decide to call it, may be a magnificent beginning.

The mistake would be thinking the alphabet is the conversation.
