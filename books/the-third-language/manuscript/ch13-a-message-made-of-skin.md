# A Message Made of Skin

A male mourning cuttlefish can show two social worlds at once.

On one side of his body, toward a female, he displays a pattern associated with male courtship. On the other side, toward a rival male, he can display a female-like pattern that reduces the chance of attracting aggression or competition. In field observations reported by Culum Brown, Mark Garwood, and Jane Williamson, this split display occurred in a specific social configuration: a male positioned between a female and another male.

One body.

Two audiences.

Two displays at the same time.

Humans find this almost indecently clever because our own faces are bad at it. We can direct a whisper toward one person while smiling at another, but the skin on the left half of our torso cannot deliver one visible social signal while the right half delivers another.

Cuttlefish bodies can.

Their skin contains chromatophores and other structures that allow rapid changes in color and pattern. They can alter appearance for camouflage, threat, courtship, communication, and other functions. Body patterning is dynamic enough that “what the animal looks like” is not a stable property. Appearance is behavior.

This should change how we think about communication technology.

Most human interfaces assume the message can be separated from the body that produced it. Speech becomes text. Text crosses a network. The receiver can read it hours later without the sender present.

A cuttlefish display may be inseparable from orientation.

Who is on which side matters.

The body itself routes the signal.

A camera that records only one angle can therefore record one conversation and miss another happening simultaneously.

The data problem is architectural.

This is the chapter where the translation fantasy finally runs into another animal’s physics.

We began with channels humans can enter fairly easily: gaze, sound, gesture, movement. Now consider communication produced through skin patterns our own bodies cannot answer, electric fields we cannot sense unaided, chemical information we experience only as crude smell, vibration that travels through substrate, and frequencies outside our normal hearing.

What does it mean to “talk” with an animal when the animal’s channel has no human equivalent?

The easy answer is technology.

Build a sensor.

Build an actuator.

Convert the signal.

If a fish communicates through electrical discharges, detect the waveform and generate one back. If an animal uses ultraviolet visual patterns, capture and display them. If a cuttlefish uses skin, build a programmable surface. If a bee uses vibration, reproduce vibration. Once the machine bridges the sensory gap, the species can interact.

Technically, this may be possible in specific systems.

Conceptually, it is incomplete.

A channel is not merely a carrier. It is embedded in the receiver’s body and the social rules around use.

Humans can reproduce a frequency without knowing what it does.

We can draw a cuttlefish pattern without knowing whether the animal reads the geometry, motion, orientation, brightness, polarization, context, body posture, or some combination. We can generate electric pulses without understanding how the receiver integrates them with distance, movement, identity, and its own discharge timing.

The device can enter the physics before we enter the conversation.

Electric fish make this clear.

Weakly electric fish generate electric organ discharges that create fields around their bodies. They use changes in those fields for electrolocation, detecting objects and features in the environment. In social settings, the discharges also carry information and can change in interaction with other fish.

To a human standing beside the tank, much of this social world is invisible.

The fish are not silent.

We are electrically deaf.

Researchers have built robotic fish and signal-generating systems capable of interacting with weakly electric fish through this channel. Work by Jan Benda, Jan Grewe, Vera Worm, Tim Landgraf, Gerhard von der Emde, and others in the broader field has explored how artificial electric signals can influence fish behavior and how fish adjust timing in response to conspecific-like discharges.

This is interspecies communication in an unusually literal technological form.

The machine acts as a prosthetic sense for the human experimenter.

Human intention becomes an electric waveform.

Fish behavior becomes human-readable data.

But who, exactly, is communicating with whom?

The fish receives the artificial signal, not the human concept. The human receives the fish response through instruments. The machine translates modality rather than meaning.

This distinction is essential.

A microphone converts air pressure into electrical information. Nobody claims the microphone understands speech. A future device that converts a human command into an animal-perceptible signal may bridge channels without bridging semantics.

The third language emerges only when the signal becomes part of reciprocal learning.

Suppose a robotic electric fish produces one waveform when a real fish approaches and another when it withdraws. Over repeated encounters, the fish learns a contingency. Researchers adjust signals based on fish response. The fish responds differently. A local system can form that neither species uses naturally with the other.

That would be a genuine third language mediated by a machine.

It would not be a translation of electric fish into English.

This is where the concept becomes technologically powerful.

We have assumed throughout the book that third languages grow from overlapping capacities: humans point, dogs follow; cats blink, humans can imitate; horses feel our movement, we feel theirs; dolphins hear playback, humans can synthesize sound.

Machines expand the overlap.

They can let us signal in channels our bodies do not possess.

That expansion could become the great frontier of interspecies communication.

Not an app that makes a dog say *I love you*.

A set of interfaces that allow two species to build reliable shared contingencies across otherwise incompatible senses.

Think of it as a modem between bodies.

The phrase is imperfect because a modem assumes agreed protocols. Here, the protocol itself may have to be discovered through interaction.

A human wants to communicate “stop approaching.” A cuttlefish or fish has no reason to map that human concept onto the first artificial signal we choose. We would need to find a signal the animal perceives, associate it consistently with a consequence, observe whether the animal uses the new information, and perhaps give the animal a reciprocal signal capable of changing human behavior.

The system becomes negotiated through learning.

That is not translation.

It is protocol formation.

This may be the most original practical implication of the third-language idea.

Most animal communication technology is framed as decoding an existing animal language or training animals to use a human-designed interface. A third-language technology would instead optimize for mutual learnability.

What signal can the animal produce reliably?

What signal can the human detect through sensors?

What output can the device generate that the animal perceives clearly?

What contingencies can both participants learn?

How is misunderstanding detected?

How can either side terminate interaction?

How does the system expose uncertainty?

The endpoint need not be English.

It needs to be influence.

This way of thinking makes simple devices look more important. A bell by a door is primitive third-language technology. The dog can physically activate it. The human can hear it. The dog learns that activation changes the human’s behavior. The human learns to treat the bell as a request for access. The device bridges canine action and human attention.

The bell does not translate dog.

It creates a shared affordance.

Buttons do the same thing at greater symbolic ambition. We will examine them soon.

But imagine moving beyond human words printed on plastic. What would an interface designed from the animal’s body outward look like?

A horse might interact with large spatial targets, pressure-sensitive surfaces, or movement zones rather than spoken labels. A pig could select options through touch or nose targets. An ape might use gesture-sensitive visual displays. A dolphin could interact acoustically or through underwater objects. A parrot could use vocal labels, colors, and manipulable symbols. A cuttlefish interface might use visual pattern and orientation. An electric fish interface could be electrical.

The design question becomes: what is easiest for this species to use deliberately and easiest for humans to respond to reliably?

We stop forcing every animal through the bottleneck of human speech.

That change is overdue.

Human-computer interaction learned long ago that the interface should fit the user rather than require the user to understand the machine’s internal representation. Animal-computer interaction is extending the same principle across species, with researchers designing systems around animal sensory, motor, motivational, and welfare needs.

The ethical stakes are higher because animals cannot read terms of service or explain usability problems verbally.

Their behavior is the feedback.

A badly designed interface may produce nonuse, frustration, learned helplessness, accidental activation, or behavior humans misinterpret as meaningful. A well-designed interface should allow voluntary engagement and withdrawal, minimize coercion, and make consequences predictable.

The third language supplies one additional requirement: the human side must be trainable too.

An animal communication device fails if the animal can signal but humans ignore the signal.

This sounds trivial. It is not.

Emergency buttons in human systems matter only because institutions promise a response. A call bell in a hospital, an elevator button, a pedestrian crossing signal, and a help line are communication technologies backed by social commitments. Pressing creates an expectation that someone will notice.

An animal button without a human response policy is a toy.

If a dog can press OUTSIDE but the human responds only when convenient, the word’s reliability changes. If a captive animal can select STOP during handling but the stop signal is routinely overridden, the interface communicates the animal’s lack of control more efficiently.

Agency is not produced by the ability to press.

It is produced by the consequence.

This is why the cuttlefish’s split display is more than a curiosity. It shows a sender actively managing different receivers. Communication belongs to social geometry.

A future interface should preserve that specificity.

Who is the signal for?

A device in a multi-animal household may need to identify the signaler. A system in a zoo may need to distinguish whether a behavior is directed toward keeper, conspecific, visitor, or device. A farm system may need to distinguish herd-level patterns from individual requests. A wildlife playback system may need to know which individual or group is within range.

Broadcast destroys information that targeted communication preserves.

Humans learned this socially long before technology. We lower our voice. We whisper. We direct gaze. We stand closer. We message one person instead of a group.

The cuttlefish can put privacy on half its skin.

The electric fish can inhabit a field whose range is physically local.

Animal communication technologies should not assume every detected signal is public data.

That sentence sounds almost absurd because privacy is a human legal concept. But the biological issue is real: animals use communication strategically. Some signals are directed. Some are concealed from rivals. Some are audience-sensitive. Recording and rebroadcasting them can alter social consequences.

A future whale dataset may contain individually specific calls. A parrot interface may capture household speech. A wearable dog device may record location, health, and social contacts. A farm system may infer reproductive state. A conservation system may reveal where endangered animals gather.

Animal communication data can create risks to animals.

Poachers do not need to understand a call’s semantics if a model tells them where the caller is.

The better our sensors become, the more the ethics of animal communication will resemble data governance.

Who gets access?

Who benefits?

What behaviors are made legible?

Can the information be used for control, exploitation, hunting, surveillance, or commercial manipulation?

A third-language framework makes this visible because communication is treated as a relationship rather than a resource to extract.

Relationships have obligations.

Extraction has owners.

The difference will matter as commercial interest grows.

“Pet translator” is an obvious consumer category. Agricultural monitoring systems promise health and welfare gains. Conservation bioacoustics can automate species detection. Zoos can use behavior sensing. Researchers can build large animal-vocalization foundation models. Each application may be valuable.

Each can also turn the animal into a data source while leaving the human side unchanged.

The deepest promise is not extracting more information from animals.

It is creating more ways for animals to affect the systems that govern their lives.

This is where alien channels become politically interesting.

Imagine a dairy system that detects early signs of discomfort before a human would notice and automatically changes handling or flags an animal for examination. Imagine a zoo enclosure where animals can control access to certain social or sensory conditions through species-appropriate interfaces. Imagine a working dog that can indicate uncertainty in a detection task through a trained signal rather than being forced into a binary alert. Imagine wildlife crossings that respond dynamically to animal movement and communication cues.

None of these requires animal sentences.

They require animal information to have consequences.

The cuttlefish does not need to say *Do not reveal my courtship to the rival on my right.*

The body has already solved the routing problem.

The human task is to notice what kind of problem the animal was solving before we replace it with our own.

That is the discipline of alien communication.

Do not begin with the words we wish the animal could say.

Begin with the channel it already has, the social problem it already faces, and the response it already seeks.

Then ask whether we can build an overlap without erasing the difference.

A message made of skin does not need to become speech to reach us.

We may need to invent a new body for the conversation.

That new body will create new scientific obligations because every interface has affordances and blind spots.

A human keyboard assumes fingers, vision, learned symbols, and the ability to tolerate a flat surface at arm’s length. None of those assumptions is neutral. Animal interfaces are even more constrained. A device can fail because the target is too small, the contrast is poor, the sound is outside a comfortable range, the surface is unpleasant, the response latency is confusing, or the consequence is not worth the effort.

When a human user fails to use a product, designers usually blame usability eventually.

When an animal fails to use a device, humans are tempted to blame cognition.

That is a dangerous asymmetry.

Before concluding that an animal cannot communicate through an interface, we should ask whether we built an interface the animal can perceive, operate, and trust.

This sounds like ordinary design research because it is. The difference is that the user cannot fill out a survey afterward.

The animal’s pattern of approach, withdrawal, repetition, abandonment, accidental activation, persistence, and successful consequence becomes the usability report.

That means the third language should borrow one of the best habits from human-centered design: treat failure as information about the interface, not only about the user.

Imagine a dolphin research device that produces synthetic whistles but requires the animal to approach within a range it usually avoids. Failure to engage may say nothing about the signal. Imagine a horse choice interface installed in a location associated with restraint. The site itself may overwhelm the intended option. Imagine a parrot touchscreen whose visual elements make sense to human eyes but not to avian perception.

The medium enters the message before the animal has made a choice.

A truly interspecies interface therefore needs at least two rounds of validation.

First: can the animal perceive and operate the channel reliably without the semantic task?

Second: once the channel is usable, do learned signals produce the intended reciprocal consequences?

Only then should we begin making claims about symbolic understanding.

This distinction could prevent years of confusion. Researchers often ask sophisticated cognitive questions through apparatus that imposes hidden sensory or motivational demands. The same risk will multiply when engineers arrive with beautiful hardware and large models.

The cuttlefish should be the mascot for refusing one-size-fits-all design.

It reminds us that communication can be distributed across the body in ways no standardized human interface anticipates.

It also reminds us that the receiver can be physically located relative to the signal. A display meant for one side of the body is not merely information; it is addressed in space.

Future systems may need to preserve such geometry rather than convert everything into a broadcast token. A robotic display could orient toward one animal and remain neutral toward another. An acoustic system could beam or localize sound. An electric interface could operate only within a short field. A visual cue could be positioned where one individual, but not another, can see it.

That is not technical ornamentation.

It is part of meaning.

Humans have built digital communication around the idea that content can be separated from recipient. We copy, forward, repost, archive, and broadcast messages effortlessly. Animal systems may punish that assumption. The same signal delivered to the wrong audience can have a different effect or create a conflict that never existed in the original exchange.

A receiver-specific elephant call, a copied dolphin signature whistle, a territorial bird signal, or a split cuttlefish display all contain versions of the same lesson.

Who gets the message is part of the message.

The third language will therefore require a technology of address as much as a technology of decoding.

And because humans will build the machines, we will have to become responsible for the audiences our machines create.
