# The Clever Hans Problem

In Berlin at the beginning of the twentieth century, a horse appeared to know arithmetic.

Clever Hans could answer questions by tapping a hoof. Add two numbers and he tapped the sum. Ask the day of the month and he tapped. Problems could be spoken or written. Crowds gathered. His owner, Wilhelm von Osten, was convinced that patient education had revealed capacities other people had underestimated.

The story became a scientific problem because Hans often succeeded even when von Osten was not the questioner.

Simple fraud did not explain it.

A commission examined the horse and found no obvious trickery. Then psychologist Oskar Pfungst investigated the conditions under which Hans succeeded and failed. The decisive variable was not whether the owner was present. It was whether someone who knew the correct answer could see the horse and whether the horse could see that person.

Hans watched people.

As the taps approached the expected answer, questioners made small changes in posture and tension. At the correct number, they relaxed. The cues were often involuntary. People did not know they were giving the horse the answer.

Hans was not doing arithmetic.

He was doing humans.

That second sentence deserves as much attention as the first.

The Clever Hans story is usually deployed as a cautionary tale in animal cognition. It names an entire class of experimental problem: inadvertent cueing by a human who expects a particular response. The lesson is indispensable. If the experimenter knows where the food is hidden, where the correct object sits, which button should be pressed, or what outcome counts as success, tiny differences in gaze, body position, timing, facial expression, movement, or reward readiness can guide an animal.

A person can create the phenomenon she believes she is measuring.

Modern experimental design tries to remove or blind these sources of information. The person interacting with the animal may not know the correct answer. Automated apparatus may deliver trials. Conditions are randomized. Video is coded by observers who do not know the hypothesis. Controls are constructed to separate the proposed mechanism from simpler alternatives.

This is not scientific fussiness.

It is how respect for animals becomes rigorous.

Without controls, we risk attributing an ability the animal does not have.

Without curiosity, we risk missing the ability the animal actually demonstrated.

Hans is the perfect patron saint of this book because the false interpretation and the real phenomenon point in opposite directions.

The false story said: the horse can calculate like a human.

The real story said: the horse can detect changes in human behavior so subtle that humans themselves are unaware of producing them.

The failure of anthropomorphism revealed interspecies sensitivity.

That is exactly the kind of reversal the third language requires.

Humans tend to ask whether animals can enter our symbolic world. Can the horse add? Can the ape use signs? Can the parrot label colors? Can the dog speak with buttons? Can the whale’s call be translated into a sentence?

When the strongest version fails, disappointment can flatten the result.

But perhaps the animal was solving a different social problem all along.

Hans had learned a private code whose human senders did not know they were speaking.

The code was not consciously negotiated. It was not language. It was not even intentional communication on the human side. Yet it demonstrates how quickly an animal can build meaning from regularities in a human body.

This creates a strange problem for the idea of a third language.

If two participants do not both know they are communicating, is it still a shared language?

Not necessarily.

The phrase should be reserved for systems with some reciprocal stability—patterns both participants use or respond to in ways that can alter interaction. Hans’s cue reading is better described as exploitation of human cues.

But cue exploitation can be the raw material from which a shared system grows.

Once the human becomes aware of the cue and begins using it deliberately, the relationship changes.

A trainer discovers that a slight shoulder turn directs a dog.

A rider realizes that breathing out reliably precedes a horse slowing.

A keeper notices that an animal orients to a particular hand position.

The human can decide to remove the accidental cue, preserve it, exaggerate it, or formalize it.

The animal’s discovery becomes part of the human’s repertoire.

A private dialect can begin with a misunderstanding about who taught whom.

This is why clean experimental science and messy everyday relationship are not enemies.

The laboratory asks: what can the animal do when human cueing is controlled?

The relationship asks: what can this pair do when human cueing is allowed to become useful?

Both questions are valid.

Confusing them is the problem.

Suppose a dog reliably finds a hidden object when its owner knows where the object is but fails when the owner is ignorant.

If the claim is that the dog tracks scent from the object, owner knowledge is a confound.

If the claim is that the dog uses human cues, owner knowledge is the phenomenon.

The same behavior can be contamination in one experiment and communication in another.

Clever Hans teaches us to name the question before judging the cue.

This becomes urgent around animal communication buttons.

The image is irresistible: a dog stands over a board of large buttons labeled OUTSIDE, PLAY, MOM, FOOD, LOVE YOU, MAD, LATER, or WHY. The dog presses a sequence. The human interprets. A video circulates. Viewers see not merely behavior but apparent speech.

The medium invites linguistic interpretation because the output is a human word.

That creates a structural bias no horse hoof ever had.

When Hans tapped seven times, the taps themselves did not say *seven*. Humans counted them and assigned the answer. With buttons, the device produces spoken English. A press of LOVE outputs “love.” The machine places a human semantic label in the room whether or not the dog represents anything like the concept.

The interface speaks more confidently than the animal.

This does not mean button use is meaningless. Recent peer-reviewed research has produced evidence that soundboard-trained dogs can respond appropriately to some button words produced by humans and that their own two-button combinations are not fully explained by random pressing or simple imitation of the human’s immediately preceding presses. Those findings are genuinely interesting.

They do not make the Clever Hans problem disappear.

They make experimental design more important.

The owner may react differently to presses that form a plausible phrase. The dog may be sensitive to body position, gaze, routine, time, object availability, or emotional tone. Videos selected for social media may overrepresent astonishing sequences. Owners who believe their dogs are composing messages may interpret ambiguous combinations generously and mundane presses narrowly. Training histories differ. Buttons may be placed in stable spatial locations that animals learn as action cues independent of the spoken labels.

Every one of these possibilities can coexist with real communication.

That is what makes the problem difficult.

The false choice is: either the dog is talking or the whole thing is nonsense.

Science should be greedier.

What does the dog discriminate?

What does the dog anticipate?

Does the spoken output matter or only the location of the button?

Does the dog use the same button appropriately when context changes?

Can the animal respond to a human pressing a button it cannot see being pressed?

Does the behavior persist when the owner does not know the correct target?

Are combinations predictable from immediate context but not human modeling?

Does the dog invent sequences that produce consistent consequences across repeated situations?

How do owners learn the dog’s patterns in return?

These questions turn a viral spectacle into a serious research program.

They also protect the third-language idea from becoming a permission slip for fantasy.

If every owner interpretation counts as evidence, the concept becomes unfalsifiable.

A private dialect cannot mean “whatever the familiar human believes the animal meant.”

It needs behavioral anchors.

A useful minimum is recurrence with consequence.

The signal should occur often enough in relevant contexts that coincidence becomes less plausible. The receiver should respond in a way that changes the interaction. The sender’s future use should reflect those consequences. Misunderstandings should sometimes be visible. Alternative explanations should be testable.

A person may say, “When my dog stands there, he wants water.”

Fine. Put water somewhere else. Make the bowl full. Offer outside access. Have another person present. Observe whether the behavior changes. Does the dog redirect when the first human misunderstands? Does standing there recur specifically when water access is a problem?

Relationship knowledge becomes stronger when it survives gentle falsification.

This is a humane kind of science because the tests can often be low-cost.

We do not need to frustrate or deceive animals dramatically to learn. We can take advantage of natural variation, blind human observers, change object placement, vary which person knows, record complete sequences rather than highlight reels, and compare predictions made before observing the outcome.

The principle is simple: make it possible to be wrong.

Humans resist this in intimate relationships because interpretation is part of attachment.

People like believing they know their animals.

The claim can be loving. It can also be defensive. “I know him” sometimes means “do not challenge my reading.”

The animal then loses the ability to surprise the person.

A true private dialect should create the opposite effect. Familiarity gives us better hypotheses and more responsibility to revise them.

If the animal does something unexpected, the dialect expands.

Clever Hans is the extreme example of why surprise matters. The humans believed the horse was answering arithmetic questions. Pfungst changed the conditions and the ability disappeared. A weaker story would have ended there: horse fails math test.

Instead, the surprising variable—human knowledge and visibility—opened a new field of attention to unconscious cueing.

Good experiments do not merely destroy claims.

They redirect wonder.

This is the stance we will need as AI enters animal communication.

Machine learning creates a Clever Hans problem at industrial scale.

A model can exploit correlations humans did not intend to provide. In image classification, systems have learned background textures, watermarks, hospital scanner artifacts, and other shortcuts rather than the biological feature researchers thought they were detecting. In language models, benchmark contamination can make performance appear more general than it is. In animal datasets, models can learn recording equipment, location, individual identity, handler routines, microphone distance, or time-of-day patterns instead of the communicative feature researchers hope to isolate.

The model can be right for the wrong reason.

So could Hans.

Suppose a pet-emotion model predicts “anxious” with high accuracy from dog videos. Later we discover the training set contains most anxious examples at veterinary clinics and most relaxed examples at home. The model may be classifying floors, lighting, leashes, or human clothing.

The English output still says ANXIOUS.

The confidence score still looks scientific.

The animal pays for our shortcut.

The solution is not to reject machine learning. It is to design tests that force the model out of its comfort zone.

New individuals.

New environments.

New equipment.

Blinded conditions.

Counterbalanced contexts.

Playback or interactive validation.

Predictions made before outcomes.

A model of communication should generalize to the variables its interpretation claims matter.

If the output says a whale call addresses one individual, change the audience.

If it says a dog button refers to an object, move the object.

If it says an elephant call contains a receiver label, test receivers.

If it says a bat vocalization predicts an addressee, separate identity from context.

If it says an animal is requesting an action, let the action occur and observe whether signaling stops.

The reply should constrain the label.

This is the book’s recurring test because it is the best defense against our own narrative appetite.

Humans are story-generating animals. We see a sequence and infer motive. We are good enough at this to cooperate in enormous societies and bad enough to fight over texts from people we know. With animals, where direct correction is harder, the interpretive space widens.

Technology will not automatically shrink it.

A fluent machine can fill the gap faster than a human.

That is why uncertainty must remain visible.

Imagine two versions of an animal-communication device.

The first says: *Your dog says: “I am sad because you left me.”*

The second says: *The dog is producing a pattern associated in this individual with departure routines and reduced social proximity. Current evidence does not distinguish anticipation, arousal, learned solicitation, or negative affect. Try delaying departure without leaving and observe whether the pattern persists.*

The first is a product.

The second is a collaborator.

Most people will buy the first.

The second is more likely to teach us something.

This creates a market problem. The commercial incentive will favor certainty, personality, humor, and emotional payoff. A dog that “talks” generates engagement. A probability distribution does not. A whale that appears to say “mother” can anchor a documentary trailer. A model output describing a context-dependent acoustic cluster cannot.

The scientific community will therefore need strong norms around how findings are communicated.

The distinction between signal classification and semantic translation should be explicit.

Claims should specify whether evidence is observational, correlational, experimental, or interactive.

Model performance should not be presented as animal comprehension without receiver validation.

First-person animal sentences should be treated as interpretation or creative rendering unless supported by extraordinarily strong evidence.

Uncertainty should survive the press release.

This sounds defensive, but it protects the possibility of a much larger discovery.

If researchers overclaim early, public trust will collapse when flashy translations fail. Animal communication will be dismissed as another cycle of AI hype or anthropomorphic wishfulness. The real science—culture, individual recognition, multimodal signaling, social learning, audience effects, combinatorial structure, repair, and reciprocal adaptation—will be buried beneath embarrassment.

Clever Hans has already shown us this pattern once.

The arithmetic story failed.

The horse’s sensitivity survived.

A century later, we should be capable of keeping both lessons at the same time.

Be skeptical of the glamorous claim.

Do not become skeptical of the animal.

There is a moral difference between those attitudes.

Skepticism toward claims protects animals from being turned into mirrors of human expectation.

Skepticism toward animals can become an excuse not to look.

The third language requires disciplined generosity: assume there may be structure worth finding, then demand evidence strong enough to survive our desire for it.

Hans deserved that.

He was not a failed mathematician.

He was a successful reader of people trapped inside a human story about mathematics.

The next generation of animal communication research will encounter many Clever Hans moments. Models will appear to decode signals and turn out to be using confounds. Owners will report astonishing messages that shrink under blinded tests. Researchers will publish exciting correlations that fail in playback. Some claims will survive. Some will become more interesting when the original interpretation disappears.

We should welcome the correction.

If a dog does not mean what we thought, the dog has not disappointed us.

We have received a reply.
