# What the Cat Does With Its Eyes

Cats suffer from being compared with dogs in a world where dogs usually volunteer for the comparison.

Call a dog’s name and many dogs make the experiment easy. They look, approach, wag, orient, or at least produce a visible change that flatters the human idea of being acknowledged.

Call a cat’s name and the response may be an ear.

For a long time, this difference encouraged a familiar story. Dogs are social with us; cats merely tolerate us. Dogs understand people; cats are indifferent. Dogs participate; cats have staff.

The jokes survive because they contain recognizable household truth. Cats often do manage social contact differently from dogs. They are less likely to perform conspicuous obedience simply because a human requests it, and their domestication history, social ecology, and selective breeding are not canine copies.

But the comparison confuses one species’ style of response with the existence of response itself.

A cat can hear you without coming.

That simple possibility changed the design of several clever experiments. Instead of asking whether cats would obey familiar voices, researchers asked whether their behavior changed when they heard them.

In 2013, Atsuko Saito and Kazutaka Shinozuka reported evidence that domestic cats could distinguish their owners’ voices from unfamiliar voices. Many of the cats did not walk dramatically toward the speaker. They showed orienting responses—ear movement, head movement, pupil changes, shifts in attention. The animal had discriminated something while declining to perform the human’s preferred proof.

Years later, Saito and colleagues tested whether cats could discriminate their own names from other spoken words. Again, the point was not that cats had human-like concepts of names. The experiments asked whether repeated presentation of similar words led to habituation and whether the cat’s own name produced renewed responding. The results supported the conclusion that many cats distinguish the sound pattern associated with their name, including under some conditions with unfamiliar speakers.

The cat heard the word.

Whether the cat considered your request important remained the cat’s problem.

This distinction is central to the third language.

A receiver is not obligated to comply in order to demonstrate reception.

Humans know this perfectly well when dealing with one another. You can understand an instruction and refuse it. You can hear your name and keep reading. You can recognize that someone wants your attention and choose not to give it. We do not normally conclude that another adult lacks language because she heard us and declined to move.

With animals, compliance becomes entangled with comprehension because humans often design the test and control the reward. The animal succeeds by doing the thing that makes its understanding visible to us.

Cats are useful because they make that arrangement awkward.

They force us to separate *Did you receive me?* from *Will you do what I want?*

The eyes offer another example.

People who live with cats often describe slow blinking as a friendly signal. A cat narrows or closes its eyes slowly in a calm interaction; a person does something similar back. For years the practice occupied that murky zone where animal people insist something is obvious and science has not yet decided whether the obvious thing is measurable.

In 2020, Tasmin Humphrey and colleagues published experiments examining slow-blink interactions between cats and humans. In one, owners were instructed to slow blink at their cats or simply remain present. Cats were more likely to produce slow-blink movements in response to the human slow blink. In another experiment involving an unfamiliar researcher, cats were more likely to approach an outstretched hand after the researcher slow blinked than after a neutral condition.

The experiments did not prove that a slow blink means “I love you.”

That translation would be exactly the move this book is trying to resist.

They showed something more precise: a particular human facial behavior can affect a cat’s facial behavior and subsequent approach.

That is a loop.

The loop matters because the expression itself is almost contentless without the recipient. A slow eye closure can occur for reasons unrelated to social communication. Put it inside a reciprocal sequence, control the conditions, and a social function becomes more plausible.

Now notice the practical lesson.

The human who wants to communicate friendliness with a cat may be better served by doing less.

Do not advance. Do not reach immediately. Do not stare continuously. Narrow the eyes. Pause. Allow the cat’s next action to matter.

This is almost the opposite of the human impulse toward an animal we want to like us.

We approach.

We extend hands.

We speak.

We touch.

We increase contact to communicate affection.

For an animal that values control over distance and contact, intensity can make the message illegible. The content in the human’s mind—*I am friendly*—is less important than the pattern the cat can use.

Communication belongs to the receiver too.

This seems obvious until you watch people repeatedly pet an animal that keeps moving away.

The third language requires an uncomfortable discipline: the intended meaning of my action does not determine its meaning for you.

Human relationships teach the same lesson. I can intend a joke and cause hurt. I can intend help and produce pressure. I can intend reassurance and sound dismissive. Communication is measured partly at reception, then renegotiated through repair.

Across species, the gap between intention and reception is wider because the bodies are different.

A human smile exposes teeth. A direct approach closes distance. A hug restrains movement. A hand descends from above. A face moves close. These behaviors carry benign or affectionate meanings in many human contexts. Another animal has no obligation to inherit those meanings.

Cats teach human beings to negotiate touch.

A cat approaches and rubs against a leg. The person assumes petting is requested. Often it may be welcome. Sometimes the cat’s social rubbing does not authorize the amount, location, or duration of touch the human supplies. The cat remains, shifts, flicks, turns, moves, or leaves. The person who treats departure as feedback learns a finer system than the person who treats departure as betrayal.

The cat’s body becomes part of the grammar of continuation.

Not grammar in the linguistic sense. Grammar in the practical sense that some sequences make later actions more or less likely.

Touch, pause, remain.

Touch, tail change, continue, bite.

Touch, pause, lean in.

Reach, withdraw, follow.

Reach, withdraw, leave.

The same human action can be accepted, solicited, tolerated, or rejected. The next turn disambiguates.

This is why interaction is a stronger unit than expression alone.

A chart of cat body language can be useful. Ear position, tail carriage, pupil dilation, body tension, whisker position, vocalization, posture, and movement all contain information. But charts become dangerous when they turn dynamic behavior into one-to-one definitions.

Tail flick equals irritated.

Purr equals happy.

Belly up equals wants belly rub.

No animal body works like a set of labeled dashboard lights.

Purring can occur in multiple contexts, including stress and pain. Exposing the abdomen can have different meanings depending on context and does not necessarily invite human touch. A tail movement is one variable among many. The point of observation is not to memorize a dictionary but to become better at seeing configurations and consequences.

The third language is learned statistically.

This cat, in this posture, in this location, after this event, with this person, tends to do what next?

Familiarity gives the human more data, but it can also make the human overconfident.

Veterinarians encounter this problem in both directions. An owner may notice a tiny departure from normal behavior that would be invisible during a short examination: the cat is sleeping in a different place, hesitating before jumping, grooming less, approaching food and walking away, or hiding at an unusual time. That relational knowledge can be medically valuable.

The same owner may explain the behavior through a confident psychological story that obscures illness: the cat is “mad,” “getting even,” “being dramatic,” or “jealous.”

Good observation and bad explanation can coexist in the same sentence.

Science helps separate them.

So does humility.

Cats have also been studied in social referencing tasks. In human development, social referencing describes the use of another individual’s emotional response to help interpret an ambiguous situation. Experiments with dogs have shown forms of this phenomenon, and researchers have asked whether cats do something similar.

In a 2015 study, Isabella Merola and colleagues placed cats and their owners in a situation involving a novel potentially frightening object—a fan with streamers—and instructed owners to behave positively or negatively toward it. The cats looked between owner and object, and their behavior showed some sensitivity to the owner’s emotional message.

The effect was not a simple feline copy of human infant behavior. That would be too easy a story. But it added another piece to a larger picture: cats monitor human beings for socially relevant information.

A cat does not need to adore a person’s instructions to use the person as part of its environment.

That environment includes voice.

Cats vocalize with humans in ways that may differ from their vocal interactions with other adult cats. People, in turn, develop cat-directed speech. Recent work has begun examining not just whether cats recognize voices but how human prosody and feline vocal interaction become coordinated. This is exactly the kind of research that makes the boundary between two species interesting. The human does not merely “decode cat.” The human changes how the human speaks to cats. Cats respond inside that altered vocal ecology.

We build channels around one another.

The same happens with names.

Saito and colleagues later published evidence that cats can learn associations involving the names of other cats living in the same household, and under some conditions human family members as well. The result was intriguing because the cats were not explicitly trained in a laboratory to memorize a vocabulary list. They had been exposed to names embedded in ordinary social life.

This does not mean the cat represents *Momo* or *Sophie* exactly as a human represents a proper name. It does suggest that repeated human labeling can become informative to an animal listening from the edge of our conversations.

Think about the implication.

We have been teaching animals pieces of human life without realizing that we were teaching.

A household is a continuous experiment in unsupervised learning.

Names recur around individuals. Food words recur around food. Departure words recur before people leave. Greetings recur after absence. Emotional tone changes with events. Objects produce sounds. Doors predict access. Devices predict attention. Humans repeat routines on schedules and then express amazement that the cat appears in the kitchen three minutes before dinner.

The animal does not need a lesson plan.

It needs regularities.

Humans also learn feline regularities without a lesson plan. We distinguish one meow from another. We learn which cabinet matters. We learn that a cat who sits in one place wants access to a window and a cat who sits in another place wants a faucet turned on. We discover that a particular sound occurs before vomiting, that another predicts play, that another summons a specific human more effectively.

Researchers have investigated whether cats alter vocalizations toward humans and whether people can classify feline vocal contexts above chance, with mixed results depending on design and familiarity. The more general point does not require a universal meow dictionary.

The household creates local meaning.

Local meaning can be absurdly specific.

A cat taps one piece of furniture because years ago it produced a response. A human leaves a cupboard open because the cat has made that location part of an evening routine. The cat waits beside a sink. The human turns on a thin stream of water. Another person witnessing the scene for the first time sees only a cat near plumbing.

Shared history supplies the missing clause.

This is where the concept of a private dialect becomes stronger than the concept of animal translation.

A translator aims for generality. If a word means one thing only between two speakers, it is a poor dictionary entry.

A relationship can thrive on exactly that specificity.

Human couples invent words, gestures, references, nicknames, routines, and compressed expressions that are useless outside the pair. Families do the same. Their communicative efficiency comes partly from the fact that they do not need the system to generalize.

An interspecies relationship may depend even more heavily on private context because the shared symbolic vocabulary is smaller.

The cost is scientific difficulty.

If a cat reliably uses one behavior to make one person open one closet, is that communication? In the practical sense, almost certainly. Is it evidence that cats as a species use that behavior with a stable meaning? No. Could the human be cueing the animal? Possibly. Could the behavior have developed through reinforcement without any rich mental representation? Certainly.

None of those caveats erases the local system.

Learning is not the enemy of communication.

Human language is learned too, though through capacities and developmental pathways of extraordinary complexity. If a signal works because two participants have reinforced it across a history, that history is part of its meaning.

The better scientific question is what flexibility the learned behavior has.

Can the cat recruit the signal in a new context?

Does it direct the behavior toward the person who can actually solve the problem?

Does it persist if ignored?

Does it switch tactics?

Does the human’s response alter later signaling?

Does the system depend on one individual partner?

These are testable questions that honor both skepticism and relationship.

Cats also expose a deeper human problem: we confuse sociability with accessibility.

An animal is accessible to us when its signals are easy for us to read, its motivations fit our routines, and its responses reward our attempts at interaction. Dogs often score high on accessibility because their evolutionary and domestication histories have produced remarkable accommodation to human cues.

A less accessible animal is not necessarily less social or less communicative. Its social world may simply be organized around different channels, distances, tempos, and incentives.

This becomes crucial once we leave domestic mammals.

A sperm whale’s communicative life is not failing because humans cannot translate codas. A bee’s dance is not primitive because it lacks a face. A cuttlefish is not silent because our skin cannot answer in pattern. The error begins whenever we make our ease of participation the measure of another species’ communicative complexity.

Cats live close enough to correct us gently.

They make their correction by not coming when called.

There is a useful ethical lesson in that refusal.

The point of understanding an animal cannot be to eliminate the animal’s difference.

If every advance in interspecies communication simply produces a more efficient command interface, we have learned less than we think. The animal becomes easier to operate, but not necessarily more able to affect us.

The third language is interesting because it preserves difference while creating coordination.

A person slow-blinks.

The cat slow-blinks back.

Perhaps the cat approaches. Perhaps it does not.

The human has done something unusual: offered a signal without demanding the next turn.

That is a small act, almost laughably small compared with the technological dream of decoding whale speech or building an animal translator.

But it contains the entire argument.

The human entered the animal’s channel.

Then waited to see what the animal would do with it.
