# The Translator’s Mistake

In April 2025, Google announced DolphinGemma, an audio model developed in collaboration with researchers working on dolphin communication. The model could take dolphin vocalizations as input and predict likely subsequent vocalizations. It could also generate dolphin-like sound sequences. The announcement sat beside work using an experimental system known as CHAT, designed to help researchers and dolphins establish shared associations around synthetic whistles and objects.

The headlines had an obvious destination.

AI is learning to speak dolphin.

The actual project was more careful.

Prediction is not translation.

Generation is not conversation.

A model that predicts what sound tends to follow another has learned structure. That structure can be scientifically valuable. It may help identify recurring sequences, contextual patterns, and candidate units for controlled testing. Generating plausible calls can support experiments if researchers understand enough about the signals and conduct them responsibly.

But the gap between “likely next vocalization” and “what the dolphin means” is the entire problem.

Modern AI has trained us to underestimate that gap because next-token prediction in human language can produce astonishingly fluent behavior. A model trained to predict the next word becomes capable of writing essays, answering questions, coding, translating, summarizing, and conducting dialogue. It is natural to assume that next-signal prediction in an animal system will eventually make the same leap.

Perhaps in some domains it will.

We should not assume the path is identical.

Human text arrives already embedded in a symbol system we understand. The training data contains books, explanations, dialogues, dictionaries, translations, instructions, code, labels, stories, and descriptions written by humans for humans. The model learns correlations inside a language whose meanings are densely reflected across the corpus.

A whale coda dataset contains clicks.

A dolphin corpus contains whistles, burst pulses, clicks, movements, contexts, and whatever annotations researchers can attach.

A bat corpus contains vocalizations plus social labels humans infer from observation.

The animals have not written dictionaries explaining their calls in another modality.

The grounding problem is severe.

A model can become excellent at the statistics of a signal system while remaining uncertain about function.

This is the translator’s mistake: confusing the ability to model form with the ability to recover lived meaning.

Human linguistics has versions of the same problem. A person can imitate the phonology of a language without understanding a sentence. A model can generate grammatical nonsense. A codebreaker can identify symbol frequencies before knowing what the symbols refer to. Structural competence and semantic grounding are related but not identical.

Animal communication adds embodiment and ecology.

The meaning of a call may depend on who is present, what the receiver knows, the sender’s relationship to that receiver, movement, location, reproductive state, danger, recent interaction, or the recipient’s response. A model trained primarily on audio may learn the song and miss the dance around it.

The obvious solution is multimodal data.

Add video.

Add location.

Add individual identity.

Add proximity.

Add physiology.

Add environmental context.

Add social networks.

Add what happened before and after.

Projects such as CETI and Earth Species Project are moving in this direction at large scale, building tools, models, datasets, and field systems meant to connect animal vocalization with behavior and context. Advances in biologging, robotics, passive acoustic monitoring, computer vision, and foundation models make it possible to collect and search forms of data that would have overwhelmed previous generations of researchers.

This is the right direction.

It still does not remove the receiver.

A multimodal model can tell us that a call occurs when a calf is separated from a mother, that it predicts approach, that its acoustic structure varies by individual, and that receivers orient strongly. The model may produce a plausible label: *contact call*.

To move toward translation, we need intervention.

Play it back.

Alter a component.

Change the apparent caller.

Change the recipient.

Control for context.

Observe whether the predicted behavior occurs.

Let the animal reject the hypothesis.

This is slower than scraping a larger dataset and training a larger model.

It is also the difference between correlation and communication.

The temptation to skip the slow part will be overwhelming because AI development has a scaling culture. More data, more compute, larger models, better benchmarks. In many domains, scaling has produced capabilities that surprised even the people building the systems.

Animal communication may benefit from scaling too. Vast datasets can reveal rare patterns and population variation. Large models can transfer across species or modalities in unexpected ways. Self-supervised learning can extract acoustic structure without expensive manual labels. Generative models can help design controlled stimuli.

But no amount of scale changes the fact that meaning is partly a relationship between signal and world.

If the world is missing from the data, the model is learning shadows.

This is why a human-language analogy can mislead. Human text is already the output of creatures embedded in the same broad human world as the researchers. The corpus contains descriptions of that world. Animal vocalizations do not contain human-readable captions about what the animal was doing.

The field has to build the captions experimentally.

And even “caption” is too simple, because the best unit may not be a sentence.

Perhaps a model eventually learns that a particular elephant call contains receiver-specific information, an arousal dimension, and context about movement. What should the translation interface output?

*Hey Ama, come here.*

Maybe.

But the sentence has chosen a grammatical mood, a lexical verb, a level of intentionality, and a social tone that the evidence may not support.

A structured output could be more honest:

Likely receiver: Ama.

Receiver-specific acoustic evidence: high.

Context: caller and receiver separated by 400 meters.

Observed consequence in similar events: receiver often approaches or answers.

Function hypothesis: contact / directed recruitment.

Confidence: moderate.

That interface looks like an instrument panel rather than a translator.

It may be what responsible animal communication needs first.

The public will hate it.

The public wants the animal to speak.

This desire is ancient. Mythologies, folktales, fables, children’s stories, religious traditions, fantasy novels, cartoons, and films are full of animals whose silence is removed by magic. Talking animals let us imagine moral worlds where species difference survives while human language makes companionship easy.

AI offers technological magic.

The dog does not merely bark; the collar says *I missed you.*

The whale does not click; the app says *my family is below.*

The elephant does not rumble; the speaker says *Mara, wait.*

The emotional demand for this product will exist before the science can satisfy it.

That means someone will sell the approximation.

Consumer pet translators already exist in primitive forms, often offering entertainment rather than validated interpretation. As models improve, the output will become more convincing. Cameras, microphones, wearables, and individual training data will let systems personalize. A model may learn that one dog’s behavior around one door predicts outside access and render that prediction as speech.

At that point the sentence might even be useful.

The danger is not usefulness.

The danger is forgetting the sentence is a user interface.

When a navigation app says “turn left,” the road does not speak English. The sentence is a rendering of a model and a map. We understand the abstraction because we built the system.

When an animal translator says “I’m scared,” users may treat the sentence as direct testimony.

The difference is morally enormous.

An interface for animal states should therefore distinguish observation from narration.

This can be done without making the product unusable.

Show the evidence.

Show alternatives.

Show confidence.

Show change from the individual’s baseline.

Ask for a reversible confirmation step.

Record whether the predicted intervention worked.

Let the model learn from the reply.

Do not put quotation marks around an inference.

A design rule could be simple: if the animal did not produce a validated symbol with a validated interpretation, the system should not pretend the animal authored the English sentence.

The difference between *“I am afraid”* and *behavior is consistent with fear or high arousal* is not stylistic fussiness.

It marks who is speaking.

This becomes even more important when first-person animal voices are used for advocacy.

There will be enormous temptation to give whales, elephants, farm animals, and endangered species synthetic testimony because human audiences respond to narrative. A generated voice saying *the ship noise hurts us* may move public opinion more than an acoustic ecology paper.

Advocacy can be morally sincere and epistemically reckless at the same time.

If the sentence exceeds the evidence, it makes the animal a character in a human campaign.

We do not need fabricated testimony to care.

Measured changes in calling behavior, stress, movement, reproductive success, habitat use, or communication range can justify action without pretending an animal made a political speech.

Respect sometimes means resisting the urge to make another being eloquent in our language.

AI can also help in a less glamorous, more transformative way: by making humans better receivers.

This is the application I would bet on.

Imagine a system that learns an individual dog’s baseline across months. It notices that pacing plus door orientation plus a particular gaze sequence usually precedes successful outside access. It does not say *I need to pee*. It alerts the human that a familiar request pattern is occurring and records the outcome.

Imagine a horse-monitoring system that detects a subtle, individual-specific change during saddling that has preceded pain-related behavior in the past. It flags the pattern before a conflict.

Imagine a zoo system that notices which keeper interactions correlate with voluntary approach and which with withdrawal, helping staff refine handling.

Imagine conservation acoustics that identifies likely contact calls and movement changes around ship noise, allowing dynamic management of noisy activity.

Imagine a dolphin research interface that surfaces candidate call-response sequences humans would not have noticed, then helps design blinded playback tests.

These tools do not speak for animals.

They increase the bandwidth at which animals can affect human decisions.

That is more important.

The translator’s mistake comes from believing the human sentence is the end product.

The real end product should be improved mutual prediction with preserved uncertainty and the ability to correct.

This is where the third language gives AI a different objective function.

A translator optimizes semantic equivalence between source and target.

A third-language system optimizes successful reciprocal coordination.

The distinction sounds abstract until we put it in a household.

The dog has a need.

The human misunderstands.

System A produces a sentence with 72 percent confidence.

System B proposes the most likely interpretation and a low-cost action that will produce informative feedback.

The human takes the action.

The dog either confirms or redirects.

Which system is more useful?

If the goal is entertainment, System A.

If the goal is communication, System B.

System B can also improve over time because the relationship generates labels through consequences.

This suggests a new form of personalized machine learning built around active interaction. Instead of humans manually annotating thousands of pet behaviors with speculative emotions, the system can learn from outcomes.

Pattern occurs.

Human opens door.

Dog exits.

Pattern-to-outcome association strengthens.

Pattern occurs.

Human offers food.

Dog does not engage and moves toward door.

Food hypothesis weakens; access hypothesis strengthens.

Over time the machine builds a relational model grounded in what actions resolve sequences.

This is not enough to infer inner experience. It does not tell us why the dog wanted out. But it can support practical communication without pretending to read minds.

The model becomes a memory extension for the human.

It can notice that a pattern has changed.

It can say, in effect: *This usually ends when X happens. Today X did not work.*

That deviation may be more important than any translation.

A changed dialect can signal illness, environmental change, altered relationship, or new learning.

The system helps humans notice surprise.

This is the opposite of the generative instinct to smooth surprise away.

We should build animal AI that becomes more interested when prediction fails.

Failure means the animal may be telling us something new.

The machine should not explain it instantly.

It should preserve the anomaly.

This idea has implications for research foundation models too. A good animal-vocalization model should make it easy to inspect outliers, compare individuals, detect context shifts, and test whether learned representations correspond to receiver behavior. Benchmarking should reward generalization across recording conditions and populations rather than only in-dataset classification.

Researchers should be suspicious of models that “translate” without intervention.

A benchmark of human-labeled contexts is not a benchmark of meaning.

An animal communication benchmark should eventually include reciprocal tests: can the model predict receiver response to controlled signal variation? Can it identify when a signal is addressed to a particular individual? Can generated stimuli preserve function without causing harmful disruption? Can predictions transfer to new social relationships?

The receiver becomes part of the benchmark.

This is technically difficult, expensive, species-specific, and ethically constrained.

Good.

Hard validation is protection against easy fiction.

There is one more translator’s mistake: assuming success means removing difference.

A perfect translation between two human languages tries, as far as possible, to preserve meaning while making the source accessible in the target language. The ambition is interoperability.

With animals, difference may be part of the information.

A dog’s odor world cannot be compressed into words without loss. A bee’s dance integrates a sensory and navigational system we do not inhabit. An electric fish’s signal participates in a field our bodies cannot feel. A cuttlefish’s display is routed across skin and orientation. A sperm whale’s coda exists inside a cultural and acoustic life we observe from outside.

The goal should not be to make these systems disappear into English.

The goal should be to make enough of them accessible that humans can respond intelligently while preserving what remains alien.

This is a more mature form of understanding.

We do not understand another human by making them identical to ourselves. We understand partly by learning where our models fail.

Animals deserve the same courtesy.

The future may still contain spectacular translation breakthroughs. Dolphins may turn out to use signals with functions far more word-like than current evidence supports. Elephant vocal labels may prove to be part of a richer system of reference. Whale codas may yield structures that justify linguistic analogies stronger than we can responsibly make today. AI may uncover patterns no researcher anticipated.

I hope it does.

The third language is not a hedge against discovery.

It is a way to make discovery matter.

If we learn a signal’s meaning, we should be able to enter the loop more carefully.

If we can generate it, we should ask what right we have to do so.

If an animal responds, the response should update us.

If a machine is uncertain, the uncertainty should remain visible.

If a relationship has local meaning, a population model should not erase it.

If understanding reveals refusal, the human should not call the translator broken.

The first truly successful animal translator may not sound like a talking animal at all.

It may sound like a quieter human.

There is an engineering consequence to that line.

Most AI products are evaluated by how often they produce useful output. An animal-communication system may also need an explicit **abstention rate**: how often it refuses to translate because the evidence does not support a stable interpretation.

In ordinary consumer software, abstention can feel like failure. The camera saw the dog. The microphone heard the sound. The user expects an answer.

But “I do not know” can be a capability.

A model that abstains when an individual is outside its training distribution, when context is missing, when two interpretations are nearly tied, or when a signal has not been behaviorally validated is protecting the animal from invented certainty.

This could become a publication standard for the field.

Animal-communication systems should report not just accuracy on known classes but calibration, uncertainty, out-of-distribution behavior, population coverage, and the conditions under which the system declines to infer meaning.

A model should also separate layers of claim.

Detection: an acoustic or behavioral event occurred.

Classification: the event resembles a learned category.

Prediction: a receiver or outcome is likely.

Function hypothesis: the event may serve a communicative role.

Semantic rendering: a human-language description is offered.

These are different achievements.

The product interface will want to collapse them into one sentence because sentences are easier to use. The scientific record should refuse that collapse.

Imagine a label on every translation showing where the claim came from.

Observed repeatedly in this individual.

Validated across unfamiliar individuals.

Receiver response tested.

Generated playback tested.

Human-readable paraphrase only.

No direct evidence for internal emotional state.

Such provenance would make an animal translator less magical and more trustworthy.

It would also educate users in a new kind of literacy: learning to distinguish what the animal did from what the machine inferred.

That literacy may matter beyond animals. Humans increasingly live among systems that turn sensor data into fluent explanations. Cars diagnose themselves. health devices narrate sleep. financial tools explain markets. cameras classify emotion. Models make probabilistic inferences sound like observations.

Animal communication is simply the case where the moral cost of confusing inference with testimony becomes impossible to ignore.

The animal cannot correct the English sentence in English.

So the interface must leave room for correction by behavior.

That requirement may teach us to build better AI everywhere.
