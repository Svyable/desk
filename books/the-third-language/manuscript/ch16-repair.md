# Repair

The most interesting moment in a conversation may be the moment it fails.

A smooth exchange can be explained too easily. One animal performs a behavior. Another does something that looks appropriate. We see coordination and infer shared meaning.

Then the sequence breaks.

The receiver does nothing.

The receiver does the wrong thing.

The sender repeats.

The sender changes the signal.

The sender moves into view, becomes louder, touches, approaches, points differently, abandons the attempt, or tries a new route.

Failure reveals what the sender expected.

Human conversation depends so heavily on repair that we barely notice it. We say “what?” We repeat a name. We substitute a word. We point. We spell. We slow down. We restart a sentence. We clarify a reference. We notice the other person’s face and ask whether something sounded wrong. We repair pronunciation, hearing, reference, intention, social offense, and shared knowledge.

Language is not powerful because misunderstanding never happens.

It is powerful because misunderstanding can become part of the interaction.

This is why repair deserves its own chapter in a book about animals.

If an animal changes communicative behavior after evidence that a recipient did not understand or did not respond, the animal is doing something more than emitting a fixed signal into the world.

It is troubleshooting another participant.

Studies of great ape gestural communication have made this especially vivid. Researchers have documented persistence after apparent communicative failure and, under some conditions, changes in gesture when the recipient does not produce the expected response. Orangutans have been reported to repeat or elaborate gestures after partial success and switch more substantially after complete misunderstanding. Chimpanzees can persist and elaborate when social goals remain unmet.

The details and interpretations are debated, as they should be. Demonstrating intentional communication requires more than seeing a gesture twice. Researchers have to infer the apparent goal, identify recipient response, distinguish persistence from arousal, and account for context.

But the structure is powerful.

The recipient’s failure becomes input to the sender.

This is the clearest behavioral signature of the idea we have been circling since the first page.

Communication is not in the signal alone.

It is in the update.

Repair can take several forms, and the differences matter.

A sender can repeat the same signal more strongly.

That suggests the signal itself may still be considered appropriate but insufficiently perceived or acted upon.

A sender can switch modalities—vocalize after a visual gesture, touch after a gaze cue, move into the recipient’s field of view.

That suggests sensitivity to channel.

A sender can use a different behavior that appears to pursue the same outcome.

That suggests flexibility around means and goal.

A sender can abandon the interaction.

That may show the cost has exceeded the value, that the recipient is unavailable, or simply that the original behavior was never communicative at all.

The sequence lets us discriminate among hypotheses.

In ordinary human-animal life, repair is everywhere once you start looking.

A dog stands by the door.

No response.

The dog looks at the person.

No response.

The dog approaches, then returns to the door.

The person follows.

The dog goes outside.

The human has just watched a multimodal clarification sequence and will probably describe it as “he told me he needed out.”

That summary is fine as household language.

The deeper point is how the dog handled failure.

The signal became more explicit because the receiver was incompetent.

Animals have to manage incompetent humans constantly.

We miss small signals. We look at phones. We misunderstand. We apply species-general advice to individuals. We assume one motivation. We do not smell what the dog smells or hear what the horse hears. We are often late.

The animal’s repair strategy reveals what kind of receiver it thinks we are.

A dog may learn that one household member responds to a stare while another requires a bark. A cat may wake one person by touching and another by knocking something off a surface. A parrot may call differently depending on who is home. A horse may use a stronger signal with a novice rider whose timing is poor.

These differences can look like personality.

They may partly be audience design.

Humans do the same thing. We explain a technical idea differently to a colleague and a child. We speak louder to someone who appears not to hear. We use more explicit instructions with a novice and shorthand with an expert. Communication adapts to models of the recipient.

How sophisticated those models must be in animals is a separate empirical question. A dog does not need a theory of mind in the philosophical sense to learn that Person A responds to one pattern and Person B to another.

Learning partner-specific contingencies is enough to produce audience-sensitive repair.

This matters because private dialects should predict repair asymmetries.

Familiar partners should, in some contexts, require less repair because they share more history.

When repair is needed, they may use smaller changes because both understand the local repertoire.

Unfamiliar partners may require escalation or modality switching.

That prediction can be tested.

The idea is simple enough for comparative research: place animals in communicative tasks with familiar and unfamiliar humans, record complete sequences, and examine how signaling changes after misunderstanding. Do not ask only whether the animal succeeds. Ask how it fails.

Failure may be the richer dataset.

This is true in artificial interfaces too.

A dog presses a button. The human performs an action. The dog does not achieve the apparent goal.

What comes next?

If the animal presses the same button again, that is one pattern.

If it chooses a different button associated with the same outcome, that is another.

If it walks to the relevant location, combines button and natural gesture, or recruits a human physically, the board has become part of a larger multimodal repair system.

This is far more informative than counting how often button combinations resemble human phrases.

A communication technology should make misunderstanding observable.

Most current AI systems do the opposite.

They are optimized to produce an answer even when uncertain. A language model can infer what a user probably intended, smooth ambiguity, and respond fluently. This is useful until the system’s confidence outruns the evidence.

Animal communication tools will need explicit repair mechanisms because the cost of silent guessing is high.

Imagine an AI system monitoring a horse during handling. It detects a pattern associated with discomfort and tells the human “pain.” The handler stops. The horse’s behavior changes.

Did the system identify pain?

Maybe.

It identified a pattern whose predicted intervention produced a consequence. More evidence is needed.

A stronger system would treat the intervention as a test. It would record whether reducing pressure, changing location, pausing, or seeking veterinary assessment changes the pattern. It would update rather than lock the first label.

The animal’s response becomes part of model learning.

This could create a radically different design philosophy for animal AI.

Instead of one-shot translation, use hypothesis and repair.

The system says: likely seeking distance.

The human steps back.

If the animal relaxes or remains, confidence changes.

If the animal follows and seeks contact, the initial hypothesis weakens.

The system does not need to pretend it heard a sentence. It helps the human choose a reversible next action that lets the animal clarify.

This is conversation as active inference.

Humans already do it intuitively.

“Do you mean outside?”

We move toward the door.

The dog’s behavior answers.

“Do you want me to stop?”

We stop touching.

The cat’s next move answers.

“Is this too close?”

We create distance.

The horse’s next posture answers.

Good animal communication is often a sequence of small reversible questions asked with behavior.

This is more humble than translation and more useful.

It also changes the ethics of uncertainty.

When we are unsure what another human means, we can ask directly. With animals, our clarification options are constrained. We therefore have a responsibility to make our tests low-cost.

If I am uncertain whether a dog wants space, stepping back briefly is cheaper than advancing and discovering through a bite.

If I am uncertain whether a cat wants continued touch, pausing is cheaper than restraining.

If I am uncertain whether a horse’s refusal reflects pain, investigating is cheaper than assuming defiance and escalating force.

Reversible action is a moral technology.

It makes room for correction.

This principle can scale from households to research.

Playback studies are a form of repair. Researchers propose a meaning through a signal manipulation and watch whether animals respond as predicted. Failure forces revision.

Machine learning can accelerate proposal generation but should not shorten the correction loop. A model can discover a cluster in whale codas. Fine. Generate controlled variants where ethically appropriate. Observe. Update. The goal is not a model that is never wrong. The goal is a system whose wrongness becomes informative quickly.

That is how science works at its best.

It is also how good relationships work.

There is another kind of repair that matters: social repair after conflict.

Many social animals reconcile, reaffiliate, groom, approach, vocalize, or otherwise alter interaction after aggression. The details vary by species. Calling every post-conflict contact an apology would be anthropomorphic. But the broader phenomenon reveals that relationships can survive local breakdown.

Humans often assume communication exists to prevent conflict.

Sometimes its job is to preserve a relationship after conflict has already happened.

A dog growls during handling. The human stops. Minutes later the dog approaches. The person allows contact without punishing the earlier warning.

The relationship has carried information through conflict.

The growl did not end trust. It may have protected it because the human treated the signal as useful.

Contrast that with a system in which warning is punished.

The animal learns that early communication produces cost. The person learns that absence of warning means absence of discomfort. The next conflict becomes more dangerous because the repair channel has been removed.

Suppressing a signal is not the same as resolving the problem the signal expressed.

This is one of the most practically important lessons in the book.

Humans love quiet animals.

Quiet dogs in apartments.

Quiet horses during procedures.

Quiet livestock in facilities.

Quiet zoo animals around visitors.

Quiet laboratory animals during handling.

Sometimes quiet reflects comfort and habituation. Sometimes it reflects training. Sometimes it reflects fear, shutdown, helplessness, exhaustion, or a history in which signaling changed nothing.

Silence is ambiguous.

A good communication system must therefore preserve ways for the animal to indicate failure before behavior becomes extreme.

Repair is one of those ways.

A signal says something did not work.

The human has to allow the correction to alter the next move.

This idea exposes a weakness in conventional obedience as a measure of understanding.

Perfect compliance can conceal misunderstanding.

An animal may follow habitual cues despite conflicting context. It may perform under pressure. It may have learned that improvisation is not rewarded. A flexible partner is sometimes more communicative because it can interrupt the routine when the routine no longer fits reality.

Search-and-rescue dogs, guide dogs, detection dogs, and other working animals illustrate this tension. Training requires reliability, but real environments contain situations the human cannot fully perceive. In some tasks, the animal’s independent judgment is part of the value. A guide dog trained in “intelligent disobedience,” for example, may refuse a cue when following it would be unsafe.

The phrase is rhetorically irresistible and the training reality more complex, but the principle is powerful: the best interspecies system sometimes gives the animal authority to break the human’s expected sequence.

That break is repair at a higher level.

The human says go.

The animal says no.

The human updates because the animal may know something the human does not.

We will devote the next chapter to refusal because it is where communication and power become inseparable.

For now, notice that repair depends on a receiver willing to admit error.

A human who cannot be wrong cannot communicate well with an animal.

Every ambiguous response becomes disobedience.

Every failed cue becomes the animal’s fault.

Every refusal becomes defiance.

Every unusual behavior becomes personality rather than information.

The third language cannot grow under those conditions because only one participant is allowed to revise.

This is true in human organizations too. A boss who cannot be corrected receives less information. A system that punishes bad news becomes informationally blind. People learn to tell power what power can hear.

Animals learn the same structure through consequences.

If the human never responds to subtle discomfort, subtle discomfort becomes useless.

If the human responds reliably, subtle signals can become stable.

The receiver trains the sender.

The sender trains the receiver.

Repair trains both.

This may be the deepest reason to call the emergent system a third language rather than a set of commands. A command system can succeed without repair if the hierarchy is simple enough. One side issues; the other complies.

A language between species has to survive mismatch.

Bodies differ.

Senses differ.

Motivations differ.

Context is incomplete.

Human interpretations are wrong.

Animal signals are ambiguous.

Tools fail.

Attention wanders.

There is no path to mutual understanding that does not pass through error.

The future of animal communication will therefore depend less on perfect decoding than on elegant correction.

Can the system tell us when it is uncertain?

Can the animal reject the interpretation?

Can the human try a cheaper alternative?

Can a device preserve the interaction history so repeated misunderstandings become visible?

Can the model learn the individual rather than forcing every animal into a population average?

Can the interface make it easier for the animal to say, in whatever form its body permits, *that is not what I meant*?

We may never translate that sentence.

We can still build a world in which the correction works.

Repair also gives us a way to distinguish two superficially similar systems: one in which an animal has learned to get a result and one in which the animal has learned to communicate with a particular partner.

Imagine a dog trained to ring a bell for outside access. If the usual person does not respond, the dog rings again. That may be simple persistence. Now replace the person with someone who never responds to the bell but does respond when the dog approaches and touches a knee. If the dog gradually recruits the new human through the new channel while continuing to use the bell with the old human, partner history has entered the system.

The animal is not merely performing the strongest behavior.

It is navigating receivers.

A similar design could be used experimentally with horses, parrots, goats, or zoo animals. Establish two human partners with different reliable response contingencies. Then create controlled misunderstandings. Does the animal repeat, escalate, switch modality, or select the human whose history predicts success?

The result would not prove language.

It would reveal something more directly relevant to the third-language thesis: whether repair is audience-specific.

This matters because most consumer claims about animal communication focus on successful “messages.” Success is visually satisfying. Repair is harder to package. Yet a system that produces many correct-looking outcomes without any capacity to handle misunderstanding may be brittle pattern matching rather than robust communication.

Human language would be unusable without repair.

A pet interface should therefore be judged partly by the same standard.

Can the animal indicate that the interpretation was wrong?

Can the human ask for clarification without imposing the answer?

Can the interface represent an unresolved state instead of forcing a label?

Can either participant end the exchange?

These questions suggest a design that looks less like translation software and more like a conversation protocol.

The first output is provisional.

The second turn tests it.

The third turn updates.

The sequence ends when uncertainty has fallen enough for a low-cost action or when the animal disengages.

This may sound computational because it is. It also describes good ordinary interaction.

Humans can become more scientific with animals simply by slowing the moment between interpretation and action.

The dog paces near the door.

Hypothesis: outside.

Move toward the door.

The dog walks instead toward the water bowl.

Revision.

That tiny failure prevented a false story from becoming a habit.

Now imagine a thousand such sequences recorded over months. A personalized model could begin estimating not just what signals predict outcomes but which human responses produce informative clarification. It could learn that one dog redirects through movement, another through repeated gaze, another through sound, and another gives up quickly.

The model would be learning the animal’s repair style.

That may be more valuable than learning a vocabulary.

Vocabulary tells us what a signal usually predicts.

Repair tells us how the animal handles our ignorance.

Every cross-species relationship contains a lot of ignorance.

A mature communication system should be built around it rather than pretending it has disappeared.
