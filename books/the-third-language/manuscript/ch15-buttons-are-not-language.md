# Buttons Are Not Language

The dog presses BEACH.

The human laughs because they went to the beach yesterday.

The dog presses BEACH again.

Then OUTSIDE.

The sequence is immediately irresistible. Perhaps the dog is asking to return. Perhaps BEACH has become a category for a kind of outing. Perhaps the dog remembers yesterday and is referring to an absent place. Perhaps the dog has learned that BEACH produces a particularly animated human response and is pressing it because animated humans are useful. Perhaps the first press was accidental and the second was reinforced by laughter. Perhaps OUTSIDE carries the request and BEACH is noise.

The buttons do not tell us which story is true.

They tell us what sound played.

This distinction is almost aggressively unromantic. It is also what makes communication buttons scientifically interesting rather than merely adorable.

Augmentative interspecies communication devices—large buttons that play recorded words when an animal presses them—have spread through pet households, especially among dog owners. Some animals use boards containing dozens of buttons. Their humans report combinations that appear novel, context-sensitive, emotionally expressive, and sometimes astonishingly apt. Online audiences follow particular animals as if watching a new language emerge in real time.

The phenomenon deserves study because several different things could be happening at once.

Animals can learn that pressing a location produces a consequence.

They can discriminate recorded sound patterns.

They can associate sounds with objects, actions, people, and routines.

They can learn sequences.

They can exploit human reactions.

They can develop habits specific to a board’s spatial layout.

They may also use some buttons intentionally to recruit human action in ways that go beyond simple reward seeking.

The scientific problem is separating these layers.

In 2024, researchers led by Federico Rossano’s Comparative Cognition Lab published controlled work on soundboard-trained dogs. One study asked how dogs responded when humans pressed familiar buttons, comparing words such as PLAY and OUTSIDE with other controls. Dogs showed behavior consistent with understanding at least some of the button-associated words, and importantly, the effect was not confined to owners producing the sounds. Another analysis of two-button combinations reported that dogs’ sequences were not adequately explained as random combinations or simple imitation of immediately preceding human button presses.

These results matter.

They justify moving the discussion beyond “owners are imagining everything.”

They do not justify “dogs have acquired human language.”

Between dismissal and declaration lies the interesting territory.

A button is a strange communicative object because it combines several things humans normally keep separate.

It has a location.

It produces a sound.

The sound is a human word.

The word has a human meaning.

The press can trigger a human reaction.

The reaction can produce an environmental consequence.

For the dog, any subset of these features may matter.

Suppose OUTSIDE is always in the same place on the board. A dog could learn a spatial motor contingency: press this surface, door opens. The recorded word may be irrelevant.

Suppose the dog also responds when a hidden speaker plays “outside,” or behaves appropriately when a person presses the OUTSIDE button from a different position. Now the sound pattern becomes more important.

Suppose the button is moved and the dog finds it.

Suppose several buttons are rearranged.

Suppose the door is already open.

Suppose the dog presses OUTSIDE in a novel location.

Each manipulation separates possible explanations.

Science earns meaning by making alternatives fail.

A household usually does the opposite. It rewards a useful signal regardless of mechanism.

If the dog presses OUTSIDE and the human opens the door, both participants have achieved something even if the dog has no lexical representation resembling the human concept *outside*.

This is where buttons become important to the third language.

Their communicative value may be greater than their linguistic value.

A doorbell does not need to mean “I conceptualize exterior space” to function as a reliable request for access. The animal has gained a way to alter the human’s behavior. The human has gained a signal easier to perceive than standing silently near a door.

The interface makes one turn in the relationship more legible.

That is a real achievement.

Human language status is a separate question.

We should care about both without making either answer hostage to the other.

This separation would improve the public discussion immediately.

Skeptics often point out, correctly, that button videos are vulnerable to selection bias, cueing, owner interpretation, spatial learning, reinforcement, and the Clever Hans effect. Enthusiasts point out, also correctly, that dogs can learn many arbitrary associations and that some button uses appear systematic, contextually appropriate, and flexible enough to deserve investigation.

The two sides then argue about whether the dog is “talking.”

The word carries too much weight.

Ask smaller questions and the field opens.

Can dogs distinguish the recorded words?

Can they generalize a button’s function across location?

Do they use buttons differently with different humans?

Do they persist or reformulate when misunderstood?

Do combinations carry information beyond individual button frequencies?

Do dogs use buttons when the referenced object or action is absent?

Can they indicate events not currently happening?

Can they learn a new button through social exposure rather than explicit shaping?

Do buttons give animals meaningful control over daily outcomes?

Do humans become better observers after using them?

Does the board crowd out more natural signals?

Does use increase frustration when humans fail to respond?

These questions do not require a premature vote on language.

They also recognize that the interface changes the relationship even if no breakthrough in animal syntax occurs.

Once a dog has an obvious OUTSIDE button, the human’s moral position shifts slightly.

Before the board, the person could plausibly miss a glance toward the door.

After the board, a clear signal is harder to ignore.

Technology makes the animal’s request legible and therefore makes human nonresponse more visible.

This is a recurring pattern in accessibility technology. A person who lacked an easy means of expression can be mistaken for lacking something to express. Once an interface makes communication possible, the receiver’s obligations change.

The analogy must be handled carefully. Disabled humans using augmentative and alternative communication are language users with human linguistic and social capacities; pet buttons are not equivalent. Borrowing the vocabulary of AAC for animals can obscure this difference.

The structural insight is still useful: an interface can change whose preferences are actionable.

That is why animal-computer interaction should be interested in more than vocabulary.

An animal may benefit from a reliable mechanism to request access, play, social contact, cessation, temperature change, sensory enrichment, or another choice without ever composing a sentence.

The design challenge is to make those choices real.

A button labeled STOP is meaningless if nothing stops.

A button labeled PLAY is degraded if it produces play unpredictably.

A choice system whose options are all acceptable to the human but none desirable to the animal is not much of a choice.

The interface has to be backed by consequences.

This sounds obvious until commercial products enter the room.

A talking-pet device has incentives different from an animal-agency device.

The talking-pet product is rewarded for generating emotionally compelling output. It wants lots of words, surprising combinations, shareable moments, and the impression that a hidden human voice has been discovered inside the animal.

An agency-oriented interface might be boring.

It might contain six choices that reliably matter.

Outside.

Water.

Play.

Contact.

Alone.

Stop.

If those choices genuinely alter the animal’s environment, six buttons could be more consequential than sixty words producing ambiguous performances.

The distinction resembles the difference between a toy keyboard and an emergency call system. One produces expressive sound. The other carries institutional force.

Animals need force behind their signals—not physical force, but consequence.

This also changes how we should evaluate “success.”

A device manufacturer may count presses.

A researcher may count correct responses.

A third-language approach asks whether the pair becomes better at resolving uncertainty with less escalation.

Does the dog need to scratch the door less because OUTSIDE works?

Does the animal show fewer conflict behaviors because STOP is honored?

Does the human identify needs earlier?

Does the animal use the system voluntarily?

Does misunderstanding lead to repair rather than abandonment?

Does the interface support individual differences rather than forcing every animal into the same vocabulary?

These are relationship outcomes.

They are harder to market and more meaningful.

Buttons also reveal something uncomfortable about human interpretation: printed labels create semantic gravity.

If a button says MAD, every press invites the human to search for anger.

If a button says LOVE YOU, every press invites intimacy.

The label can bias the observer before the animal has done anything else.

Imagine the same board with arbitrary symbols—triangle, circle, wave, star—whose consequences humans know but whose words are never spoken aloud. We would interpret combinations differently. The animal behavior could be identical while the emotional story changed.

This is one reason controlled research needs to distinguish spoken-word comprehension from button location and consequence.

It is also a reason consumer interfaces should be humble about abstract emotional vocabulary.

Concrete actions are easier to ground.

Outside can be tested by opening a door.

Play can be tested by offering play.

Water can be tested against access and thirst-related context.

Love is not a manipulable outcome.

Mad is an inference.

Why is a philosophical abyss.

The more abstract the label, the greater the room for human narrative to do the animal’s work.

This does not mean animals lack emotions or abstract cognition. It means a button press is weak evidence for a specific human lexical category when the category cannot be behaviorally anchored.

A serious interface should separate what can be validated from what is interpretive.

The same standard will matter when AI generates the labels automatically.

Suppose a camera watches a dog pace, vocalize, orient toward a person, and move toward the door. A model outputs *I’m anxious because I need to pee.*

The sentence contains at least three claims:

There is an internal affective state.

There is a bodily need.

The animal represents the need as a reason for the affect.

The observed behavior may support none, one, or two of those claims with different confidence.

Fluent language bundles them.

A button at least leaves the press visible. Generative translation can hide the inference chain entirely.

This is why I am unexpectedly optimistic about simple buttons.

They are crude enough to keep the mechanism in sight.

The dog physically presses.

The word plays.

The human responds.

Everyone can see where interpretation enters.

The board is not magic.

A sophisticated AI translator may make the same uncertainty disappear behind a clean voice.

Buttons also teach humans to wait.

Owners who work seriously with soundboards often describe learning not to immediately supply the answer they expect, not to point or hover over the board, not to interpret every sequence dramatically, and to give the animal time to choose. Good practice begins to resemble good experimental hygiene.

The human has to become less helpful.

This is difficult because we are compulsive completion machines. We finish sentences. We anticipate requests. We model what we think another being wants and act before being asked. In close relationships this can be efficient. In a learning interface it can prevent the other participant from showing what they can do.

Waiting creates evidence.

It also creates agency.

An animal cannot initiate if the human always initiates first.

That principle reaches beyond buttons. Many interspecies relationships are structured so densely by human schedules that animals rarely get to start a sequence. We decide feeding, walks, training, contact, transport, work, medical care, social access, and rest. Then we wonder whether the animal can communicate preferences.

A communication system needs open turns.

The animal requires moments in which its action can begin something rather than merely respond to a human cue.

Buttons can create those moments because they sit available.

The dog can press when the human has not asked a question.

That feature may be more important than vocabulary size.

Initiation changes the direction of control.

Researchers should study it explicitly. How often does the animal initiate versus respond? Which buttons are used spontaneously? Which produce consistent human consequences? Does spontaneous use change as the human becomes more responsive? Does the animal recruit the board more during ambiguity or when other signals fail?

These questions connect buttons directly to repair.

Suppose the dog presses OUTSIDE. The human opens the door. The dog does not leave.

What happens next?

A weak interpretation says the dog used the button incorrectly.

A romantic interpretation invents metaphor.

A third-language interpretation waits.

Perhaps the dog presses PLAY.

Perhaps it walks to the car.

Perhaps it stares at something outside.

Perhaps it abandons the sequence.

The failed first hypothesis creates an opportunity for clarification.

This is how we should want an interface to behave. Not as a sentence generator, but as a platform for iterative disambiguation.

Human conversation works this way constantly.

“Can you open that?”

“The window?”

“No, the file.”

We do not regard the first ambiguity as evidence that language failed. Repair is part of language working.

Animal interfaces should be judged partly by whether they allow repair.

A fixed classifier does not. It emits its answer.

A shared board might.

The animal signals. The human acts. The animal redirects. The human updates.

The sequence contains more information than the first button.

That is the third language becoming visible in plastic.

There is one final reason not to call buttons language too quickly.

Doing so can make us underestimate every animal without them.

If pressing recorded English is the standard for “talking,” a dog with no soundboard becomes silent again. A horse signaling through orientation and movement becomes inarticulate. A cat regulating distance becomes aloof. A whale’s combinatorial coda becomes pre-language until translated. A cuttlefish’s body becomes display rather than conversation.

The board should expand our idea of communication, not narrow it around a new human device.

Buttons are useful because they make one kind of reciprocity obvious.

They are not the destination.

A dog standing quietly by the door may already have said more to the right person than sixty buttons can say to a stranger.

The future of talking with animals will not be measured by how many English words we can make them produce.

It will be measured by how many reliable ways we give them to change our next move.
