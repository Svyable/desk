# The Private Dialect

Every long relationship becomes difficult to translate for outsiders.

A couple exchanges one look at dinner and changes the subject. A parent hears a child say “fine” and knows from one syllable that the word means its opposite. Two coworkers share a phrase whose significance depends on a project nobody else remembers. Friends send each other images that would be meaningless without ten years of accumulated references.

Public language made the interaction possible.

Private history made it efficient.

Animal relationships can develop the second property without possessing the first in anything like the same form.

That is the third language.

I have used the phrase throughout this book before fully defining it because I wanted the definition to be earned by examples rather than announced as a framework and forced onto the world. We have seen dogs incorporate humans into problem solving, cats respond through channels people overlook, horses and goats recruit people around inaccessible resources, ravens manage attention, dolphins and elephants use individually specific vocal information, sperm whales organize codas with combinatorial structure, bees alter communication in relation to audience, bats direct everyday social vocalizations, cuttlefish route displays across their bodies, and animals of many kinds repair when a receiver fails.

None of these phenomena proves the existence of a universal category called the third language.

The phrase describes what can happen when some of those capacities cross a species boundary and become historical.

A third language is a relationship-specific system of mutually consequential cues and signals that no participant brings complete to the relationship.

It can contain deliberate signals and accidental cues.

It can borrow pieces of human language.

It can borrow pieces of the animal’s species-typical communication.

It can include tools.

It can include locations, routines, timing, objects, and shared tasks.

Its defining feature is reciprocal learning: each participant’s behavior becomes partly organized around what the other has learned to notice and do.

That definition is intentionally broader than language in the linguistic sense.

Human language has properties a dog-human household system does not: combinatorial grammar, open-ended productivity, conventional symbolic reference, cultural transmission at population scale, and the ability to communicate richly about absent and hypothetical events, among many others. Calling a gaze-and-door routine “language” literally would obscure those differences.

The phrase *third language* is therefore a provocation with limits.

It asks us to treat the relational system as an object worth studying without pretending the system is English with fewer words.

Other scholars have approached neighboring territory from multiple directions. Zoosemiotics has long studied sign processes in animals. Anthropology and multispecies scholarship have examined how humans and animals become socially meaningful to one another. Donna Haraway wrote about companion species and “contact zones” in which humans and animals train and transform each other. Barbara Smuts described interspecies relationships as emergent, co-created dances rather than one-way observation. More recent work in pragmatics and interaction analysis has examined embodied human-animal communication, and contemporary reviews of interspecies communication increasingly describe meaning as co-created.

The third language does not claim that nobody has noticed reciprocity before.

That claim would be false.

The narrower contribution is to put the **shared code itself** at the center.

Not the human language.

Not the animal signal system.

Not the machine translator.

The code that exists because these particular participants have repeatedly changed one another.

Once we center that object, several predictions follow.

The first is that familiarity should matter in specific ways.

A familiar human should sometimes interpret an individual animal more accurately than a stranger, not because love grants telepathy but because the pair shares more observations and contingencies. The effect should be strongest for local signals whose meaning depends on routine and weaker for species-typical signals obvious to experienced outsiders.

An owner may know that one dog’s quiet stance beside a cabinet means medication time while any competent observer can recognize a full defensive threat. Familiarity adds local detail; it does not replace general ethology.

This prediction is testable.

Show complete interactions to familiar partners, unfamiliar experts, and unfamiliar novices. Ask them to predict what the animal will do next or what outcome will end the sequence. Compare accuracy. Then manipulate context. Which judgments depend on shared history and which generalize?

The second prediction is that communication within a familiar pair should often become smaller.

Signals that reliably work do not need to remain dramatic.

A dog whose glance opens the door need not bark. A horse whose weight shift produces a pause need not pull away. A cat whose departure stops touch need not bite. A human whose quiet cue is noticed need not repeat it.

Efficiency should therefore increase while visible intensity can decrease.

This creates a methodological paradox: the better the relationship works, the less obvious the communication may be to an outsider.

We already see this in human expertise. Skilled teams coordinate with fewer explicit instructions because shared models do work silently. A surgeon asks for an instrument with a hand movement. Musicians anticipate phrasing. Athletes read positioning. Couples finish practical tasks with fragments.

The third language should compress too.

That can be measured through latency, signal intensity, number of repair attempts, or the amount of behavior required to achieve a shared outcome.

The third prediction is that misunderstanding should reveal the private code.

Change the human.

Change the routine.

Move the object.

Interrupt the usual sequence.

A local signal should become less effective or require repair when the shared history is removed.

The animal may then escalate, switch channel, or abandon the attempt.

This is not merely a nuisance. It identifies which parts of the system were relationship-specific.

The fourth prediction is that humans should have idiolects too.

We usually study how animals respond to “human cues” as though humans form one standardized stimulus class. In reality, people point differently, speak differently, move differently, time reinforcement differently, maintain different distances, and carry different histories with the animal.

An animal living with several humans may build separate models of each.

One person opens the door after a stare.

Another responds only to a bark.

One rides with a slight forward weight shift.

Another uses a stronger leg cue.

One cat approaches one household member after vocal contact and another after sitting nearby.

The animal’s communication should be audience-designed around human individuality.

This is already supported in pieces by research showing animals discriminate familiar people, human voices, faces, attentional states, and knowledge-related cues. The third-language hypothesis asks for the next step: do animals develop partner-specific signaling strategies that reflect the response histories of those humans?

The fifth prediction is that machines trained on population averages will systematically miss some of the most useful meanings.

A universal dog model can identify broad postural patterns and perhaps classify common vocal contexts. It may outperform an inexperienced owner on species-general signals.

But it could miss that this dog circles clockwise twice before asking one person for access to a particular room, or that this horse’s earliest sign of discomfort is a change relative to its own baseline, or that one parrot uses a household sound in a local social routine.

Personalization will matter.

Not because every private interpretation is true, but because individual baselines and relationship histories carry information.

The ideal system may combine three layers.

Species knowledge: what patterns generally predict across animals.

Individual knowledge: what differs reliably for this animal.

Relational knowledge: what this animal does with this person under this shared history.

Current animal technology often jumps from the first layer directly to a sentence.

The third layer may be where the useful communication lives.

The sixth prediction is the most important: a healthy private dialect should contain correction.

If only the human’s interpretation can define meaning, the system is not reciprocal.

The animal needs ways to reveal that the human got it wrong.

Approach after the person steps back.

Refuse the opened door and lead somewhere else.

Press another button.

Repeat a gesture.

Switch modality.

Return after touch stops.

Leave when it continues.

The human needs to treat these outcomes as revisions rather than disobedience.

A private dialect without repair is just a private story.

This distinction protects the concept from the softest form of anthropomorphism: the belief that intimacy validates interpretation.

“I know what she’s thinking.”

Maybe.

What prediction follows?

What would change your mind?

What does the animal do when your response is wrong?

The third language should make us more empirical, not less.

Shared history is a source of hypotheses.

The animal’s next move is evidence.

There is a beautiful practical consequence to this view. People do not need to wait for a scientific breakthrough to become better interspecies communicators.

They need to become more consistent observers and more corrigible partners.

Notice before labeling.

Respond in small reversible ways.

Allow pauses.

Track what ends a sequence.

Distinguish a familiar animal’s baseline from generic body-language charts.

Do not punish useful warnings merely because they are unpleasant.

Give low-cost refusal real consequence.

Notice when your own body has become the cue.

Ask whether the animal behaves differently with different people.

Treat repeated misunderstanding as information about the system, not just the animal.

These are not steps in a branded method. They are habits of attention.

The most important one is learning to wait for the reply.

Waiting is hard because humans enjoy completion. We see an animal look at the leash and narrate *walk*. We see a horse hesitate and narrate *stubborn*. We see a cat leave and narrate *aloof*. We see a whale cluster and narrate *conversation*. The label ends uncertainty.

Waiting keeps the animal present.

What happens next?

That question is almost embarrassingly powerful.

It turns behavior into sequence.

Sequence reveals contingency.

Contingency reveals learning.

Learning reveals relationship.

The private dialect is made from the accumulated answers.

Consider how one might document such a dialect without pretending it is a dictionary.

Take a dog-human pair.

Record one month of naturally occurring interactions around recurring events: door access, food, play, rest, contact, separation, reunion, and ambiguous requests. Code both participants, not just the dog. Note who initiates, which channel appears first, latency to human response, whether the apparent outcome occurs, whether the dog repeats or changes behavior, and how the human repairs.

Now compare the same dog with a second household member.

Some patterns will generalize.

Others may not.

The difference is the relational layer.

Do the same with a horse and multiple handlers. A parrot and family members. A zoo animal with familiar keepers and unfamiliar staff. A working dog and handler versus a substitute handler.

Suddenly the third language becomes measurable without claiming to have discovered animal syntax.

This research could matter in practical settings where relationships influence safety and welfare.

Veterinary teams could identify how familiar caregiver presence affects animal response.

Working-animal programs could distinguish handler-specific cues from generalized training.

Shelters could study how new human relationships form and which communication patterns transfer from previous homes.

Zoos could measure keeper-animal interaction at the relational level rather than only individual behavior.

Livestock systems could examine whether some handlers elicit lower stress because of timing, predictability, or response to early signals.

Conservationists could study how wild animals develop localized responses to human communities.

The concept scales without requiring romance.

It also introduces a problem of loss.

When a relationship ends, a private dialect can disappear.

An animal rehomed after years with one person loses more than familiarity. It may lose a communicative system. Signals that once worked stop working. Human cues that predicted safety or routine vanish. The new household may interpret the animal through generic categories while the animal continues producing local behavior whose history nobody knows.

This could help explain part of the difficulty of transition for some animals.

The new person is not merely unfamiliar.

The new person is linguistically incompetent in the private system.

Again, not language literally. The metaphor points to a practical loss of mutual predictability.

The same is true when an experienced handler leaves a working partnership, when a keeper retires, or when an animal is moved between institutions.

Documentation could preserve pieces of the dialect.

What signals predict discomfort?

What routines reduce stress?

What cue forms are actually effective?

What does the animal do before requesting access?

How does it indicate a pause?

Which unusual behaviors have stable meanings in context?

Veterinary records document bodies. Behavioral handovers could document relationships.

Technology could help here if it records rather than overwrites.

A personalized system might learn patterns across months and generate summaries grounded in observed contingencies: *When this dog stands at the laundry-room threshold after 8 p.m., the next successful interaction is usually access to the yard; if the door is opened and the dog remains, it typically walks toward the water station.*

That is not as charming as *I need something else, human.*

It is far more transferable to a new caregiver.

The machine becomes a historian of the private dialect.

This is one of the rare places where AI could preserve relationship knowledge instead of replacing it.

The system would need strict safeguards. Behavioral histories can be misread. Correlation is not meaning. Owners can encode bias into labels. The model should expose examples and uncertainty rather than issue character judgments.

But the opportunity is real.

A long relationship produces data no population model has.

We should not throw it away because it is anecdotal, nor treat it as truth because it is intimate.

We can study it.

The private dialect also changes how we think about animal intelligence.

Cognitive tests often seek species capacities that generalize. Can dogs follow points? Can parrots discriminate same and different? Can elephants distinguish human categories? Can apes communicate intentionally?

Those questions matter because science needs replicable phenomena.

But intelligence in life is partly the ability to become competent in one’s local relationships.

A dog who knows one household deeply may fail a laboratory task with an unfamiliar experimenter. A horse who coordinates exquisitely with one rider may look less skilled with another. A wild animal may know one landscape, family network, or human community in detail.

Local intelligence is not lesser because it fails to travel.

Humans are like this too. We know our kitchens, workplaces, families, routes, devices, and social expectations with a depth no general intelligence test captures. Much of adult competence is context-bound expertise.

Why should other animals be different?

The third language treats relationship competence as a form of knowledge.

It is knowledge of another being as a predictable but revisable system.

That phrase sounds cold until you remember that affection is full of prediction.

You know who will reach for the check.

Who needs silence after bad news.

Who will laugh at the wrong moment.

Who says “nothing” when something is wrong.

Love does not eliminate statistical learning. It makes the statistics matter.

With animals, affection can motivate the attention required to build a private dialect. It can also distort it. The discipline is to let love make us more observant rather than more certain.

This is why the third language belongs neither to romantic animal lovers nor to reductive behaviorists alone.

The romantic is right that relationships create something unique.

The behaviorist is right that consequences shape signals.

The cognitive scientist is right that flexible representation and social inference matter.

The interaction analyst is right that meaning emerges in sequence.

The technologist is right that new sensors can reveal channels humans miss.

The ethicist is right that communication means little if the weaker participant cannot influence the stronger.

The object in the middle is the relationship itself.

We have studied animals.

We have studied humans.

We have studied signals between animals.

We are beginning to build machines that decode those signals.

The next frontier is to study the **between** as rigorously as the endpoints.

That is the third language.

It will not produce one dictionary because it is not one language.

It will produce maps of negotiated meaning: species-wide capacities layered with individual history, social relationships, tools, routines, and repair.

Some of those maps will be scientifically generalizable.

Some will remain beautifully local.

The dog at the door may never help us understand another dog in another house.

It can still teach us something astonishing about communication.

Two species met inside an architecture neither evolved to inhabit.

They found a signal.

They repeated it.

They corrected one another.

The door began to open.

Meaning accumulated around the hinge.
