# Names Across the Savannah

In 2024, a team studying wild African elephants published evidence that made one of the oldest human metaphors feel newly literal.

The elephants appeared to address one another with individually specific calls.

The work, led by Michael Pardo and colleagues, analyzed low-frequency elephant calls known as rumbles from two populations in Kenya. Machine-learning models could predict the receiver of a call from its acoustic structure better than chance. More importantly, playback experiments provided behavioral evidence: elephants responded more strongly to calls that had originally been addressed to them than to calls from the same caller that had been addressed to someone else.

The result was widely summarized as elephants having names.

That headline was irresistible for the same reason dolphin signature whistles are irresistible. Naming feels like a human door hidden in another species’ house.

But the elephant work may be even stranger than the shorthand suggests.

Dolphin signature whistles are individually distinctive signals developed by the individual and can be copied by others. The elephant study suggested that calls addressed to the same receiver shared some acoustic properties even across different callers, while the receiver’s own vocalizations did not simply provide an obvious template that callers were copying. If that interpretation survives further work, elephants may be using vocal labels for others that are not merely imitations of the addressee’s own signature.

The scientific details remain under active investigation. A later author correction adjusted parts of the published analysis without overturning the qualitative central finding. That should make us more, not less, attentive to the difference between an exciting result and a completed theory.

“Name-like” is a strong description.

It is not a license to fill in everything the word *name* means to us.

The better question is what individual addressing does inside elephant society.

Elephants live in social worlds built from memory.

Female African elephants often remain in matrilineal family groups, while broader social networks can include related families and more fluid associations. Individuals separate and reunite. They move over large landscapes. Low-frequency calls can travel beyond visual range. Older animals can carry social and ecological knowledge accumulated over decades. Relationships persist through time and distance.

A system capable of directing a call toward one individual would be useful in exactly this kind of society.

The receiver is not just “an elephant.”

It is someone.

That word—*someone*—is dangerous if used carelessly. It can smuggle human personhood categories into biology without argument. Yet the alternative word *something* is worse. The science itself is telling us that individual identity matters.

An elephant’s social environment contains named or name-like others, or at least acoustically individuated recipients.

The population is not an undifferentiated herd from the elephant’s point of view.

This has been obvious to field researchers for a long time from social behavior. Communication adds another layer because individuality is encoded into how animals address one another.

Elephants have also shown remarkable sensitivity to information carried in human voices.

In a 2014 *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* study, Karen McComb and colleagues played recordings of human speech to wild elephants in Amboseli National Park. The recordings varied the ethnic group, sex, and age of the speaker. Elephants reacted more defensively to voices associated with the human group posing greater historical danger to them, and they also discriminated sex and age categories relevant to risk.

The result did not require elephants to understand the spoken words.

They extracted socially useful information from human voices.

That distinction is crucial.

Humans usually imagine language comprehension from the inside out: what did the word mean?

An elephant can make the human voice meaningful without sharing the language. Acoustic properties predict categories of human beings. Those categories predict danger. The animal does not need a dictionary entry to participate intelligently in the social consequences of speech.

Human speech becomes an environmental signal inside elephant life.

This is the reverse of our usual animal-communication fantasy.

We want to decode their calls.

They have already been decoding us.

Not semantically in the human sense. Ecologically.

The human voice carries identity, age, sex, emotional state, location, movement, and cultural history. Animals living around us can learn which of these features matter. A crow learns a dangerous face. A dog learns the sound pattern of a name. A horse learns a caretaker’s routine. An elephant learns that some human voices predict more risk than others.

We broadcast continuously.

The third language begins before we intend to speak.

This is why the dream of a future animal translator can be so anthropocentric. It imagines a silent world waiting for humans to invent the interface. In reality, other species have been reading our bodies, voices, vehicles, smells, schedules, fences, weapons, food, trash, and movement for as long as we have shared environments.

We are already inside their data.

The ethical question is whether we are willing to enter ours.

Elephants make this question difficult because human-elephant communication occurs across enormous power and danger.

People living near elephants may face crop loss, property destruction, injury, and death. Elephants face habitat fragmentation, killing, harassment, fences, vehicles, changing water access, and lethal retaliation. Conservation language can become sentimental when it ignores the costs borne by people who share landscapes with large animals.

Communication does not dissolve conflict.

It may, however, change the tools available for managing it.

Researchers and communities have experimented with acoustic deterrents, bee-related barriers, lights, fences, early-warning systems, and other techniques for reducing conflict. Some methods exploit elephant sensory capacities. Others try to change human response times. The best interventions are not “conversations” in any rich sense, but they reveal a larger principle: if we understand what animals perceive and how they respond, we can design boundaries that communicate before conflict becomes lethal.

A fence is a message only if the animal can interpret it reliably enough to alter behavior.

A warning system is a message to humans only if people can act on it.

The landscape becomes a network of interspecies signals whether anyone calls it language or not.

The problem is that humans often communicate inconsistently.

One field is defended aggressively; another is not. One vehicle pursues; another observes. One person feeds; another threatens. A tourist habituates an animal to proximity; a farmer later pays the cost. An elephant learns from a pattern that humans themselves do not coordinate.

The animal’s “behavior problem” can be partly a human signaling problem at population scale.

This is the third language enlarged beyond a pair.

Most of this book uses dyads because the logic is easiest to see when one animal and one human respond to each other. Real systems are often many-to-many. Animals learn categories of people. People inherit local knowledge about animals. Communities develop practices. Animals teach one another. A young crow learns danger socially. A calf follows experienced elephants. Dogs learn routines from other dogs. Human families transmit beliefs about what animals mean.

A third language can therefore become cultural.

This possibility complicates the claim that it is private.

A private dialect begins in shared history. But shared histories can spread. A stable signal used by one human with many animals can become a local convention. An animal’s behavior can be copied socially. A husband learns the cat’s request from his wife. A new stable worker learns the subtle release timing used by the old stable worker. A community learns that one elephant approach pattern predicts retreat and another predicts danger.

Private systems can become communal without becoming species-wide.

Human language itself contains layers like this: idiolect, family shorthand, professional jargon, regional dialect, national standard, technical notation. Interspecies communication may also exist at multiple scales.

The elephant “name” work gives us a fascinating species-scale element—an addressing system among elephants. Human-elephant relationships add local layers on top of it.

Imagine what happens when machine learning can reliably detect which elephant is calling, whom a call is likely directed toward, what social group is involved, and whether a sequence predicts movement or agitation.

The immediate temptation will be translation.

A phone displays: *Nala is calling Mara.*

Perhaps that interface would be useful. But it could also conceal uncertainty about identity, function, and context behind a clean sentence.

A better system might help field teams make operational decisions without pretending to speak elephant. It could detect that a known family group is approaching a corridor, identify an individually distinctive caller, estimate whether calls resemble contact or arousal patterns previously observed, and combine acoustic evidence with movement and location.

The purpose would not be to make elephants sound human.

It would be to make humans more responsive to elephant information.

That reversal is one of the book’s central commitments.

The question is not “When will animals finally be able to tell us what they want?”

Many already alter behavior in ways that contain useful information about what they seek, avoid, attend to, or recognize.

The question is “When will our systems be designed to treat that information as actionable?”

A name-like call is exciting because it makes animal individuality legible to us. But elephants have been individual all along.

The technology did not create the relationship.

It created evidence that humans could recognize.

This distinction is easy to forget whenever a scientific discovery receives a headline suggesting that animals have just acquired a new capacity. “Scientists discover elephants have names” can sound as if naming began when our statistical model detected it.

Discovery happened to us.

The elephants were busy.

This is more than a philosophical point. It should affect how we build the next generation of research.

If we assume communicative structure exists only where humans have identified a signal, we design studies around known categories. If we assume animals may organize interaction along dimensions we have not noticed, we collect richer context and allow models to reveal unexpected structure.

The elephant study combined machine learning with playback because neither alone was enough.

Computational analysis found acoustic patterns associated with receiver identity.

Playback asked elephants whether those patterns mattered.

This pairing is a model for the field.

Machines search.

Animals answer.

Humans revise.

The loop itself is a scientific third language.

There is a practical difficulty, however. Animal responses are not always clean.

A playback may produce stronger approach, vocalization, orientation, or arousal under one condition, but no single behavior necessarily equals recognition. Field conditions vary. Social context matters. Individuals differ. Sample sizes can be limited by the reality of studying long-lived wild animals. Statistical models can overfit. Correction papers happen. Replication matters.

The public prefers a translated noun.

Science lives in the error bars.

A responsible bestseller about animal communication has to preserve both—the astonishment and the uncertainty.

The astonishment is that elephants appear capable of individually specific vocal address.

The uncertainty concerns exactly how the acoustic coding works, how broadly it is used, how labels are learned, whether different callers converge on shared forms, what other information is embedded in calls, and how these findings generalize across populations and call types.

Those open questions are not holes in the story.

They are the story continuing.

One day, researchers may know enough to synthesize an individually addressed elephant call with confidence. If that day comes, the ethical stakes will resemble the dolphin problem.

Being able to call someone is not the same as having something legitimate to say.

A synthetic call could alter movement, attention, reunion, vigilance, or social expectation. It could help guide animals away from danger. It could also disrupt social behavior or become another tool of control.

The first question should not be whether the call sounds convincing to humans.

It should be what elephants do with it.

That sounds obvious, but generative AI pushes us toward the opposite criterion. We judge synthetic human language largely by whether it sounds plausible to us. Fluency is perceptual. An animal communication system requires a different evaluator: the animal’s behavior.

A model can fool a person and fail an elephant.

That is good news.

The elephant gets a vote.

This is the most important methodological idea in the book and perhaps the most important ethical one too. Other species should not merely supply training data for systems that produce human-readable stories. Their responses should constrain what we claim the systems know.

The animal reply is ground truth only imperfectly—we can still misinterpret behavior—but it is better than human plausibility alone.

Imagine a model that claims to decode an elephant call as *Come here*.

If controlled playback of the relevant structure does not change approach behavior, the translation has a problem.

If response depends entirely on which individual caller produced it, the generic translation has a problem.

If the call works only in reunion contexts, the translation has a problem.

If receivers react differently depending on prior social history, the translation has a problem.

Each failure makes the model less fluent and more true.

This is how a third-language approach disciplines AI: it refuses to let a sentence erase the relationship.

Elephants also remind us that communication can travel across scales humans are poor at inhabiting.

Low-frequency rumbles can propagate over substantial distances. Elephants also use seismic information transmitted through the ground. Their sensory world includes vibrations that human bodies can notice only crudely without instruments. A social call may therefore exist in an acoustic and vibrational environment we experience only in fragments.

A human translator is not merely decoding another vocabulary.

We are translating another physics.

This makes the subtitle dream increasingly strange. Before asking what the signal “says,” we have to ask what the signal *is* to the animal. Which frequencies carry information? Which components travel? What does the receiver perceive at distance? How do ground conditions alter transmission? Which body structures detect it? What other elephants are calling simultaneously? Which channel is primary in this context?

Meaning is built on sensation.

A species cannot communicate through a world it cannot perceive.

Humans entering animal communication therefore need humility not only about cognition but about embodiment. We are often the sensory-disabled participant in the room.

The elephant hears a voice and extracts who is dangerous.

The human hears a rumble and needs microphones, databases, machine learning, behavioral observation, and playback experiments to begin asking whom it addresses.

Which species is the better decoder?

The answer depends on the problem.

Elephants do not need to read our papers. They need to survive our presence.

We do not need to become elephants. We need to understand enough of their communicative world to stop treating silence as absence and individuality as projection.

A call moves across the savannah.

Somewhere, one elephant responds more strongly because that call was for her.

The astonishing thing is not that the signal can be translated into a familiar human category.

The astonishing thing is that, before we knew how to listen, someone was already being addressed.
