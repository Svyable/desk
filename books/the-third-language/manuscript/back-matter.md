# Back Matter

## A note on evidence

*The Third Language* makes an argument across animal behavior, comparative cognition, bioacoustics, interaction research, animal-computer interaction, and emerging machine-learning work. Those fields do not use the word *communication* in exactly the same way, and they should not be forced into one definition.

The manuscript therefore tries to keep three levels separate.

The first is observation: what an animal or human did.

The second is demonstrated contingency: what changed reliably with receiver, context, signal, audience, knowledge, or consequence.

The third is interpretation: what psychological or semantic account best explains the pattern.

Many public claims about animal communication collapse the three. A dog looks at a person and becomes “asking for help.” An elephant call contains receiver-specific information and becomes a “name.” A button press becomes a “word.” A structured whale coda becomes “language.” These short descriptions can be useful, but the underlying evidence is usually narrower than the headline.

The source ledger for this book records the primary studies and major reviews underlying its concrete scientific claims. Wherever possible, the manuscript prefers original research over popular summaries. Current technology claims about projects such as CETI, Earth Species Project, and DolphinGemma use project or company material for descriptions of the systems themselves, while scientific conclusions are kept tied to peer-reviewed work where available.

This is a first trade-book draft, not a systematic review. The research trail is intended to make fact checking, correction, expansion, and skeptical reading easier.

## What “the third language” does not claim

The phrase *third language* is the book’s organizing idea, not a claim that ordinary human-animal routines possess all the formal properties of human language.

It does not claim that:

- every animal signal is intentional;
- every learned contingency is communication;
- animals secretly think in English sentences;
- a familiar human is necessarily an accurate interpreter;
- co-created interspecies meaning has never been discussed before;
- artificial intelligence has translated any animal communication system in a general human-language sense;
- every refusal can always be honored regardless of safety or medical necessity;
- richer communication automatically produces more humane treatment.

The core claim is narrower: repeated interaction can produce relationship-specific systems of mutually consequential cues and signals that neither species brings complete to the relationship. These systems can be studied as systems rather than treated merely as failed human language or as noise around the animal’s “real” species-typical communication.

That claim sits near existing work in zoosemiotics, multispecies anthropology, companion-species scholarship, interspecies pragmatics, animal-computer interaction, and the analysis of human-animal embodied interaction. The research notes describe that prior-art boundary directly.

## On anthropomorphism and anthropodenial

Animal communication attracts two symmetrical mistakes.

Anthropomorphism can put human motives, words, and emotions into an animal before the evidence supports them.

Anthropodenial can treat every resemblance to human social or cognitive ability as suspicious merely because it occurs in another species.

Neither posture is rigorous by itself.

The manuscript’s preferred discipline is experimental humility: state what happened, identify plausible alternatives, design conditions that can separate them, and let the animal’s response constrain the claim. Clever Hans remains the perfect warning because the glamorous claim—arithmetic—was wrong while the less glamorous ability—extraordinary sensitivity to subtle human cues—was real.

## On names

The book uses familiar shorthand such as “name-like calls,” “asking for help,” “repair,” “address,” and “refusal” when those terms improve readability, but those phrases should be understood in relation to the studies described. They do not imply full equivalence with human linguistic or legal categories.

Similarly, examples involving hypothetical future animal interfaces are design thought experiments unless a source is provided for an existing system.

No composite animal is presented as a real observed individual. Household scenes are deliberately generic illustrations of interactional structure rather than reported biographies of identifiable animals.

## The research invitation

The third-language idea makes several empirical predictions that could be tested more directly than the metaphor might suggest.

Familiar partners should sometimes coordinate with smaller signals and shorter repair sequences than unfamiliar partners.

Animals should develop human-specific signaling patterns when different people have reliably different response histories.

Relationship-specific patterns should degrade, require repair, or change form when the familiar human is replaced.

A model that combines species-level, individual-level, and relationship-level history should outperform a species-only model on some practical prediction tasks.

Interfaces that preserve uncertainty and allow iterative correction should produce fewer persistent misunderstandings than one-shot “translation” interfaces.

Animals whose early low-intensity signals reliably alter human behavior should, in some contexts, need less escalation than animals whose early signals are ignored.

These are not all easy experiments. They involve long observation, individual histories, ethical intervention, and the risk that “relationship” becomes a catch-all variable. But difficulty is not vagueness. The claims can be narrowed, operationalized, and falsified.

If the idea survives, it could provide a useful bridge between animal communication research and the practical systems humans build around animals.

If it fails, the failures should tell us where relationship history matters less than species-wide signaling, conditioning, individual temperament, environmental context, or other explanations.

Either result would improve the conversation.

## Author

Sven Hardy Benson writes about systems, technology, incentives, institutions, uncertainty, and the ways apparently technical changes alter ordinary human life. *The Third Language* extends that interest across a species boundary: not by treating animals as metaphors for people, but by asking what communication becomes when the participants do not share a body, a sensory world, or a native language.
