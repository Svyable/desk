# The Bird Points

A raven picks up an object in its beak.

That by itself means little. Ravens manipulate things constantly. They inspect, carry, play with, cache, steal, drop, and destroy objects. A beak is a tool and an object is often just an object.

Then the raven holds the object up toward another raven.

The other bird looks.

In observations of wild ravens, Simone Pika and Thomas Bugnyar documented behaviors they described as referential gestures: birds would show or offer nonfood objects to another individual, apparently directing the recipient’s attention toward the object. The work was striking because “showing” and “offering” gestures had long occupied a special place in human developmental research and great ape communication. Here they appeared in a bird with a very different body and evolutionary history.

The raven does not have a pointing finger.

It has a beak, an object, a recipient, and the ability to notice whether the recipient is attending.

That is enough to unsettle a human assumption.

We often think the form of a signal reveals the sophistication of the mind behind it. A finger extended toward an object looks communicative because we recognize the gesture from ourselves. A beak holding moss toward another bird looks like object manipulation until the social sequence is examined.

Interaction rescues us from anatomy.

The question is not whether the animal possesses our gesture.

The question is whether an action has been organized around another participant’s attention.

Ravens and their corvid relatives have become famous in animal cognition because they repeatedly do things that make the word “birdbrain” embarrassing. They cache food and respond to the social risk of theft. They remember relationships. They solve physical problems. They play. Crows can remember dangerous human faces for years and can learn socially from one another about human threats. Ravens form complex social relationships outside breeding pairs. Their intelligence evolved on a branch far from primates, which makes similarities especially informative.

Convergent solutions are intellectual gifts.

If two very different nervous systems arrive at some similar social capacity, we are less able to dismiss that capacity as a quirk of primate hands or mammalian faces.

The Pika and Bugnyar observations are useful for another reason. The gestures occurred in wild, nonhuman interaction.

The birds were not pressing a human-designed symbol, following a trainer’s cue, or solving a laboratory task for food.

They were doing something with each other.

This matters because animal communication research often becomes human-centered by necessity. We ask whether a dog understands our point, whether a horse asks us for help, whether a parrot can learn our labels, whether a dolphin follows our gesture. Those are sensible questions if the topic is interspecies communication. But before we ask an animal to enter our channel, we should understand something about the channels it already uses with its own species.

The bird pointing with an object gives us a clue about what kinds of social problems it is already equipped to solve.

Attention matters.

Objects can become triadic: bird, partner, thing.

Interaction can be about more than immediate food or threat.

Those capacities create an overlap with human communication even if the signal itself is not inherited from a common gestural language.

A third language does not begin at zero.

It begins where capacities overlap.

Corvids have another capacity particularly relevant to humans: they can remember us as individuals or at least discriminate reliably among human appearances associated with different histories.

John Marzluff and colleagues conducted a now-famous series of experiments with American crows in Seattle. Researchers wearing a particular mask captured and banded crows, creating a negative experience associated with that face. Later, people wearing the dangerous mask were scolded by crows, and the response persisted over years and spread socially to birds that had not been captured themselves.

The theatrical detail—the mask—makes the experiment easy to remember. Its consequence is more serious.

A human face can become part of a crow social information network.

To the crows, “human” is not necessarily one category. Some humans have histories.

This is a precondition for a private dialect because individual recognition allows communication to become partner-specific. If an animal cannot distinguish one human from another, a relationship-specific system is difficult to build. If it can, then one person’s behavior can acquire meaning not shared by another’s.

Domestic animals make this obvious. Wild animals can do it too.

Anyone who works around urban wildlife eventually discovers that animals learn local human patterns. Gulls know where food appears. Pigeons discriminate danger and indifference. Crows notice people who feed them and people who harass them. Squirrels calibrate flight distance around pedestrian behavior. These interactions do not all qualify as communication. Much of the information is cue use: animals exploiting predictable human routines.

But cue use can become social contingency when the human notices the animal noticing.

A person places food after a particular crow arrives.

The crow begins arriving at a particular time.

The person alters the placement.

The crow changes position.

The human responds again.

A local system forms without a single deliberate “word.”

The scientific challenge is to distinguish that system from a chain of independent conditioning events.

The practical distinction may be less sharp than we want. Conditioning is one mechanism by which a communicative system can form. The relevant question is whether participants can use the learned contingency flexibly around one another.

Can the bird recruit the human outside the original routine?

Can it direct attention?

Can it modify behavior when the human fails to respond?

Does the signal depend on which human is present?

Does the bird behave differently when the human is looking?

Those questions move us from mere regularity toward interaction.

Parrots take us farther because humans have spent decades explicitly building symbolic systems with them.

No discussion of this history can avoid Alex, the African grey parrot studied by Irene Pepperberg. Beginning in the 1970s, Pepperberg used a model/rival training method in which humans demonstrated exchanges rather than treating the parrot as a passive conditioning subject. Across years, Alex learned vocal labels associated with objects, colors, shapes, materials, quantities, and relational concepts. Published experiments reported performance on tasks involving same/different relations and numerical labeling that could not reasonably be dismissed as a bird simply mimicking sound without discrimination.

The public story became “the talking parrot.”

The science was more disciplined.

Pepperberg’s work did not show that Alex possessed human language. It showed that a parrot could learn and use a substantial set of human vocal labels in experimentally testable ways, including some abstract relational tasks.

The difference matters because the more spectacular claim would actually make the research less interesting.

If Alex were simply a small feathered human, the result would tell us that human cognition had appeared twice.

Instead, an animal with a radically different brain and vocal system learned to enter a narrow part of a human symbolic world.

That is interspecies engineering.

Humans changed their behavior to make the system learnable. The parrot changed behavior to participate. The training method itself depended on social interaction: observing humans exchange roles, labels, objects, and consequences.

The code was borrowed from English, but the communicative arrangement was built between species.

This gives us a useful distinction between a *borrowed language* and a *third language*.

When a parrot learns the spoken label “green” and uses it in a task as humans use the target category, the animal has entered a piece of our symbolic system.

When a human and parrot develop a unique call-and-response routine, gesture, timing pattern, object game, or attention signal that works only between them, they have built something local.

Real relationships often contain both.

Dogs learn human words and household signals.

Horses learn formal cues and accidental rider movements.

Cats learn names and private routines.

Parrots can learn labels while simultaneously shaping their human partners through species-typical vocal and social behavior.

The channels braid.

One reason birds are so important to the larger story is vocal learning.

Humans are vocal learners. We hear socially significant sounds and learn to produce new ones. This capacity is limited or absent in many mammals but has evolved powerfully in several bird lineages, including parrots and songbirds. Vocal learners can alter signals through social experience, making culture visible in sound.

Birdsong dialects are a literal reminder that communication systems can vary geographically and socially within a species.

Young birds learn from tutors. Populations can carry local song forms. Signals change over time. A bird moving into a new social environment may encounter acoustic conventions that differ from those elsewhere.

The idea of a private dialect between species therefore becomes less metaphorical around animals already capable of socially learned vocal variation.

What happens when a vocal learner lives with humans?

Sometimes it imitates phones, alarms, laughter, phrases, household sounds, other animals, and individual voices. Much of this can be mimicry without semantic use. But the selection of what to imitate is itself shaped by social attention. A parrot may discover that one sound reliably produces a spectacular human response.

The human laughs.

The sound happens again.

A cultural feedback loop forms across species.

This is one reason parrots can become such effective household comedians. The bird is not merely copying the environment; the environment responds selectively, and human amusement becomes part of what is worth repeating.

We are the parrot’s audience and sometimes its editor.

The same process works in reverse. Humans imitate birds badly but meaningfully. We whistle back. We reproduce contact calls. We create verbal labels for specific parrot sounds. We learn that one vocalization precedes a request, another marks alarm, another recruits attention. We may begin answering one call and ignoring another.

The bird notices.

The channel changes.

It is tempting to say we are teaching parrots to talk.

Often they are teaching us which sounds make us move.

The raven’s gesture brings us back to attention because attention is the currency beneath all these exchanges.

To point is not merely to make a shape with a finger. It is to alter another individual’s attention toward something.

Humans become capable of following pointing early in development, and the gesture plays a foundational role in joint attention. A child and caregiver attend to the same object while remaining aware, in some sense, of each other’s attention. This triadic structure supports later language learning.

When a raven shows an object to another raven, the superficial similarity to human declarative pointing is exciting, but we should not rush into equivalence. The bird’s gesture can have different functions, evolutionary origins, and cognitive underpinnings.

The safer question is whether the bird is managing another bird’s attention to an external object.

If yes, that capacity matters enormously for interspecies communication.

Joint attention is one way two minds can occupy the same scene.

Dogs do versions of this with humans. A dog looks toward a ball, then a person. A person follows. A human points toward a hidden object and the dog searches. The shared target stabilizes meaning because both parties can test their interpretation against the world.

Objects are communicative anchors.

This may be why so much successful interspecies work begins with things: food, toys, containers, targets, locations, obstacles, tools.

Abstract translation is difficult. Shared objects give the conversation something to be wrong about.

If I think the dog means the red ball and the dog goes to the blue ball, the world corrects me.

If I think the raven is directing attention toward the object it holds and the recipient consistently orients to it, the sequence provides evidence.

If a parrot labels a material and chooses the wrong object, performance can be scored.

External reference gives interspecies communication a foothold.

The hardest meanings are those with no such foothold.

What does a whale coda mean if it has no obvious immediate consequence?

What emotional nuance sits inside a bat call during an argument?

What does a cat communicate through proximity after a social conflict?

What does an elephant call encode about relationship beyond identity?

The temptation is to force these signals into words because words make ambiguity feel resolved.

Birds suggest another strategy.

Follow attention.

Who attends to whom?

What changes orientation?

What object becomes shared?

What response ends the sequence?

What does the sender do when attention fails?

What changes when the audience changes?

The answer may reveal function before it reveals translation.

Audience will become increasingly important as we move through the animal world.

A signal is never merely produced. It is produced where somebody might receive it.

Animals sensitive to audience can change what they do based on who is present, whether the receiver is looking, what the receiver knows, or how the receiver is socially related to the sender. Those effects are evidence that communication is not a broadcast emitted blindly into space.

The raven holding the object is not interesting because the object resembles a human pointer.

It is interesting because another raven is there.

Remove the recipient and the act may change.

Change the recipient and the act may change.

Lose the recipient’s attention and the sender may have a problem to solve.

That problem—how to get another being to notice—is the same problem the dog at the door solved.

Different body.

Different evolutionary history.

Same social fact.

Someone else’s attention can become useful.

Once an animal knows that, conversation has somewhere to land.
