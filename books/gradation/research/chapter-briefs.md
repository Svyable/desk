# Gradation — chapter research map

This map records the empirical hinge of each chapter, the principal sources, and the caution that should travel with the claim.

## Chapter 1 — The Temperature Between

**Hinge:** Measurement preserves degree; decision systems often reintroduce thresholds; continuous shifts can change tail frequency and can also cross genuine phase transitions.

**Sources:** NIST phase-transition reference; IPCC threshold language for later climate analogies.

**Caution:** A thermostat set point is chosen; a material phase transition is not. The chapter must keep those authorities distinct.

## Chapter 2 — The Smallest Difference

**Hinge:** Human sensation is graded and context-sensitive; equal physical increments need not create equal perceptual increments.

**Sources:** Weber/Fechner psychophysical tradition; Stevens' power-law work and modern summaries.

**Caution:** Classical psychophysical laws are useful models, not universal exact laws across every modality and range.

## Chapter 3 — The Border We Draw

**Hinge:** Continuous sensory spaces can coexist with categorical language and tasks; dichotomizing continuous variables can discard information.

**Sources:** Witzel & Gegenfurtner on color perception; lexical color-category review; Apfelbaum et al. on gradient speech categorization; Altman & Royston on dichotomization.

**Caution:** Categories can improve communication and action. The criticism is not that categories are unreal, but that their borders may not mirror a physical discontinuity.

## Chapter 4 — When More Becomes Different

**Hinge:** Some systems contain genuine nonlinear transitions; “tipping point” should be reserved for a defensible change in system organization or propagation.

**Sources:** NIST phase-transition data; IPCC AR6 definitions and discussion of tipping points.

**Caution:** Social and ecological analogies should not borrow the certainty of laboratory thermodynamics.

## Chapter 5 — The Shape of a Slope

**Hinge:** Marginal effect depends on local slope and curvature; equal changes in input can have unequal consequences.

**Sources:** Standard marginal-analysis and dose-response literature; empirical examples from later chapters.

**Caution:** Do not imply that every process has a stable, knowable functional form.

## Chapter 6 — Threshold Machines

**Hinge:** Graded inputs can trigger discrete events; engineered thresholds trade false positives against false negatives and often use bands, persistence, or hysteresis to avoid chatter.

**Sources:** Standard neurophysiology references for action potentials; signal-detection/engineering practice; Schmitt-trigger control concept.

**Caution:** A neuron is not a digital transistor and a person is not a circuit. The transfer is structural, not literal.

## Chapter 7 — The Dose Curve

**Hinge:** Effect depends on dose, response type, route, timing, susceptibility and uncertainty; regulatory limits are decision points built from evidence, not necessarily biological cliffs.

**Sources:** EPA Reference Dose guidance; EPA human-health risk-assessment materials; dose-response pharmacology literature.

**Caution:** “The dose makes the poison” is not a license to minimize low-dose risk. Some endpoints are modeled without a threshold, and vulnerable populations differ.

## Chapter 8 — The Risk Gradient

**Hinge:** Risk is probabilistic and depends on consequence, exposure, vulnerability, buffers and time horizon; categorical labels compress this structure.

**Sources:** Population-risk literature associated with Geoffrey Rose; actuarial and safety-management practice; climate/flood-probability references where used.

**Caution:** Do not collapse risk to expected value when tails, irreversibility or distribution of harm matter.

## Chapter 9 — Saturation

**Hinge:** Marginal returns can fall as receptors, channels, markets, attention or capacity fill; the bottleneck can move after one constraint is relieved.

**Sources:** Receptor/dose-response literature; queueing and capacity principles; organizational examples treated as analogies.

**Caution:** Saturation in biology and saturation in markets are not the same mechanism.

## Chapter 10 — Memory in the Curve

**Hinge:** The same measured state can produce different behavior depending on the path that produced it.

**Sources:** Blanchard & Summers on unemployment hysteresis; engineering hysteresis as a structural reference.

**Caution:** Persistence is not automatically hysteresis. Current omitted conditions may explain apparent path dependence.

## Chapter 11 — Status by Degrees

**Hinge:** Health and opportunity often vary across social hierarchies rather than only across a poverty/non-poverty divide.

**Sources:** Whitehall studies and later reviews of the social gradient in health.

**Caution:** Rank, income, job control, education, neighborhood and selection are entangled. The chapter should resist one-factor explanations.

## Chapter 12 — Trust in Fractions

**Hinge:** Trust is domain-specific, accumulates gradually, can collapse abruptly, and is often asymmetric in recovery.

**Sources:** Institutional-trust and behavioral literature where factual claims are made; otherwise chapter examples remain conceptual.

**Caution:** Avoid pretending trust has one universal scalar measure.

## Chapter 13 — Learning Curves

**Hinge:** Cumulative production and practice can lower cost or improve capability; learning can compound, plateau or lock organizations into obsolete competence.

**Sources:** Wright on aircraft production; Arrow on learning by doing.

**Caution:** Learning curves are empirical regularities, not guarantees. Repetition without informative feedback can stabilize error.

## Chapter 14 — The Price Staircase

**Hinge:** Firms and governments convert willingness-to-pay, cost, income or eligibility gradients into tiers and brackets; threshold design changes behavior around the edge.

**Sources:** Shapiro & Varian on versioning; CBO on effective marginal tax rates and transfer phaseouts.

**Caution:** Tiered pricing can improve access and fund fixed costs; a visible staircase is not automatically exploitative.

## Chapter 15 — The Geography of Opportunity

**Hinge:** Place is exposure over time, not merely a label; nearby geographies can differ in adult outcomes, access and effective distance.

**Sources:** Chetty, Hendren & Katz on Moving to Opportunity; Chetty & Hendren county-level estimates; Opportunity Atlas; spatial-mismatch literature.

**Caution:** Place effects are difficult to separate from selection. Stronger causal evidence should carry more weight than maps alone.

## Chapter 16 — The Violence of the Cutoff

**Hinge:** Near-identical cases can receive sharply different treatment when policy uses a hard boundary; the boundary itself can then alter outcomes and behavior.

**Sources:** CBO effective marginal tax-rate work; Altman & Royston; low-birth-weight regression-discontinuity research by Almond, Doyle, Kowalski & Williams.

**Caution:** Some hard cutoffs improve transparency, due process, safety or causal identification. The argument is to earn sharpness, not abolish it.

## Chapter 17 — Cascades

**Hinge:** Aggregate transitions depend on threshold distributions, network structure, feedback and slack; one event must alter the conditions of later events for a true cascade.

**Sources:** Granovetter threshold models; Schelling segregation models; Card, Mas & Rothstein on segregation tipping; later empirical challenges; Sugiyama et al. traffic-wave experiment.

**Caution:** Clusters can be caused by a common outside shock rather than contagion. “Tipping point” language needs mechanism.

## Chapter 18 — The Judgment of Degree

**Hinge:** The book's three central errors are confusing a continuous reality with a category, confusing an imposed cutoff with a natural one, and assuming smooth response where the system contains a real nonlinear threshold.

**Sources:** Synthesis of the full ledger.

**Caution:** The synthesis should remain judgment, not a universal checklist. The correct representation depends on purpose, mechanism, uncertainty and consequence.
