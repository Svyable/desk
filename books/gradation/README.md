*The Hidden Architecture of Change.*

The working thesis is that much of modern life is damaged by a category error: we force continuous realities into hard bins, then fail to notice the opposite phenomenon, when a smooth increase crosses a genuine threshold and the system becomes qualitatively different. Gradation is the discipline of telling those two cases apart.

The book follows degree across perception, biology, physics, risk, economics, status, trust, learning, geography, public policy, networks, and climate. It asks what a slope means, when a cutoff is merely administrative, why some systems saturate, why history can make the same numerical state behave differently, and how small differences can accumulate into cascades or cliffs.

The argument is practical as well as philosophical. Better judgment depends on seeing the curve before naming the category: preserving continuous information when the world is continuous, respecting thresholds when feedback makes them real, and designing institutions that do not manufacture unnecessary cliffs.
