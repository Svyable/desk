# The Price Staircase

A price looks like a point.

Nine dollars and ninety-nine cents.

Twenty-four dollars a month.

Seventy-five thousand dollars a year.

One million dollars for the house.

But almost every serious market contains a staircase hidden behind the number.

Different buyers pay different amounts. Different quantities carry different marginal prices. Different qualities form tiers. Discounts activate at thresholds. Taxes and fees appear at particular levels. Financing changes the effective cost across time. Search effort changes which price a buyer encounters at all.

The sticker is a category imposed on a much larger surface.

This is why pricing is one of the purest commercial forms of gradation.

A seller is trying to convert differences in willingness to pay into revenue without making the pricing system so complex, unfair or transparent that customers rebel.

A buyer is trying to infer quality, alternatives and bargaining room from incomplete information.

Both sides operate on gradients they cannot observe directly.

The consumer's maximum willingness to pay is usually hidden.

The seller's minimum acceptable price is often hidden too.

The transaction occurs when those private curves overlap enough for a deal.

Markets make a point from two distributions.

That point can look natural after it happens.

It was negotiated by structure.

Retail pricing simplifies the structure because millions of transactions cannot each become a bargaining session. Put the item on a shelf for one price. The rule reduces transaction cost and creates a sense of equal treatment.

Then the exceptions arrive.

Sales.

Coupons.

Membership discounts.

Student pricing.

Senior pricing.

Bulk pricing.

Dynamic pricing.

Loyalty tiers.

Negotiated enterprise contracts.

Peak and off-peak rates.

The seller keeps rediscovering the gradient beneath the single price.

Information goods made this especially visible because copying cost can be tiny relative to the cost of creating the first version. Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian wrote influentially about versioning: designing different forms of an information product so customers sort themselves according to willingness to pay.

The product becomes a staircase.

Basic.

Pro.

Enterprise.

The underlying software may share most of its code. The tiers create boundaries around features, support, limits and rights.

A customer with 99 users fits one plan. Add the hundredth and the organization may cross into a more expensive tier.

The marginal cost of that employee is no longer merely another seat.

The pricing threshold amplifies it.

Companies create these cliffs deliberately because cliffs can capture value.

They can also distort behavior.

A customer delays hiring. Shares accounts. Removes a contractor. Avoids one feature. Searches for a competitor.

The price staircase changes the quantity demanded around each riser.

This is why well-designed pricing often smooths some boundaries and sharpens others.

A usage-based product can charge continuously, preserving gradation but creating uncertainty. A flat subscription creates predictability but makes light users subsidize heavy users. Tiers simplify choices but create thresholds. Bundles reduce decision complexity but force customers to buy features they do not value.

There is no pricing architecture without a shape.

The question is which shape fits the economics and psychology of the market.

Human perception makes price thresholds especially interesting.

Retailers have long used prices ending in 9 because buyers do not process every digit with equal psychological weight. A price of 9.99 is only one cent below 10 in arithmetic. It can occupy a different mental category because the leftmost digit changes.

Whether the effect is large in a given market varies. The larger principle is familiar: perception introduces discontinuities into a continuous price scale.

A buyer does not experience every cent equally.

Reference points matter.

A seventy-dollar bottle of wine feels expensive in one store and ordinary on a list where neighboring bottles cost two hundred. A four-dollar coffee can feel cheap in an airport and expensive in a diner. A salary offer is judged against prior salary, peers, expectations and alternatives.

Price is relational.

This creates another gradient: fairness.

People do not object only to high prices. They object to prices whose differences feel unjustified.

Two passengers on the same flight can pay radically different fares because they bought at different times under different restrictions. Most experienced travelers accept some of this even while resenting it. The industry has normalized a complex pricing surface around inventory, demand and willingness to pay.

Now imagine two people standing next to each other at a store being quoted different prices by an algorithm based on inferred income.

The economics may be similar.

The social meaning changes.

Acceptable price discrimination depends on what dimensions society permits the seller to use and how visible the differentiation becomes.

Students receive discounts because the category has a social story. Children and seniors often receive different prices. Business customers expect negotiated contracts. Charging more because a person's phone battery is low would feel predatory even if it accurately predicts urgency.

The same gradient of willingness to pay can be segmented in morally different ways.

Data makes this tension more acute.

A modern company can estimate individual demand at astonishing resolution. Browsing behavior, location, device, purchase history, timing and many other signals can help predict conversion probability.

Perfect price discrimination becomes more imaginable technically and more troubling socially.

If every customer pays exactly their maximum willingness to pay, the seller captures nearly all consumer surplus.

Efficiency in the narrow sense of completing mutually beneficial trades can coexist with a world customers experience as extraction.

The ability to see the gradient creates power over the gradient.

This is a recurring theme of the book.

Better measurement is not morally self-executing.

The same issue appears in labor markets, where wages are prices with human lives attached.

A company may use salary bands to reduce arbitrary negotiation and improve equity. The band creates floors, ceilings and levels.

That simplifies administration.

It also produces boundary problems.

An employee reaches the top of a band and further raises become difficult without promotion. The next dollar of pay now requires a categorical job change.

Skill may be continuous.

Compensation is stepped.

Organizations respond with broader bands, bonuses, equity, promotions or new titles. Each response creates another shape.

Broad bands preserve gradation but can make comparisons opaque.

Narrow bands create transparency and visible ceilings.

Promotions can recognize real differences in responsibility and also become artificial gates to pay growth.

The wage structure is a staircase laid over a skill distribution.

If the risers are too tall, people focus on title. If too shallow, responsibility may not feel rewarded.

Again the design is in the derivative.

Taxes make the staircase politically famous.

Progressive income taxes use brackets, but the common misunderstanding is that crossing into a higher bracket causes all income to be taxed at the higher rate. Under a marginal-rate structure, the higher rate generally applies only to the income inside the higher bracket, subject to the details of the system.

This is a clever way to make the response function more continuous than the bracket labels suggest.

The rate changes at the threshold.

Total tax does not jump by applying the new rate backward to all prior income.

The staircase is in the slope, not the level.

Means-tested benefits can create harsher cliffs when eligibility ends abruptly.

Then earning one more dollar can trigger a much larger loss of support.

The effective price of earning changes discontinuously.

A worker is paid more and can become worse off in disposable resources over some interval.

This is not a theoretical curiosity. Public-finance analyses of effective marginal tax rates explicitly include the reduction of transfers as earnings rise because that reduction affects work incentives.

Policy has created a price on advancement.

The cleanest fix—never withdraw benefits—would make programs vastly more expensive or universal. The cheapest fix—end benefits sharply—creates cliffs. Gradual phaseouts smooth the transition and spread elevated marginal rates across a wider income range.

The staircase cannot be redesigned without distributional choices.

Pricing teaches us to stop asking whether a system has incentives.

Every response function has incentives.

A flat price encourages heavy use relative to metered pricing.

A metered price encourages conservation and creates uncertainty.

A volume discount encourages larger orders.

A congestion charge changes timing or route choices.

A tax credit encourages behavior defined by eligibility.

A salary band encourages promotion seeking near its ceiling.

A free tier encourages adoption and can create conversion pressure at the limit.

The shape is the incentive.

This is why small details in pricing rules can produce large behavioral effects at boundaries.

Suppose parking is free for the first hour and costs ten dollars after sixty minutes. A person leaving at fifty-nine minutes does not experience the fifty-ninth minute as an ordinary minute. The next minute has a shadow price of ten dollars.

The clock is continuous.

The tariff makes time discontinuous.

People respond by rushing, moving cars or avoiding entry near the threshold.

Now imagine that effect across thousands of rules.

Benefit eligibility.

Overtime.

Shipping thresholds.

Credit-card rewards.

Tax deductions.

Insurance deductibles.

Free-trial limits.

Data caps.

Minimum order sizes.

People live inside a landscape of manufactured cliffs.

Much of “gaming the system” is ordinary optimization against that landscape.

Sometimes the gaming reveals misconduct. Sometimes it reveals a badly shaped rule.

If thousands of people contort behavior around one threshold, the system should ask whether the threshold is doing more work than intended.

A company sees customers splitting orders to stay under a fee. A government sees firms remaining just below a regulatory size. A workplace sees people timing hours around overtime rules.

The naive response is to scold optimization.

The analytical response is to study the curve.

People are telling you where the derivative is too steep.

Markets also reveal the opposite phenomenon: smooth prices can hide unequal consequences.

Raise the price of a necessity by ten dollars for everyone.

The nominal change is equal.

The burden is not.

A wealthy household absorbs it. A low-income household may cut food, medicine or transportation elsewhere. The same price slope creates different welfare slopes across households.

This is why distribution matters in inflation.

Two households facing the same headline inflation rate can experience very different strain because spending baskets and buffers differ. A household that spends most income on necessities has less room to substitute or delay purchases.

Price indices summarize.

Budgets feel the gradient.

The same is true for interest rates.

A higher rate is not one uniform burden across the economy. Borrowers with floating debt, imminent refinancing or high leverage feel it first. Cash-rich savers can benefit. Banks face changes in asset and liability values. Housing affordability changes through monthly payments.

A central bank moves one policy rate.

The economy receives a distribution of doses.

This is why monetary policy works through lags and channels rather than one immediate lever.

The price of money changes many gradients at once.

The deepest lesson of pricing is that no number stands alone.

A price lives in a schedule.

A schedule lives in a market.

A market lives in a distribution of resources and alternatives.

The same price can be a bargain, barrier, signal, penalty or rounding error depending on where the buyer sits.

This is not a reason to abandon prices for moral intuition. Prices coordinate enormous amounts of decentralized information remarkably well.

It is a reason to remember what coordination compresses.

The market-clearing point is not the whole curve.

Neither is the sticker.

Look for the staircase.

Some staircases are almost invisible because the advertised price is only the entrance fee.

A mobile plan has a base price, overage rules, roaming rules, device financing and cancellation terms. A mortgage has an interest rate, points, fees, insurance, taxes and refinancing risk. A cloud service has storage prices, request prices, egress prices, support tiers and volume commitments.

The economically relevant object is the tariff, not the headline.

That distinction matters because a tariff can hide its steepest slope where attention is weakest.

The first number is easy to compare.

The marginal number appears only after use changes.

A customer chooses a vendor on the visible fixed fee and later discovers that one particular kind of growth—data transfer, seats, transactions, support incidents—crosses a pricing boundary much faster than expected.

The product was not expensive at the starting point.

It was steep in the direction the customer happened to grow.

This is why pricing architecture can shape product architecture.

Engineers compress data because egress is expensive. Teams archive less because storage is cheap. Companies centralize accounts to capture volume discounts. Customers keep headcount below a tier. Developers redesign workflows around API limits.

The tariff becomes a hidden technical specification.

When enough customers respond, the seller's pricing function begins shaping the ecosystem around the product.

A discount can do the same thing in reverse.

Consider an all-units volume discount: buy 99 units and pay one price per unit; buy 100 and receive the lower price on every unit. The hundredth unit can effectively have a negative marginal price because crossing the threshold reduces the cost of the first ninety-nine as well.

The buyer may purchase more than needed because the staircase contains a downward cliff.

The same geometry appears in “free shipping over $50.” A shopper with a $44 basket may add an item not because the item itself is worth its full price, but because it also removes the shipping charge.

The additional item carries two values.

Its own value.

And its effect on the tariff applied to everything else.

Threshold pricing makes marginal analysis discontinuous.

This matters well beyond consumer tricks because regulatory and tax systems can have the same structure. A threshold can change the treatment of the whole entity rather than only the next unit. When that happens, behavior near the line can become intense because the marginal consequence includes a reclassification of the base.

The design question is whether that jump is intentional.

Sometimes it is. A compliance regime may genuinely need a different architecture once a firm becomes large enough. An enterprise support contract may carry fixed costs that only make sense above a scale threshold. A bulk shipment may become cheaper because logistics change discontinuously when a pallet or truck is filled.

The staircase can reflect a real change in cost.

Other times it is there because the billing system was easier to build that way.

The customer cannot tell from the price alone.

This is why one of the most useful questions in pricing is the same one the book asks everywhere else:

What made the line?

A cost discontinuity?

A capacity constraint?

A segmentation strategy?

A legal rule?

A historical artifact?

A psychological reference point?

A software limitation?

Different origins deserve different judgments.

There is also a tradeoff between complexity and exploitation.

A perfectly smooth personalized price could eliminate cliffs while making the market impossible to understand. Every buyer receives a bespoke number derived from hundreds of variables. The staircase disappears from view, but so does the ability to know whether one is being treated similarly to others.

Simple posted prices sacrifice some revenue extraction in exchange for legibility.

Legibility is a market good.

It lets customers compare, plan and contest.

A complex tariff can be economically efficient and cognitively expensive. The buyer pays part of the price in attention.

That attention cost is rarely shown on the receipt.

Yet it changes competition. A market full of difficult-to-compare tariffs can reduce switching because evaluating alternatives becomes work. The seller does not need an explicit cancellation fee if comparison itself is costly enough.

Opacity creates a friction gradient.

This is why pricing deserves to sit beside policy in a book about cutoffs.

Both convert continuous differences into rules that people must navigate.

Both can make the edge more consequential than the underlying change.

And both reveal their true philosophy not in the headline rate, but in what happens to the next unit.
