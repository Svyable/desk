# The Violence of the Cutoff

The cruelest thing about a cutoff is how little it has to know about the people it separates.

Two households can differ by a dollar and receive different treatment. Two students can differ by a fraction of a point and enter different rooms. Two patients can sit on opposite sides of a diagnostic threshold even when their bodies differ less than the measurement error. Two businesses can be nearly identical in size and face different reporting rules because one crossed a statutory line. Two workers can perform the same job with the same competence and find that one extra month, one extra hour, one extra unit of income, one birthday or one score changes what the institution is allowed to do with them.

The number can be tiny.

The consequence can be large.

That is the violence of the cutoff.

Violence here does not require malice. Often the rule was created for an understandable reason. Administrators need eligibility criteria. Schools have limited seats. Safety systems need intervention points. Tax agencies need brackets. Hospitals need protocols. Regulators need definitions. Courts need jurisdictions. Firms need approval limits. Insurance contracts need terms.

A system that refuses every boundary can become a system that never decides.

The problem is not that cutoffs exist.

The problem is that their consequences can become much sharper than the reality they are trying to organize.

The administrative line is often a discontinuity imposed on a continuum.

Income is continuous over a large range. Need does not normally disappear when earnings rise by one dollar. Academic ability does not transform at a single test score. Creditworthiness does not become a different species at one point. Disability does not always emerge on a clean side of a measurement. Job competence does not materialize on a birthday.

Yet institutions take these gradual conditions and create binary states because binary states are cheap to process.

Eligible or ineligible.

Pass or fail.

Approved or denied.

Inside or outside.

Qualified or unqualified.

The category is legible to the system.

The person is forced to live with the discontinuity.

This creates an important distinction between a threshold and a cliff.

A threshold is a point at which a rule or response changes.

A cliff is what happens when the change in consequence is large relative to the change in the underlying condition.

The same threshold can be designed with or without a severe cliff.

Suppose a household receives a benefit worth several thousand dollars and loses all of it when income crosses a single boundary. The household can become financially worse off after earning slightly more. The policy has created a region where advancement is locally punished.

Now imagine the same benefit phases down gradually. Additional earnings still reduce assistance, but the household keeps part of each extra dollar. There is still a threshold in the sense that the formula changes. There may still be high effective marginal rates. But the discontinuity is smaller.

The line did not disappear.

The violence did.

This sounds like an obvious design improvement until the constraints arrive.

Gradual phase-outs cost money because more people receive partial benefits. They complicate administration. They can extend high marginal rates across a wider income range. They may interact badly with other programs that have their own schedules. A policy designed smoothly in isolation can become jagged when stacked with taxes, subsidies, childcare costs, housing assistance, insurance premiums and work expenses.

The household does not experience one curve.

It experiences the sum of all of them.

That is where cutoff design becomes difficult.

A government can eliminate one cliff while leaving five others. A firm can make one bonus schedule smooth while promotion, equity vesting and benefits remain lumpy. A school can replace one exam cutoff with holistic review and discover that the review process contains less visible thresholds in recommendations, extracurricular access, family resources or reviewer judgment.

Gradation does not guarantee fairness simply by replacing a visible line with a continuous-looking process.

Sometimes the visible line is the most honest part of the system.

A lottery among qualified applicants can be crude, but at least the randomness is acknowledged. A supposedly holistic ranking can preserve distinctions that are harder to audit. A precise score can look harsh, but a discretionary decision can import favoritism. A categorical rule can be challenged because everybody can see it. A gradient implemented through an opaque model can make the boundary impossible to locate.

This is why the politics of cutoffs cannot be reduced to “smooth is good.”

A cutoff has at least three dimensions.

There is the underlying variable. There is the decision rule. And there is the consequence attached to the rule.

People often argue about the first while ignoring the third.

Take age.

Age is continuous in the only sense that matters here: the body does not wake up on a birthday and become categorically different from the day before. Yet societies attach enormous legal consequences to birthdays because age is observable, universal, difficult to manipulate at the margin and correlated with developmental, biological or social capacities that are much harder to measure directly.

The age line is a proxy.

Proxies are useful because direct measurement can be expensive, intrusive or contestable.

They are dangerous because we begin to speak as if the proxy were the property itself.

A minimum age for a legal activity does not mean every person below the line lacks the relevant capacity or every person above it possesses the capacity. It means the institution has chosen a rule that trades individualized accuracy for administrability, predictability and other values.

That trade may be justified.

But it is still a trade.

The same problem appears with income thresholds.

Income is attractive to policy because it is measurable enough to be audited, even though actual need depends on family size, geography, health, assets, debt, caregiving obligations, volatility, local prices and future uncertainty. A single number compresses a complicated household into something the program can process.

Compression always discards information.

The important question is whether the discarded information matters near the boundary.

It often matters most there.

Far below a need threshold, the classification may be easy. Far above it, likewise. Near the line, uncertainty about the person and arbitrariness in the rule are both at their highest relative importance.

Yet many systems behave with the greatest confidence exactly where they should be most humble.

The score says 699.

Denied.

The score says 700.

Approved.

The numerical precision can make the decision feel mathematically inevitable even when the threshold was chosen for business, historical or administrative reasons.

This is one of the recurring seductions of scoring systems.

A score creates an ordering.

An ordering invites a cutoff.

A cutoff creates categories.

The categories inherit the prestige of the score.

Soon the system forgets that ranking and classification were separate acts.

A credit model may estimate a probability. A lender then chooses a threshold based on expected return, capital, regulation, portfolio strategy and risk tolerance. Another lender can use the same estimate and draw the line somewhere else.

The model did not discover approval.

The institution decided it.

This becomes more consequential as machine learning spreads into hiring, fraud detection, insurance, moderation, medicine and public administration.

Continuous scores are natural outputs for many predictive systems. Probability of default. Likelihood of fraud. Estimated risk. Predicted response. Similarity. Confidence.

Operational systems then need actions.

Investigate or ignore.

Approve or deny.

Escalate or close.

Admit or discharge.

Flag or allow.

The threshold is where prediction becomes power.

A model can be statistically sophisticated and a threshold can still be badly designed.

Consider a fraud system. Lower the threshold for review and more suspicious cases are caught, but more legitimate transactions are delayed. Raise it and fewer customers are inconvenienced, but more fraud passes. There is no threshold independent of the cost of false positives and false negatives.

Those costs are not symmetric.

They are not always borne by the same people.

A false positive may be a minor inconvenience to the firm and a missed rent payment to the customer whose card is frozen. A false negative may be a portfolio statistic to one team and a devastating theft to another person.

Choosing the line means choosing whose error matters.

This is why cutoff debates become moral debates even when they begin as technical ones.

The threshold allocates error.

Medicine has lived with this problem for a long time.

Many clinical measurements are continuous. Blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol, bone density, kidney function and countless other values do not divide themselves into ordinary language categories. Clinical practice creates bands and thresholds because treatment, monitoring and communication require decisions.

Those thresholds can improve care enormously.

They can also be misunderstood.

A patient just below a diagnostic criterion can still have meaningful risk. A patient just above it can differ only slightly from the patient below. Measurement varies. Context matters. Multiple risk factors interact. A number can be important without being sufficient.

One of the most revealing medical examples uses birth weight.

For decades, newborns under 1,500 grams have often been classified as very low birth weight, a category associated with more intensive medical attention. Douglas Almond, Joseph Doyle, Amanda Kowalski and Heidi Williams asked what happened on either side of that line. They used the fact that 1,499 grams and 1,501 grams describe infants who are nearly identical in weight but can receive different treatment because one sits inside a recognized risk category and the other does not.

The pattern was extraordinary precisely because the weight difference was so ordinary.

Using large U.S. birth datasets, the researchers found that infants just below the 1,500-gram threshold received more intensive care and had lower one-year mortality than infants just above it, even though, in general, mortality risk improves as birth weight rises.

The lighter babies did better at the margin because the line changed what medicine did.

The cutoff did not merely classify risk.

It altered treatment, and treatment altered outcome.

This is a near-perfect illustration of why thresholds cannot be interpreted by staring at the variable alone. A gram did not carry a magical biological effect. The classification attached institutional behavior to the gram.

The discontinuity was partly made by medicine.

That does not make the threshold foolish. The extra treatment appeared to save lives near the boundary. The case instead reveals something more interesting: an administrative category can be arbitrary in its exact placement and still be consequential, even beneficial, because systems act on it.

It also exposes the moral difficulty of local cliffs.

If a baby just below the line receives care that improves survival, what should we conclude about the baby just above it?

The correct answer is not automatically to treat every infant identically. Intensive care has costs and risks, and medical resources are finite. The answer is that the edge contains information about the rule.

When a tiny difference in an underlying measure produces a measurable jump in treatment and outcome, the institution has learned something about the value of what happens after the cutoff.

Good medicine therefore often uses thresholds as invitations to further judgment rather than as replacements for judgment.

Poor administration does the opposite.

Once a number crosses, the protocol stops seeing the person.

This is the deepest form of cutoff violence: not the existence of a line, but the disappearance of nearby information after the line is applied.

The category becomes sticky.

Someone receives a label and future decisions condition on the label rather than the underlying evidence. A student placed in a remedial track gets different coursework. A borrower denied credit cannot build the history that might improve future access. A neighborhood labeled risky receives less investment, which can reinforce the conditions that produced the label. A worker passed over for the first promotion misses experiences used to evaluate the second.

The cutoff changes the future distribution.

Classification becomes intervention.

Intervention becomes data.

The next classification then appears to confirm the first.

This is how an arbitrary line can become a real difference over time.

The distinction between constructed and natural thresholds is therefore unstable.

A line can begin as administrative and end by reorganizing behavior around itself.

Tax thresholds influence timing. Benefit rules influence hours and reported income. School boundaries influence housing demand. Bank capital rules influence balance-sheet choices. Corporate headcount thresholds can influence outsourcing or firm structure. Exam cutoffs influence how students allocate study time.

People do not passively receive thresholds.

They optimize around them.

This is one reason bunching appears around important policy boundaries. When a rule changes sharply at a point, behavior can accumulate just to one side.

The curve acquires a kink because the institution put one there.

This response is sometimes criticized as gaming.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes it is simply adaptation to incentives.

If one extra employee triggers a costly compliance regime, a firm has reason to remain below the line or contract work out. If earning additional income causes a sharp loss of assistance, a household has reason to consider whether extra hours are worth it. If a student needs a particular score, studying will concentrate around what moves the score.

The institution designed the gradient.

Then it complains when people notice the slope.

There is another strange benefit to sharp cutoffs.

Researchers can use them.

When treatment changes at a known threshold and people just above and below are otherwise comparable enough, the discontinuity can create an opportunity to estimate causal effects. A scholarship rule based on a score, a program based on age, a policy based on population or an intervention based on income can produce something close to a local experiment near the line.

The newborn study used exactly this logic.

The 1,500-gram threshold was medically consequential enough to change treatment but narrow enough that infants on either side could be compared locally. The same arbitrariness that made the cutoff philosophically uncomfortable made it empirically useful.

The statistical method is valuable because the policy is discontinuous.

The line that may be unfair administratively can be informative scientifically.

This creates a useful intellectual discipline.

If people on either side of the cutoff are similar enough to serve as comparison groups for research, then the institution should be careful before talking as though they are fundamentally different kinds of people.

The method works because near the line, near still means near.

The decision is discontinuous.

The person is not.

The question then becomes what to do when a cutoff cannot be removed.

One answer is to narrow the consequence.

The more uncertain the classification, the less irreversible the first action should be. A borderline score can trigger review rather than denial. A threshold can change monitoring frequency rather than rights. A safety system can use escalating responses rather than one dramatic switch. A benefit can phase down. A school can reserve reconsideration for applicants close to a line.

Another answer is to use bands.

Machines often do this already. A thermostat with hysteresis avoids rapid switching around one point. Industrial controls distinguish warning from alarm from shutdown. Financial risk systems may use several tiers rather than one binary boundary.

Bands acknowledge that a region can be operationally ambiguous.

Humans dislike this because ambiguity feels like indecision.

But uncertainty does not vanish because a database requires a boolean.

A third answer is to preserve reversibility.

A false positive is less damaging when it can be corrected quickly. An appeal process, temporary classification, probationary period, manual override or scheduled reassessment can prevent one noisy measurement from becoming permanent fate.

The cost of a cutoff is partly the cost of being wrong multiplied by how long the wrongness lasts.

A fourth answer is to separate eligibility from intensity.

Some programs need a line to determine who enters, but the amount of support can still vary with need. Some safety systems need a hard stop, but earlier warning can rise gradually. Some regulatory regimes need categories, but obligations can scale with exposure or size.

Binary entry does not require binary treatment everywhere.

A fifth answer is to measure the cliff itself.

Institutions are good at measuring how many people qualify.

They are often worse at measuring what happens just after someone fails to qualify.

How many people lose more benefit than they gain in earnings? How many applicants sit near a score threshold? How often do overrides reverse automated denials? How large is the difference in outcomes between those barely on either side? How many people alter behavior to avoid crossing the line?

The gradient around the rule is data about the rule.

A well-designed threshold should be curious about its own edge.

There are cases where a hard cutoff is exactly what we want.

A circuit breaker should break the circuit. A containment protocol may need an unmistakable trigger. A structural safety limit can justify a large response because the downside is nonlinear and catastrophic. A legal deadline can provide finality that would be impossible if every late case were negotiated forever.

Gradation does not ask us to soften every boundary.

It asks us to earn the sharpness.

A sharp consequence makes sense when the underlying risk is itself sharply nonlinear, when capacity is genuinely discrete, when delay creates disproportionate danger, when coordination requires one common line, or when the cost of ambiguity exceeds the cost of local unfairness.

Even then, the threshold deserves margins, monitoring and explanation.

The strongest boundaries are often supported by continuous information on both sides.

Pilots do not fly safely because aviation has one magic number. Safety emerges from envelopes, checklists, redundant measurements, warnings, procedures and thresholds nested inside a larger understanding of the system.

Likewise, a surgeon does not reduce a patient to one lab value. A good lender does not reduce a business to one ratio. A good manager does not reduce an employee to one performance score.

The line can organize attention.

It should not end attention.

This matters because modern society is producing more scores, not fewer.

Sensors convert behavior into streams. Models convert streams into estimates. Institutions convert estimates into decisions. The temptation will be to believe that finer measurement solves the old problem of crude categories.

It can do the opposite.

The more precise the score appears, the easier it becomes to draw a line with false confidence.

A prediction to three decimal places can still rest on uncertain data, shifting populations and value judgments about error.

The cutoff may become computationally exact while remaining morally approximate.

We should therefore be suspicious whenever a smooth score feeds a sudden consequence.

Not because the consequence is necessarily wrong.

Because that is the point where the institution's values are hiding.

The score before the threshold is description.

The action after the threshold is governance.

Between them sits a choice.

Gradation makes that choice visible.

And once a choice becomes visible, it can be redesigned.

Sometimes by moving the line.

Sometimes by adding another line.

Sometimes by widening the band.

Sometimes by shrinking the consequence.

Sometimes by allowing review.

Sometimes by preserving the continuous information even after the category is assigned.

And sometimes by admitting that no administrative elegance can make nearly identical people truly different.

The cutoff is a tool.

The cliff is a design decision.

When the cliff is unnecessary, its violence is ours.
