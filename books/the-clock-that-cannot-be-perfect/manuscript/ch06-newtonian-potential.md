# Chapter 6 — The Newtonian Potential Is Not Just Old Physics

“Newtonian” sounds obsolete until one remembers that weak-field general relativity spends much of ordinary life wearing Newton’s clothes.

Near Earth, the gravitational potential provides the leading correction to clock rate. Move a clock through a potential difference and its accumulated proper time changes. Precision clocks exploit this relationship so effectively that geodesy can be done with frequency comparisons. Height becomes time.

The collapse calculation works in this weak-field territory. Suppose the potential is not a perfectly specified classical background but contains a stochastic component. Then the rate of proper time inherits a stochastic component. Integrate that rate and a clock accumulates timing uncertainty.

This sounds almost obvious once stated. The work lies in specifying the noise correlations implied by a collapse model and carrying them into a clock observable without smuggling in a full relativistic theory that does not exist.

That last limitation is essential. The models under discussion are nonrelativistic. The paper does not solve the problem of constructing a complete relativistic objective-collapse theory coupled consistently to dynamical spacetime. It identifies a relativistic consequence of weak gravitational-potential uncertainty.

There is a useful lesson in scale here. Physicists often imagine quantum gravity as Planckian violence: black holes, early-universe singularities, inaccessible energies. But gravity also writes itself into the rate of a clock sitting quietly on a shelf. If a proposed modification of quantum theory perturbs gravity at low energy, timekeeping can become a quantum-foundations experiment without approaching the Planck scale.

The resulting effect may be hopelessly small. That is a numerical fact, not a conceptual defeat. A derived scale tells us whether an idea belongs to present instrumentation, future instrumentation, or perhaps only to consistency arguments. The paper places its predicted clock uncertainty firmly below foreseeable atomic-clock sensitivity.

The headline says time has a flaw. The calculation says something more useful: here is the size of one particular hypothetical flaw, under stated assumptions, and here is why your clocks do not care.
