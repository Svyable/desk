# Chapter 4 — Diósi and Penrose Put Gravity in the Room

Gravity enters the measurement problem because mass is the source of spacetime curvature and quantum theory permits mass to occupy superposed configurations. What gravitational field belongs to a mass that is here and there?

Lajos Diósi and Roger Penrose approached this tension differently, and the phrase “Diósi–Penrose model” can conceal real distinctions among formulations. The shared intuition is that gravitational self-energy associated with competing mass distributions may set a timescale for the instability of superposition. The larger the relevant gravitational mismatch, the shorter the lifetime.

This is seductive because it appears to make classicality emerge from the one interaction that is both universal and geometrical. Seduction is not evidence.

Stochastic implementations of Diósi–Penrose-type collapse have concrete side effects. In particular, noise that kicks charged constituents can produce spontaneous radiation. Underground measurements have therefore constrained, and in particular formulations excluded, simple parameter choices. Later theoretical work continues to dispute exactly which gravitational-collapse formulations a given experimental bound reaches. That argument is not a loophole to be waved away; it is a reminder to attach every experimental exclusion to the equations actually tested.

The 2025 clock paper is careful about its own reach. It works with nonrelativistic collapse models and uses the weak-field relation between Newtonian potential and clock rate. The authors do not present a finished relativistic quantum-gravity theory. They ask what temporal uncertainty follows from the gravitational fluctuations associated with the models they study.

That modesty is productive. Quantum gravity is often discussed at energies or curvatures so remote that experimental contact becomes an aspiration. Here the logic runs backward from low-energy precision. If gravity participates in collapse, and if collapse produces stochastic potential fluctuations, precision timekeeping provides another place to look for the consequences.

The book’s central object is therefore not “quantum gravity” in the grand sense. It is a seam: mass density, stochastic collapse, weak gravitational potential, clock phase. Seams are where large theories become small measurements.
