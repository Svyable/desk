# Chapter 11 — Gran Sasso and the Sound of Nothing

Deep underground, absence becomes an instrument.

Low-background experiments are built to make ordinary radiation rare enough that hypothetical radiation has nowhere to hide. Collapse noise can accelerate charged particles, and accelerated charge can radiate. In particular stochastic collapse formulations, this produces a calculable spontaneous photon spectrum.

Germanium therefore becomes philosophical hardware.

Dedicated analyses, including work using underground data, have placed strong constraints on CSL parameters and on specific stochastic Diósi–Penrose implementations. The important phrase is specific stochastic implementations. A 2026 paper proposing a different deterministic gravitational self-interaction explicitly argues that the spontaneous-radiation exclusions do not apply to its mechanism. Whether that alternative succeeds is a separate question. The epistemic point is that experimental defeat attaches to equations, not family names.

This creates an odd map of foundational physics. A theory invented to explain why measurement has outcomes can be cornered by a detector listening for X rays in a mountain. Another consequence may someday be sought in clock phase. A third in a levitated particle’s motion.

The measurement problem has escaped the philosophy department because objective-collapse theories allowed it to.

There is also a warning here for the clock story. If future clock data ever show unexplained correlated noise, that observation will not automatically mean “time fluctuates.” Environmental gravitational changes, reference-transfer noise, oscillator systematics, electronics, synchronization protocols, and conventional stochastic processes must be defeated first. Gran Sasso teaches the right aesthetic: reduce the world before naming the residue.

A null result can be extraordinarily informative when the background model is strong. A positive anomaly can be nearly meaningless when it is not.
