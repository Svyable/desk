# Chapter 9 — How Small Is Small?

“Many orders of magnitude below current sensitivity” is simultaneously precise in spirit and dangerous in prose. Readers hear “not yet.” Physics may mean “not remotely.”

The correct response to an inaccessible prediction is not to inflate future technology. It is to identify the scaling. Does the timing variance grow with interrogation time? Mass? Geometry? Correlation length? Clock separation? Does averaging suppress it like ordinary uncorrelated noise, or does a shared gravitational stochastic field create correlations that survive comparison?

Those questions determine whether a nominally tiny effect is merely difficult or structurally inaccessible.

Collapse physics has repeatedly advanced by finding better scaling variables. A macroscopic mechanical resonator contains many particles and can amplify mass-proportional effects. A radiation detector integrates enormous numbers of charged constituents and long exposures. Cold atoms can make tiny diffusion visible through expansion. Gravitational-wave detectors provide extraordinary displacement sensitivity over useful bands.

Clock tests will need their own trick.

The naive experiment—wait for a better atomic clock—may be the wrong one. A purpose-built search could compare clocks at different masses or environments only if the model predicts a useful dependence. It could seek cross-correlated timing noise across separated clocks if the stochastic field has spatial coherence. It could compare clock noise with mechanical diffusion predicted by the same collapse parameters, forcing one stochastic mechanism to explain two observables.

That last possibility is especially attractive. New physics should not get a separate parameter story for every instrument. If one collapse kernel predicts both force noise and time noise, the combined experiment can overconstrain it.

The paper opens that door conceptually. Walking through it requires quantitative work this first draft still needs: extracting the exact spectral densities, parameter assumptions, and scaling relations from the published equations and mapping them onto real clock architectures.

Until then, “tiny” remains a boundary, not a promise.
