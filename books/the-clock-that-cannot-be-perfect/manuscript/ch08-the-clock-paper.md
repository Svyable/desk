# Chapter 8 — The Clock Paper

The paper is six pages of physics carrying a headline large enough to swallow a century.

Published in Physical Review Research in November 2025, “Fundamental limits on clock precision from spacetime uncertainty in quantum collapse models” studies Diósi–Penrose and Continuous Spontaneous Localization frameworks. Its premise is not that clocks have exhibited unexplained jitter. It is that collapse models can be associated with stochastic gravitational-potential fluctuations, and weak-field relativity tells us how potential changes clock rate.

The authors calculate the resulting uncertainty in measured time. Their practical conclusion is reassuring: the contribution is negligible for available clocks.

Three things deserve separation.

First, there is a mathematical result inside specified models. That is documented.

Second, there is a physical interpretation: collapse noise corresponds to real gravitational fluctuations. That is model-dependent.

Third, there is the ontological leap: time itself is fundamentally fuzzy. That is a possible reading if the model and mapping describe nature, not an observation established by the paper.

Science communication tends to compress those layers because “a conditional model predicts an undetectably small clock-time variance” does not travel as well as “time has a glitch.” But the conditional version is scientifically richer. It tells us what would have to be true for the headline to become literal.

The paper also performs an interesting service for CSL. Diósi–Penrose already wears gravity on its sleeve. CSL is a general collapse framework. By relating its collapse kernel to potential fluctuations, the authors extend the clock-time argument beyond the obviously gravitational model.

The unresolved problem is relativistic completion. A stochastic Newtonian potential is not a full quantum theory of spacetime. Any claim that the paper unifies quantum mechanics and gravity would therefore outrun the work.

What it does is smaller and better: it finds a bridge where two theories disagree about the status of time and asks whether modified quantum dynamics could make that disagreement measurable.
