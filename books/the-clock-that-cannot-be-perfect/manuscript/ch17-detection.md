# Chapter 17 — What Would Count as a Detection?

Write the detection standard before the anomaly arrives.

A credible claim of collapse-associated time noise would need more than excess instability in one clock. At minimum it should survive clock-species substitution, independent reference transfer, environmental gravitational modeling, relocation or baseline changes, and blinded analysis. Better still, it should reproduce the parameter dependence inferred from an entirely different collapse observable.

The experiment should publish null channels. A nearby oscillator insensitive to the atomic transition but sharing electronics can expose technical noise. Two clocks co-located in the same potential can isolate species-specific effects. Separated clocks can test spatial correlations. Mechanical accelerometers can monitor local gravity-gradient disturbances. Atmospheric, hydrological, seismic, and tidal models can remove ordinary changes in potential.

The analysis should be preregistered around a specific model kernel rather than tuned after an unexplained spectrum appears.

A positive result would then face the hardest step: standard quantum mechanics plus conventional stochastic gravity must fail to account for it while a collapse model predicts it quantitatively.

Even that would establish a new stochastic phenomenon before it established an interpretation. Replication would need to move hardware, teams, and geography.

The reason to be severe is not skepticism as performance. The claim is enormous. If a universal irreducible timing noise were traced to the same dynamics that suppress macroscopic superpositions, two old problems—measurement and the physical status of time—would touch experimentally.

Extraordinary claims do not need extraordinary evidence because they are emotionally extraordinary. They need discriminating evidence because the hypothesis space is crowded.
