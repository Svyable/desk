# Chapter 5 — CSL and the Noise Nobody Ordered

Continuous Spontaneous Localization is an engineer’s nightmare in the best sense: the mechanism has outputs besides the one you wanted.

You wanted definite macroscopic positions. You added stochastic localization. Now the noise can push matter around. Pushing matter means diffusion. Diffusion means heating. Charged matter can radiate. Quiet mechanical systems can register excess force noise. Cold atoms can expand differently. Astrophysical environments can constrain accumulated heating over enormous times.

The history of CSL testing is therefore a history of unintended consequences converted into detectors. Some of the strongest limits have come from non-interferometric experiments. That is counterintuitive. If the theory concerns superposition, surely the cleanest test is to put something enormous into a superposition. Sometimes it is. But keeping a massive object coherent is hard. Watching a quiet object for extra noise can be easier.

This changes how to read the clock proposal. The clock is not primarily a quantum superposition experiment. It is another noise detector. The question is whether the stochastic ingredient needed to make macroscopic outcomes definite also perturbs the gravitational potential sufficiently to leave a timing residue.

Bortolotti and colleagues establish a quantitative connection for CSL to gravitational fluctuations under their framework. This is one of the paper’s genuinely interesting moves. Gravity-associated collapse is expected in Diósi–Penrose discussions; CSL is not automatically a gravity theory. The bridge is therefore a model construction, not a revelation that CSL “was gravity all along.”

That distinction will matter whenever the result is retold. A mathematical mapping can expose a new phenomenology without proving an ontology. The clock limit is conditional twice over: on objective-collapse dynamics and on the physical identification of its stochastic structure with gravitational-potential fluctuations.

This is how frontier science should be allowed to be strange. Not by relaxing standards, but by making strange ideas acquire more ways to fail.
