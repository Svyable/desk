# Chapter 10 — The Experiments Already Hunting Collapse

Collapse models are not waiting politely for clocks.

Matter-wave interferometry asks whether increasingly massive objects retain coherence. Cold-atom experiments search for anomalous diffusion and heating. Mechanical resonators look for unexplained force noise. Spontaneous-radiation searches use low-background detectors. LIGO, LISA Pathfinder, and AURIGA data have been used to constrain stochastic motion associated with collapse models. Cosmological and astrophysical heating arguments reach parameter regions laboratories approach differently.

The patchwork matters because no single null “disproves collapse.” CSL has parameter space. Variants alter noise spectra, dissipation, mass coupling, and relativistic behavior. Diósi–Penrose implementations likewise differ. A bound is only as broad as the assumptions translating an instrument’s silence into excluded theory.

This is tedious, and therefore important.

The cultural temptation around foundational physics is to speak in brands: “CSL survives,” “Penrose collapse was ruled out,” “quantum mechanics wins.” The scientific object is usually an exclusion region conditioned on a kernel, cutoff, correlation length, noise spectrum, geometry, and analysis.

A clock test would enter the same ecology. Its value would not be that time is philosophically central. Its value would be orthogonality. Different instruments respond to different consequences of the same hypothesized noise. A clock sees accumulated phase. A cantilever sees force. Germanium sees photons. An interferometer sees coherence loss.

When several observables attack one mechanism, model-building becomes expensive in the right way. A modification introduced to evade a radiation bound may change its clock signature. A colored-noise spectrum that weakens one experiment may strengthen another. A dissipative extension that cures runaway heating has other consequences.

The book’s wager is not that clocks will discover collapse. It is that the clock paper reveals how mature the experimental attack on the measurement problem has become. We are no longer restricted to arguing about cats.
