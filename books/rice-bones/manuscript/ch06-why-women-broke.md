# Chapter 6 — Why Women Broke

The overwhelming majority of officially recognized Itai-itai patients were women. Any serious account has to explain that pattern without turning female biology into an excuse for pollution.

The distinction is delicate because susceptibility can be narrated in two opposite ways. One version says: the pollutant was responsible, full stop, and any discussion of pregnancy, lactation, nutrition, age, or endocrine state distracts from corporate responsibility. The other says: because those factors mattered, cadmium was only one cause among many and industrial responsibility is therefore diluted.

Both are too crude.

The Japanese government’s 1968 formulation explicitly combined chronic cadmium poisoning with factors that promoted the development of severe osteomalacia: pregnancy, breastfeeding, endocrine changes, aging, and inadequate calcium among them. Later research has continued to examine why older women in the basin were disproportionately represented.

Biology provides part of the answer. Pregnancy and lactation alter calcium demands. Menopause changes bone metabolism. Low calcium intake can worsen vulnerability to mineralization problems. Smaller body size and differences in iron status can influence cadmium absorption. Kidney injury then adds another layer by disturbing mineral handling.

But social history belongs in the model too. Who ate locally produced rice, for how many decades, and in what quantities? Who remained in farming households? Who experienced repeated pregnancies and nutritional scarcity in particular historical periods? Who was likely to seek care, be recognized, or survive long enough to meet diagnostic criteria? A sex ratio in a case series is produced by bodies living inside social systems.

This is why “women were more susceptible” is not an explanation. It is a prompt to specify mechanisms.

The phrase *itai-itai* itself risks feminizing the history in another way. The suffering woman can become an icon—bent body, repeated fracture, cry of pain—while the industrial and agricultural systems that produced her exposure fade into background. The victim becomes visible precisely as the infrastructure becomes invisible.

A better narrative keeps them connected. A woman’s skeleton was not an isolated tragedy. It was the downstream end of mining, water management, paddy agriculture, food distribution, household labor, reproductive history, renal physiology, and delayed regulation.

The numerical record is stark. A 2016 medical review reported that, as of October 2015, 200 people had been officially recognized, 195 of them women and five men. By the end of 2023 Toyama’s official total had reached 201 certified patients. These are certification counts, not a complete census of all cadmium-related biological effects in the basin.

That boundary matters especially for gender. If formal diagnosis requires severe osteomalacia, the official case definition preferentially captures the people in whom cadmium exposure, renal damage, bone vulnerability, and life history converged strongly enough to produce that endpoint. It does not follow that men or younger residents were untouched.

The story also warns against a familiar move in environmental health: blaming the body for revealing the hazard. When a toxin harms one subgroup more severely, the subgroup can be treated as unusually fragile rather than as an early-warning population. Children, pregnant people, workers with prior disease, elderly residents, and nutritionally stressed communities have all played this role in different pollution histories.

A safe environment is not one that protects only the median physiology.

Toyama’s women made the system’s failure undeniable because their bodies were where several vulnerabilities met. The correct conclusion is not that their bones explain away the river. It is that susceptibility determines where a shared exposure becomes impossible to ignore.
