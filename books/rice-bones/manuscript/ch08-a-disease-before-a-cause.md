# Chapter 8 — A Disease Before a Cause

A disease can exist socially before it exists scientifically, and scientifically before it exists legally.

The people along the Jinzū did not need a ministry statement to know that severe illness existed. The pain, fractures, weakness, and household consequences were present before consensus. Yet without an accepted cause, suffering remained vulnerable to reinterpretation.

This is the limbo period common to environmental disasters. Patients have symptoms. Doctors have partial theories. Companies have incentives to demand precision. Governments have incentives to avoid premature attribution. Researchers disagree. The public hears “uncertain” and may translate it as “probably nothing.”

But uncertainty has structure.

Some uncertainty concerns whether an exposure occurred. Some concerns dose. Some concerns mechanism. Some concerns whether a particular patient’s disease was caused by that exposure. Some concerns whether a source was responsible. Treating all of these as one fog helps whoever benefits from delay.

By the 1960s, the Toyama evidence increasingly constrained that fog. Cadmium was present in the contaminated system. The mining operation was an identified source. Exposure pathways through water and rice were plausible and measurable. Renal abnormalities and bone disease fit chronic cadmium toxicity. Geographic patterns tied disease to polluted irrigation areas.

There were still open questions. Open questions are not the same as an empty case.

The 1968 Ministry of Health and Welfare statement was therefore a threshold event. The state accepted that chronic cadmium poisoning from the Kamioka discharge caused renal injury and subsequent osteomalacia, with other physiological and nutritional factors influencing development of the full syndrome. Itai-itai became Japan’s first officially recognized pollution disease.

Administrative recognition changed the balance of power. A company could continue to dispute legal liability, but it could no longer insist that the disease itself was merely an unexplained local oddity.

Recognition also created new boundaries. To compensate patients, government needed criteria. Criteria stabilize a category, but they also create edges: inside, outside, observe, reject, reassess. People whose health effects did not fit the full syndrome could remain medically important while legally excluded.

Toyama eventually developed a special observation category for residents who might progress toward recognized disease. That category is revealing. It acknowledges that certainty is not binary. Someone can be neither officially ill nor simply healthy in the relevant sense.

The same principle should guide the historical record. We should not inflate every compatible symptom into certified disease. Nor should we let the certification line erase subclinical or precursor injury documented in population studies.

A disease before a cause teaches epistemic humility. A cause before complete mechanism teaches the opposite lesson: humility must not become paralysis.

The question is not whether uncertainty exists. It is who gets to use it, and for how long.
