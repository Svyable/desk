# Chapter 14 — Forty Billion Yen of Soil

Pollution changes the accounting of land.

A rice paddy is normally valued for what it can produce. Once contaminated with cadmium, it becomes an inventory of past emissions. The soil itself carries liability.

Toyama’s restoration program makes this visible at extraordinary scale. In 1979, a special land-improvement project began to address cadmium-contaminated agricultural areas along the Jinzū. Work continued for thirty-three years. A 2012 summary reported 1,686 hectares in the overall countermeasure area, with 863 hectares restored under the prefectural public project and another 120 hectares restored voluntarily by farmers, for 983 hectares restored as agricultural land. The reported public-project cost was 40.7 billion yen.

Numbers at that scale can become abstract. Divide them back into actions: surveys, boundaries, excavation, replacement soil, grading, irrigation work, transport, testing, compensation, administration, maintenance. Every step exists because contamination changed where rice could safely be grown.

The cleanup also reveals why environmental harm should not be priced only through medical damages. The same cadmium created costs in bodies, crops, soil, water monitoring, engineering, legal work, health surveillance, and decades of institutional labor.

A narrow polluter-pays calculation can miss temporal transfer. The company receives revenue when ore is sold. Restoration expenses may arrive decades later and be shared among the company, national government, prefecture, city, and farmers. Society becomes the long-duration balance sheet for pollution whose economic benefits were realized earlier.

Soil restoration can also produce new technical questions. Replacing or covering contaminated layers changes drainage and elevation. Restored fields can subside. Recent local discussions have noted continuing problems in some restored areas. Completion ceremonies are necessary administrative markers, but landscapes keep moving after a project is declared complete.

The paddy therefore becomes a second archive after the body. A kidney can retain evidence of chronic exposure. Soil can retain evidence of historic discharge.

Both archives are imperfect. Metals migrate. Farming changes. Medical pathology changes with age and treatment. The task is not to imagine contamination as frozen in place but to reconstruct its transformations.

Toyama eventually turned restoration into a public memory project as well. The prefectural museum teaches visitors not only about patients but about source control and land recovery. School tours can move from the museum to Kamioka Mining to restored paddies and victim-history sites. In 2026, the prefecture again advertised such a tour for elementary students.

That itinerary is unusually honest. It refuses to isolate disease from industry or cleanup from land.

A child can stand at the source, then the field, then the archive.

The causal chain becomes geography again.
