# Chapter 5 — Kidneys First

The bones made the disease famous. The kidneys made it intelligible.

Cadmium is not a bone toxin in the simple sense of a chemical that travels directly to the skeleton and dissolves it. Chronic exposure can injure the kidney, particularly the proximal tubules. Those structures normally reclaim substances filtered from blood. When tubular function is damaged, abnormal urinary losses can appear, including low-molecular-weight proteins and substances involved in mineral homeostasis. Severe, long-standing dysfunction can help produce the conditions in which bone mineralization fails.

That sequence is why the 1968 Japanese government statement described renal damage first and osteomalacia second.

It also explains a diagnostic problem. By the time a person has fractures, deformity, and excruciating pain, the disease is advanced. Environmental medicine, however, needs to recognize harm before the most spectacular endpoint. Later health surveys in cadmium-polluted areas therefore examined renal tubular markers, not merely full-blown Itai-itai disease.

This creates a boundary problem between a disease label and a toxic effect. Official certification requires criteria. Criteria make compensation administratively possible. But the body does not obey the clean threshold implied by a form. People can have cadmium-associated renal dysfunction without satisfying all requirements for the legally recognized syndrome. People can have early or subclinical bone changes that do not appear as classic radiographic disease. The medical spectrum is broader than the compensation category.

Japanese researchers have continued to examine this distinction. Histopathological work on Itai-itai cases has studied kidneys and bones together, attempting to relate the severity of tubulopathy to osteomalacia. Reviews emphasize that the full syndrome represents a severe stage of chronic environmental cadmium poisoning, while milder renal effects can occur outside the narrow certified category.

This matters ethically because official numbers are easy to misuse. Counting certified patients is essential. Treating that number as the total number of people biologically affected is a different claim and requires evidence. The same is true in the other direction: invoking a broad exposed population does not justify attributing every later illness to cadmium.

The evidence classes have to survive contact with sympathy.

Class 1 includes the established toxicological sequence linking chronic cadmium exposure to renal tubular injury and, in the severe Toyama syndrome, osteomalacia. Class 2 includes individual historical cases with incomplete dose reconstruction but compatible geography and clinical findings. Class 3 includes specific disease attributions unsupported by adequate exposure or pathology. Class 4 is the larger interpretation: that administrative disease categories can conceal a continuum of environmental injury even when those categories are necessary for law.

The kidney also changes how we think about latency. A contaminant may accumulate and injure slowly. By the time symptoms become undeniable, the relevant emissions may have occurred years earlier. Records can be gone. People can have moved. Diet changes. Production methods change. The source may point to current discharge levels while the body is reporting past exposure.

This is one reason long-term surveillance matters after visible pollution falls. Toyama continued health examinations in the basin decades after the causal controversy was settled. Even after the last living certified patient died, officials did not conclude that history had become biologically irrelevant.

The first lesson of the kidney is mechanistic: the organ connects exposure to bone disease.

The second is political: the earliest measurable injury may be less visible than the endpoint that finally forces institutions to act.
