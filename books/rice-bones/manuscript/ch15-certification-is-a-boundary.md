# Chapter 15 — Certification Is a Boundary

Environmental disease becomes governable when someone draws a line.

Toyama’s system eventually required four conditions for formal recognition as an Itai-itai patient. The precise medical criteria evolved within an administrative framework, but the purpose is universal: government needs a reproducible method for deciding who qualifies for compensation.

A boundary solves one problem and creates another.

Inside the category, a patient gains recognition, treatment support, and compensation. Outside it, a person may still have cadmium-associated renal dysfunction or other health effects but not the defining osteomalacia required for the full syndrome. The line can therefore be medically narrower than the exposure’s biological footprint.

Japanese researchers have repeatedly returned to this issue. A 2016 review noted that new applications continued even decades after the pollution crisis became history and that some applicants were rejected because osteomalacia could not be established by the required evidence. Toyama also maintained a separate category of people requiring observation because progression to the full disease could not be excluded.

This is not evidence that the system was fraudulent. It is evidence that compensation systems need categories while toxicology produces continua.

The danger comes when those categories are reused for purposes they were never designed to serve. “Only 201 people were certified” can be a correct statement. “Only 201 people were harmed by cadmium” is a different statement.

The reverse error is equally serious. Because a larger population was exposed or showed renal markers, it does not follow that every illness among them was caused by cadmium.

Four evidence classes help prevent both expansions.

Class 1 can include officially certified disease and well-established population-level renal effects where supported by good studies. Class 2 can include compatible individual illness with incomplete historical dose data. Class 3 includes attribution that cannot survive alternative explanations. Class 4 includes the interpretive claim that administrative boundaries systematically underdescribe biological spectra.

Certification also reflects time. Early in a disaster, tests are primitive, records sparse, and criteria contested. Later, scientific knowledge improves but the most exposed cohort ages and dies. The state can become better at diagnosis precisely as direct historical evidence disappears.

Toyama’s long surveillance program tried to bridge that gap. Health examinations continued in the affected basin, and review bodies continued to consider residents long after the main exposure period.

By 2025, the last living certified patient and the last living designated observer had died.

That did not abolish the boundary.

It made the boundary historical.
