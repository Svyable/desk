# Chapter 16 — Zero Living Patients

In August 2024, the last living officially certified Itai-itai patient died at ninety-three. In March 2025, the last living person in Toyama’s special observation category also died. For the first time since the prefecture’s recognition system began, both living counts were zero.

It would be easy to write a triumphant sentence here: the disease is over.

The evidence does not permit it.

Zero living certified patients means exactly that. It does not mean zero cadmium remains in the regional environment. It does not mean every person affected was certified. It does not mean restored agricultural land no longer requires attention. It does not mean the source-control agreement has become unnecessary. It certainly does not mean the causal history can safely be forgotten.

Toyama’s government continued health examinations among residents born in the basin and continued convening review processes. In August 2026, local reporting said no new patient or observer designation had been made after examination of residents, while giving cumulative totals of 201 certified patients and 345 people placed in the observation category over the history of the system.

The absence of living recognized patients therefore coincides with continued governance.

This is a useful corrective to the way disasters are periodized. Histories like clean dates: outbreak, discovery, judgment, cleanup, end. Bodies and landscapes do not.

The last patient’s death is a demographic event, not an environmental half-life.

The same applies to memory. Once no living patient can testify directly, the archive changes character. Museum recordings, family testimony, medical records, newsletters, court files, inspection logs, and restored landscapes bear more of the burden. Every year increases the temptation to convert a contested industrial history into a settled civic lesson with the rough edges removed.

That is why the continuing inspection tradition matters symbolically as well as technically. It keeps the causal relationship spatially enacted. People from the affected basin still go upstream.

The mine is not only a historical source in a museum diagram. It remains a place that can be entered and asked to account for itself.

The moral challenge now is generational transfer. The people performing future oversight will increasingly have no personal memory of the worst disease. Their motivation must come from institutions, records, education, and inherited rights rather than grief.

This transition is fragile. Pain is a powerful organizer. Memory is quieter.

The next generation therefore needs more than a warning that pollution is bad. It needs the actual machinery of how Toyama learned: map the pathway, separate disease from certification, preserve counterevidence, secure source access, measure repeatedly, restore contaminated media, and never confuse disappearance of witnesses with disappearance of responsibility.

Zero is a number.

Closure is an argument.
