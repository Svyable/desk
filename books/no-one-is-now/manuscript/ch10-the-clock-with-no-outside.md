# Chapter 10 — The Clock With No Outside

A laboratory clock has somewhere else to stand.

You can put a clock beside an experiment. You can say the laser pulse began at this reading and the detector fired at that one. The apparatus changes while an external parameter called time advances through the equations. This is so ordinary that it is hard to notice the privilege.

Now make the laboratory the universe.

Where does the clock go?

There is no table outside everything on which to set it.

This is one version of the problem of time in quantum gravity, and it is where every popular sentence about a “timeless universe” should slow down before becoming a headline.

Quantum mechanics and general relativity treat time differently.

In ordinary quantum mechanics, time usually appears as an external parameter. A quantum state evolves with respect to it according to the Schrödinger equation. Position can be represented by an operator. Momentum can be represented by an operator. Time, in the standard formulation, is not simply another operator of the same kind. The clock in the laboratory is treated classically enough to label when measurements happen.

General relativity does not allow spacetime to remain an external stage. Geometry is dynamical. Matter and energy affect spacetime, and spacetime affects motion. Coordinates can be changed without changing physical content. The theory has no universal external time flowing independently of the gravitational field.

Trying to quantize gravity therefore brings two successful theories together at exactly the place their bookkeeping differs most.

That is a real research problem.

It is not evidence that time is fake.

The distinction matters because frontier physics is a magnet for metaphors that outrun results. A paper asks how relational observables can recover dynamics from a constrained quantum system. A magazine says physicists prove time does not exist. A social-media account adds that consciousness therefore creates reality. By dinner, a technical problem in canonical quantum gravity has become permission to believe anything.

We can do better.

One famous route into the problem comes from the Wheeler-DeWitt equation in canonical quantum gravity. Very roughly, when general relativity is reformulated in Hamiltonian terms and quantized, the resulting constraint equation for the wavefunction of the universe does not contain an external time parameter in the way the ordinary Schrödinger equation does. Symbolically, one often writes something resembling HΨ = 0.

A stationary-looking equation is startling if the universe obviously contains change.

Where do clocks, motion and history come from?

There are many proposed answers and no experimentally established final theory.

One family of ideas makes time relational. Instead of asking how a system changes relative to an external cosmic parameter, ask how one part changes relative to another part chosen as a clock.

Don Page and William Wootters developed a striking version in the early 1980s. They showed how an overall stationary quantum state could contain correlations such that one subsystem appears to evolve relative to the readings of another subsystem serving as a clock. Their 1983 paper was titled “Evolution without evolution,” which has probably caused several decades of excitable summaries all by itself.

The basic intuition is less mystical than the title.

Imagine a closed system divided into a clock and the rest. The joint state contains correlations. Conditional on the clock being in one state, the rest appears one way; conditional on another clock state, the rest appears another way. From inside the relational description, there is dynamics even though the global state can be stationary in an appropriate sense.

This is not the same as saying nothing happens.

It is a proposal about what change is relative to.

Experiments and theoretical work have explored Page-Wootters-style relational time in limited quantum systems. More recent papers continue refining how clocks behave, what measurements mean and how relational dynamics can be recovered. None puts the entire cosmological problem to bed.

The frontier is alive because the easy slogans are inadequate.

Quantum clocks are physical systems. They have finite energy, uncertainty and back-action. A perfect classical time parameter has none of these inconveniences. The moment the clock becomes part of the quantum system, timekeeping inherits quantum limitations.

This is a theme the classical chapters have prepared us for.

There is no free clock.

In relativity, a clock has a worldline and gravitational environment. In quantum theory, a clock can have quantum uncertainty and become entangled. In cosmology, the large-scale clock depends on a spacetime model. In metrology, common time depends on ensembles and transfer. The fantasy object—an infinitely precise clock outside the universe, unaffected by anything—keeps retreating as physics improves.

Perhaps it was never a physical object at all.

The temptation is then to declare relational time the deepest truth. That would be premature.

There are multiple approaches to quantum gravity: canonical programs, loop quantum gravity, string theory, causal sets, asymptotic safety, causal dynamical triangulations and others. The role of time differs across formulations. Some recover familiar spacetime in semiclassical limits. Some place causal structure in a fundamental role. Some treat spacetime as emergent from more primitive quantum degrees of freedom. The field has not selected one experimentally confirmed ontology.

What we have is a pressure point.

A theory of everything cannot casually assume the one thing that, by definition, would have to sit outside everything.

That pressure is enough for this book.

It sharpens the central thesis without pretending to settle it.

Relativity already denies us a universal observer-independent simultaneity relation of the Newtonian kind. Quantum gravity asks whether even the smooth spacetime in which local clocks trace paths is fundamental. The present may therefore be less basic than ordinary intuition assumes at two different levels.

One level is established: relativistic simultaneity.

The deeper level is open: the quantum structure from which spacetime and temporal relations emerge, if emergence is the right word at all.

Keeping those levels separate is part of intellectual hygiene.

There is a practical reason too. When people hear that fundamental physics may be timeless, they often infer fatalism. If all events are somehow “already there,” choice must be an illusion. Or they infer the opposite: if time is emergent, consciousness can select reality. Neither conclusion follows from the equations simply by choosing an adjective.

A block-universe interpretation of relativity, relational quantum time and human agency belong to different arguments. They can interact philosophically, but one does not settle the others by vocabulary.

Physics tells us what structures a model contains and what observations it predicts.

Agency asks what kinds of processes inside that structure count as deliberation, control and choice.

A decision can be a physical process without needing to sit outside physics.

The fact that a worldline can be represented as part of a spacetime geometry does not mean a brain’s computations cease to be among the causes of later events. A hurricane can be represented in four-dimensional spacetime and still knock down a house. Description from outside a modeled history and causal participation from inside it are not competitors.

This matters because the “no universal now” thesis can otherwise become emotionally sterile.

If there is no cosmic present, why should this moment matter?

Because consequences originate locally.

A local event does not need universal simultaneity to change what follows in its future light cone. Your hand on a switch matters because current flows afterward. A vote matters because it changes a count and triggers institutional processes. A sentence matters because someone hears it and their state changes. A measurement matters because it enters a record and constrains later theory.

Causal importance is not granted by a cosmic clock.

It is granted, if that is the word, by connection.

Quantum mechanics complicates connection without erasing it. Entanglement produces correlations that cannot be explained by local hidden variables of the ordinary kind, as Bell tests have shown with increasing rigor. But entanglement does not provide controllable faster-than-light messaging. Relativistic causal structure survives in the no-signalling behavior of quantum theory.

This is another place popular language causes damage.

“Spooky action at a distance” is repeated until it sounds as if two entangled particles are telephoning instantaneously. The correlations are genuinely nonclassical. They do not let one laboratory send a chosen bit outside the light cone.

No universal present returns through the quantum back door.

Quantum field theory is built to respect relativistic causality in its operational predictions. Operators associated with spacelike-separated regions satisfy commutation relations that prevent ordinary superluminal signalling. The technical story is richer than one sentence, especially around measurement and interpretations, but the broad point is secure enough: quantum weirdness does not license faster-than-light communication.

That is almost disappointing.

We have spent a century discovering increasingly strange descriptions of reality, and causality remains stubbornly difficult to cheat.

Perhaps that stubbornness is itself a clue.

Causal-set theory takes it as one. Other approaches also assign causal or relational structure an important role. In quantum information, researchers study indefinite causal order, where processes can be arranged in quantum superpositions not describable by one definite classical sequence under certain frameworks. The quantum switch is a prominent example. Experiments have demonstrated operational advantages associated with such structures.

This sounds like causality finally dissolving.

It does not mean anything can cause anything. “Indefinite causal order” is a technical term inside carefully specified quantum-process frameworks. It does not imply messages to your own past or the collapse of relativistic light cones. Some of the deepest interpretive questions remain active.

The frontier rewards readers who can tolerate a result being interesting before it becomes cosmic.

That tolerance is rare because humans like ontological closure. We want to know what reality *really is*.

A clock laboratory offers a healthier attitude. Build a device. State what it measures. Quantify uncertainty. Compare with another device. Improve the transfer. Find a discrepancy. Ask whether the discrepancy is apparatus, environment or physics. Repeat.

The frontier of time needs the same discipline.

A relational-time model must reproduce ordinary temporal experience and tested physics in the regime where those work. A quantum-gravity theory must recover general relativity at accessible scales and make distinctive predictions somewhere. An emergent-spacetime claim becomes science by finding consequences that distinguish it from alternatives.

Until then, the metaphysics should remain lighter than the mathematics.

This is not timidity. It is how one protects the interesting unknown from becoming decorative certainty.

There is an irony in the human desire for a clock outside the universe.

We have built external clocks for almost every system we study. A chemical reaction is timed by lab equipment. A race is timed by sensors. A computer program is profiled against a hardware timer. A spacecraft is tracked against ground clocks. We have learned to understand change by comparing it with another change chosen for regularity.

Then we ask for the time of the whole universe and instinctively reach for the same arrangement.

There is no second universe beside it holding the stopwatch.

Cosmology gets around this with internal structure: comoving observers, atomic processes, expansion, radiation, relational measurements. Quantum cosmology pushes the problem further: perhaps the clock must always be inside the state it helps describe.

That possibility makes the human obsession with synchronization look less like a mundane administrative habit and more like a local implementation of a general principle.

We choose one process as clock and compare others with it.

A pendulum against a work shift.

Cesium against civil time.

A satellite oscillator against a coordinate system.

A pulsar against terrestrial clocks.

An internal quantum degree of freedom against another subsystem.

Timekeeping is comparison all the way down until, perhaps, there is no down left.

That last sentence is deliberately provisional.

Maybe a future theory will restore a more fundamental temporal structure in a form we have not imagined. Maybe relational accounts will win. Maybe the word time will divide into several quantities the way temperature divided from heat and mass from weight. Physics has repeatedly improved by discovering that ordinary language bundled distinct structures together.

We should be ready for time to suffer the same fate.

The important thing is not to make uncertainty sound like emptiness.

We know a great deal.

Atomic clocks work. Relativistic corrections work. causal ordering works. quantum mechanics predicts experiments with astonishing precision. General relativity predicts gravity across scales from laboratories to binary systems and gravitational waves. The conflict at the foundations appears because both theories are so successful in their domains.

The clock with no outside is not a proof that science has failed.

It is the place success has made unavoidable.
