# Chapter 15 — Memory Is a Causal Device

A memory has to be made before it can be remembered.

This is so obvious that we rarely state it. Something happens. A nervous system changes. Later, part of that changed state contributes to reconstruction of the earlier event. The memory is not the event preserved whole. It is a physical consequence of the event, modified by everything that happened between encoding and recall.

Memory belongs to causality before it belongs to autobiography.

The same is true of a photograph.

Light interacts with a sensor. Charge accumulates. Electronics convert the signal into numbers. Those numbers are stored in physical media. The file may be copied through networks, transformed by compression, backed up, displayed on a screen and eventually seen by someone who was nowhere near the original scene.

A photograph is an event causing a durable pattern that causes later events.

An archive is a machine for lengthening that chain.

This matters in a universe without a shared present because local observers need ways to bring absent events into current decisions. We cannot consult the past directly. We cannot consult distant present events faster than signals arrive. We operate on traces.

Civilization is trace-rich.

A building permit carries a decision forward for decades. A land deed links current ownership to earlier transactions. A scientific paper lets a laboratory encounter an experiment performed elsewhere and earlier. DNA transmits biological information across generations. A constitutional clause changes arguments centuries after the people who wrote it are dead. A lunar retroreflector placed by Apollo astronauts still changes the photons sent toward it by observatories today.

We are surrounded by delayed consequences with labels.

The labels matter because natural traces do not explain themselves.

A rock layer contains evidence. A stratigrapher has to know what processes could have formed it. A tree ring contains evidence. A dendrochronologist has to distinguish growth conditions from injury and species effects. An atomic spectrum contains evidence. A physicist needs a model linking lines to transitions.

A timestamp also contains evidence only inside a model of the clock.

Was it synchronized?

Which time scale?

Did the software record send time or receive time?

Was daylight saving applied twice?

Did the clock step after NTP correction?

Was the file copied, preserving or changing metadata?

The more we rely on records, the more we need provenance.

This is the temporal equivalent of chain of custody.

Modern data systems make provenance feel like an optional technical feature until something goes wrong. A machine-learning model is trained on a dataset. Months later, someone discovers that some records were duplicated or labeled after the cutoff date, leaking future information into training. Performance looked excellent because the model had quietly seen traces of answers it was supposed to predict.

Causal order became a validity condition.

Scientific studies face the same problem under different names. If an outcome influences which data are included, causal interpretation can break. If a measurement is taken after treatment but treated as a baseline variable, analysis can become incoherent. If a historical source was written decades after an event, it may contain hindsight unavailable to participants.

Good reasoning asks when information entered the system.

That is a causal question disguised as methodology.

Memory itself is unreliable partly because reconstruction happens later.

Psychology has repeatedly shown that human memory is not a literal recording. Recall can be altered by suggestion, later information, repeated retelling and the conditions under which a memory is retrieved. Confidence is not a perfect proxy for accuracy. Eyewitness testimony can be sincere and wrong.

This does not mean memory is useless.

A system can be fallible and indispensable.

We compensate by cross-checking local traces. Multiple witnesses. contemporaneous notes. photographs. physical evidence. logs. independent measurements. The same architecture that builds objective science from local instruments builds reliable history from local records: comparison.

There is no master memory of the world.

This may be the social version of no universal now.

We like to speak of “what history remembers,” as if history had a nervous system. In practice, institutions select and preserve records. Archives burn. Empires destroy documents. Families keep letters. Governments classify files. Languages become unreadable. Digital platforms disappear. Some communities maintain oral traditions. Others are represented mainly in records made by their opponents.

The past that can influence us is filtered by survival.

This should make us humble about absences.

No record does not mean no event.

No surviving voice does not mean no experience.

No telescope signal does not mean no distant phenomenon.

Causal access and existence are separate questions.

But the reverse mistake is also common.

A gap in evidence does not license any preferred story.

If signals are missing, uncertainty should widen. The discipline of history and science lies partly in refusing to fill causal silence with decorative certainty.

The same discipline becomes essential at cosmic scale.

Everything astronomy knows comes from messengers that arrive here: photons, cosmic rays, neutrinos, gravitational waves, meteorites and samples from the Solar System, plus the local physical context in which we interpret them. Even a spacecraft visiting another world sends observations home through signals. No astronomer jumps to the galaxy being measured.

Knowledge is a delivery problem.

Different messengers carry different histories.

Light can be absorbed by dust. Neutrinos pass through matter more easily but are difficult to detect. Gravitational waves encode motions of compact masses in a way electromagnetic light does not. Cosmic rays are deflected by magnetic fields and lose directional information. Samples preserve chemistry but come from a tiny set of reachable bodies.

A multi-messenger event is powerful because independent causal traces meet.

In 2017, gravitational-wave detectors observed a binary neutron-star merger designated GW170817. Gamma-ray telescopes detected a burst shortly afterward. Optical observatories found a transient in a nearby galaxy. Follow-up across the electromagnetic spectrum continued. Different signals, instruments and teams converged on one astrophysical event.

No observer occupied the merger.

Objectivity arrived later, assembled from messengers.

This is the opposite of omniscience and, I think, more impressive.

A local species can infer a collision of dead stars more than a hundred million light-years away by comparing tiny distortions in interferometers with photons received by instruments scattered around one planet and in orbit.

We did not need a universal present.

We needed clocks good enough to compare arrival times.

This pattern repeats in less dramatic science. Seismology reconstructs Earth’s interior from waves arriving at stations. Medical imaging reconstructs hidden anatomy from interactions measured outside tissue. Radar infers range from delayed reflections. Archaeology reconstructs vanished settlements from surviving material. Cosmology reconstructs expansion history from redshifts, distances and statistical structure.

Intelligence is inference under delayed access.

Our personal sense of knowing hides this because small-scale perception is fast. You look at a cup and feel direct contact with the cup’s present existence. But photons left the cup and entered your eyes. Neural processing followed. The delay is negligible for pouring coffee and fundamental for understanding what observation is.

You never see an object without causality doing the delivery.

This is not an argument that perception is unreal. It is an argument that reality can be known through interaction rather than through instantaneous presence.

The distinction becomes emotionally important around death.

When someone dies, their worldline no longer produces new ordinary interactions in the way a living body does. Yet causal consequences remain: memories in other brains, photographs, children, work, debts, injuries, habits transmitted to others, rooms arranged by their choices, words in files. A person can remain causally active through structures they changed.

We use the word legacy for this.

It is often inflated into reputation. The physical version is plainer.

A dead carpenter’s joint still holds a roof.

A dead engineer’s code still routes packets.

A dead parent’s phrase comes out of an adult child’s mouth.

A dead scientist’s measurement remains in a table that changes a modern estimate.

Causality is generous about absence.

An event does not have to remain present to remain consequential.

This should ease some of the anxiety created by a universe without a privileged now. If only the present feels real, the loss of global present can sound like a loss of solidity. But physics gives us a sturdier currency: effects.

The past matters because it is in the present as structure.

Not all of it. Never all of it.

Enough.

Memory is selective because a finite system cannot preserve every microscopic detail of its interactions. Archives are selective because storage and attention cost resources. Scientific instruments are selective because they measure chosen variables. Even a camera discards most of the electromagnetic field.

Every record is compression.

Compression creates power and blindness simultaneously.

A timestamp compresses a complicated clock-comparison system into a number. A photograph compresses a light field into pixels. A census compresses millions of lives into categories. A history book compresses overlapping events into narrative. A neural memory compresses experience into a reconstructable pattern.

Good compression preserves what the future task needs.

Bad compression removes the causal detail the future needs to reason correctly.

This is why standards and metadata matter so much. Units, reference frames, calibration records, timestamps, uncertainty estimates and source citations are ways of preserving context alongside a measurement.

A number without provenance is a severed signal.

The human role, if we want one, may include preserving causal legibility.

This sounds modest compared with conquering the stars. It becomes less modest when extended across generations.

A society decides what its future can know by what it records now.

Do we preserve raw scientific data or only papers?

Do we record the assumptions behind a model?

Do we keep software needed to read old formats?

Do we preserve languages, ecological observations, climate measurements, legal changes, failed experiments?

Do we retain enough provenance that future people can distinguish measurement from interpretation?

These are temporal design choices.

A future observer cannot send us a request for missing metadata.

The asymmetry is absolute enough for practical life: influence moves forward along ordinary causal paths.

Archives are messages to receivers who cannot answer until later.

This makes them a form of trust.

The writer assumes someone may care.

The archivist assumes the record may outlive the current dispute.

The scientist writes methods so a stranger can reproduce work.

The engineer leaves documentation for a maintainer not yet hired.

The parent labels photographs because names disappear faster than faces.

These acts are not heroic. They are civilization at low voltage.

They also reveal a species that has learned to act outside its own experiential present.

Animals cache food. Ecosystems leave traces. Genes transmit information. Humans add symbolic planning across centuries. We can let a dead person affect an unborn person through deliberate records.

That is a causal reach no universal present could improve.

A universal now would tell every event which slice it belonged to. It would not preserve anything.

Memory does.
