# Chapter 3 — The Argument Between Clocks

In October 1971, four cesium-beam atomic clocks took commercial flights around the world.

The clocks were not passengers in the usual sense. Joseph Hafele, a physicist at Washington University in St. Louis, and Richard Keating of the United States Naval Observatory carried them aboard airliners first eastward and then westward. The experiment was inelegant in the way many good experiments are inelegant. Airline seats, schedules, refueling stops, portable equipment, hotel rooms. This was not a satellite built around an exquisite payload. It was luggage containing clocks good enough to start an argument with time.

Before the flights, the traveling clocks were compared with reference clocks at the Naval Observatory. After the journeys, they were compared again. Relativity predicted different accumulated times depending on direction because both velocity and gravitational potential mattered. The airborne clocks spent time higher in Earth’s gravitational field, tending to make them run faster than clocks lower down, while their motion relative to an appropriate reference affected them in the other direction. Eastward and westward travel combined these effects differently because Earth itself rotates.

The reported changes were tiny: tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. They were also not zero.

A clock can leave home, travel, return, sit beside the clock it left, and disagree with it about how much time passed.

Once the clocks are together again, there is no simultaneity convention left to blame. You can put them on the same bench. They carry different elapsed times because they took different paths through spacetime.

This is proper time.

The term sounds like physics trying to award manners to one kind of clock. It means the time measured along a particular timelike worldline by an ideal clock traveling on that worldline. Proper time is local. A clock does not consult the rest of the universe to know how much it has accumulated. Its reading belongs to its path.

The point becomes theatrical in the twin paradox. One twin remains on Earth. The other travels at very high speed, reverses direction and returns. The traveling twin has aged less. Popular accounts often frame this as a trick question: if motion is relative, why doesn’t each twin see the other as moving and conclude the other should be younger?

The short answer is that the twins do not follow equivalent spacetime paths. The traveling twin changes inertial frames in order to return. The geometry of the paths gives different proper times. The difference is no more contradictory than two routes between the same cities having different mileages.

That analogy is imperfect, but it points in the right direction. Relativistic elapsed time is closer to path length in spacetime than to water poured from one universal reservoir into every clock.

A person raised on Newtonian time expects all good clocks to agree once corrected for mechanical error. Relativity turns disagreement into information.

This is a major change in temperament.

Before precision clocks, timekeeping failures were mostly defects. A pendulum changed rate with temperature. A marine chronometer responded to motion, lubrication, wear and position. Clockmakers fought these problems because navigators needed a portable approximation of a common time. John Harrison’s eighteenth-century marine chronometers are usually celebrated for making longitude practical: compare local solar noon with the time kept for a reference meridian, and the difference tells you how far east or west you have traveled.

The ambition was to make a clock indifferent to the journey.

Relativity says a perfect clock cannot be indifferent to every journey, because the journey is part of what the clock measures.

There is a useful distinction here between a clock being perturbed and a clock recording a different proper time. If I heat a cheap quartz watch until its oscillator drifts, I have damaged the quality of the measurement. If two ideal clocks follow different relativistic paths and accumulate different elapsed times, neither clock is wrong. Their disagreement is the result.

The engineering problem is therefore not to force every clock to agree naturally. It is to model enough physics that we know what agreement means for the system we are building.

Satellite navigation is again the obvious case because the numbers become operational. A GPS satellite orbits at roughly 20,200 kilometers above Earth’s surface. Its atomic clock is moving relative to clocks on Earth, producing a special-relativistic rate shift that makes it run slower in the relevant coordinate comparison. It is also higher in Earth’s gravitational potential, producing a larger general-relativistic shift that makes it run faster. The net effect is on the order of 38 microseconds per day faster than a comparable clock on Earth’s geoid.

Thirty-eight microseconds sounds harmless until it is multiplied by the speed of light.

Light travels more than eleven kilometers in that interval.

A navigation system that pretended all clocks naturally shared a universal rate would become useless quickly. Instead, the system is designed around relativity. Satellite clock frequencies are offset before launch and additional corrections are applied for orbital eccentricity and other effects. The common coordinate time is engineered so users can infer position from local signal arrivals.

The extraordinary thing is not that Einstein was right. The extraordinary thing is how domesticated the result has become.

A century after time lost its universal rate, a delivery driver can complain that a phone map put the pin on the wrong side of a building.

Precision breeds ingratitude.

There is another experiment, quieter than flying atomic clocks around Earth, that shrinks the same principle to the scale of a room. In 2010, NIST researchers compared two aluminum-ion clocks and measured gravitational time dilation after raising one clock by about thirty-three centimeters. In 2022, researchers at JILA/NIST resolved the gravitational redshift across a millimeter-scale sample of ultracold strontium atoms in an optical lattice clock.

A millimeter.

The top of the atomic sample and the bottom occupy slightly different gravitational potentials. General relativity says their rates differ. The fractional shift is about one part in 10^19 across that tiny height, far beyond anything a person experiences directly, but within the reach of the instrument.

This has a peculiar consequence for the future of timekeeping. As clocks improve, knowing where a clock is becomes part of knowing what time it keeps. Height uncertainty becomes time uncertainty. Geodesy and metrology begin to merge. A sufficiently good clock can serve as a sensor of gravitational potential.

The old fantasy was that the best clock would escape place.

The new reality is that the best clock can reveal place.

That reversal reaches beyond clock laboratories. It changes the way we should think about our own duration.

People often ask whether someone living on the top floor of a skyscraper ages faster than someone in the basement. In the strict relativistic sense associated with gravitational potential, yes, by an absurdly small amount. Motion complicates any real comparison. The effect is not a health intervention and does not accumulate to anything socially meaningful in a human lifetime. But the joke conceals a fact: there is no single amount of elapsed time assigned by nature to every worldline between two broadly described moments such as “Monday morning” and “Friday evening.”

Your body is a clock only in the loose biological sense, but it is also a physical system following a worldline. The proper time along that path is yours.

This does not make time subjective. Two people can calculate the proper time accumulated along a specified worldline and agree. It makes time local in a way our grammar resists.

We say time *passes*, as if there were one fluid moving through all things.

Clocks accumulate readings.

That is a less poetic sentence and a more useful one.

In relativity, an ideal clock does not measure an invisible current called time. It traces duration along its path. If two paths differ, the durations can differ. The geometry is enough.

There is a temptation at this point to inflate the physics into a life lesson. Take your own path. Everyone has their own timeline. Stop comparing yourself to others. Relativity becomes a motivational poster.

It deserves better.

The real human implication is not that personal development is relative. It is that coordination has physical limits and local histories are irreducible.

Suppose two probes leave Earth in different directions, travel for years and later return. Their onboard clocks and records contain the history accumulated along each path. There is no master clock whose elapsed time invalidates either record. To reconstruct what happened, we need trajectories and a model of spacetime. The histories can be compared only after the paths meet again or exchange signals.

This is close to the practical problem of every distributed civilization.

Records travel. Observers occupy places. Messages arrive after delays. Institutions construct reference scales so records can be compared. When precision matters, the reference scale includes models for motion and gravity. The universe does not provide a free synchronization service.

The phrase *time dilation* contributes to confusion because it makes one clock sound distorted relative to a true clock. In special relativity, the symmetry between inertial observers is genuine. Each inertial observer can describe the other moving clock as running slow. There is no contradiction because comparing distant clocks requires a simultaneity convention, and the observers’ simultaneity slices differ. When worldlines separate and reunite, the invariant proper time along each path resolves the comparison.

The reunion matters.

Physics is full of quantities that acquire meaning at intersections. A decay occurs here. A detector records a particle there. A traveler departs and returns. Signals meet instruments. The event where two clocks sit side by side again permits direct comparison without assigning distant simultaneity.

Human life is also more local than our schedules admit.

A calendar gives a family one line: 1998, 2004, 2011, 2026. The dates coordinate records but do not contain the experienced durations between them. One person spent those years in one city, another moving through several countries, a child grew, a parent declined, a friendship went quiet. I do not mean that emotional time is relativistic proper time. Mixing those domains cheaply makes both worse. I mean that the physical theory gives us a disciplined example of something ordinary language tends to erase: a common coordinate does not replace a path.

“Twenty years passed” can be administratively exact and physically incomplete.

Even at ordinary terrestrial speeds, proper-time differences among human paths are fantastically small. Yet the conceptual structure is clean enough to resist metaphorical abuse. A timestamp tells us where an event sits in a chosen coordinate system. A clock carried through events tells us what duration accumulated along that worldline. Those are related descriptions, not identical things.

The distinction becomes severe near compact objects.

Imagine two clocks, one far from a black hole and another lowered toward it while remaining outside the event horizon. In standard descriptions, the gravitational difference in clock rates can become enormous. The local clock remains locally ordinary. A person beside it does not feel their own second stretching. The discrepancy appears when histories are compared using signals and coordinate descriptions.

This is one of relativity’s most persistent lessons: local normality can coexist with global disagreement.

You can make a room quiet enough to hear a clock tick and learn nothing directly about how its rate compares with a clock elsewhere. The clock has no sensation of being slow. It simply runs along its worldline.

A civilization made of local observers therefore needs translation.

The first translation is between clocks. The second is between coordinate systems. The third is between records made under different conditions. None of these requires a universal background time. They require theory, procedure and enough causal connection to compare results.

This should affect how we think about measurement itself.

Measurement is sometimes imagined as a passive act: the universe possesses a number, the instrument reads it. But the most precise time measurements are relationships. One oscillator is compared with another. A signal traverses a path. A frequency ratio is measured. The apparatus is part of the geometry being investigated. If a clock is carried upstairs, the difference between clocks is not contamination around the phenomenon. It is the phenomenon.

The same is true of synchronization. The signal path is not an inconvenience to be abstracted away until we reach “real” simultaneity. The finite speed and route of the signal are why the comparison has physical structure.

This is a subtle change from thinking in nouns to thinking in relations.

A clock is a noun. A clock comparison is a relation.

A timestamp is a noun. A synchronization protocol is a relation.

A moment sounds like a noun. In relativity, what we can establish physically among distant events is relational: separation, causal order, frame-dependent coordinates, proper times along paths.

The habit of reifying time is ancient and useful. We save time, waste time, spend time, make time, lose time. We speak as if a quantity had been allocated to us and could be moved between accounts. That language organizes behavior beautifully. It should not be mistaken for a theory of spacetime.

The clocks carried by Hafele and Keating did not return with philosophical essays. They returned with offsets.

The original experiment has been criticized and reanalyzed over the years, as experiments should be. Later clock comparisons, aircraft experiments, satellites and modern optical clocks have tested relativistic effects with vastly greater control and precision. The 1971 flights matter here because of their scale. They took a phenomenon associated with equations and put it in an airline cabin.

A person bought a ticket. A clock went around the world. It came home disagreeing with the clock that stayed behind.

The disagreement was not a failure to keep time.

It was timekeeping finally becoming accurate enough to notice spacetime.
