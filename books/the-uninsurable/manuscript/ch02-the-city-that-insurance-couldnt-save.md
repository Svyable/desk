# The City That Insurance Couldn't Save

At 5:12 in the morning on April 18, 1906, the ground beneath San Francisco moved. The rupture ran along the San Andreas Fault for roughly 296 miles, from the area north of San Juan Bautista up past Cape Mendocino, and the city itself sat almost directly on top of it. The shaking lasted less than a minute. What followed lasted three days. Chimneys had toppled onto stoves, gas lines had ruptured, electrical wires had come down across streets still full of splintered wood, and within minutes fires were burning in dozens of separate locations across the city at once. The fire department's chief, Dennis Sullivan, had been fatally injured when the earthquake collapsed part of the fire station where he lived, at almost the first instant of the disaster, and the water mains that firefighters needed to fight the blazes had been shattered by the same shaking. The fires burned largely unchecked for the better part of three days, and by the time they were finally contained, they had destroyed a majority of the city. Estimates of the dead have been revised sharply over the intervening century, from an original official count near 700 to modern estimates that exceed 3,000, a discrepancy that says as much about how thoroughly the city's own institutions were disrupted as it does about the disaster itself. More than 28,000 buildings were gone. Of the structures destroyed, historians generally attribute something on the order of 98 percent of the loss to fire rather than to the earthquake's direct shaking — a fact that would matter enormously, and in ways nobody in the smoking ruins that week could have anticipated, to how the disaster's cost was eventually divided.

San Francisco in 1906 was insured, in the ordinary commercial sense, about as thoroughly as any American city of its size. Homeowners and merchants carried standard fire policies, the same product sold in Chicago or Baltimore or London, written by American companies and dozens of foreign ones that had opened offices on the Pacific coast to capture a booming western market. What none of those companies had priced for, in any rigorous sense, was the possibility that an enormous fraction of an entire city's insured property might burn down on the same afternoon, for the same underlying reason, faster than any fire department on earth could contain it. Fire insurers understood conflagration risk in the abstract — great urban fires had leveled parts of Chicago in 1871 and Boston in 1872 — but the standard actuarial approach to fire insurance assumed, at bottom, that individual fires were largely independent events: a stove left burning in one building, a lantern knocked over in another, unrelated accidents scattered across a city and across a calendar. An earthquake violated that assumption instantly and completely. It did not start one fire. It started dozens, simultaneously, and disabled the water system that would ordinarily have kept them from merging into one.

The total losses from the earthquake and fire have never settled into a single, universally agreed figure, and it is worth pausing on that disagreement rather than resolving it artificially, because the disagreement is itself informative. Some accounts put total property damage at roughly 500 million 1906 dollars, on the order of 1.8 percent of the entire United States' economic output that year — an almost unthinkable share for a single city's disaster to represent in a national economy, and a useful measuring stick for how small San Francisco's fire department and water system were relative to the fire they were suddenly asked to contain. Insurance-specific figures vary depending on which historian or trade source is doing the counting: one widely cited reckoning holds that 243 insurance companies together paid out roughly 225 million dollars in claims, an amount that by itself exceeded the entire cumulative profit the American fire insurance industry had earned over the preceding 47 years combined. Another accounting puts total insured losses at roughly 235 million dollars, of which only about 180 million was ultimately paid, the gap reflecting insurers that could pay only a partial share of what they owed before running out of capital entirely. Both figures cannot be precisely correct at once, and reconciling them requires knowing exactly which losses, which companies, and which time window each historian is counting — work that, more than a century later, remains genuinely contested among the people who study it. What is not contested is the scale: whichever number is closest to the truth, San Francisco in 1906 produced a fire-insurance bill that dwarfed anything the industry had budgeted for, absorbing decades of accumulated profit in a single catastrophe.

The most immediate reason the losses were so large is the one modern actuaries would recognize instantly and insurers of 1906 were only beginning to grapple with: geographic accumulation. An insurance company writing fire policies across San Francisco had, without necessarily realizing it, taken on a portfolio of risks that were not independent of each other at all. They were bound together by geography — by the fact that they all sat within reach of the same earthquake, the same collapsed water mains, the same unstoppable firestorm moving block by block through wooden Victorian construction packed tightly along narrow streets. A company that believed it had diversified its risk by insuring thousands of separate buildings, owned by thousands of separate policyholders, engaged in thousands of separate businesses, discovered that all of that apparent diversity collapsed into a single, correlated event the moment the ground shook. This is the same failure that ruined the underwriters caught by the 1780 convoy capture off the Azores, translated from ships on an ocean to buildings on a hillside: risk that looks spread out on paper can be concentrated in reality, and the concentration is invisible right up until the day something forces it into view.

What happened next divided the insurance industry into two camps whose reputations, in some cases, never fully recovered from the choice they made that spring. Many standard fire policies of the period contained clauses excluding damage caused directly by earthquake, and the legal question of how much of any given building's destruction was properly attributable to earthquake shaking versus subsequent fire became, almost immediately, a matter of enormous financial consequence and correspondingly furious dispute. Since the overwhelming majority of the physical destruction had in fact been caused by fire rather than by the initial shaking, insurers who wanted to avoid paying claims had, in principle, a difficult argument to win — a burned building is a burned building, whatever set the first spark. Several major companies chose not to fight that argument at all. English insurers, drawing on a tradition and a market discipline built up over Lloyd's own long history of honoring difficult claims, largely paid without contesting the earthquake-versus-fire distinction. The most famous individual decision belonged to Cuthbert Heath, a leading Lloyd's underwriter and one of the market's most respected names for insuring earthquake risk at the time. Heath cabled his San Francisco agent with an instruction that has been quoted in insurance histories ever since: pay all of our policyholders in full, irrespective of the terms of their policies. It was, in the narrowest sense, bad business — Heath had no legal obligation to construe ambiguous or unfavorable policy language in his own policyholders' favor, and the earthquake ultimately cost Lloyd's more than fifty million dollars, a figure equivalent to over a billion dollars in modern terms. It was also, in a longer and more durable sense, an extraordinarily good bet: American businesses remembered which insurers had paid without argument when it mattered most, and Lloyd's standing in the American market, which had been a minor curiosity to most U.S. merchants before 1906, was transformed within a few years into something close to a byword for reliability.

Other insurers made a different calculation. Some German insurance companies, facing what they judged to be legitimate grounds for contesting claims under the earthquake exclusion, chose to fight rather than pay, calculating that American claimants would find it difficult and expensive to pursue a foreign company through its home courts. At least four German insurers, by some accounts, simply abandoned the American market altogether rather than settle their San Francisco obligations, leaving policyholders with worthless paper. One Austrian company and a number of American companies failed outright, unable to raise the capital to cover what they owed; a widely cited estimate puts the number of insurers driven into insolvency by the disaster at roughly twenty, a mix of American, German, and other foreign firms. Fireman's Fund, a San Francisco-based insurer whose own headquarters and records had been destroyed in the fire, faced an existential threat of its own: within a week of the disaster the company's estimated claims stood at roughly 11.2 million dollars against assets of only about 7 million. Rather than collapse, Fireman's Fund settled roughly 8,600 claims — taken largely on policyholders' word, since the paper records proving what had been insured had themselves burned — by paying half in cash and half in newly issued company stock, covering the resulting shortfall by assessing its own stockholders an additional 300 dollars per share. It was an improvised, almost desperate piece of financial engineering, and it worked: the company survived, eventually thrived, and outlived the crisis that nearly destroyed it by more than a century.

The disaster reshaped the industry's thinking in ways that went beyond who paid and who didn't. In the years immediately following 1906, a group of German and Swiss reinsurance companies, whose own capital had been badly strained by the California claims, formed what they called an Earthquake Commission specifically to push for a standardized clause that fire policies around the world could adopt to explicitly exclude losses caused, directly or indirectly, by earthquake — shifting the burden of proving otherwise onto the policyholder rather than the insurer. That single piece of contract language, refined and re-litigated for decades afterward, is the direct ancestor of the separate earthquake-insurance market that exists in California and other seismically active regions today, in which standard homeowners' policies exclude earthquake damage by default and buyers who want that coverage must purchase it as a distinct, separately priced product, frequently through a state-created entity rather than the open private market — an arrangement California eventually formalized through its own California Earthquake Authority, established generations later, in 1996, specifically because private insurers, having learned the lesson of 1906 and relearned it in subsequent quakes, had grown unwilling to hold concentrated seismic risk on their own balance sheets without that risk being ring-fenced and separately capitalized.

The industry's response was not limited to rewriting the language of individual policies. Before 1906, reinsurance — the practice by which an insurer that has taken on a risk it considers too large hands off a share of that risk to another company, in exchange for a share of the premium — had mostly been arranged risk by risk, or through simple proportional treaties covering ordinary, independent losses. San Francisco exposed the limits of that approach for the same reason it exposed the limits of ordinary underwriting: what insurers actually needed protection against was not any single large fire, but the accumulation of thousands of small, individually modest fire policies into one correlated citywide loss. In the years after the disaster, American insurers led by the Hartford traveled to London specifically to negotiate new reinsurance arrangements built around that problem, seeking coverage not for isolated large risks but for the kind of catastrophic accumulation that a single earthquake, storm, or citywide fire could produce across an insurer's entire book of business at once. That shift in the structure of reinsurance — from covering individual bad risks to covering correlated accumulations across a portfolio — is one of the more direct institutional legacies of 1906, and it is a thread that runs through nearly every subsequent chapter of this book: a recognition that the danger insurers most needed to manage was never the exceptional single loss, but the ordinary loss that stopped being independent of all the other ordinary losses around it. Property insurers also began writing so-called anti-concurrent-causation language into their policies in the disaster's aftermath, explicit clauses stating that if an excluded peril like earthquake combined with a covered peril like fire to produce a loss, the exclusion would govern regardless of how much the covered peril contributed — a defensive rewriting of contract language aimed squarely at preventing the next generation of "was it the shaking or the flame" disputes that had consumed San Francisco's courts for years.

It would be tidy, and it would fit the arc of this book rather too conveniently, to say that catastrophe modeling — the discipline of using computers, geology, meteorology, and engineering data to estimate in advance how a specific disaster would damage a specific portfolio of insured property — was born in the ashes of 1906. That is not quite true, and the temptation to claim it should be resisted. Formal, computerized catastrophe modeling did not exist as an industry until the 1980s, more than three-quarters of a century later, and its real founding catastrophe, the event that convinced insurers they could no longer rely on historical averages to price rare, geographically concentrated disasters, was Hurricane Andrew in 1992, which bankrupted a dozen insurers in Florida and is the subject of a later chapter in this book. What 1906 did accomplish, more modestly but no less importantly, was to demonstrate the specific failure mode that catastrophe models would eventually be built to address: that a portfolio of thousands of individually underwritten, individually priced risks can behave, in the moment that matters, as one single, undiversified risk, because the thing insurers had been treating as statistical noise — where buildings happened to be located relative to one another — was in fact the single most important variable in the entire calculation. San Francisco taught the industry, in the plainest and most expensive terms available, that geography is not a detail to be noted on a policy application. It is the hidden thread that ties an entire city's worth of supposedly independent risks together, and it stays hidden for exactly as long as the ground underneath them stays still.

Not every act of trust in the days after the earthquake ran through an insurance policy. Amadeo Giannini, an Italian-American businessman who had founded a small institution called the Bank of Italy two years earlier, reached his building on the morning of the eighteenth to find it still standing but threatened by the approaching fire. Rather than wait for the flames or for looters, he loaded the bank's gold, coin, and securities onto a horse-drawn produce wagon, hid the valuables under a layer of oranges, and hauled them out of the city to his home in San Mateo. Accounts differ on how much he actually saved that morning — some put the figure near two million dollars, others as low as eighty thousand, a discrepancy the surviving record has never fully resolved — but within days Giannini had set up a makeshift bank on a North Beach wharf, a plank laid across two barrels serving as his desk, and begun making loans to small merchants and homeowners who had no collateral left to offer and, in many cases, no identification papers to prove who they were, since those too had burned. He lent on a signature and a face, at a moment when the city's more established banks stayed shuttered behind locked vault doors still too hot to open. The loans were, by most later accounts, repaid. Giannini's improvised wharf-side bank eventually grew into what became Bank of America. His decision belongs in this story not because it was insurance — it wasn't — but because it demonstrates the alternative that exists whenever a formal risk-pooling system is either too slow or too compromised to function: personal trust, extended in the absence of any actuarial basis for extending it, filling a gap that institutions built for exactly this purpose had, in the moment, failed to fill.

The insurance companies that did try to honor their obligations faced years, not weeks, of working through what they owed. Legal historians who have studied the wave of litigation that followed 1906 — one influential account bears the title "Six Bits or Bust," a nod to how many disputes were ultimately resolved not through a court's clean verdict on the earthquake-versus-fire question but through negotiated compromise, insurers and policyholders splitting the difference on claims neither side wanted to fight to a final judgment — have documented a legal landscape in which the underlying factual question, precisely how much of a given building's destruction to attribute to shaking versus flame, was often close to unanswerable after the fact, since the physical evidence needed to settle it had usually burned along with everything else. California's insurance commissioner and the state legislature both took up the resulting mess in the years afterward, eventually pushing the industry toward the standard-form fire policy that would make the earthquake exclusion an explicit, negotiated piece of contract language rather than an ambiguity fought out case by case in front of juries who had, in many instances, lost their own homes in the same disaster they were being asked to adjudicate. The city government pursued its own, non-insurance answer to the same underlying problem: after 1906, San Francisco and other American cities revised building codes to require greater fire resistance, widened streets to slow the spread of flame from block to block, and began treating water-system redundancy as a matter of civic survival rather than routine infrastructure. Insurance had been asked to absorb a risk that, in the end, could only be partly priced; some of that risk had to be engineered out of the city instead.

There is a final, uncomfortable layer to the San Francisco story, one that historians of the disaster have long noted and that deserves inclusion here rather than tidy omission: city boosters, newspaper editors, and business leaders had every incentive to describe the catastrophe as a fire rather than an earthquake, and did so, consistently, in the weeks and months that followed. San Francisco's economic future depended on convincing outside investors, railroads, and prospective settlers that the city remained a safe place to build; a city sitting on top of a fault capable of leveling itself in under a minute was a far less attractive proposition than a city that had suffered an unlucky, ordinary fire, the kind that had already struck and been rebuilt from in Chicago and Boston. That reframing dovetailed conveniently with the insurance question, since fire losses were more straightforwardly covered under standard policies than earthquake losses were, and it is difficult, at this historical distance, to fully separate the civic motive from the financial one. The city was rebuilt within a few years, faster and more completely than almost anyone in the smoking wreckage of April 1906 would have predicted, financed in substantial part by the insurance payments — honored, contested, or defaulted upon, depending on which company had written the policy — that flowed into the hands of homeowners and merchants trying to start over. The rebuilding itself is sometimes told as an uncomplicated story of American resilience. It is more accurately told as a story about who absorbed a shock that no single actuarial table had anticipated: some insurers absorbed it and kept their word at ruinous cost to themselves; some absorbed it by going out of business entirely; and some tried, with mixed success, to avoid absorbing it at all, leaving individual policyholders to bear losses a piece of paper had promised to cover. The earthquake itself lasted less than a minute. Deciding who would actually pay for what it broke took years, and in the case of the earthquake-exclusion clauses those insurers wrote into the fine print afterward, the argument over who pays for a shaking city is still, in its essentials, being had today.
