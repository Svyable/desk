# Back Matter

## A Note on Evidence

This book rests on public reporting, regulatory filings, court records, actuarial and economics research, and the published materials of insurers, reinsurers, and the institutions that study them, rather than on anonymous sources or private access. Loss figures, settlement amounts, and market-size estimates are drawn from contemporaneous reporting, government and regulatory data (FEMA, NOAA, NAIC, the U.S. Treasury), and insurer or reinsurer disclosures where available; academic claims are drawn from the original papers rather than secondary summaries wherever the original was accessible. The detailed source trail for each chapter is kept in `research/README.md` alongside the manuscript, separated from the narrative so the chapters can read cleanly while the claims underneath them remain checkable.

A few boundaries are worth stating directly. Catastrophe loss estimates routinely vary by tens of percent depending on who is counting, what currency and inflation adjustment is used, and whether indirect economic effects are included; this book has tried to show that disagreement rather than launder it into a single tidy number. Insurance-market figures such as premium volumes, protection-gap estimates, and reinsurance pricing move from year to year and should be read as a snapshot of the period in which this book was written, not a permanent state of the market. Where a company's or program's own materials are the only available source for a claim about its own performance, that dependency is noted rather than hidden.

## Acknowledgments

This book owes its existence to the actuaries, regulators, journalists, and researchers who have spent careers making an unglamorous but essential fact legible: that risk does not disappear when it is transferred, only relocates, and someone eventually has to be able to explain where. The insurance and reinsurance trade press, the state and federal officials who publish loss and claims data even when it is politically uncomfortable to do so, and the academic economists who have spent decades studying why people misjudge rare disasters all made this book possible. None of them are responsible for how their work has been synthesized here, or for any error that synthesis may have introduced.
