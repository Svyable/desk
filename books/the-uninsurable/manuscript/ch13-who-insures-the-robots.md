# Who Insures the Robots?

On the afternoon of October 2, 2023, a woman crossing a street in downtown San Francisco was struck by a Nissan Sentra whose driver did not stop. The impact threw her into the adjacent lane, directly into the path of a Chevrolet Bolt with no one behind the wheel. The Bolt was a Cruise robotaxi, one of several hundred General Motors had licensed to carry paying passengers through the city's streets. Its software registered the collision, classified it as a side-impact event, and executed the maneuver its engineers had built for exactly that scenario: pull over. The car dragged the woman roughly twenty feet across the pavement before stopping.

Within five weeks Cruise had recalled all 950 of its vehicles, California's Department of Motor Vehicles had suspended its driverless permit outright, and the company had halted operations in every city where it ran. The technical failure was narrow and, in a sense, almost reassuring: a misclassification in the post-collision logic, not a wholesale inability to perceive pedestrians. Cruise's own account, submitted to federal regulators, was that the system had "inaccurately characterized the collision as a lateral collision" and tried to clear the road rather than hold still. The company fixed that specific line of behavior. It could not so easily fix the fact that nobody watching the car that day — not the passenger it wasn't carrying, not the pedestrian, not the city of San Francisco — had any clear sense of who was answerable when a piece of software decided, badly, what should happen next.

That is the question this chapter is about, and it turns out to be a harder one than it looks. Insurance has spent three centuries building rules for a world in which the entity that causes harm is a person, or a person operating a machine, or a company whose machine was built with an identifiable flaw. A self-driving car exists somewhere between those categories, and the industry has responded by cutting the problem into pieces that fit machinery it already understands — auto liability policies, product liability law, commercial fleet coverage — while leaving the piece that is actually new, the possibility that a trained model simply judged wrong, without any settled place to live.

Start with the two companies that get compared most often, because they have made almost opposite choices about where responsibility sits, and the contrast tells you something about how unresolved the underlying question still is.

Waymo, the Alphabet subsidiary that operates driverless taxis in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Austin, and a growing list of other cities, does not put a person behind the wheel at all in its rider-only service. There is no driver to insure, so the company insures the vehicles as a fleet and treats its automated driving system as the thing legally standing in the driver's seat. California requires autonomous ride-hailing operators to carry at least $5 million in liability coverage, and Waymo carries commercial policies well beyond that floor. When a Waymo vehicle is at fault in a collision, the company's insurance responds the way an at-fault driver's policy would; Waymo's head of risk and insurance has described the shift plainly, telling reporters that where there used to be a human being behind the wheel, "now there's a shift... the autonomous system is the driver." The company has leaned into that framing publicly, in part because it has evidence to back it up.

In December 2024, Waymo and the reinsurer Swiss Re published a joint study comparing Waymo's own liability claims against Swiss Re's baseline of more than 500,000 claims and 200 billion miles of human driving exposure. Over 25.3 million fully autonomous miles, Waymo's vehicles generated nine property-damage claims and two bodily-injury claims — eleven total, versus a human-driven benchmark of 63 property-damage and 21 bodily-injury claims over the same distance. That works out to an 88 percent reduction in property-damage claims and a 92 percent reduction in bodily-injury claims. A separate peer-reviewed analysis Waymo published in 2025, covering 56.7 million rider-only miles, found 92 percent fewer crashes involving serious injury and 82 percent fewer crashes involving any injury at all, including for pedestrians and cyclists specifically. These are Waymo's own figures, produced in partnership with an insurer that has every commercial incentive to be skeptical rather than credulous about a technology it might someday have to price, and independent researchers have generally found the methodology sound even as they note the obvious limitation: the miles are concentrated in a handful of sunny, well-mapped cities, not the full range of roads and weather a human driver contends with. Still, the direction of the numbers is not in serious dispute, and Waymo's insurance posture reflects that confidence. If the software is usually the safer driver, the company reasons, it should carry the liability of a driver.

Tesla has built its business on the opposite bet. Autopilot and its more capable successor, Full Self-Driving, are marketed and legally classified as driver-assistance systems — SAE Level 2 automation — which means a human is still, by design and by contract, the one operating the vehicle. The owner's manual tells drivers to keep their hands available and their attention on the road regardless of what the software is doing, and Tesla's insurance arrangement follows from that framing: the driver's personal auto policy covers the car whether or not any driver-assist feature is engaged, the same as it would for cruise control or lane-keeping assist on a car built by anyone else. Responsibility, in other words, has been left largely where it always sat, on the human in the seat, even as the software's role in choosing when to brake, when to turn, and when to change lanes has grown far beyond what "assistance" usually implies to a driver holding a wheel that increasingly turns itself.

That framing was tested directly in a Miami courtroom in the summer of 2025. The case involved a 2019 crash in which a Tesla Model S, with Autopilot engaged, drove through a T-intersection at highway speed and struck a parked SUV, killing 22-year-old Naibel Benavides Leon and severely injuring her boyfriend. The driver, George McGee, had dropped his phone and reached for it in the moment before impact, and admitted as much on the stand. A jury found McGee responsible for two-thirds of the fault — but assigned Tesla the remaining third, awarding $43 million in compensatory damages and $200 million in punitive damages, a total north of $240 million, on the theory that Autopilot's design and Tesla's marketing of it had contributed independently to the crash. It was the first American verdict to hold Tesla liable in a wrongful-death suit tied to Autopilot's actual operation, rather than to a defect claim about the car's hardware. Tesla has appealed, and the legal reasoning behind the split verdict will likely be argued for years. But the outcome illustrates the seam in Tesla's model: a system built and marketed to keep liability with the driver can still expose the manufacturer once a jury decides the software's own behavior, and the company's claims about it, were part of what went wrong.

The federal government has its own, more modest way of watching this unfold, and it is worth pausing on because of how little it actually settles. Since 2021, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has run a Standing General Order requiring companies to report crashes involving automated driving systems and advanced driver-assistance systems within specific windows — thirty seconds of the system being in use — and thresholds of severity, from any airbag deployment to any crash involving a vulnerable road user. The database this produces is the closest thing the country has to a comprehensive AV crash ledger, and it has been useful for spotting individual patterns, the way the Cruise incident and a string of comparable Tesla reports drew regulatory attention. But for years the order collected numerators without denominators: it counted crashes without requiring companies to report how many miles their systems had actually driven, so a reader could see that a given company reported, say, several hundred crashes in a period, with no reliable way to know whether that represented a soaring rate or a rounding error against billions of vehicle-miles. NHTSA has since proposed refinements requiring fleet size and mileage disclosures, and revised the order again in 2025 to sharpen what must be reported. Consumer advocates who commented on the proposed changes made a version of the same point from the other direction: without exposure data, the raw counts are nearly impossible to interpret responsibly, which means the single most direct public window into how these systems perform in the real world has spent most of its existence unable to answer the most basic actuarial question there is — how often, per mile driven, does this system produce a harm.

Between Waymo's model, where the company is the insured driver, and Tesla's model, where the human is, sits a wide and largely unoccupied space: insurance built specifically around the possibility that a machine-learning model produced a bad output — misclassified an object, hallucinated a fact, gave a confidently wrong recommendation — as its own distinct category of loss, independent of who was nominally in control when the output was acted on. That kind of coverage barely exists. What does exist is instructive precisely because there is so little of it.

Munich Re began underwriting something along these lines in 2018 through a product it calls aiSure, a performance-guarantee policy that pays out not because someone was negligent but because a predefined accuracy or performance threshold was breached — the AI, in effect, warranted to work as advertised, with the insurer standing behind the warranty. To offer that kind of cover, Munich Re first puts the model itself through technical due diligence, testing it against the performance claims before agreeing to insure them, which is a fundamentally different underwriting exercise than assessing a driver's history or a building's construction. A newer entrant, Armilla — backed by Lloyd's capacity through Chaucer and Axis Capital, and reinsured in part by Swiss Re — sells liability cover specifically for AI model failures: hallucinations, performance drift over time, regulatory noncompliance, up to limits around $25 million. As of the mid-2020s, one industry tally put the number of insurers worldwide writing dedicated AI-model-performance coverage, as opposed to folding AI risk quietly into existing technology-errors-and-omissions or cyber policies, at roughly five. Five, globally, for a technology already embedded in medical diagnosis, credit decisions, hiring screens, and the driving of physical vehicles through crowded streets. Munich Re and Armilla are not filling a gap so much as marking its edges; the interior of the gap — the ordinary enterprise chatbot that gives a customer wrong legal advice, the diagnostic model that misses a tumor, the driving policy trained on millions of miles that nonetheless encounters the one scenario it wasn't trained for — remains largely uninsured as a peril in its own right, wherever an existing category of policy doesn't happen to reach it by accident.

Regulators in Europe tried to build a bridge to that interior and, so far, have mostly failed. In September 2022, the European Commission proposed an AI Liability Directive designed to sit alongside the EU's broader AI Act. Its central innovation was procedural rather than substantive: it did not create new categories of AI harm, but it would have made it easier for a claimant to prove causation against an AI system's provider, through a "presumption of causality" that shifted the evidentiary burden in cases involving opaque, high-risk systems, plus a right to demand disclosure of evidence from the company that built the system. The logic was straightforward. Under ordinary tort law, a person harmed by an algorithm often cannot show what the algorithm actually did, because the model is proprietary, the training data is confidential, and the decision path through a large neural network isn't the kind of thing a plaintiff's lawyer can subpoena and read. The directive tried to correct that imbalance without rewriting liability law from scratch.

It never passed. Industry groups — a coalition of a dozen organizations including MedTech Europe among them — lobbied hard against it through 2023 and 2024, arguing that a separate, AI-specific liability regime layered on top of the AI Act's own compliance obligations would create duplicative, unpredictable exposure just as companies were still absorbing the Act itself. Their joint statement, published in late January 2025, called for outright withdrawal rather than amendment, on the theory that the directive's causation presumptions would expose ordinary software vendors to speculative litigation risk they had no reliable way to insure against or price into their contracts — an argument that, notably, took for granted the very insurability gap this chapter has been describing, and treated it as a reason to leave the liability question unresolved rather than a reason to solve it. On February 11, 2025, the European Commission's 2025 work programme formally withdrew the proposal, citing the lack of consensus among member states and stakeholders and, more broadly, a political push toward simplifying rather than expanding the EU's digital rulebook. The notice of withdrawal wasn't published in the Official Journal until October 2025, a bureaucratic lag that in its own small way captured how quietly the effort had died: a proposal built to answer one of the decade's more consequential legal questions ended not with a vote but with a line in an annual work-planning document.

What survived, and will actually take effect, is narrower but not trivial. The EU's revised Product Liability Directive — Directive (EU) 2024/2853, which entered into force in December 2024 and must be reflected in national law by December 9, 2026 — extends the definition of "product" to explicitly include standalone software and AI systems, which the older 1985 product liability regime, written for cars and toasters, had never contemplated. Under the revised directive, a manufacturer can be held strictly liable for damage caused by a defective AI system without the injured party needing to prove negligence, and the directive includes new presumptions of defectiveness and causation specifically calibrated for cases where the complexity of an AI system would otherwise make that proof practically impossible for an ordinary claimant to assemble. It also extends liability to failures to provide security updates or software patches that a reasonable manufacturer should have issued — a nod to the reality that software, unlike a toaster, keeps changing after it leaves the factory. It is real progress on paper. It is also, notably, strict product liability law rather than an insurance mechanism: it tells a court how to assign fault after something has already gone wrong, and says nothing about who is paying premiums in advance to spread that cost before the harm occurs, which is the piece the withdrawn Liability Directive and the still-thin market for AI performance insurance were each, in their different ways, trying to build.

The reason this gap persists is not that insurers haven't thought about it. It is that the actuarial machinery which makes ordinary insurance work depends on a kind of pattern that AI failure does not obviously produce. A pool of driver risk works because one driver's bad decision — running a red light, following too close — is, for the most part, independent of another driver's bad decision somewhere else in the country on the same day. Underwrite enough of those independent bad decisions and the law of large numbers turns an unpredictable individual event into a predictable aggregate cost, which is the entire trick insurance is built on. A flaw in a widely deployed model does not behave that way. If the object-detection system misjudges a specific lighting condition, or a large language model has learned a subtly wrong pattern from its training data, that flaw exists everywhere the model is deployed, all at once, waiting for the triggering condition to appear. It is closer, in its statistical shape, to a design defect that recalls every car in a fleet than to the ordinary, scattered mischance an auto insurer prices every day — and design-defect risk, historically, has always been harder and more expensive to insure than ordinary accident risk, because a single mistake doesn't cost one claim. It costs however many claims the deployment reaches.

Insurers have a name for what happens when a new kind of loss keeps arriving through the back door of policies never written to expect it: silent exposure. Cyber risk went through the same phase a decade earlier, when general liability and property policies that had never mentioned data breaches or ransomware turned out, on close reading, never to have excluded them either, and insurers found themselves paying claims for a peril they had never priced because nobody had thought to write it out of the contract. AI is repeating the pattern. A professional-indemnity policy written for an accounting firm, a public-liability policy written for a retailer, a directors-and-officers policy written for a corporate board — none of them, in most cases, say anything at all about algorithmic decision-making, which means a claim arising from an AI system's bad output can land inside coverage that was never underwritten with that risk in mind. Insurers have started responding the way they did with cyber: not by building the affirmative AI-performance products this chapter has described, which remain rare, but by writing exclusions into the policies they already sell. Standard-setting bodies filed new generative-AI exclusion language for commercial general liability policies that began taking effect in major markets in 2026, and large carriers moved quickly to adopt it. The practical effect is that the insurance industry's dominant response to the AI liability gap, so far, has not been to figure out how to price the risk. It has been to make sure it isn't holding a risk nobody priced.

There is also the simpler problem that nobody yet agrees on what "the AI was wrong" would even mean as an insurable event, separate from an existing category the claim could be filed under instead. Was the harm a product defect, litigated the way a faulty airbag would be? Professional negligence, if a licensed professional relied on the model's output? A cybersecurity failure, if the model was manipulated by an adversarial input? Ordinary auto liability, if the model in question happened to be steering a car? Each of those framings routes the claim into a body of law, and a corner of the insurance market, that already exists and already has actuaries who know how to price it. The distinct peril — that a general-purpose reasoning system, doing exactly what it was trained to do, produced a judgment that was simply, catastrophically wrong, with no component failure and no human negligence anywhere in the chain — keeps getting absorbed into categories built for something else, because building an entirely new category requires loss data that, by definition, does not exist yet for a class of machine that has only been making consequential decisions in the world for a few years.

The revised Product Liability Directive tries to anticipate at least one version of this problem by reaching up the supply chain rather than stopping at the company whose name is on the vehicle. Because a modern autonomous-driving stack is assembled from a perception system built by one vendor, a mapping and localization layer built by another, a chip built by a third, and a software update pushed by a fourth sometime after the car left the factory, the directive extends potential liability to component manufacturers and to whoever supplied the software update that changed the system's behavior post-sale — recognizing that a defect introduced after purchase, through an over-the-air patch, is not meaningfully different from a defect built in at the factory. That is a sensible response to how these systems are actually built. It is also, from an insurance perspective, a way of multiplying the number of parties who each need their own liability coverage for a single car's single decision, without making it any easier for an underwriter to say in advance which of those parties' policies will end up paying when the decision goes wrong. Waymo, notably, builds much of its sensing and software stack in-house specifically to keep that chain of responsibility short and internal rather than distributed across suppliers it would have to coordinate with after a crash; Tesla has made a similar choice for different commercial reasons. Robotaxi operators that lean more heavily on third-party lidar, mapping, or chip suppliers — Amazon's Zoox among them — inherit a longer chain of potential defendants and a correspondingly murkier insurance picture, one more party removed from the accident and one more contract's fine print away from a clear answer about who pays.

Cruise's pedestrian-dragging incident sits at exactly that seam. The company's insurance responded, presumably, the way commercial auto liability coverage responds to any at-fault collision — the injury and the immediate cause of harm were concrete enough to litigate in familiar terms. But the deeper failure, the one that got the entire fleet's operating permit pulled, was a judgment error baked into the software's decision logic, replicated across every vehicle running that code, waiting for one specific and previously unencountered situation to expose it. No policy in the market today is built to price that kind of risk on its own terms, distinct from the crash it happened to cause. Until one is, the industry will keep doing what it did after Cruise's recall and after the Miami verdict: settling each case within whatever existing category of law and coverage it can be made to fit, and leaving the harder question — who insures the judgment of the machine itself — open for the next incident to reopen.
