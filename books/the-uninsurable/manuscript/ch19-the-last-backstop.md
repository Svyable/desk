# Chapter 19 — The Last Backstop

At its peak in October 2023, Citizens Property Insurance Corporation — Florida's state-run insurer of last resort — carried 1.42 million policies, more than any private company in the state, an outcome its own founders would have considered a sign of failure rather than success. Citizens was created in 2002 explicitly as a narrow backstop: a place for homeowners who could not find coverage anywhere else, priced high enough and restricted enough that nobody would choose it over a private policy if a private policy were available. By 2023, after a wave of private insurer insolvencies and withdrawals following a string of expensive hurricane seasons and a surge of litigated claims, it had become, for over a million Florida households, not the insurer of last resort but simply the insurer. Then, over the following two years, the state ran a deliberate "depopulation" program, paying private insurers incentives to take blocks of Citizens policies back onto their own books, and the number fell hard: to roughly 840,000 by the end of 2024, and to an estimated 385,000 by the close of 2025, a decline of more than seventy percent from the October 2023 peak in a little over two years. Citizens' own actuaries reported that the exposure to a single severe hurricane season had fallen with it — the reserve surplus that would be at risk in a modeled worst-case, hundred-year storm dropped from roughly $5 billion to $3.87 billion over that stretch, and the company's own estimate of its maximum probable loss fell to just over $11 billion.

That swing — a backstop swelling to unsustainable size and then being deliberately shrunk back down — is worth holding in mind through the rest of this chapter, because it is the clearest recent illustration of an idea this book has been building toward across its final chapters: that the machinery for spreading catastrophic risk is not one mechanism but a stack of them, each layer functioning as a backstop for the layer immediately below it, and each layer having its own point past which it simply cannot absorb more. A homeowner's savings back up a modest deductible. A private insurer's capital, plus the reinsurance it buys, backs up the homeowner's policy. Capital markets — catastrophe bonds, sidecars, sponsored by companies from USAA after Hurricane Andrew onward — back up the reinsurer when its own retained risk gets too large or too concentrated to hold alone. And government programs — state-run insurers like Citizens and California's FAIR Plan, federal programs like the National Flood Insurance Program, the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act's federal reinsurance backstop, and the Price-Anderson Act's industry-and-federal pool for nuclear liability — sit above all of it, absorbing whatever the private market and the capital markets together decline to underwrite. Every layer in that stack exists because the layer below it eventually runs out of capacity, and every layer, including the government's, has a limit of its own — fiscal, political, or simply actuarial — past which it, too, has to either shrink its exposure, as Citizens just did, or find some new backstop above itself that does not yet exist.

The National Flood Insurance Program is the starkest illustration of what happens when a government backstop reaches its own limit without a clean way to shrink. Congress created the NFIP in 1968 because private insurers had largely abandoned flood coverage as fundamentally unpoolable — flood losses cluster by watershed and by storm track in a way fire losses do not, meaning a single event can hit every policyholder in a program at once rather than a scattered, independent handful — and the federal government stepped in as the insurer nobody else would be. That structure has never achieved actuarial soundness in the ordinary sense. The program currently owes roughly $22.5 billion to the US Treasury, against a statutory borrowing limit of just over $30 billion, debt accumulated mostly during a handful of catastrophic years — Katrina in 2005, Sandy in 2012, and the trio of Harvey, Irma, and Maria in 2017 — when claims arrived so far in excess of premium income that the program had no realistic path to repay what it borrowed through premiums alone. Congress has intervened directly exactly once, canceling $16 billion of that debt in 2017 to let the program keep paying Harvey and Irma claims, an act of forgiveness rather than a repayment. In February 2025 the program borrowed again for the first time since that cancellation — $2 billion, to keep pace with claims — a reminder that the debt problem was never actually solved, only reset. The NFIP's limit, in other words, is not a wall it might someday hit. It is a wall it has already hit twice, and the "backstop" behind the NFIP itself has turned out to be nothing more exotic than Congress's periodic willingness to write the debt off, which is itself a political resource that can run thin, particularly as the properties the program insures become both more numerous and more expensive to rebuild with each passing decade.

California's experience with its own FAIR Plan shows a government backstop straining in real time rather than after the fact. Conceived in the 1960s as a narrow markets of last resort for urban properties private insurers wouldn't touch, the FAIR Plan absorbed a growing share of the state's wildfire-exposed housing stock as State Farm, Allstate, Farmers, and other major carriers pulled back from that risk over the 2020s, and the January 2025 fires that burned through the Pacific Palisades and Altadena drove a fresh surge of both new FAIR Plan policies and claims against the ones already on its books, pushing the plan's own finances into a position where it needed to assess private insurers operating in the state to help cover the shortfall — a mechanism, built into the plan's original design, that essentially lets the state-run backstop pass part of its own losses back onto the private market it was created to relieve. Commissioner Lara's Sustainable Insurance Strategy, discussed in the previous chapter, is best understood as an attempt to break that loop before it tightens further: let insurers price wildfire risk using real, forward-looking catastrophe models in exchange for a binding commitment to keep writing more policies in the areas those same models flag as risky, so that the FAIR Plan's growth can be slowed rather than merely managed after the fact. Whether that bargain holds is a live question the state will be answering in real time over the rest of this decade, but the structural point stands regardless of the outcome: California's insurer of last resort has grown well past the scale its designers intended, for the same underlying reason Florida's Citizens did — a private market retreating from a risk it has concluded is no longer diversifiable at a price homeowners can pay, leaving the public backstop to absorb exactly the correlated exposure the private layer was built to avoid.

Reinsurers and the capital markets sitting above individual insurers have their own version of this strain, and Munich Re — one of the two or three companies whose balance sheet effectively underwrites much of the global insurance industry's own risk — has said so in plain language rather than leaving it to outside analysts to infer. In public commentary tied to its 2025 renewal season, the company stated flatly that the reinsurance market alone cannot absorb the systemic risk climate change now represents, and called for public-private partnerships to fill the gap it cannot fill by itself — an admission, from one of the industry's most conservative and best-capitalized players, that the backstop above the insurer is itself approaching a ceiling. The market's own pricing behavior in the years just before that statement told a related story: reinsurance rates hardened sharply after 2022, as a string of expensive years — driven by Hurricane Ian in Florida, European windstorms, and a run of secondary perils like wildfire and severe convective storms that had historically been treated as minor — pushed reinsurers to demand higher prices and tighter terms from the primary insurers who buy their coverage, only for that hard market to begin softening again at the January 2026 renewals, with brokers reporting roughly a two and a half percent decline in average prices as fresh capital, drawn by several profitable years in a row, flowed back into the market. That softening is not evidence the underlying risk has gotten smaller. It is evidence that reinsurance pricing, like any insurance pricing, moves in cycles driven as much by how much capital is chasing the business as by the physical risk itself — hardening when recent losses have scared capital away, softening when a run of profitable years draws it back in, largely independent of whether the climate trend underneath any of it has actually reversed. Insured natural catastrophe losses reached roughly $80 billion globally in just the first half of 2025 alone, the second-highest first-half total on record, even as the pricing cycle that same year began tipping toward easier terms — a reminder that the reinsurance market's mood and the world's actual accumulating risk are, at times, moving in opposite directions.

It is worth pausing on the two backstops in this stack that have not, so far, shown the same strain, because the contrast clarifies what makes a government layer durable rather than merely large. The Terrorism Risk Insurance Act, passed in 2002 after the September 11 attacks drove commercial insurers to exclude terrorism coverage entirely almost overnight, works by having the federal government absorb a defined share of losses above a industry-wide deductible once an act of terrorism is officially certified, with insurers retaining a meaningful first layer and paying the government back over time through policyholder surcharges after any triggering event. The Price-Anderson Act, older still, dating to 1957, caps the nuclear industry's total liability for any single accident and requires plant operators to pool contributions into an industry-funded second layer before any residual gap falls to the federal government, a structure that has never actually been tested by a catastrophic American nuclear accident and has functioned, for nearly seven decades, mostly as an insurance-industry-plus-government commitment that gave a fledgling nuclear power industry the coverage certainty it could not have obtained commercially at any price. Both of these backstops share a feature the more strained layers in this chapter lack: the risk they cover, however catastrophic in a single instance, is bounded and rare rather than chronically escalating. Terrorism attacks and nuclear accidents do not compound with atmospheric warming, absorb more housing stock every wildfire season, or attach to a piece of software running inside half the world's laptops. A backstop built for a bounded, if terrible, tail risk can sit quietly for decades, collecting confidence rather than losing it. A backstop built for a risk that is measurably growing every year — more homes in the wildland-urban interface, more of the economy running on the same handful of cloud platforms, more AI systems making consequential decisions with less established case law behind them — does not get that luxury, because the ground underneath it keeps shifting before the institution built to sit on it has finished settling.

The 2008 financial crisis belongs in this stack too, not as a climate or cyber case but as the sharpest historical warning about what happens when a backstop itself becomes the source of correlated risk rather than a shelter from it. AIG Financial Products had used the parent company's insurance-grade credit rating to sell hundreds of billions of dollars in credit-default swaps — instruments that functioned, economically, exactly like insurance policies against bond defaults, without being regulated as insurance or reserved against the way an actual insurer's liabilities are. When the mortgage-backed securities those swaps were written against began failing simultaneously across the entire market in 2008, AIG discovered it had, in effect, sold insurance against a single, massive, correlated event to nearly every major bank on Wall Street at once, with nowhere near the capital to pay all of them. The federal government's $182 billion rescue of AIG was not a normal insurer-of-last-resort operation like the NFIP or Citizens; it was an emergency recognition that a company wearing an insurance company's structure, without an insurance company's regulatory discipline, had become so deeply correlated with the rest of the financial system that its failure would have taken much of that system down with it. The lesson embedded in that episode — that treating something as diversified simply because it is spread across many counterparties does not make it diversified, if all those counterparties are exposed to the same underlying shock — is the same lesson the Lloyd's LMX spiral taught a different generation of underwriters a few years earlier, and it is the lesson this book's chapters on cyber aggregation and AI liability are still in the process of learning in real time.

Cyber risk stresses the stack in a different way than climate risk does, because its correlation does not respect the geographic boundaries that catastrophe models were originally built around. A hurricane, however destructive, stays inside a knowable footprint — Munich Re can model a Category 4 storm's likely path across Florida with a fair degree of confidence, because hurricanes obey physics that has been studied for a century. A single flawed software update, as this book's earlier chapters on NotPetya and the CrowdStrike outage documented, can degrade or disable computers on every continent within hours, with no geographic logic at all governing which companies get hit — only which companies happened to be running the same vendor's software. That is a fundamentally different kind of correlation than the ones catastrophe modelers spent decades learning to price, and the insurance industry's own tools for pricing it remain, by its own admission, immature. Merck's yearslong legal fight to get its NotPetya losses paid, decided in the insurer's favor only after Merck argued successfully that a "war exclusion" clause written for tank invasions had no business being applied to a self-propagating piece of malware, exposed how much of the cyber-insurance stack was still running on policy language borrowed from other risks entirely. The CrowdStrike outage of July 2024, meanwhile, demonstrated the aggregation problem in its purest form: a single vendor's error generated claims across airlines, hospitals, banks, and retailers simultaneously, all of whom may have carried entirely separate cyber policies from entirely separate insurers, each of whom had probably modeled that customer's risk in isolation, without adequately pricing the possibility that thousands of their other customers, and thousands of other insurers' customers, would all be affected by the exact same root cause on the exact same afternoon.

AI liability is the newest and least settled layer in the stack, and it is currently in the position flood risk occupied before 1968 or asbestos liability occupied before the Lloyd's crisis fully revealed itself: a risk large insurers can see coming but have not yet built durable, well-capitalized machinery to hold. Munich Re has been more forward-leaning here than most of the industry, offering a specific product line insuring against AI model underperformance since 2018, and by early 2026 a Lloyd's-backed facility, with Swiss Re among the capacity providers, had extended similar cover with limits reported as high as $25 million. But the more consequential development moving through the market in 2025 and 2026 has been defensive rather than innovative: mainstream insurers writing commercial general liability and professional errors-and-omissions policies have begun adding explicit AI exclusions at renewal, closing off the "silent AI" coverage that existed, until recently, only because older policies were written before autonomous software agents were a category anyone needed to exclude. The specialty market built to fill that gap remains, by most industry estimates, in the single-digit billions of dollars — tiny next to the scale of AI deployment already underway across the economy — while US litigation naming AI systems as a proximate cause has grown from a handful of cases in 2020 to several hundred by 2025, an increase reported at well over 900 percent over that span by firms tracking the docket. Nobody yet knows whether AI liability will settle into a well-functioning market the way marine and fire insurance eventually did, curdle into a crisis the way asbestos liability did for Lloyd's, or simply remain a gap large corporations fill with captives of the kind described in the previous chapter, because the risk is moving faster than any insurer's data can describe it.

Because it would be easy to read California and Florida as unusually mismanaged outliers rather than as the leading edge of a national pattern, it is worth noting what the industry's own regulators found when they finally looked at the whole country at once. In August 2026, the National Association of Insurance Commissioners published its first nationwide analysis of homeowners insurance market conduct data covering 2018 through 2024, compiled from the annual filings insurers are required to submit in every state. It found insurer-initiated non-renewal rates rising in every region of the country over that seven-year span, with the sharpest increases concentrated exactly where the physical risk had changed the most: non-renewals up 216 percent in the Southeast and 147 percent in the Northeast, alongside inflation-adjusted premium increases ranging from roughly 18 to 43 percent depending on region, with the Southeast's average annual premium, at $1,818 in 2024, running well above the Northeast's $1,396. The NAIC's own framing of the report was measured rather than alarmed — it described a market that remained "operationally strong overall," with broad insurer participation and improving underwriting results by 2024 — but the underlying trend line was the same one visible in Citizens' policy count and State Farm's non-renewal filings, generalized across the entire country rather than confined to its two most fire- and hurricane-exposed states. What looks, from inside California or Florida, like a series of locally mismanaged crises is, from the regulator's own nationwide vantage point, a single gradual repricing working its way through every region at a different speed, calibrated to how fast each region's actual physical risk has moved.

What distinguishes this moment from the crises the rest of this book has documented is not any single one of these strains — Florida has weathered hard hurricane markets before, the NFIP has needed debt relief before, reinsurance has hardened and softened before, and no previous chapter's disaster was really a surprise to the industry that had priced around similar risks for decades. What is different is the simultaneity. In 1992, when Hurricane Andrew bankrupted a dozen insurers and inspired the first catastrophe bonds, climate-driven property risk was under acute strain while cyber risk did not meaningfully exist as an insurance category and nuclear and terrorism risk sat in their own separate, well-established government backstops built decades earlier. In 2008, when AIG's credit-default-swap book required a federal bailout because a company wearing an insurance charter had quietly become a systemically important derivatives dealer, climate and cyber risk were not simultaneously testing every other layer of the stack at the same time. Today, California and Florida's state backstops are absorbing historically unprecedented policy volumes at the same moment the NFIP carries structural debt it has only ever escaped through congressional forgiveness, at the same moment reinsurers are publicly stating the market cannot absorb climate's systemic risk alone, at the same moment cyber aggregation risk remains inadequately modeled after two of the costliest events in the category's short history, at the same moment an entirely new risk category — AI liability — is emerging faster than any backstop, private or public, has been built to hold it. Historically, this system has adapted one layer at a time: an Andrew forces the invention of cat bonds, a 2008 forces new derivatives regulation, a Merck ruling forces insurers to rewrite exclusion language. What nobody in this book's earlier chapters had to contend with is several layers straining toward their limits in the same handful of years, with no obvious next layer above the ones already under pressure, and no historical precedent for what happens when a stack built to fail one rung at a time is instead pushed on from every rung simultaneously.

None of this means the system is about to collapse in some singular, dramatic event; that would be exactly the kind of tidy prediction the evidence in this book does not support, since every one of its layers has, so far, found some way to bend rather than break, however awkwardly. Florida's Citizens shrank rather than defaulted. The NFIP borrowed rather than stopped paying claims. Munich Re raised prices and called for partners rather than exiting the market. What the simultaneity does mean is that the slack the system has always relied on — the ability of one backstop to absorb strain while the others quietly rebuild capacity — is thinner than it has been at any point since this book's opening chapters described a coffeehouse full of merchants inventing the whole idea from scratch. The pooling machine has always worked by moving risk somewhere it could be absorbed. The open question, heading into the years this book cannot yet see past, is not whether that machine keeps working. It is whether there remains, above every backstop currently under strain, one more layer left willing and able to hold what none of the others can.
