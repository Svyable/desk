# The Insurer of Last Resort

In the first week of October 2001, the Insurance Services Office, the industry group that drafts standard policy language used across most of the American market, filed a request with insurance regulators in every state. It asked for permission to exclude terrorism from essentially all commercial property and casualty coverage, effective immediately. The World Trade Center towers had fallen three weeks earlier, and the insurance and reinsurance industry was still totaling what it had already lost — a figure that would eventually settle somewhere around $40 billion, at the time the costliest insured event in history — when it concluded, with unusual speed for an industry that normally moves in fiscal quarters, that it could not go on selling a product whose next loss might be uncorrelated with nothing at all.

That was the real diagnosis, even if nobody at ISO phrased it that way. Ordinary property insurance survives because one office building catching fire in Chicago has nothing to do with another one catching fire in Atlanta. Terrorism broke that assumption in the plainest way imaginable: a single decision, made once, by a small number of people, had destroyed two of the tallest buildings in the largest city in the country, killed nearly 3,000 people, and generated claims across dozens of lines of coverage — property, life, workers' compensation, business interruption, aviation liability — simultaneously, in the same few minutes, with no warning and no way to have priced the probability in advance. Reinsurers, who exist specifically to absorb the outsized claims that would otherwise sink a single insurer, looked at the loss and asked a question that had never seriously needed asking before 2001: what is the actuarial basis for pricing an attack that could, in principle, happen anywhere, target anything, and be timed for maximum simultaneous damage by an adversary actively trying to defeat whatever precautions the pricing assumed? There wasn't one. Without a probability distribution to price against, terrorism reinsurance simply left the market, and without reinsurance behind them, primary insurers followed ISO's lead and began stripping terrorism coverage out of new and renewing commercial policies across the country.

The economic consequences showed up fast, in places far from lower Manhattan. Developers and banks discovered that construction loans routinely required terrorism coverage as a condition of financing, and by the fall of 2002 the mortgage industry was reporting that projects representing tens of billions of dollars in commercial real estate were stalled or at risk because lenders would not close on buildings that could not get terrorism insurance at any price. A risk that had been effectively free a year earlier — priced into general property policies as an assumed, negligible add-on — had become, almost overnight, uninsurable at any price the market could offer, which is a different and more dangerous condition than being merely expensive. Congress spent more than a year arguing over what to do about it, and on November 26, 2002, passed the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act, which President Bush signed a week later.

TRIA did not have the government take over terrorism insurance, and it did not set a price. It built a backstop: a federal reinsurance program that shares losses from a certified act of terrorism with the private insurance industry, but only after an aggregate industry loss threshold is crossed and only up to a program-wide cap on total federal exposure. Insurers remain the primary underwriters, and they remain on the hook first, up to a deductible tied to their own premium volume; only once losses climb past that point does the federal share begin, and it currently begins only if the certified event's total insured losses exceed $200 million industry-wide — a threshold that has been raised over the life of the program, from $100 million to $200 million in 2015, precisely because $100 million no longer represented the kind of catastrophic, market-threatening event the program was built to backstop. The overall program cap on federal payments has sat at $100 billion since a 2015 reauthorization, a figure that has not moved even as exposure, inflation, and the value of insured commercial property in dense urban centers have all continued to climb — which means the backstop that is supposed to handle the truly catastrophic case is, by design, a fixed number written against a moving target.

The mechanics of how losses actually flow through the program are worth walking through, because they reveal how carefully Congress tried to avoid simply handing insurers a blank check. Each insurer carries its own deductible before any federal share kicks in at all, set as a percentage of that company's own earned premium in the relevant lines — originally 7 percent, raised to 20 percent by the 2007 reauthorization, meaning a bigger insurer with more premium volume has to absorb a bigger loss on its own before the government contributes anything. Above that deductible, for a moderate-sized certified event, the federal government advances money to keep insurers solvent and claims paid, but the statute then requires the government to recoup a substantial share of what it paid out, after the fact, through a surcharge levied broadly across commercial insurance policyholders — which means that for a mid-sized attack, TRIA functions less like a subsidy and more like an emergency loan that policyholders nationwide quietly repay over the following years. Only for the largest, most catastrophic losses does the government's share stop being recoupable and start being an actual absorbed cost. Congress has adjusted these dials in nearly every reauthorization — in 2005, in 2007, in 2015, and again in 2019 — nudging the insurer deductible up, the recoupment percentage up, and the program's aggregate retention up, each time shifting slightly more of the small-and-medium loss burden back onto the private market and preserving the federal backstop for the tail that private capital still cannot hold.

What TRIA actually did, in practice, was restore a market that had simply stopped functioning. Within a year of its passage, terrorism coverage was again widely available and, for most commercial policyholders, affordable, because insurers no longer had to hold capital against an unbounded, unpriceable tail risk on their own balance sheets — the federal government had agreed to hold the tail, and insurers could go back to pricing and selling the ordinary, mid-sized layer of the risk they actually knew how to underwrite. Congress has reauthorized the program five times since 2002, most recently extending it through the end of 2027, and each reauthorization fight has replayed a version of the same argument: insurers and their lobbyists warning that letting TRIA lapse would immediately reopen the 2001–2002 gap, real-estate and construction interests pointing at financing that depends on the backstop remaining in place, and a smaller number of critics arguing that two decades without a program-triggering mega-attack shows the backstop has quietly become a permanent subsidy for a risk the private market could, by now, absorb on its own. Nobody has yet been willing to test that argument by letting the program actually expire.

Britain solved a narrower version of the same problem a decade earlier, for a narrower reason, and built something that has aged into one of the odder and more durable institutions in the insurance world. On April 10, 1992, the Provisional IRA detonated a truck bomb outside the Baltic Exchange in the City of London, killing three people and causing roughly £350 million in insured losses — at the time the most expensive single terrorist attack Britain's insurance market had ever absorbed. The following year, a second and even larger IRA bomb at Bishopsgate, on the edge of the City, added roughly £1 billion more. Two attacks in eighteen months, both aimed with obvious deliberation at London's financial district, convinced insurers that commercial terrorism risk in central London was no longer a diversifiable peril they could keep pricing into ordinary commercial property policies, and coverage began disappearing from the market in exactly the pattern that would repeat in America nine years later.

The British response, formed in 1993, was Pool Re: a mutual reinsurer owned and funded by the insurance industry itself, into which member insurers cede the terrorism portion of the commercial property risk they write, pooling it across the whole industry rather than each carrier holding its own unhedgeable slice. Behind Pool Re sits a guarantee from HM Treasury — historically described as unlimited, a backstop with no stated ceiling, standing behind the mutual's own retrocession and reinsurance layers in the event of a loss large enough to exhaust them. The structure is subtly different from TRIA's: where the American program is a government cost-sharing arrangement layered on top of private insurers who keep underwriting the risk directly, Pool Re is industry-owned and industry-run, with the government positioned as reinsurer of last resort behind a pool the insurers themselves built and finance. Pool Re has spent much of the past decade quietly reducing its dependence on that Treasury guarantee rather than leaning further into it, expanding the retrocession — reinsurance for the reinsurer — that it buys from the private market; by 2025 that retrocession program had grown to roughly £2.75 billion in aggregate cover placed with more than sixty international reinsurers, up from £2.4 billion the year before, even as the Treasury's backstop, still uncapped, remained in place behind it. It is a rare case of a government-created insurance mechanism gradually handing more of the risk back to private capital rather than absorbing more of it over time — though the fact that the state guarantee remains and remains open-ended suggests the industry does not consider the underlying risk safe to hold entirely on its own even three decades on.

Pool Re's scope has also had to keep widening to match a threat that never stayed still. The pool was built to cover conventional bombs against commercial property, the specific danger of 1992 and 1993, but terrorism itself changed shape faster than the original statute anticipated. In 2018, Pool Re extended its cover to include material damage and direct business interruption caused by cyber-triggered terrorism, placing an expanded £2.9 billion retrocession program with a panel of 47 international reinsurers to support the broader scope. The following year, Parliament amended the underlying 1993 Reinsurance (Acts of Terrorism) Act to let Pool Re cover non-damage business interruption as well — the case, increasingly familiar after attacks on London Bridge and elsewhere, in which a street or a shopping district is cordoned off and businesses lose weeks of revenue without a single window being broken, a loss that a policy triggered only by physical property damage was never built to reach. Each expansion was itself a small, explicit acknowledgment that correlation in terrorism risk does not sit still inside the boundaries of whatever technology or tactic the last attack used; a backstop built for truck bombs has had to be rebuilt, piece by piece, into a backstop for whatever the next method turns out to be.

Price-Anderson's own recent history makes a related point from the opposite direction: how long a temporary-sounding fix can last once the industry it protects becomes politically load-bearing. The act's authorization has been extended repeatedly since 1957 — through the 1980s and 1990s, then for twenty years under the Energy Policy Act of 2005, out to a 2025 expiration — and in March 2024, with that deadline approaching, Congress folded a further extension into a bipartisan government funding bill, pushing the authorization out to December 31, 2065. Forty more years, attached with little public debate to a must-pass appropriations vehicle, for a liability cap that was originally sold to Congress in 1957 as a temporary measure to help a nascent industry get off the ground. Nobody seriously argues anymore that the American commercial nuclear industry is nascent. The cap has simply become one of the fixed assumptions the industry, its lenders, and its insurers all build around, in the same way the mortgage industry came to assume TRIA would always be renewed before its own deadlines arrived.

Both TRIA and Pool Re emerged from a specific, almost narrative logic: an attack, a market failure, a government response built to look temporary and calibrated to a threat that felt, at the time, contained and comprehensible. Nuclear power's insurance backstop has a different and in some ways more honest origin story, because it was built before the industry it covers had produced a single large accident to react to. The Price-Anderson Act became law in September 1957, as an amendment to the Atomic Energy Act, at a moment when American utilities were being invited by the federal government to build commercial nuclear reactors and were, to a considerable degree, refusing. The reason was insurance, or the absence of it. A 1957 Atomic Energy Commission study — the so-called WASH-740 report — modeled a worst-case reactor accident near a major population center and produced numbers stark enough to explain the industry's reluctance on their own: as many as 3,400 immediate deaths, 43,000 injuries, and roughly $7 billion in property damage across an area of some 150,000 square miles, an estimate that dwarfed anything the existing insurance market had capital to underwrite. No utility wanted to build a plant that could bankrupt the company several times over on the strength of a single accident, and no insurer wanted to write a policy against a loss of that scale for an unproven technology with no actuarial history behind it at all.

Congress's answer, sponsored by Senator Clinton Anderson of New Mexico and Representative Melvin Price of Illinois, was to build a liability structure in two tiers and cap the whole thing at a fixed number, on the theory that an industry which could see its maximum possible exposure would build reactors, and an insurance market that could see the same fixed number would agree to write the first layer of coverage. Under the original act, plant operators carried the maximum private liability insurance available — a relatively modest sum, since insurers had almost nothing to base a nuclear rate on — and the federal government indemnified the industry above that private layer, up to the statutory cap, effectively becoming reinsurer of first and only resort for a technology whose entire commercial existence in the United States was made possible by a subsidized ceiling on how much its investors and operators could ever be forced to pay for the worst thing that could happen.

The structure has been rebuilt several times since, and the current version replaces the original government indemnity with something closer to a private mutual insurance pool, not unlike Pool Re's design decades later. Every reactor licensed under the act now carries primary private liability insurance — currently $500 million per site, the maximum commercially available — and, layered above that, every one of the roughly ninety-five reactors covered by the pool is obligated to pay a retrospective premium, currently capped at just over $165 million per reactor with a possible surcharge, into a shared industry fund if any single plant anywhere in the country causes an accident large enough to exhaust its own primary coverage. The combined secondary layer, spread across the entire fleet, brings total available compensation for a single incident to roughly $16 billion — still, by design, a capped number, and one Congress has periodically had to renew, most recently extending the act's authorization out toward the middle of the century, because without the cap the entire commercial nuclear insurance arrangement the country has relied on since Eisenhower's presidency simply stops functioning. Damages beyond that ceiling would require a separate act of Congress, which the statute anticipates without specifying where the money would come from, leaving the outer edge of nuclear catastrophe risk resting, in the end, on the same kind of open political promise that stands behind Pool Re's guarantee and TRIA's program cap: a number written into law today, and a legislature's word that it will find more if the number is ever actually tested.

It is worth asking, of each program, how often the promise has actually been called in, because the answer says something about what these backstops are really for. TRIA, more than two decades after its passage, has never once resulted in an actual federal payment: no certified terrorism loss since 2002 has crossed the industry-wide threshold that would trigger the government's share, which means the entire federal exposure has so far existed purely as a contingent guarantee, never drawn on, its only visible effect being the confidence it restored to a market that would otherwise have refused to sell the coverage at all. Pool Re's experience has been busier and darker: across sixteen certified terrorist attacks since 1993, it has paid out roughly £650 million in claims, with the single largest, the 1993 Bishopsgate bombing, accounting for £262 million of that on its own, and the bulk of its total losses concentrated in a handful of attacks in the pool's first few years. Price-Anderson's own defining test came early and, relative to the worst-case numbers Congress had been shown in 1957, modestly: the partial meltdown at Three Mile Island's Unit 2 reactor in March 1979 generated claims and litigation that, by the time the last of it was resolved in 2003, had cost the industry's insurance pools roughly $71 million — a real sum, but one paid entirely out of the accident's primary insurance layer, never coming close to testing the secondary, industry-wide pool or the outer statutory cap at all.

What connects a mutual bombing-risk pool built in the ashes of the Cold War's last domestic insurgency, a federal terrorism backstop built after the deadliest single day of civilian death on American soil since the Civil War, and a nuclear liability cap built before a single American reactor had produced a large accident, is not the industry each protects. It is the shape of the risk each was built to absorb: a loss too large, too correlated, or too unprecedented for private capital to hold on its own, priced not by an actuary running historical loss data but by a legislature deciding, more or less explicitly, how much of the worst case it was willing to guarantee and how much it was willing to leave unresolved. None of the three programs eliminated the underlying risk. A large enough terrorist attack, a large enough reactor accident, would still generate losses beyond what TRIA's cap, Pool Re's retrocession, or Price-Anderson's pooled fund could fully cover, and each statute's architects knew that when they wrote the ceiling into law. What each program actually did was move the unpriceable tail of the risk off the balance sheets of insurers and operators and onto the balance sheet of the state — not because a government is better at calculating the odds of a terrorist attack or a reactor meltdown than a reinsurer is, but because a government, unlike a private insurer, does not need the risk to be diversifiable to agree to hold it. It only needs the political will to promise that it will, and to keep that promise on the one day the promise gets called in.

That is the quiet bargain sitting underneath all three programs, and underneath every insurer-of-last-resort arrangement like them: the losses they backstop were never made statistically independent, never pooled the way an actuary would want them pooled, and never priced the way an ordinary premium is priced. They were simply declared coverable, by legislatures willing to stand behind a promise that private markets had already concluded they could not keep on their own. Whether that promise holds depends less on any model than on whether the country making it, decades later, still remembers why it was made.
