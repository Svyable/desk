# Betting on a Better Trigger

In April 2016, the government of Malawi declared a national state of emergency. The rains that should have fallen across the country's growing season the previous year had largely failed, the maize crop that most of the population depends on for food had collapsed across wide stretches of the south and center of the country, and roughly 6.5 million people — nearly forty percent of Malawi's population — faced acute food insecurity by the time international aid agencies finished their assessments. Malawi's government had, in fact, planned for something like this. The previous year it had paid almost $5 million for a drought insurance policy from African Risk Capacity, a specialized mutual insurer the African Union had helped set up specifically so member governments could pay a premium in a good year and receive a fast payout in a bad one, rather than waiting months for the slow, uncertain machinery of an international disaster appeal. The policy existed precisely for a season like the one Malawi was living through.

African Risk Capacity's software, when it ran the numbers for that growing season, said no payout was warranted. Rainfall and vegetation data fed into the model — the Water Requirement Satisfaction Index, a formula that estimates crop water stress from satellite rainfall and evapotranspiration data rather than from any report filed by a farmer or a district agricultural officer — indicated that the drought had not been severe enough to cross the contractual trigger. Malawi's government, watching its own emergency unfold in real time, disagreed sharply, and after months of public argument and outside technical review, ARC eventually revised its assessment and paid out $8.1 million in November 2016 — money that arrived seven months after the state of emergency had already been declared, and against total drought-response costs the Malawian government put at around $395 million. An investigation commissioned by the aid group ActionAid later identified a specific technical flaw at the root of the dispute: Malawi's policy had been calibrated to a "long-cycle" maize variety, one bred to survive longer dry spells, but most Malawian farmers in the affected season had actually planted a shorter-cycle variety that failed under conditions the long-cycle crop, in the model, was assumed to withstand. The insurance had been sold, in effect, against a crop that wasn't the crop in the ground.

ARC disputed the ActionAid report's harsher conclusions and said it had worked closely with Malawian technical experts to build and calibrate the model in the first place. Both things can be true. The dispute was not primarily about fraud or bad faith on either side; it was a demonstration, at national scale and with real hunger riding on the outcome, of the single structural weakness that sits at the center of every parametric insurance product ever built: the payout is triggered by a measurement standing in for the loss, not by the loss itself, and a measurement is only as good as its fit to the thing it is approximating. When the fit is close, parametric insurance is faster, cheaper, and more transparent than anything the traditional claims-adjustment model can offer. When the fit is off — because the crop changed, because the weather station sat forty miles from the field that actually failed, because the index measured average rainfall across a region while the drought concentrated in one corner of it — the gap between what the formula says happened and what actually happened is called basis risk, and Malawi's 2016 crisis is one of the clearest cases on record of what basis risk costs when it goes wrong at the worst possible moment.

The idea underneath parametric insurance is old enough to predate satellites by millennia — a merchant who insures a shipment against a specific measurable event, rather than an adjuster's later judgment of what happened to the cargo, is doing a version of the same thing — but the modern form of it, priced against an objective, third-party-verified index rather than an individually assessed loss, is a product of the 1990s. In 1997, Koch Industries and Enron struck what is generally credited as the first purpose-built weather derivative: an over-the-counter swap based on heating degree days in Milwaukee for the coming winter, designed to let an energy company hedge against a warm winter's effect on natural gas demand without either party needing to prove a specific dollar loss occurred. Two years later, in 1999, the Chicago Mercantile Exchange launched the first exchange-traded weather contracts, structured around heating and cooling degree days at reference cities, giving utilities, agricultural firms, and eventually hedge funds a liquid, standardized way to bet on or hedge against temperature outcomes without any of the underlying parties needing to have suffered a verifiable loss at all — the payout is purely a function of the measured index crossing an agreed threshold, settled against public weather-station data nobody can dispute after the fact.

The weather-derivatives market those early Enron and CME contracts opened has since grown into a substantial, if still largely private and unpublicized, corner of finance. By the mid-2020s, industry estimates put the broader climate-risk-transfer derivatives market above $25 billion, with the narrower weather-derivatives segment valued around $4.6 billion in 2025 and forecast to keep growing; trading volume on CME's own weather contracts surged more than 260 percent in 2023 alone as extreme-weather years piled up. The buyers are exactly the firms one would expect once a weather outcome can be hedged like a commodity price: energy companies protecting themselves against a warm winter denting natural-gas demand or a windless summer cutting into renewable output, agricultural firms hedging a poor growing season, ski resorts and amusement parks hedging a snowless or rain-soaked one, and increasingly reinsurers themselves, using weather derivatives as a tool to lay off pieces of the same catastrophe exposure that products like CCRIF's are built to absorb on the other side of the transaction. Roughly nine-tenths of the market still trades over the counter, negotiated directly between counterparties, rather than through the standardized, exchange-listed contracts CME popularized in 1999 — a reminder that even a quarter-century on, weather risk has never fully standardized into the kind of liquid, anonymous market that trades in equities or oil.

That mechanism — pay against an index, not against an assessed loss — turned out to be exactly what a certain category of catastrophe risk had been missing, and nowhere more urgently than among small, poor, disaster-exposed governments that could not wait the months or years an ordinary claims process takes to settle. The Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility, formed in 2007 with World Bank technical support and funding from Japan, Canada, the European Union, Britain, France, and a handful of Caribbean governments themselves, became the first regional catastrophe pool in the world built entirely on parametric triggers — hurricane wind speed and central pressure, earthquake ground-shaking intensity, and later excess rainfall, each measured against a public, independently verified data feed rather than adjusted property by property after the fact. The facility's founding logic was explicit: Caribbean governments, individually, were too small and too correlated in their exposure — a single hurricane can flatten several island economies in one pass — to get affordable catastrophe coverage from the ordinary commercial market, but pooled together and paid out against an objective trigger rather than a slow claims process, the same risk became insurable at a price members could actually afford.

The facility's defining moment came just three years after its founding. On January 12, 2010, a magnitude-7.0 earthquake struck close to Port-au-Prince, killing more than 200,000 people by most estimates and levelling much of Haiti's capital. Within two weeks — a timeline that would be unthinkable for a conventional claims-adjustment process trying to inspect a shattered capital city building by building — CCRIF paid Haiti's government $7.8 million, calculated purely from the earthquake's measured ground-shaking intensity against the parameters of the policy Haiti had bought. It was not remotely enough money to rebuild a capital city, and nobody involved claimed it was; parametric sovereign insurance of this kind was never designed to cover total reconstruction costs, only to supply the kind of immediate, unencumbered liquidity a government needs in the first days after a catastrophe to keep paying salaries, clear rubble, and move emergency workers, before slower forms of aid and reconstruction financing can arrive. Haiti's government used the money for exactly that — salaries for medical personnel and rescue workers, emergency medical assistance, debris clearance, and basic civilian security — and by CCRIF's own account it was the first money the Haitian government actually had in hand after the earthquake, arriving faster than any pledge, loan, or traditional insurance claim could have.

CCRIF's own capital structure is worth a closer look, because it shows how a parametric pool actually spreads a correlated regional risk outward once the facility itself cannot absorb it alone. The facility retains only the smallest first layer of loss on its own account, historically around $20 million; above that, successive layers are reinsured by private reinsurers, and the largest layer at the top has been financed in part through the capital markets directly, via a catastrophe swap with the World Bank that mirrors the terms of an actual catastrophe bond. In 2014, the World Bank issued its first bond under a newly created capital-at-risk notes program specifically to back CCRIF's coverage — a three-year, $30 million instrument whose investors would lose principal if a qualifying Caribbean hurricane or earthquake triggered the underlying swap, exactly the mechanism a later chapter of this book examines in a different context. The point to notice here is that CCRIF's parametric trigger and its capital structure are two separate inventions doing two different jobs: the trigger solves the speed and moral-hazard problem, deciding fast and without dispute that a payout is owed, while the layered reinsurance and catastrophe-bond structure behind it solves the much older problem of finding a balance sheet large and diversified enough — global reinsurers, bond investors scattered across unrelated asset classes — to actually stand behind a correlated regional promise that no single Caribbean government, or even all of them pooled together, could underwrite from their own resources.

CCRIF's largest single payout came six years later, after Hurricane Matthew tore across the western Caribbean in the fall of 2016 and hit Haiti with catastrophic force. The facility paid a combined $23.4 million to Haiti under its tropical-cyclone and excess-rainfall policies — a single event that on its own accounted for something like 46 percent of every dollar CCRIF had paid out since its founding nine years earlier, a reminder of how concentrated catastrophe losses in a small pool of correlated island economies can be, even inside an institution built specifically to pool that correlation across borders. The facility's broader track record since 2007 runs to scores of payouts across its member governments — Anguilla, Barbados, the Bahamas, Belize, Jamaica, and others — each one processed against a public wind, seismic, or rainfall index rather than an on-the-ground damage survey, each one arriving in days or weeks rather than the months a conventional catastrophe claim typically takes to settle after a disaster of comparable scale.

African Risk Capacity, founded in 2012 under African Union auspices and structured explicitly on the same theory CCRIF had already tested in the Caribbean, extended the model from wind and earthquake risk to drought — a harder peril to index than a hurricane's wind speed, because drought unfolds slowly over a growing season rather than arriving in a single measurable event, and because its impact depends heavily on exactly which crop is in the ground, exactly when the rain failed relative to the planting calendar, and how a given community's food security depended on that specific harvest. ARC's first payouts, in the 2014-15 season, went to Mauritania, Niger, and Senegal: a combined $26.3 million paid out against a combined premium of roughly $8 million the three governments had contributed, reaching an estimated 1.3 million people and more than half a million livestock in West Africa's Sahel. Those first payouts were held up, reasonably, as proof of concept — a mechanism that let African governments pay a modest premium and receive many multiples of it back, quickly, exactly when a drought actually struck. Malawi's 2015-16 dispute, arriving in the very next season the pool operated, showed the same mechanism's other face: the model that had worked cleanly for the Sahel's rainfall and crop patterns produced a contested, delayed, and ultimately embarrassing result once it met a drought and a cropping pattern its calibration hadn't quite captured.

The tension between those two outcomes is not a flaw unique to ARC's software or to any one insurer's competence. It is the central tradeoff parametric insurance asks a buyer, public or private, to accept in exchange for speed. A conventional indemnity policy pays what an adjuster determines the actual loss to be, which is slow — sometimes agonizingly slow, when the loss is large and the adjusting process contested — but by design tracks the real loss closely, because that is the entire point of sending someone out to assess it. A parametric policy pays whatever a predetermined formula says the measured index implies the loss should be, which is fast and cheap to administer, resistant to fraud and moral hazard because neither party can influence a satellite rainfall reading or a public wind-speed measurement, and transparent in a way a negotiated claim rarely is — but it is only as accurate as the model connecting the index to the loss, and that model is built in advance, from historical data, for conditions that the actual disaster is under no obligation to replicate. Insurance researchers distinguish two directions this mismatch can run: positive basis risk, when the index triggers a payout larger than the loss actually sustained, and negative basis risk, the more dangerous version from the policyholder's perspective, when real damage occurs but the index simply never crosses the line the contract was written around — Malawi's 2016 experience in its purest form.

The industry's response to a decade and a half of this tradeoff playing out has not been to abandon the parametric model but to make the triggers themselves more granular and harder to miss. Modern parametric drought products increasingly combine multiple data streams — rainfall, satellite-measured vegetation health, soil moisture, sometimes localized crop-yield surveys — rather than relying on a single rainfall index the way early ARC and CCRIF products often did, precisely to narrow the gap between what the formula sees and what a farmer's field actually experienced. Some newer products build in graduated payout scales rather than a single binary threshold, so that a season falling just short of the old trigger still yields a partial payment rather than nothing at all, softening the sharp edge that made Malawi's dispute so damaging in the first place. None of these refinements eliminate basis risk; they narrow it, at the cost of a more complicated model that is itself harder for a farmer, a finance minister, or an aid worker to independently verify — trading one kind of opacity, the slow and sometimes arbitrary judgment of a claims adjuster, for a different kind, the technical assumptions buried inside an index formula that very few of the people relying on its output are actually equipped to audit. There is an irony worth sitting with here: parametric insurance was built, in large part, to escape the discretion and the disputes that plague conventional claims adjustment, replacing a human judgment call with an objective number nobody could argue with. Malawi's 2016 crisis shows that the argument simply relocated. Instead of a policyholder and an adjuster disagreeing over how much damage a particular field or a particular roof sustained, a government and an insurer spent months disputing whether the model itself — its choice of crop assumption, its satellite data source, its threshold — had been built correctly in the first place. The formula didn't remove the dispute. It moved the dispute upstream, from the moment of the claim to the moment the contract was written, which is arguably a better place for a dispute to happen but is not the same thing as no dispute at all.

The model built for cash-strapped sovereign governments has, over the past decade, migrated back into wealthier markets it originally borrowed its basic logic from, now aimed at ordinary businesses and homeowners rather than finance ministries. Swiss Re, one of the major reinsurers whose capital ultimately stands behind pools like CCRIF, has built a standing parametric-solutions practice selling coverage triggered by wind speed, rainfall, or earthquake shaking directly to corporate clients. Smaller, newer entrants have taken the concept further down-market: FloodFlash sells flood coverage in the United States and Britain that pays out once a sensor installed at the insured property itself records water above a contracted depth, cutting the index down from a regional weather station or satellite reading to a single physical device bolted to the customer's own wall; StormPeace offers Florida homeowners and businesses a hurricane product triggered by wind-speed and storm-surge measurements from the National Hurricane Center's own public data, rather than by a post-storm inspection queue that, after a major hurricane, can stretch on for months while an adjuster works through a backlog of thousands of claims. A homeowner in coastal Florida choosing that kind of policy is, whether the sales material says so or not, making the identical trade Haiti's government made in 2010: speed and certainty, purchased at the cost of accepting that the payout might not exactly match the loss.

What makes parametric insurance worth a place in this book's argument, rather than a footnote to it, is that it represents a genuine and mostly successful attempt to solve one specific piece of the pooling problem — the piece involving speed, cost, and the moral hazard that comes from paying out based on a report from the very party being paid — while leaving the harder piece, the one this book keeps returning to, almost entirely intact. A parametric index does nothing to make the underlying losses statistically independent of each other. If anything, the products built on top of an index are more exposed to correlated failure than an ordinary indemnity policy, not less: a wind-speed trigger set for an entire island nation, or a rainfall index covering a whole region of the Sahel, pays every policyholder under its umbrella at once when the storm or the drought arrives, with no possibility of the kind of idiosyncratic, geographically scattered loss pattern that let an old-fashioned actuary spread ordinary fire or accident risk across a large, uncorrelated pool in the first place. CCRIF and ARC do not solve correlation; they were built, explicitly, to be paid out against it, on the theory that a fast, formulaic, all-at-once payment is worth more to a government facing an all-at-once disaster than a slower, more precisely calibrated one would be, even though every policyholder in the pool is, by definition, going to need that money at the same time. That is a reasonable trade for the specific problem sovereign disaster financing has to solve. It is also a trade that keeps the underlying risk exactly where it always was — sitting on a pool of international reinsurers and donor capital who have agreed, in advance, to be the one counterparty large and diversified enough to take the correlated hit when the trigger fires, because nobody smaller ever could.
