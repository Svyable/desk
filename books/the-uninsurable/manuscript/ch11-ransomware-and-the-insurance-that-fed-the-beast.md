# Ransomware and the Insurance That Fed the Beast

Just before 5 a.m. on May 7, 2021, an employee at Colonial Pipeline's control center in Alpharetta, Georgia, found a ransom note on a computer screen along with a demand for payment in Bitcoin. Colonial operates a 5,500-mile pipeline system running from Houston to New Jersey, carrying roughly 45 percent of the fuel — gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, home heating oil — consumed on the East Coast of the United States. Within hours the company had shut the entire pipeline down, not because the ransomware had touched the pipeline's operational technology directly, but because Colonial's billing and business systems had been compromised and the company could not be confident it could safely bill customers or verify how much fuel was moving through the line. The group behind the attack, a Russia-based ransomware-as-a-service operation called DarkSide, had gained entry on April 29, more than a week before the ransom note appeared, through a single VPN account that had no multi-factor authentication enabled. The account belonged to a legacy system no employee was actively using, and its password — complex enough to look secure at a glance — turned up later in an unrelated batch of leaked credentials circulating on the dark web, evidence that someone had reused it on another account somewhere else that had, at some point, been breached. A pipeline system moving roughly 2.5 million barrels of fuel a day up the Eastern Seaboard went dark, in the end, because of one recycled password on one account nobody remembered was still switched on. Colonial's chief executive, Joseph Blount, later told the Senate that he authorized the payment of 75 bitcoin, worth about $4.4 million at the time, less than twenty-four hours after the attack began — a decision he made, he said, without much time to deliberate, given the scale of what a prolonged shutdown of nearly half the East Coast's fuel supply might mean.

What followed over the next several days looked less like a contained corporate incident than a regional emergency. Governors across the Southeast declared states of emergency; the Biden administration issued waivers of the Jones Act to let foreign-flagged tankers move fuel along the coast and eased hours-of-service rules for tanker trucks; television coverage showed long lines at gas stations and, in some cases, people filling plastic bags with gasoline, a scene that circulated widely enough to become one of the more embarrassing images of the pandemic-era supply-chain years. DarkSide itself was not a conventional criminal gang so much as a business, operating what the security industry calls a ransomware-as-a-service model: a small core group built and maintained the malware, the payment infrastructure, and the negotiation playbook, then leased those tools to a rotating roster of "affiliates" who did the actual work of breaking into victim networks, splitting the ransom with DarkSide's operators in exchange for the platform. It was, in its own dark way, a division of labor recognizable from any ordinary franchise business, which is part of why ransomware scaled as quickly as it did in 2020 and 2021: an attacker no longer needed to write functioning encryption malware, negotiate a Bitcoin payment, or run a leak site to threaten publication of stolen files, only to buy access to a platform that already did all three. Colonial restored full service on May 12, five days after the shutdown began, and a month later the Justice Department announced that the FBI had managed to claw back roughly $2.3 million of the ransom by tracing the Bitcoin to a wallet it could seize — a partial, technically impressive recovery that still left the pipeline operator and its insurers on the hook for the rest. Colonial, it turned out, carried a cyber insurance policy arranged through the broker Aon, with Lloyd's of London-linked underwriters AXA XL and Beazley among the carriers, providing at least $15 million in coverage for cyber extortion, business interruption, and incident-response costs. The ransom payment consumed something on the order of $4.5 million of that limit — almost exactly the sum Colonial had wired to DarkSide, absorbed by a policy built for precisely this purpose. DarkSide's own business did not survive the attention its most famous customer's affiliate had brought down on it: within two weeks, the group told its affiliates it had lost control of its own servers and payment infrastructure, blaming unspecified law-enforcement pressure, and DarkSide effectively dissolved, though the people behind it and the code they had built resurfaced under other names soon after, a pattern common enough in ransomware that security researchers have mostly stopped treating any single group's shutdown as a lasting victory.

That a company as consequential as Colonial Pipeline could buy a standalone insurance policy specifically to cover the cost of paying criminals is itself a fact worth sitting with, and it marks a real change from the property and casualty world examined in the previous chapter. Where NotPetya exposed insurance that was never designed to cover a cyberattack at all, ransomware gave rise to an entirely new line of business built for exactly this risk: standalone cyber insurance, sold separately from ordinary property and liability coverage, explicitly pricing the possibility that a policyholder's data would be encrypted or stolen and that recovering it might mean paying an extortionist. The market for this coverage had existed in a modest form since the early 2000s, mostly covering data-breach notification costs and privacy liability, but ransomware transformed it. U.S. insurers wrote roughly $4.1 billion in direct standalone and package cyber premiums in 2020, itself an increase of about 29 percent over the prior year, according to figures the National Association of Insurance Commissioners compiles from state cyber supplements. That growth accelerated sharply in 2021, the same year Colonial and its pipeline were making national news, with premiums in the admitted market climbing an estimated 74 percent for the year and rate increases in some quarters reportedly exceeding 130 percent for renewing accounts. Insurers were not raising prices out of caution. They were raising prices because they were losing money: the industry's aggregate direct loss ratio for standalone cyber coverage ran to roughly 72 percent in 2020 and, despite the price increases, some of the largest cyber insurers reported loss ratios above 130 percent at the peak of the ransomware wave, meaning they paid out more than a dollar in claims for every dollar collected in premium on that portion of their book.

Colonial Pipeline was not the ransom payment that most alarmed the industry that year, if only because it was not the largest. In March 2021, two months before Colonial, the insurance company CNA Financial — itself a major seller of cyber coverage, which made the irony hard to miss — was hit by a ransomware variant called Phoenix Locker, tied by researchers to the Russian-linked group known as Evil Corp. CNA reportedly paid $40 million to regain access to its own systems and recover stolen data, after negotiating the attackers down from an initial demand of $60 million, a sum that multiple people familiar with ransomware negotiations described at the time as the largest publicly known ransom payment on record. Weeks after Colonial, in late May, the meat processor JBS Foods — whose ransomware attack shut down all of its U.S. beef plants for a day and threatened a meaningful share of the country's meat supply — paid $11 million to the Russia-linked group REvil, even though the company said it had already restored most of its own operations without the attackers' help, choosing to pay anyway to guard against the possibility that stolen data would otherwise be leaked or sold. Three ransom payments, three household-name victims, all within about ten weeks of each other in the spring of 2021: CNA's insurance-funded $40 million, Colonial's insurance-funded $4.4 million, and JBS's payment, whose funding source the company did not disclose in as much detail. Together they turned a category of crime that had mostly run in the technology trade press into front-page news and, in Washington, into a policy problem that could no longer be waved off as a private matter between a company and its extortionist.

The policy problem had an uncomfortable structure, and it is one that appears nowhere else in this book in quite the same form: unlike a fire, a flood, or even a cyberattack that merely destroys data, a ransomware payment is a transaction that puts money directly into the hands of the party causing the loss, and a reasonable person could worry that insuring the payment makes the crime more attractive to commit. If ransomware gangs know that a meaningful share of their victims carry cyber insurance, and that insurers would often rather pay a six- or seven-figure ransom than absorb a much larger business-interruption loss from weeks of downtime, the economics of the whole criminal enterprise shift: it becomes more rational to target insured companies specifically, to calibrate a ransom demand to sit comfortably within a typical policy limit, and to treat the insurance industry, in effect, as a reliable payer of last resort standing behind millions of individually under-defended organizations. This is not a hypothetical raised only by outside critics. Johanna Brousse, France's top cybercrime prosecutor, told a French Senate hearing in the spring of 2021 that the message needed to reach the public plainly: "the word to get out today is that, regarding ransomware, we don't pay and we won't pay," and days later the insurer AXA announced it would stop writing new cyber policies in France that reimbursed extortion payments at all — the first major insurer to draw that line publicly, though the change applied only to new French policies and did not touch coverage for the cost of responding to and recovering from an attack.

The U.S. Treasury Department had already weighed in from a different direction. In October 2020, the Office of Foreign Assets Control issued an advisory warning that companies facilitating ransomware payments — a category the advisory named explicitly to include cyber insurance firms, along with banks and incident-response companies — could face sanctions liability if the payment ended up in the hands of a group or individual on a U.S. sanctions list, regardless of whether the payer knew who was on the other end of the transaction. Many of the most active ransomware groups by 2020 and 2021, including some tied to Evil Corp, had already been sanctioned by the time of the advisory, which meant an insurer authorizing a ransom payment on a client's behalf was now taking on a real, if often difficult to quantify, legal risk on top of the underwriting risk it had already priced. The advisory did not ban ransom payments outright, and OFAC's own guidance acknowledged that victims sometimes had few good alternatives, but it put insurers on notice that facilitating a payment was not a purely commercial decision anymore.

The insurance industry's own defense against the incentive critique is less tidy than either side of the debate likes to present it. Insurers point out, correctly, that the decision to pay a ransom is rarely theirs alone to make; it belongs to the policyholder, often advised by outside breach counsel and a specialized negotiation firm, weighing the cost of the ransom against the cost of rebuilding from backups, the risk of stolen data appearing publicly, and the operational cost of extended downtime — considerations that exist whether or not insurance is in the picture. Industry data cited in cybersecurity trade coverage suggests that somewhere between roughly 30 and 35 percent of organizations that pay a ransom do so without ever notifying an insurer at all, either because they lack a policy or because they choose to handle the incident privately, which undercuts the simplest version of the claim that insurance is what makes ransom payment possible. And there is a competing, less discussed effect pulling the other way: as cyber insurers absorbed years of ugly loss ratios, many began conditioning coverage, and even the availability of coverage at any price, on policyholders adopting specific security controls — multi-factor authentication on remote access, offline and immutable backups, endpoint detection tools, tested incident-response plans — turning underwriting questionnaires into something closer to a security audit than a formality. An applicant who could not attest to multi-factor authentication on every remote-access point, the exact control missing from the legacy VPN account that let DarkSide into Colonial's network, would by 2022 have struggled to buy meaningful cyber coverage at any price from most major carriers, or would have paid a sharply higher premium for the privilege. Insurers, in this telling, were not simply funding the ransom economy; they were also, through the blunt instrument of underwriting standards, doing more than most regulators to force basic cyber hygiene onto companies that had never previously had a reason to fix it. Whether that pressure has done more to reduce ransomware's profitability than insurance-funded payments have done to sustain it is a question nobody has settled with real data, and it is worth being honest that this book cannot settle it either.

Washington's own attempt to resolve the underlying question landed in an unsatisfying place, which may be the most honest outcome available. In April 2021, weeks before Colonial, a coalition of more than sixty organizations from industry, government, and civil society calling itself the Ransomware Task Force — convened by the Institute for Security and Technology — released a lengthy report running to dozens of specific recommendations aimed at governments and industry. The task force considered, explicitly, whether to recommend banning ransom payments outright, an idea with real appeal on paper: if paying were illegal, the argument goes, the entire business model collapses. The task force did not endorse a ban. It concluded, after internal disagreement, that criminalizing payment risked punishing victims a second time — a hospital unable to access patient records, a small city unable to run its water utility — without a guarantee that ransomware would actually decline, since well-resourced criminal groups could simply shift targets to victims outside the ban's jurisdiction or to targets whose survival was not optional. Senior Biden administration cybersecurity officials, including deputy national security adviser Anne Neuberger, said publicly around the same period that whether to pay remained "typically a private-sector decision" the federal government was reluctant to override by statute, even as the same officials pushed harder for mandatory reporting requirements so the government could at least see the scale of the problem it was not banning.

Individual states moved further than the federal government was willing to go, with mixed results. New York introduced a bill, S6806A, that would have barred not only state and local government entities but private businesses and healthcare organizations from paying ransoms at all — the most aggressive proposal of its kind in the country, and, notably, one that would have made paying a ransom illegal for the same private companies whose insurance policies were meant to cover exactly that expense. The bill did not become law in the form introduced, and by 2024 New York had instead settled on a narrower measure barring state and local government funds specifically from being used to pay ransoms, alongside a modest grant program to help local governments improve their defenses before an attack rather than fund a payout after one. Several other states floated similar restrictions in the same period, almost all aimed at government entities rather than the broader private economy that New York's original bill had targeted, reflecting a general reluctance to tell a private hospital system or a private pipeline operator, in the middle of an active crisis, that its own money could not be used to make the crisis stop.

The insurance market, left to sort out the incentive problem largely on its own, converged on a narrower and more targeted response than an outright payment ban: drawing a harder line specifically around ransomware attacks that could be attributed to nation-states, rather than around ransomware generally. Lloyd's of London, in a market bulletin issued in August 2022, required every Lloyd's syndicate writing standalone cyber coverage to include, in every policy incepting on or after March 31, 2023, an exclusion addressing state-backed cyberattacks — building on a set of four model clauses the Lloyd's Market Association had drafted the previous year, ranging from the narrowest, which preserved some coverage for nation-state attacks below a certain damage threshold, to the strictest, known as LMA5564, which excluded nation-state cyber operations from coverage entirely regardless of severity. The clause that saw the widest adoption across the market, LMA5567, retained coverage for most ordinary criminal ransomware — the DarkSides and REvils of the world, operating for profit rather than as instruments of state policy — while carving out attacks a government could plausibly attribute to another government's military or intelligence services, using the affected country's own attribution statement as the primary evidence for that determination. The logic tracked almost exactly the logic explored in the previous chapter's war exclusion litigation, arriving at a more explicit version of the same distinction the Merck and Mondelez courts had been forced to improvise after the fact: ordinary criminal loss, however large, is something a properly reserved insurance pool can still absorb one claim at a time; a loss engineered by a state actor, deployed as an instrument of geopolitical conflict rather than profit-seeking crime, is not, because a state with intelligence services and unlimited patience does not behave like an independent criminal drawing lots against a diversified portfolio of victims.

By 2023, the ugliest phase of the cyber insurance market's ransomware losses had eased, at least by the numbers insurers report to regulators. Rating agency AM Best described the U.S. cyber insurance segment as having completed its first sustained hard-market cycle and returned to underwriting profitability, a combination of sharply higher premiums, tighter underwriting standards that pushed weaker-security applicants out of the market or into much higher retentions, and — insurers and cybersecurity researchers both noted — a measurable decline in ransomware attack frequency against well-defended targets, even as total dollar losses from the attacks that did succeed kept climbing. Whether that improvement reflects a genuine reduction in the underlying risk, a temporary lull as ransomware groups adjust their own tactics, or simply a market that priced itself out of covering its worst-defended customers is not a question the loss-ratio data alone can answer. Ransomware groups, for their part, showed no sign of retiring the business model; DarkSide's dissolution weeks after Colonial did not reduce the number of active ransomware-as-a-service operations so much as scatter its personnel and code into successor brands, a resilience that should temper any claim that a single insurer's policy change or a single law-enforcement action meaningfully dents an ecosystem built to survive exactly that kind of disruption. What the whole episode leaves behind, regardless of how the numbers move next year, is a case study unlike anything else in this book's account of correlated risk: an insurance product whose payout does not merely compensate a loss after the fact but flows directly back into the hands of the party most likely to cause the next one, forcing an industry built on the premise that paying a claim ends its involvement in a risk to reckon, instead, with the possibility that paying the claim was the first step in generating another. Every other chapter in this book describes a pool that breaks because losses turn out to be correlated in ways nobody priced for — a wildfire season, a software update, a housing bubble. Ransomware is the rarer case where the pool itself may be quietly manufacturing the correlation, one settled claim at a time, and where the honest answer to whether that is actually happening remains, years into the industry's own reckoning with it, unresolved.
