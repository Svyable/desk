# The War Exclusion

At Merck's offices in Kenilworth, New Jersey, and at its manufacturing and research campuses around the world, the morning of June 27, 2017, began as an ordinary Tuesday and ended with computer screens going black. The malware that hit Merck that day had entered the world hours earlier and thousands of miles away, hidden inside a routine software update pushed out by a Ukrainian company called Linkos Group, the maker of a tax-accounting program called M.E.Doc that virtually every business operating in Ukraine was required to use. Once a machine ran the update, the malware — later named NotPetya, after a superficial resemblance to an earlier piece of ransomware called Petya — spread on its own across a network, using a stolen National Security Agency exploit called EternalBlue to jump from computer to computer without anyone clicking anything. It encrypted a machine's master boot record, displayed a ransom demand for $300 in Bitcoin, and then made the demand irrelevant, because the encryption had been designed so that no key existed that could reverse it. NotPetya was not built to make money. It was built to destroy data, and it did so with an efficiency that quickly outran the borders of the country it appears to have been aimed at.

By the end of that day, NotPetya had knocked out shipping terminals for the Danish conglomerate Maersk, which would go on to reinstall software on more than 4,000 servers and 45,000 PCs to recover and estimate its own damages at $250 to $300 million. It had crippled the TNT Express delivery network owned by FedEx and disabled systems inside the advertising giant WPP, the French construction firm Saint-Gobain, and a long list of other companies that had no obvious connection to Ukraine beyond running the accounting software of a firm doing business there. At Merck, the malware moved through the network fast enough that the company would later say roughly 40,000 computers were affected within about ninety minutes, wiping systems used for manufacturing, packaging, research, and sales worldwide. Production lines for several drugs and vaccines went dark. By that autumn, Merck disclosed it had been forced to borrow doses of its HPV vaccine, Gardasil, from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's own stockpile in order to keep meeting patient demand while its production systems were rebuilt — a pharmaceutical company large enough to be counted among the world's ten biggest, reduced for a stretch to drawing down a federal vaccine reserve because a piece of malware aimed at Ukrainian tax filings had found its way into its network. Merck's own securities filings would eventually attribute roughly $260 million in lost 2017 sales, $330 million in additional manufacturing and remediation costs, and around $150 to $200 million in residual 2018 sales impact to the attack — a total in the neighborhood of $800 million by the company's own conservative accounting, though the eventual insurance claim Merck filed would be considerably larger once it incorporated the full cost of the systems rebuild across its worldwide operations.

The group later identified as responsible, tracked by Western intelligence agencies and researchers under the name Sandworm and believed to operate as a unit of the GRU's cyberwarfare directorate, had a history with Ukraine's infrastructure by 2017. The same group was blamed for cutting power to roughly 230,000 Ukrainian customers in December 2015 by remotely opening circuit breakers at three regional electricity distributors, and for a second, more automated grid attack in December 2016. NotPetya was, by that reading, not an isolated experiment but an escalation from a group that had already demonstrated it could reach into Ukrainian critical infrastructure at will. What made NotPetya different, and what would eventually matter far more to insurers thousands of miles from Kyiv, was that this attack did not stay inside the country whose tax software carried it. Maersk's exposure came through a single office in Odessa, Ukraine, that ran the compromised accounting program; within hours the infection had traveled across the shipping company's global network to headquarters in Copenhagen, and administrators there concluded that all roughly 150 of the company's domain controllers — the servers that authenticate every user and device on a corporate network — had been destroyed. Maersk was saved from a total rebuild from scratch by an accident: a domain controller in the company's Ghana office had been offline during the attack because of a local power outage, leaving it the one uninfected copy of that data anywhere in the world. An employee flew the machine's hard drive from Ghana to Nigeria, and from there it was carried to the company's UK data center, where it became the seed from which Maersk rebuilt its entire identity infrastructure. None of that was planned disaster recovery. It was luck, arriving in the form of a blackout in Accra.

Merck did what a company that size is supposed to do when a catastrophe of uncertain origin destroys tens of thousands of computers: it turned to its property insurance. Merck carried what is known in the industry as an "all-risk" policy, a form of property coverage that, true to its name, covers essentially any cause of physical loss or damage except the specific perils the policy lists as excluded — a structure that puts the burden on the insurer to point to a specific exclusion rather than on the policyholder to prove the peril was covered. Merck's program was layered across roughly thirty different insurers and reinsurers, with about $1.75 billion in total limits sitting above a $150 million deductible. When Merck submitted its claim, most of its insurers eventually paid. A group of holdouts — at various points including Ace American Insurance Company (a Chubb subsidiary), Allianz, Liberty Mutual, and a handful of Lloyd's syndicates, together accounting for close to $700 million of the disputed coverage — refused, citing a clause buried in the boilerplate of property policies for the better part of a century: the war exclusion, sometimes called the hostile-or-warlike-action exclusion, which excludes coverage for loss or damage caused by "hostile or warlike action in time of peace or war" by "any government or sovereign power," "military, naval, or air force," or an agent of any of those.

The insurers' argument was not, on its face, absurd. Within weeks of the attack, cybersecurity researchers had traced NotPetya's likely origin to Russia's military intelligence agency, the GRU, and concluded that the malware had probably been deployed against Ukraine — then in the third year of an undeclared war with Russia over Crimea and the Donbas — with the global damage to companies like Merck and Maersk collateral rather than intended. In February 2018, the White House formally attributed the attack to the Russian military, calling it "part of the Kremlin's ongoing effort to destabilize Ukraine" and, in the same statement, "the most destructive and costly cyber-attack in history." The United Kingdom, Australia, and other allied governments issued similar statements the same week. In October 2020, the U.S. Justice Department indicted six named officers of the GRU for NotPetya and other attacks, including the earlier Ukrainian power-grid intrusions. Estimates of NotPetya's total global cost vary by who is counting and what they include — figures cited across cybersecurity and insurance-trade sources cluster around $10 billion, though individual company disclosures (Merck's roughly $800 million, Maersk's $250 to $300 million, FedEx's TNT Express unit citing a broadly comparable range) do not sum cleanly to that total, since the $10 billion figure typically folds in harder-to-verify estimates of remediation costs, lost productivity, and indirect effects across hundreds of smaller firms that never filed a public disclosure at all. If a government's military intelligence service had, in fact, launched a piece of malware as an instrument of an ongoing conflict, the insurers argued, that was hostile action by a sovereign power in a war exclusion's plainest sense, whatever the software happened to look like on a claims adjuster's desk.

Merck's response reframed the question in a way that turned out to matter more than the attribution itself: even granting that the Russian government had built and deployed NotPetya, did a war exclusion drafted decades earlier, in the language of a mid-twentieth-century property policy, actually reach a case like this one? The exclusion's language spoke of "hostile or warlike action," "armed forces," "military, naval, or air force" — language with a specific institutional history. Marine insurers at Lloyd's had separated war risk from ordinary marine risk as far back as the 1890s, and the practice spread into property, casualty, and life insurance across the first half of the twentieth century as two world wars demonstrated, in the most literal way available, what happens to a risk pool when the thing it insures against stops being independent. A single fire is a fire. A single burning building does not make its neighbors more likely to burn, so an insurer can hold a large number of ordinary fire policies and expect the losses in any given year to cluster around a predictable average. A war does not respect that structure. War destroys factories, ports, and warehouses by the hundreds in the same month, in the same country, for the same reason, and no premium base built on the assumption of independent losses can absorb a peril that guarantees the opposite. The London market formally standardized its war exclusion language in 1938, on the eve of a second global war that would have bankrupted any insurer foolish enough to have kept war risk on its own books; the exclusion survived into the postwar decades less as a moral judgment about war than as an admission that this particular category of loss broke the actuarial machine on which the rest of insurance depended, and that governments, not private insurers, would have to backstop it when it occurred. The exclusion was never meant to describe every act a government might commit with hostile intent; it was meant to carve out a specific, recognizable category of catastrophic and correlated loss — invading armies, occupying forces, aerial bombardment — that insurers had decided, as an industry, they could not price or pool. A piece of malware that infiltrated a company's network through a compromised software update, wiped its hard drives, and caused no physical invasion, no military occupation, and no exchange of fire did not obviously belong in that category merely because a state intelligence service happened to have written the code.

New Jersey Superior Court Judge Thomas J. Walsh agreed with Merck in January 2022, granting partial summary judgment on the ground that the hostile-or-warlike-action exclusion did not apply. The New Jersey Appellate Division affirmed the following year, in an opinion by Judge Heidi W. Currier that turned on ordinary tools of state contract law rather than on any judgment about the geopolitics of the Russia-Ukraine conflict. The panel held that the exclusion's language — "hostile or warlike action... in time of peace or war," committed by "any government or sovereign power," "military, naval, or air force" — carried its ordinary meaning within the context of an insurance contract, and that meaning involved the use of armed forces in an armed conflict between nations, not any state-sponsored act undertaken with hostile intent. "The exclusion does not state the policy precluded coverage for damages arising out of a government action motivated by ill will," Currier wrote, rejecting the insurers' broader reading. The court leaned heavily on New Jersey's doctrine of the reasonable expectations of the insured, under which an ambiguous or overly narrow exclusion is read in the policyholder's favor: Merck, the court found, had every right to expect that a decades-old war exclusion, written for a world of declared wars and invading armies, applied to traditional warfare and not to a cyberattack that caused no military confrontation and involved no armed forces on the ground. The court noted, pointedly, that insurers had known for years that cyberattacks by both private actors and nation-states were becoming common, and that nothing had stopped them from rewriting their war exclusions to say so in plain language, the way the industry eventually would. They had not done it before 2017, and a court was not going to rewrite the contract for them after the fact to cover a risk they had simply never bothered naming.

Not everyone found the appellate court's reasoning as clean as Merck's lawyers did. Insurance-side commentators pointed out, not unfairly, that the court's opinion leaned on a distinction between "traditional" warfare and whatever NotPetya represented without ever quite defining what made a form of warfare traditional, and that a legal rule turning on that undefined line was not obviously more predictable than the ambiguity it replaced. If a cyberattack has to look enough like tanks and airstrikes to trigger a war exclusion, insurers were left asking, what happens to the next attack that falls somewhere in between — one that disables a power grid rather than an accounting program, or one that a government claims credit for rather than merely gets blamed for by outside investigators? The Merck and Mondelez rulings answered the specific question in front of them without resolving the general one, which is a fair description of what litigation does and an uncomfortable one for an industry that needs general answers to price a book of business in advance rather than one claim at a time.

The case was headed for one more hearing, before the New Jersey Supreme Court, when Merck and its remaining eight insurers reached a confidential settlement on January 3, 2024, just days before oral argument, ending the litigation without a final ruling from the state's highest court. The settlement's terms were not disclosed, and because it arrived before the Supreme Court could weigh in, the appellate ruling remains the law only within its own limited scope — a considered opinion from an intermediate court, persuasive but not the last word an insurer in another state could expect a different court to reach on similar language. A parallel case brought by the snack-food company Mondelez International against Zurich American Insurance, arising from the same NotPetya outbreak, followed a similar arc and reached a similar dead end. NotPetya had damaged about 1,700 servers and 24,000 laptops at Mondelez, and the company sought roughly $100 million under a property policy that, like Merck's, had never been written with cyberattacks specifically in mind; Zurich invoked the same hostile-or-warlike-action language against a claim arising from an attack researchers had linked to the same Russian state actors. That case, too, reached the eve of trial — years into discovery, with a jury waiting — before the parties settled on undisclosed terms in 2022, leaving no appellate ruling behind at all. The insurance industry, in other words, spent roughly six years and two separate marquee lawsuits litigating the central legal question of who pays for a nation-state cyberattack routed through a civilian company's ordinary business software, and ended both cases at settlement, without a single definitive appellate ruling — let alone a state supreme court or federal ruling — that any insurer or policyholder anywhere else could rely on with full confidence going forward. A New Jersey appellate opinion is persuasive authority, not binding precedent, outside New Jersey's own courts; a confidential settlement establishes nothing for anyone but the two parties who signed it. For an industry whose entire pricing model depends on knowing, in advance and with precision, what its own policies actually promise, that is a genuinely uncomfortable place to have landed after six years of expensive litigation.

What the Merck litigation exposed, more than any specific legal holding, was a problem the industry had taken to calling "silent cyber": the vast body of ordinary property, casualty, and liability policies written over decades, long before insurers thought seriously about cyber risk as a distinct category, that neither explicitly covered nor explicitly excluded losses caused by a cyberattack. An all-risk property policy like Merck's had never been underwritten with any actuarial model of how often malware might wipe out forty thousand computers at once, because the people who wrote it in whatever year it first took its current form were pricing fire, flood, and physical catastrophe, not a piece of code that could travel from a Ukrainian accounting firm to a New Jersey pharmaceutical company's network in the time it takes to read this sentence. The war exclusion sitting inside that policy was drafted for a different century's catastrophe, and nobody at the insurer had gone back to ask whether its old language still meant what its drafters intended once "hostile action by a government" could mean an anonymous line of code instead of an invading division. Silent cyber existed everywhere across the property and casualty market by the time NotPetya hit — in general liability policies, in property policies, in policies covering everything from grocery chains to hospital systems — because nobody had gone through the exercise of deciding, line by line, what an old contract actually promised against a risk its authors had never imagined.

Merck's lawsuit was not what alerted the industry to the problem — Britain's Prudential Regulation Authority had already ordered Lloyd's and the broader UK insurance market, in 2019, to produce concrete plans for managing what regulators were now calling "affirmative and non-affirmative" cyber exposure, non-affirmative being the polite term for coverage nobody had decided to grant but that a court might find anyway, buried inside a decades-old form. What the litigation did was make the abstraction concrete and expensive: two of the world's most sophisticated insurance buyers, with in-house risk teams and outside coverage counsel, had each bought what they believed was comprehensive all-risk property protection, and each had to sue for years to learn whether an old sentence about "hostile or warlike action" would swallow a nine-figure claim. If Merck and Mondelez, with their resources, had to litigate the question for the better part of a decade, the exposure sitting inside the policies of far less sophisticated buyers — mid-sized manufacturers, hospital systems, universities, any organization that had never separately purchased a standalone cyber policy because it assumed its ordinary property and casualty coverage already had the risk covered — was, by definition, even less examined and even less certain.

The industry's response, which began in earnest around the time Merck filed suit and accelerated once the appellate ruling came down, was to stop being silent. Lloyd's of London required, starting with policies incepting in 2020, that every first-party property policy state explicitly whether cyber-related losses were covered or excluded, closing off the ambiguity that had let Merck's claim reach a courtroom in the first place. Standard-setting bodies for the U.S. property and casualty market followed with their own mandatory endorsements clarifying cyber coverage or its absence. And insurers rewrote their war exclusions specifically, adding language addressing "cyber operations" and state-sponsored attacks in terms no court could plausibly read as limited to armed forces and battlefields — a process examined in more detail in the chapters that follow, as the market tried to draw a defensible line between the ordinary criminal ransomware attack it was willing to underwrite and the nation-state cyber operation it was not. None of that rewriting helps Merck, or the roughly thirty insurers who spent years and, by some estimates, tens of millions of dollars in legal fees litigating a $700 million dispute that a single clearly worded sentence, added a decade earlier, could have resolved before it began. It does mean the next Merck — the next global company whose ordinary business software turns out to be a vector for a state intelligence agency's malware — will at least know, before the malware arrives, exactly what its policy promises. Whether that promise turns out to be honored will depend, as it always does in this business, on whether the loss that triggers it shows up alone or arrives correlated with everyone else's.
