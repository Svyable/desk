# When Everyone's House Burns Down at Once

At approximately 6:15 in the morning on November 8, 2018, a metal hook holding a high-voltage conductor on a 99-year-old Pacific Gas and Electric transmission tower failed above a remote stretch of the Feather River canyon near a place called Pulga, in Butte County, California. The line dropped, arced against the steel tower, and threw molten metal into the dry brush below. A PG&E employee spotted the fire and called it in within about fifteen minutes. The fire moved faster than almost anyone monitoring it that morning believed possible: within roughly ninety minutes, flames driven by strong, dry winds were already burning structures in the town of Paradise, some seven miles from where the fire had started. Paradise was home to about 26,000 people. Most fatalities occurred during the evacuation itself, as vehicles carrying residents trying to flee became trapped on the town's main arteries — Skyway, Pearson Road, Pentz Road — while the fire overtook them from multiple directions at once. By the time it was fully contained, what came to be called the Camp Fire had killed 85 people, destroyed 18,804 structures, and burned more than 153,000 acres, making it, at the time, the deadliest and most destructive wildfire in California's recorded history. Cal Fire's investigation, and a separate inquiry led by the Butte County district attorney, both concluded that PG&E's poorly maintained equipment had started the blaze. PG&E ultimately pleaded guilty to 84 counts of involuntary manslaughter and one felony count of unlawfully starting a fire, and filed for bankruptcy in January 2019, citing tens of billions of dollars in anticipated wildfire liability.

Munich Re, the German reinsurer that tracks natural catastrophe losses globally as part of its ordinary business, later estimated the Camp Fire's total damage at roughly 16.5 billion dollars, of which about 12.5 billion was covered by insurance — meaning insurers absorbed something on the order of three out of every four dollars lost. That ratio matters, because it is close to the theoretical ideal risk pooling is supposed to deliver: a catastrophic loss, spread widely enough across a large and well-capitalized industry, gets paid without destroying the industry that paid it. The Camp Fire did not bankrupt the insurance industry. It did something slower and, in its way, more consequential: it became one data point in an accumulating pattern that, over the following six years, convinced several of the country's largest home insurers that California's wildfire risk had stopped behaving the way their models assumed it would, and that continuing to write new business there, at the prices state regulators would allow, was no longer a risk worth pooling at all.

The pattern the Camp Fire confirmed, rather than created, is one that will be familiar from earlier chapters of this book in a different costume. An insurer writing homeowners' policies across California in the early 2000s could reasonably believe it was diversifying its risk: it held policies in San Diego and Sacramento and the Sierra foothills and the coastal suburbs north of Los Angeles, thousands of individually underwritten homes, spread across hundreds of miles and dozens of distinct microclimates. What that diversification obscured was a shared underlying cause that had been intensifying for decades and would keep intensifying afterward: a warming climate lengthening the fire season, decades of fire suppression policy allowing fuel to accumulate in forests that historically burned in smaller, more frequent fires, expanding residential development pushing further into fire-prone wildland areas, and aging electrical infrastructure, owned by a small number of utilities, running through exactly the dry, wind-exposed terrain where a single equipment failure could ignite a fire capable of racing for miles before any firefighting response could contain it. None of those homes were independent risks in the way the actuarial mathematics of the previous chapter requires. They were bound together by wind, drought, and vegetation, the same way San Francisco's insured buildings in 1906 had been bound together by geography and a shattered water main. The bond stayed invisible in most years, because most years did not produce the specific combination of drought, wind, and ignition source that the Camp Fire did. It stopped being invisible on November 8, 2018, and on several other California fire dates before and after it.

California's insurers had, in fact, seen a warning of this pattern once before, decades earlier, though its lessons proved easier to forget than to learn. Over the weekend of October 19 and 20, 1991, a wind-driven fire that came to be known as the Oakland firestorm, or the Tunnel Fire, swept through the hills of northern Oakland and southeastern Berkeley, destroying nearly 3,300 homes and apartment units, killing 25 people, and at its peak consuming a structure roughly every eleven seconds. Insured losses came to something on the order of 1.5 billion dollars in 1991 terms, equivalent to roughly 3 billion dollars today, and at the time it was described as the costliest urban wildfire in American history and one of the deadliest since the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire. The Oakland fire briefly reordered how California insurers thought about wildland-urban interface risk, prompting new building codes and defensible-space requirements around hillside communities. But it was, in the end, treated by much of the industry as a singular, geographically contained event rather than as the first data point in a longer trend, and the underlying conditions that had made it possible — dense residential development pushed into flammable hillside vegetation, a fire season lengthening with a warming climate, aging infrastructure running through fire-prone terrain — did not go away. They intensified for another quarter century before the Camp Fire, and the fires that followed it, made the trend impossible to treat as a one-time anomaly any longer.

The years surrounding the Camp Fire were not an isolated event but a sequence: the Tubbs Fire in October 2017 destroyed more than 5,600 structures in Sonoma and Napa counties; the Thomas Fire that December burned more than 280,000 acres in Ventura and Santa Barbara counties; the Woolsey Fire in November 2018, burning almost simultaneously with the Camp Fire, destroyed homes in Malibu and the surrounding hills. By the early 2020s, insurers operating in California were looking at a run of fire seasons whose aggregate losses had exceeded, in cumulative terms, essentially all the wildfire premium the industry had collected in California over the preceding several decades combined — a pattern with an unmistakable echo of the 1906 earthquake and fire, which had likewise erased decades of accumulated fire-insurance profit in a single season. The difference was that 1906 had been one earthquake. California's insurers were now facing what looked increasingly like a structural, recurring feature of the state's climate rather than an aberration to be absorbed and then priced past.

Two of the largest insurers in the country responded by pulling back from writing new business in California altogether, within roughly a year of each other. Allstate stopped issuing new homeowners, condominium, and commercial insurance policies in California in November 2022, while continuing to renew and service its existing California policyholders; the company said publicly that the cost of insuring new California customers had grown far higher than the price regulators would let it charge, driven by wildfire losses, rising costs of home repair and reconstruction, and increasingly expensive reinsurance — the layer of protection insurers themselves buy from reinsurers to cover their own catastrophic losses, whose price had been climbing across the entire industry as reinsurers reassessed how correlated California's wildfire risk really was. State Farm, the largest homeowners insurer in California, went further. In May 2023, State Farm General announced it would stop accepting applications for new homeowners and commercial property policies in the state entirely, citing, in almost identical language to Allstate's, historic increases in construction costs outpacing inflation, rapidly growing wildfire catastrophe exposure, and a reinsurance market that had become dramatically more expensive and less willing to absorb California risk. Existing State Farm homeowners were not dropped from coverage by that announcement, but the company had, in effect, declared that it no longer wanted more of a risk it had been writing for generations.

The insurers' complaints pointed, again and again, back to a single piece of California law: Proposition 103, passed by voter initiative in 1988, which requires the state's Department of Insurance to approve most property and casualty insurance rate changes in advance, before they take effect. Prior-approval regulation of this kind exists to protect consumers from arbitrary or excessive rate increases, and for most of its history California's system worked as intended, keeping premiums lower than they might otherwise have been. But prior approval also means an insurer cannot simply raise its own prices the moment its own claims data tells it that wildfire risk has gotten worse; it must first persuade a state regulator, through a process that could take a year or more, that a rate increase is justified — a mismatch in speed that becomes acute exactly when climate risk is changing faster than the regulatory approval cycle can track. Insurers argued that California's rate rules effectively forced them to sell wildfire coverage at prices that no longer reflected the actual risk, and that the honest response to being required to underprice a growing risk was, eventually, to stop selling it. Consumer advocates and tenant groups offered a different framing, arguing that insurers had used California's fire risk as leverage to weaken a regulatory system that had, for decades, kept premiums more affordable than they were in less-regulated states, and that industry claims about the necessity of the pullback deserved more scrutiny than insurers' own public statements provided. Both readings can be true in part: a regulatory system built for a slower-changing risk landscape and an industry unwilling to hold wildfire risk without pricing power adequate to its actual cost were pulling in the same direction, and homeowners in the state's most fire-exposed areas were the ones caught in the middle.

State Farm and Allstate were the most prominent companies to pull back, but they were not alone, and the pattern of retreat says something about how thoroughly the industry's collective assessment of California wildfire risk had shifted. Farmers Insurance, the state's second-largest home insurer, announced in July 2023 that it would cap new homeowners policies at 7,000 per month statewide and stop writing new condominium and renters policies altogether. USAA, which insures military families and veterans, announced it would sharply raise its wildfire-safety requirements for new policies, planning by early 2024 to write new homeowners coverage only for properties scoring at the very lowest end of a 32-point wildfire risk scale. By some counts, seven of the state's largest property insurers — State Farm, Allstate, Farmers, USAA, Travelers, Nationwide, and Chubb — had all imposed some combination of new-business pauses, policy caps, or sharply tightened underwriting standards within roughly a two-year span. No single company's decision caused the California insurance crisis. The fact that nearly every major insurer reached a similar conclusion at nearly the same time is itself evidence that they were responding to a shared, underlying shift in the correlated risk they all held, rather than to any one company's individual miscalculation.

California's regulatory response came, in part, through a package Commissioner Ricardo Lara called the Sustainable Insurance Strategy, finalized in stages through 2024. Its central bargain, formalized in a regulation completed in December 2024, allowed insurers for the first time to use forward-looking catastrophe models, rather than solely historical loss experience, in setting wildfire rates — a significant concession, since historical experience by definition lags a changing climate and cannot capture a risk that is actively getting worse year over year. In exchange, participating insurers had to commit to writing more policies in wildfire-distressed areas, the same areas many of them had spent the prior two years retreating from, with the explicit goal of shrinking the FAIR Plan back down from the outsized role it had grown into. The trade being struck was transparent: insurers would be allowed to price wildfire risk more accurately and more quickly, using tools that model future fire behavior under changing climate conditions rather than simply extrapolating from the past, if they agreed to take back some of the concentrated risk they had spent two years pushing into the state's insurer of last resort. Whether that bargain succeeds in shrinking the FAIR Plan's exposure, or whether insurers absorb the additional wildfire-distressed policies at prices that make the arrangement durable, is a question the 2025 fire season had barely begun to answer by the time this book was written.

What happens to a homeowner an insurer no longer wants is, in California, a question with a specific institutional answer: the California FAIR Plan. FAIR Plans exist in most U.S. states as an insurer of last resort, typically a pool jointly funded and operated by the private insurers licensed to do business in that state, offering stripped-down property coverage to homeowners who cannot find a private insurer willing to write them a policy, usually because of location-specific risk a normal insurer has declined to take on. California's version had existed since 1968, originally created in response to the withdrawal of private insurance from urban neighborhoods affected by rioting, and for decades it played a genuinely marginal role in the state's insurance market, covering a small, largely stable population of hard-to-place properties. That changed dramatically as wildfire risk intensified and private insurers retreated from it. The FAIR Plan's total exposure — the aggregate value of the property it had agreed to insure — stood at roughly 50 billion dollars in 2018, the year of the Camp Fire. By December 2023 it had grown to roughly 312 billion dollars, and by March 2025 it had reached approximately 599 billion dollars, a more than tenfold increase in under seven years. Policy counts told the same story: from about 154,000 policies in force in September 2019, the FAIR Plan grew to more than 339,000 by the end of 2023, and to roughly 574,000 by March 2025. Premium revenue collected by the plan rose from about 87 million dollars in 2018 to more than 1.4 billion dollars in 2024 — a more than fifteenfold increase, reflecting both the growing number of policies and the rising cost of covering each one.

That growth is, in the plainest possible terms, a description of risk pooling failing at the exact geographic scale where it is needed most. The FAIR Plan is not a diversified national or global insurer with the balance sheet of a Munich Re or a Swiss Re behind it, holding wildfire exposure alongside an unrelated portfolio of European windstorm risk and Japanese earthquake risk that has nothing to do with California drought conditions. It is, definitionally, a concentrated pool of exactly the properties private insurers have decided are too correlated with each other to hold safely — properties clustered, disproportionately, in the wildland-urban interface zones where fire risk is highest, meaning the plan's own portfolio is less diversified, not more, than the ordinary private market it exists to backstop. When a private insurer's California wildfire book grows too large and too concentrated to hold comfortably, that risk does not disappear. It migrates to the FAIR Plan, which has no other geography to diversify into, and which is funded, when its own reserves prove insufficient after a catastrophic season, by a special assessment levied on the very private insurers that sent the risk there in the first place — a mechanism explicitly designed to spread catastrophic losses back across the broader insurance industry, precisely because the FAIR Plan itself has no other way to absorb them.

That mechanism was tested at a scale nobody had planned for barely more than a year after State Farm's announcement. In January 2025, two wildfires — the Palisades Fire in the hills above Pacific Palisades and Malibu, and the Eaton Fire near Altadena in the San Gabriel foothills — burned through some of the most valuable residential real estate in the country, driven by hurricane-force Santa Ana winds during an unusually dry winter. Estimates of the resulting insured losses varied widely depending on the modeling firm doing the counting, in a pattern that will be familiar from the disagreements over 1906: Verisk put insured property losses, including FAIR Plan exposure, at between 28 and 35 billion dollars; Moody's RMS estimated as much as 30 billion; catastrophe modeler KCC put the figure near 28 billion; and CoreLogic's range ran from 35 to 45 billion dollars for combined residential and commercial losses. No single number commanded universal agreement, but every serious estimate placed the two fires among the costliest wildfire events in American history, in some tellings the costliest, within a period of about two weeks. The FAIR Plan alone had covered roughly 22 percent of the structures destroyed in the Palisades Fire, representing more than 4 billion dollars of its own direct exposure, and about 12 percent of the structures lost in the Eaton Fire, representing more than 775 million dollars more — concentrated almost entirely in exactly the high-value, high-risk coastal and foothill communities private insurers had been retreating from for two years.

The FAIR Plan's own reserves were not large enough to absorb losses of that size. In February 2025, the plan levied a special assessment of one billion dollars on its member insurers — the companies licensed to write property insurance anywhere in California, whether or not they had ever written a policy in Pacific Palisades — the first assessment of its kind since 1994, following the Northridge earthquake. Under California's regulatory framework, insurers hit with that assessment were permitted to recover roughly half of it directly from their own policyholders statewide, meaning homeowners in Fresno or Bakersfield or San Jose who had never faced meaningful wildfire risk themselves ended up paying a surcharge to cover losses in Malibu and Altadena, because the pooling mechanism designed to insure the state's highest-risk properties had, by construction, no other source of capital to draw on once its own reserves ran out. State Farm, still the state's largest home insurer despite having stopped writing new policies twenty months earlier, disclosed that the two fires had generated direct losses to the company on the order of 7.6 billion dollars, and separately estimated its own share of the FAIR Plan's total losses at roughly 400 million dollars on top of that. The California insurance commissioner subsequently approved an emergency rate increase for State Farm of 17 percent specifically to help the company rebuild capital depleted by the fires and the assessment together, a decision immediately controversial with consumer groups who argued regulators were now approving exactly the kind of rapid rate action insurers had spent years complaining Proposition 103 made impossible, granted only after the losses had already landed rather than before.

Every piece of that sequence — the pullback, the migration of risk to a residual pool, the pool's own concentration in the highest-risk geography, the special assessment spreading losses back across insurers and then across ordinary ratepayers statewide, and finally the emergency rate relief granted only in the crisis's immediate aftermath — is a description of risk being moved rather than eliminated, precisely because none of the parties in the chain, from the individual homeowner up through the private insurer, the state-created residual pool, and eventually the broader pool of California ratepayers, actually possessed a way to make the underlying wildfire risk smaller. The Camp Fire in 2018 demonstrated that a single utility failure, on a single morning, in a single stretch of dry canyon, could destroy an entire town faster than anyone modeling the risk had expected. The Palisades and Eaton fires seven years later demonstrated what happens once an industry has already drawn the obvious conclusion from that demonstration and repositioned itself accordingly: the risk does not vanish because private insurers decline to hold it. It moves to whatever entity is left holding the door open, and in California in January 2025, that entity turned out to be a state-created insurer of last resort whose own portfolio was, by the plain logic of who had been pushed into it, the least diversified, most correlated collection of wildfire exposure in the entire state. Pooling had not failed because anyone behaved irrationally. It failed because the independence the whole mechanism depends on had been draining out of California's wildfire risk for two decades, one dry winter and one wind-driven ignition at a time, and by January 2025 there was no longer enough of it left to go around.
