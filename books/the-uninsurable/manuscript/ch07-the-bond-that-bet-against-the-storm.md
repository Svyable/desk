# The Bond That Bet Against the Storm

On the morning of August 24, 1992, Hurricane Andrew came ashore south of Miami as a Category 5 storm with sustained winds that some instruments failed to measure because the instruments themselves broke first. It tore through Homestead and South Dade County in a matter of hours and left behind what was, at the time, the costliest insured catastrophe in American history — estimates of total damage ran to roughly $27 billion, with insured losses variously put at $15.5 billion to $17 billion depending on which contemporary tally you trust, a discrepancy that never fully resolved because Andrew hit an industry that had never before had to count damage on this scale in a single American county. Eight to eleven insurance companies, depending on how the count is drawn, were driven into insolvency outright. Others survived the claims but concluded, immediately and durably, that Florida hurricane risk was no longer something they wanted much of at any price the market had previously been charging for it. Global reinsurers, the companies that insurers themselves rely on to absorb catastrophic losses, pulled back from Florida and the Gulf Coast so sharply that capacity for U.S. hurricane reinsurance simply became scarce, not merely expensive, for the first time in living memory. An industry built to spread catastrophic loss across as many balance sheets as possible discovered that the balance sheets willing to take a slice of American hurricane risk had, almost overnight, become a much shorter list.

That capacity gap is the specific problem the catastrophe bond was invented to solve, and it's worth being precise about why traditional reinsurance couldn't simply expand to fill it. The reinsurance industry, worldwide, only has so much capital committed to underwriting catastrophic risk at any given time — capital that has to earn a return, that competes with every other use of that same capital, and that reinsurers are reluctant to concentrate too heavily in the kind of correlated, all-at-once loss a major hurricane produces. After Andrew, that capital pool simply wasn't deep enough, at prices anyone wanted to pay, to cover the accumulated hurricane exposure sitting along the U.S. coastline. The insight several academics — among them Kenneth Froot at Harvard Business School and a group of researchers connected to the Wharton School, along with the commodities-market veteran Richard Sandor — converged on in Andrew's aftermath was that the reinsurance industry's capital was a rounding error next to the world's capital markets. Global bond and equity markets held trillions of dollars; even a modest slice of that pool, if it could be persuaded to accept hurricane risk in exchange for an attractive yield, would dwarf anything traditional reinsurers could offer. The problem was structural, not just financial: how do you package a risk as strange and lumpy as "a hurricane might hit Florida" into a security an ordinary bond investor, with no particular expertise in wind engineering, could evaluate, price, and hold alongside corporate bonds and Treasury notes in a diversified portfolio.

The structure that emerged, over the following several years, worked by inverting the usual relationship between borrower and lender. An insurer or reinsurer seeking protection — the "sponsor" — sets up a special-purpose vehicle, typically domiciled somewhere like Bermuda or the Cayman Islands purely for tax and regulatory convenience, and that vehicle sells bonds to investors. The investors' principal doesn't go to the sponsor. It goes into a collateral trust, invested conservatively — originally in a mix of instruments, later almost exclusively in short-term U.S. Treasury money-market funds, for reasons the market would learn the hard way. The sponsor pays the special-purpose vehicle a premium, much as it would pay a traditional reinsurer for a reinsurance contract, and that premium plus the interest earned on the collateral gets passed through to bondholders as their coupon, typically a healthy spread over prevailing short-term rates. If no qualifying catastrophe occurs before the bond matures — usually within three to five years — investors get their full principal back plus all those coupon payments, an attractive return for taking on a risk that, crucially, has almost nothing to do with the stock market, interest rates, corporate defaults, or anything else that already lives in most institutional portfolios. If a qualifying catastrophe does occur, the collateral is used to pay the sponsor's claim instead of being returned to investors, and bondholders lose some or all of their principal. Investors are, in effect, replacing a traditional reinsurer's balance sheet with their own money, in a competitive, liquid market, and receiving a bond-like yield in exchange for exactly the risk a reinsurer would otherwise have priced and held.

Wall Street tried a cruder version of this idea first, and it is worth remembering the failure before the success, because the failure explains why the eventual structure looked the way it did. Starting in December 1992, just months after Andrew, the Chicago Board of Trade began trading catastrophe insurance futures and options, exchange-listed contracts settled against an index of industry-wide insured losses, on the theory that catastrophe risk could be hedged the same way a farmer hedges the price of wheat. It didn't work. Insurers found the contracts a poor match for their actual exposures, the index moved in ways that didn't track any individual company's real losses closely enough to be useful, and trading volume never reached the depth needed to make the market liquid; the exchange tried variations on the concept twice more over the following decade and a half, once through 2000 referencing an index from the Property Claims Service and again from 2007 to 2010 on the New York Mercantile Exchange, and each version drew the same thin interest before quietly closing. What the futures markets could not do — because a standardized, exchange-traded contract cannot easily be tailored to one company's specific book of hurricane-exposed policies — a customized, individually structured bond could. Hannover Re, the German reinsurer, brought an early version of that structure to market in 1994 and again in 1997, layering catastrophe risk from Japan, Australia, Canada, and Europe into a security some historians of the market credit as the genuine first, and AIG had floated an even earlier property-catastrophe bond concept with Merrill Lynch back in 1992 that never quite reached the same scale. Which of these deals counts as the "first" catastrophe bond depends on how strictly one defines the structure, and market historians do not fully agree; what is not in dispute is that none of the earlier attempts opened the door the way USAA's did a few months later.

USAA, the San Antonio-based insurer serving U.S. military members and their families, brought the deal that made this structure real rather than theoretical. In June 1997, USAA's Residential Reinsurance transaction — usually called Res Re — came to market seeking $150 million to protect the company against a single Category 3, 4, or 5 hurricane hitting the East Coast between Maine and Texas, covering a slice of USAA's own losses between roughly $1 billion and $1.5 billion. Demand ran so far ahead of that initial target that the deal, co-managed by Merrill Lynch and Goldman Sachs, was upsized and ultimately raised $477 million — more than three times what USAA had originally planned to sell. It was not the first attempt at a catastrophe security; a handful of smaller and more experimental deals had come before it, some of them awkward hybrids of options and reinsurance contracts that never quite found investor traction. Res Re was the first to demonstrate, at meaningful scale and with a clean, replicable structure, that institutional investors would actually show up for this trade in large numbers. Every catastrophe bond issued in the nearly three decades since has been, in its bones, a descendant of that one.

The market Res Re opened grew in fits and starts rather than in a straight line, and each surge tracked a moment when some other source of catastrophe capital had just proven unreliable. Hurricane Katrina, in 2005, did to the broader Gulf Coast reinsurance market roughly what Andrew had done to Florida thirteen years earlier, and issuance climbed accordingly — outstanding cat bond capacity, which had stood at about $4.9 billion at the end of 2005, roughly tripled to $8.5 billion within a year, then rose again to a record $7.1 billion of new issuance in 2007 alone, pushing total capacity outstanding higher still. Then came 2008, and the market discovered a risk nobody had spent much time modeling at all: not a hurricane, but a bank.

Several catastrophe bonds issued in the mid-2000s had relied on a financial structure called a total return swap to guarantee the value of their collateral, with an investment bank standing on the other side of that swap as counterparty. Four of these deals had used Lehman Brothers in that role. When Lehman collapsed in September 2008, it could no longer meet its obligations under those swaps, and the collateral backing those specific bonds — much of it, thanks to the swap arrangement, exposed to instruments whose value depended in part on Lehman's own creditworthiness — lost value for reasons that had nothing whatsoever to do with hurricanes, earthquakes, or any other insured peril. Investors who had bought catastrophe bonds explicitly because the asset class was supposed to be uncorrelated with financial markets discovered that a subset of their holdings had, through an obscure piece of financial plumbing nobody had thought to stress-test, become entangled with the single most correlated event of the decade. It is a small, technical episode inside a much larger financial crisis, but it belongs in any honest account of catastrophe bonds because it captures, in miniature, this book's whole argument: a pool of investors had been assured their risk was independent of everything else in their portfolio, and it was independent of hurricanes and earthquakes right up until a piece of financial architecture nobody scrutinized closely enough turned out to correlate it with a bank they had never underwritten and had no reason to think about. New cat bond issuance essentially stopped between September 2008 and January 2009 while the market absorbed the lesson.

The fix, adopted almost universally afterward, was to strip the swap counterparty out of the structure and put collateral directly into Treasury money-market funds — securities about as close to risk-free and as close to genuinely independent of the insurance cycle as exists in modern finance. Issuance resumed within months, restarted by roughly $1.6 billion of new deals in the final quarter of 2009, and the market's subsequent growth suggests investors accepted the fix and mostly moved on: a persistent low-interest-rate environment through much of the following decade made a catastrophe bond's spread over Treasuries look considerably more attractive to yield-starved pension funds and endowments than it might have in a higher-rate world, and outstanding capacity climbed past $20 billion by the mid-2010s and past $26 billion by the end of 2017.

That year, 2017, delivered the market's first real test of what happens when several catastrophes arrive close together rather than one at a time — Hurricanes Harvey, Irma, and Maria all making landfall within about a month of each other, an unusually clustered season that produced tens of billions of dollars in combined insured losses. A number of cat bonds triggered and paid out that year, and the market, while it took losses, did not collapse or freeze the way it had after Lehman; sponsors got the protection they'd paid for, investors on the triggered tranches lost money in a way the prospectus had told them plainly they might, and the broader market kept functioning. Five years later, Hurricane Ian delivered a similar test in a single storm rather than a cluster, with estimated insured losses of $50 billion to $65 billion, and a number of bonds — particularly ones with indemnity triggers tied closely to specific Florida sponsors' actual claims experience — took real losses. In the same period, some private ILS fund investors, spooked by Ian's size and by how much retrocession capital it had left tied up in loss reserves, pulled back from the market just as insurers most needed fresh capacity, tightening supply right when demand was highest — a pattern with an uncomfortable resemblance to what conventional reinsurance capital had done to Florida after Andrew thirty years earlier, in a market that had been explicitly built to behave differently.

The trigger mechanism a given bond uses turns out to matter enormously for how cleanly and how fast it pays, and the market has never settled on one best answer because each design trades one problem for another. An indemnity trigger pays based on the sponsor's own actual, audited losses, which means the sponsor is fully hedged — precisely the protection it wanted — but investors have to trust the sponsor's own loss adjustment and accept that a payout might not be finalized for months or years after the storm, since real claims take that long to settle. A parametric trigger instead pays automatically once an independently measured physical parameter — a hurricane's central pressure and wind speed at landfall, an earthquake's magnitude and depth at a specific location — crosses a predetermined threshold, with no reference to anyone's actual claims at all; this makes payment fast, often within weeks, and eliminates any dispute about loss adjustment, but it introduces what the industry calls basis risk, the possibility that a sponsor suffers enormous real losses from a storm that technically falls just short of the trigger's threshold, or, less often, collects a payout from a storm that did the sponsor little actual damage. Industry-loss-index triggers split the difference, paying based on an independent estimate of total industry-wide losses from an event rather than the sponsor's own book, offering more transparency than an indemnity trigger and less basis risk than a pure parametric one, at the cost of relying on a third-party modeling firm's estimate rather than a physical measurement or an audited claim. None of these designs is simply better than the others. Each is a different answer to the same underlying question a pooling mechanism always has to answer: how do you decide, cleanly and in advance, whose bad luck counts.

A specialized class of investment manager grew up alongside the bonds themselves, translating catastrophe risk into something an ordinary pension fund trustee could allocate money toward without personally learning how to read a hurricane model. Nephila Capital, launched in 1998 by Frank Majors and Greg Hagood as an offshoot of the insurance broker Willis, became the first investment manager built specifically around catastrophe reinsurance and weather risk, and for most of its history it was also the largest, at times managing multiple billions of dollars across catastrophe bonds, private reinsurance contracts, and weather derivatives on behalf of pension funds and endowments that had no independent capacity to underwrite a hurricane themselves. Markel Corporation, the insurance holding company, bought the firm outright in 2018. Other specialized managers followed the same template, and their collective growth is a large part of why the phrase "alternative capital" became a standard line item in reinsurance-industry reports: capital markets money, managed by firms that speak fluent catastrophe modeling, sitting alongside traditional reinsurer balance sheets in the same risk-transfer towers, occasionally more willing to take on risk than the traditional players and occasionally, as investors learned after Ian, considerably less willing.

The scale the market eventually reached would have astonished the underwriters who structured Res Re in 1997. By 2024, outstanding catastrophe bond capacity — tracked closely by the trade publication Artemis, which has become something like the market's system of record — reached roughly $49.5 billion, on record annual issuance of $17.7 billion that year alone. It kept climbing through 2025, breaking $50 billion for the first time and finishing the year with outstanding capacity above $61 billion on record annual issuance north of $25 billion, spread across more than a hundred separate transactions in a single year for the first time in the market's history. Sponsors by then ranged far beyond U.S. hurricane risk: the World Bank has issued catastrophe bonds on behalf of Mexico's national disaster fund covering earthquake and named-storm risk, one of which paid out $150 million within weeks after a magnitude-7.1 earthquake struck near Mexico City in September 2017; the California Earthquake Authority has become one of the market's largest and most consistent sponsors, with billions of dollars of outstanding earthquake-linked bonds protecting more than a million California policyholders; and both Swiss Re and Munich Re, the reinsurance giants profiled elsewhere in this book, now run their own substantial cat bond issuance and investment operations alongside their traditional treaty business, treating the capital markets less as a threat to their franchise than as one more tool for laying off risk they don't want to hold themselves.

The returns on offer, especially in the years immediately after Ian reset pricing across the reinsurance market, have been remarkable by the standards of a supposedly boring, low-drama asset class. The widely watched Swiss Re Global Cat Bond Index posted a total return of nearly 20 percent in 2023 and 17.29 percent in 2024 — the two highest annual returns in the index's history — and cat bond fund strategies tracked separately by the data provider Plenum averaged more than 13 percent for 2024 alone, more than double the previous record set back in 2013. A Swiss pension fund, Nest, reported that its catastrophe bond and broader insurance-linked-securities allocation was one of its best-performing asset classes of 2024, delivering something on the order of 20 to 22 percent. None of that return exists because catastrophe bonds became less risky. It exists because reinsurance pricing across the market spiked so sharply after Ian and the difficult 2023 renewal that even bonds paying a healthy spread over Treasuries were, for a couple of years, underpriced relative to what a traditional reinsurer would have charged for the same coverage — a gap arbitrage-minded capital rushed in to close, which is precisely why cat bond pricing then began softening again through 2025 and into 2026, in step with the broader reinsurance market described elsewhere in this book.

Catastrophe bonds are the most visible piece of what the industry lumps together as "alternative capital" or "insurance-linked securities," but they are not the only shape that capital takes, and the differences matter for how quickly the money can move. A cat bond is a tradable security with a public or semi-public offering document, a rating in some cases, and a secondary market where investors can sell their position to someone else before maturity if they want out. A reinsurance sidecar, by contrast, is a private, often single-purpose vehicle set up by a specific reinsurer to let outside investors take a direct quota share of that reinsurer's own book of business for a single underwriting year or a short defined period, with no public trading and no ability to exit early — money locked in until the deal's term ends and its results are known. Collateralized reinsurance, a third variant, is closer still to a traditional bilateral reinsurance contract, just funded upfront with investor cash sitting in trust rather than backed by a rated reinsurer's general balance sheet. All three exist for the same underlying reason, funneling capital-markets money toward catastrophe risk that reinsurers alone won't fully absorb, and all three can suffer the same affliction after a bad year: money that was supposed to be available to pay claims and then roll into the next underwriting cycle instead gets "trapped," held in reserve against loss estimates that take months or years to finalize, unable to be redeployed to write new business even after the immediate crisis has passed. That trapped-capital problem, which hit the retrocession market hard after Hurricane Ian and tightened reinsurance supply just when Florida needed it loosened, sits at the boundary between this chapter and the next, where the traditional reinsurance industry that the capital markets were built to supplement gets its own closer look.

What the catastrophe bond actually accomplishes, stripped of the financial engineering, is not a reduction in how much a hurricane costs when it makes landfall. It is a widening of who is standing behind that cost before the storm ever forms. Every dollar of cat bond capacity sitting in a pension fund's portfolio in Zurich or a hedge fund's book in Connecticut is a dollar that does not have to come from a single Florida-based insurer's thin capital reserves, or from a state guaranty fund, or eventually from a taxpayer, if that storm actually arrives. That is a genuine achievement, and the market's growth through the 2010s and 2020s, storm losses and Lehman's collapse notwithstanding, is a reasonable argument that capital markets can do at least part of what Sandor, Froot, and their colleagues hoped they could back in the early 1990s: bring the vastly larger pool of global investment capital to bear on a risk the traditional reinsurance industry alone could never fully absorb. It has not, and structurally cannot, make the underlying hurricane smaller, or make Florida's coastline less populated, or guarantee that the specific investors holding a specific bond in a specific year will be sufficiently numerous and diversified that no single one of them is ruined by paying out. The bond only ever promised to find someone else, somewhere in the world's capital markets, willing to hold a slice of the risk for a price — and for nearly thirty years now, remarkably often, someone has.
