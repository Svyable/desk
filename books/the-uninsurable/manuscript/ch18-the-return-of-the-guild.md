# Chapter 18 — The Return of the Guild

Frederic Reiss had spent the years after his Navy service assessing fire and casualty risk at industrial plants and steel mills for the Ohio Inspection Bureau, and then underwriting reinsurance contracts in Cleveland, before he arrived at an idea that seemed, on its face, almost too simple to need a name. Youngstown Sheet and Tube, a large Ohio steelmaker, was struggling to buy commercial insurance at a price it considered reasonable for the risks running through its mills and its "captive" iron mines — the term the steel industry used for mines a company owned outright to supply its own furnaces, as opposed to mines it bought ore from on the open market. Reiss's answer, formalized in the early 1950s as Steel Insurance Company of America, was to let Youngstown insure itself: form a licensed insurance company, owned by the parent company, whose sole purpose was to underwrite the parent's own risk. He borrowed the mining industry's own word for the arrangement. A captive insurer, like a captive mine, exists to serve the company that owns it and nobody else. By 1958 Reiss had set up the first insurance company built specifically to operate this way offshore, in Bermuda, and by 1960 more than a hundred captives were running in the United States alone, each one a small, self-contained insurance company whose only customer was its own parent.

What Reiss had actually reinvented, whether he thought of it this way or not, was one of the oldest ideas in this book's entire argument: that a group of people or entities facing a shared risk can agree, among themselves, to cover each other's losses, without needing to sell that risk to an outside company that prices it, profits from it, and might one day decide it no longer wants it. Modern commercial insurance — the Lloyd's model this book opened with, the actuarial science of the nineteenth century, the reinsurance towers and catastrophe bonds of its middle chapters — is built on strangers pooling risk through an intermediary: a corporation with its own capital, its own shareholders, and its own profit motive standing between the people who are afraid of a loss and the people willing to be paid to absorb it. A captive removes the stranger. It is, in the most literal sense, a corporation deciding to be its own insurance company, which only makes economic sense when the commercial alternative has become too expensive, too restrictive, or too unwilling to write the coverage at all — precisely the condition described across the last several chapters of this book, as wildfire, cyber, and now AI liability risk push more and more categories of loss toward the edge of what any conventional insurer wants to underwrite.

That condition, as it happens, is not a new one; it is close to the oldest condition insurance has ever responded to. Long before Edward Lloyd's coffeehouse turned marine risk into a tradeable contract between strangers, and long before any of it had a name resembling "insurance," people facing a shared, unpredictable cost had already discovered the basic trick of pooling it among people they knew personally. Ancient Rome had burial collegia — associations of craftsmen, soldiers, and slaves who paid regular dues into a common fund used to guarantee each member a proper funeral, since dying without one carried both a religious and a social stigma nobody wanted to risk. Medieval European guilds, organized around trades rather than burial, extended the same logic to sickness, disability, and the support of a member's widow and children, treating misfortune as a cost the whole craft absorbed together rather than one that fell entirely on whichever household it happened to strike. By the time Daniel Defoe was writing about English social institutions at the end of the seventeenth century, mutual aid societies of this kind — soon formalized under English law as "friendly societies" — were already, in his account, widespread enough to be an ordinary feature of town life, decades before any of London's marine underwriters had built the actuarial apparatus this book traces through its middle chapters. Friendly societies collected small weekly or monthly dues from tradesmen, laborers, and their families and used the pooled fund to pay a doctor's bill, cover a burial, or provide a widow's pension, all administered by members electing their own officers rather than by an outside company charging a margin for the service. By the early twentieth century, England alone counted nearly 27,000 registered societies and branches serving more than six million members — a scale of voluntary, member-run risk pooling that dwarfed, for ordinary working people, anything the commercial insurance industry was offering them directly.

The thing that made a burial club or a friendly society work, in an era with none of the actuarial infrastructure this book's third chapter describes, was not statistical sophistication. It was trust built on proximity: members knew each other, worked the same trade, drank in the same public houses, and could see for themselves whether a fellow member's claimed illness or hardship was genuine, in a way no distant insurance company's claims adjuster ever could. That local knowledge substituted, imperfectly but functionally, for the large, independent, well-measured risk pool that actuarial science would later formalize. It also meant these societies were vulnerable in a very specific way commercial insurers generally were not: their entire membership tended to share the same trade, the same town, and often the same exposure to a single local hazard, whether that was a mine collapse, an industrial accident common to one factory, or an epidemic that moved through a crowded neighborhood all at once. A pool built on trust among people who know each other is also, almost by definition, a pool with less independence baked into it than a pool built by an actuary deliberately searching the whole country for policyholders whose risks do not correlate. The guild model traded statistical diversification for social trust, and for centuries, given the alternative of no insurance at all, that trade was worth making.

Friendly societies had one structural weakness that eventually forced most of them to either professionalize or fold, and it is worth naming because it connects directly back to this book's earlier argument about the law of large numbers. Dues were typically set by tradition, by what members could afford, or by rough comparison with a neighboring society, rather than by any actuarial calculation of the actual mortality and morbidity risk being pooled. A society that admitted mostly young, healthy tradesmen in its founding years could pay generous benefits for a generation and look, to its members, like a triumphant example of mutual solidarity, right up until that same cohort aged into its fifties and sixties simultaneously and the claims on the fund began arriving faster than the dues coming in to replace them. Actuarial science, once it matured over the course of the nineteenth century, made this failure mode visible and largely preventable — a competently run society could set dues by age at entry and reserve properly for future claims, the same techniques an insurance company use — but many smaller societies never made that transition, and a wave of them collapsed or merged into larger, better-managed federations across the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Britain's 1911 National Insurance Act, which created state-administered sickness and unemployment benefits for the first time, drew directly on the friendly society model and in some ways completed its long institutional migration, folding a function that had begun as voluntary mutual aid among trusted neighbors into a compulsory, actuarially managed, state-run program — a preview, decades early, of the pattern this book's chapter on the insurer of last resort described for terrorism, flood, and nuclear risk: a private, member-driven pooling arrangement eventually needing a government behind it once the pool grew large enough, or the underlying risk uneven enough, for informal trust alone to keep covering the bill.

Lloyd's of London itself, the institution this book opened with, ran for most of its three-century history on a version of the same trade, dressed in more sophisticated clothing. Lloyd's syndicates were never capitalized by a company balance sheet the way a modern insurer is. They were capitalized by individual people — "Names" — who pledged their personal wealth, down to their last asset, as backing for the risks a syndicate agreed to underwrite, in exchange for a share of the syndicate's profits. For most of Lloyd's existence this was considered not a burden but a privilege: membership required a substantial minimum net worth, admission was arranged through personal introduction rather than open application, and by the 1980s tens of thousands of Names — the number is usually put at around 34,000 at the market's peak — included aristocrats, retired military officers, minor celebrities, and a wide swath of Britain's professional upper-middle class, all of whom had, in effect, joined an exclusive club whose membership fee was unlimited personal liability. In theory this arrangement embodied the guild principle at its most extreme and its most honest: a Name backed a syndicate's risk with everything they owned, which meant they had every incentive to actually understand what that syndicate was underwriting, and no ability to hide behind a corporate veil if the underwriting went wrong. In practice, for more than two hundred years, it rarely went wrong badly enough to matter, because marine, fire, and casualty risk, priced by experienced underwriters and diversified across syndicates and years, mostly behaved the way actuarial theory said it should.

What broke that arrangement, starting in the late 1980s and cresting through the early-to-mid 1990s, was a category of claim nobody underwriting a policy decades earlier had priced for at all: American courts' evolving treatment of asbestos and environmental pollution liability. Policies written by Lloyd's syndicates in the 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s — often general liability or product liability cover for American manufacturers — had been priced against the ordinary claims environment of their time. Over the following decades, US courts progressively expanded the legal theories under which asbestos manufacturers, and by extension their insurers, could be held liable, eventually allowing claims from workers who had been exposed decades earlier and had not yet developed any diagnosable illness, on the theory that the exposure itself created a compensable injury. Claims that insurers had reserved for at modest levels in the 1960s and 1970s ballooned, retroactively, into what would eventually total tens of billions of dollars in asbestos-related liability alone, with one widely cited industry estimate putting the sum Lloyd's ultimately faced from asbestos and pollution claims combined above $15 billion — a bill nobody who had originally underwritten those policies had any way to see coming, because the legal environment that created the liability did not exist yet when the risk was priced.

The mechanism that turned that surprise into a catastrophe for individual Names, rather than merely an expensive one for the market as a whole, was a piece of financial plumbing called the London Market Excess of loss spiral, or LMX spiral. Lloyd's syndicates routinely reinsured portions of their own exposure with other syndicates, a standard and sensible practice that in principle spreads risk further and thins out any one syndicate's exposure to a single bad year. In the 1980s London reinsurance market, syndicates were reinsuring each other so extensively, and in such circular fashion, that the same underlying asbestos and pollution losses were effectively being passed from syndicate to syndicate, reinsured and re-reinsured, with each transaction taking a fee and appearing, on each individual syndicate's books, as diversification. It was not diversification. It was the same correlated loss circulating through the market multiple times, disguised by each hop as a separate, independent transaction, until nobody — not the syndicates, not Lloyd's own oversight committee, not the Names whose personal wealth stood behind all of it — could say with confidence how much total exposure to asbestos and pollution liability the market actually carried, or how concentrated it really was. When the claims came due, the spiral did not spread the loss thin. It revealed that the loss had never actually been diversified at all, and every syndicate caught in the spiral discovered its exposure at nearly the same moment.

The human cost landed on the Names, exactly as the unlimited-liability structure was designed to ensure it would. Lloyd's underwriters recorded aggregate losses on the order of £8 billion across the years bridging 1988 through 1992 by some contemporary tallies, and considerably higher figures — up to roughly £16 billion — by others counting a longer window and a wider set of loss years; the range itself is a small illustration of how difficult it was, even after the fact, to pin down exactly how large the correlated exposure had been. Estimates of the human toll vary in the same way. Journalistic and academic accounts from the period and its aftermath generally put the number of Names driven into bankruptcy somewhere in the hundreds to the low thousands — one frequently cited tally puts it at roughly 1,500 out of the 34,000 Names active at the market's peak, or a bit over four percent of the total membership, though other contemporaneous reporting cites smaller numbers of outright bankruptcies alongside a much larger population who lost the bulk of their savings without formally filing for bankruptcy. At least fifteen Names are reported to have taken their own lives amid the crisis, and a far larger number sold homes, liquidated pensions, and spent years in litigation against the managing agents who had placed them in the worst-affected syndicates, arguing — successfully, in the House of Lords' landmark 1994 ruling in Henderson v Merrett Syndicates — that those agents owed them a duty of care they had failed to meet. The particular cruelty of the structure, compared to a company shareholder who can only lose the value of the shares purchased, was that a Name's liability had no ceiling at all; a retired schoolteacher or a minor member of the aristocracy who had joined Lloyd's for a comfortable supplementary income could, in principle, be called upon to pay claims until every asset they owned was exhausted, for underwriting decisions made by an agent on a syndicate they may have paid only glancing attention to for years.

Lloyd's response, worked out over the following decade, effectively ended the individual-Name model as the market's foundation. In 1994 Lloyd's opened its membership to corporate capital for the first time, letting limited-liability companies — rather than only individuals with unlimited personal exposure — back syndicates, a structural shift that within a decade came to supply the overwhelming majority of the market's capacity. In 1996 Lloyd's created Equitas, a dedicated reinsurance vehicle designed to absorb essentially all of the market's 1992-and-prior-year liabilities — the asbestos, pollution, and other long-tail exposures that had caused the crisis — into a single, ring-fenced entity, funded by a reconstruction and renewal settlement that asked Names to contribute toward closing out the old years in exchange for a line drawn under further personal exposure. And in 2007, in a transaction with a certain symmetry to this book's own argument about reinsurance stacked upon reinsurance, Equitas itself transferred the bulk of its remaining liabilities to National Indemnity Company, a subsidiary of Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway, through a $7 billion reinsurance arrangement — the risk that had once been distributed among 34,000 individual British citizens with unlimited personal liability ending up, a decade and a half later, concentrated on the balance sheet of a single American conglomerate with the capital reserves to simply absorb it and wait. The wheel had come full circle in a way none of the original Names could have anticipated: risk that started as a broadly diversified pool held by strangers-turned-partners at a London coffeehouse three centuries earlier had, by the time it finished working through the asbestos crisis, been concentrated, correlated, nearly destroyed a generation of British professionals, and finally settled onto the one kind of institution built with enough capital to shrug it off.

The Names crisis is worth sitting with at length in a chapter otherwise devoted to the modern captive-insurance boom, because the two are the same idea approached from opposite directions, and the contrast tells you something about why one has thrived while the other nearly ended a three-hundred-year-old institution. A modern captive, of the kind Frederic Reiss pioneered and that more than 7,000 corporations now operate worldwide — collectively writing upward of $80 billion a year in premium, by recent industry tallies, with roughly 90 percent of Fortune 500 companies maintaining at least one — is a guild in corporate form: a single organization, or a handful of related organizations, deciding to retain a defined slice of its own risk rather than pay a commercial insurer's margin to transfer it, usually because that commercial insurer either will not offer the coverage at all or wants a premium the company considers unjustified by its own loss history. Pacific Gas & Electric, the California utility whose equipment has been found responsible for some of the state's most destructive wildfires, now self-insures its wildfire liability almost entirely through exactly this mechanism, funded by ratepayer charges building toward a $1 billion reserve, because the commercial market's appetite for that specific risk, after billions of dollars in wildfire-liability losses across the industry, had shrunk close to nothing. Technology and AI companies have begun forming captives for the same reason on a newer risk: cyber and algorithmic-liability exposure that traditional errors-and-omissions and cyber policies increasingly carve out through explicit exclusions, leaving a company that wants coverage for those losses with little choice but to write it itself.

The modern captive industry has, in the decades since Reiss's Bermuda experiment, built its own regulatory infrastructure precisely so that a company's decision to insure itself does not simply repeat the informal, undercapitalized failure mode that eventually caught up with so many friendly societies. Domiciles including Vermont, Bermuda, the Cayman Islands, and a growing list of US states compete to license captives, each requiring minimum capital, actuarial certification of reserves, and regulatory filings not unlike those imposed on a conventional insurer — a captive is still, legally, an insurance company, subject to solvency oversight, even though its only policyholder is its own parent or a small group of affiliated members. Group captives, a further variation, let several unrelated companies in the same industry pool their retained risk together, splitting the difference between a single-company captive and the old guild model: a group of trucking companies or hospital systems, none large enough to self-insure alone, can form a shared captive and get something closer to genuine diversification, provided their underlying exposures are not all correlated to the same regional storm, the same regulatory shift, or the same industry-wide liability trend. That last caveat is the whole difficulty in miniature, and it is the same caveat that undid the friendly societies and the Lloyd's Names alike: a pool of trusted, similar members is only as safe as the members' risks are actually independent of one another, and an industry group captive formed by companies who all lease equipment from the same handful of suppliers, insure buildings in the same coastal region, or depend on the same piece of software is reconstructing, on a smaller and better-capitalized scale, precisely the correlation problem this book keeps finding wherever it looks.

The crucial difference between Reiss's captives and Lloyd's Names is what each arrangement asks the risk to do once it is retained rather than transferred. A captive works, in the sense of remaining solvent through a bad year, when the risk it retains is genuinely something the parent company can absorb on its own balance sheet — a layer of loss sized to the company's own capital, sitting below whatever excess commercial reinsurance the captive buys to cap the truly catastrophic scenarios. It stops working the moment a company uses a captive to retain risk that is not actually diversifiable or boundable at all — an existential, correlated exposure whose worst case could exceed the parent's entire net worth, which is precisely the shape of risk that drove commercial insurers away from it in the first place. A captive that retains that kind of risk is not really practicing self-insurance in any meaningful actuarial sense. It is simply declaring, in corporate form, that the risk has become uninsurable and choosing to hold it anyway, hoping the worst case never arrives — which is exactly what 34,000 Lloyd's Names, for different reasons and on a scale none of them chose deliberately, ended up doing with asbestos.

The oldest lesson embedded in all of this, running from Roman burial collegia through English friendly societies through Lloyd's unlimited-liability Names to a PG&E ratepayer-funded captive, is that pooling risk among people or institutions who know and trust each other has always been a reasonable answer to a commercial market that will not price a risk fairly — right up until the moment the risk turns out to be more correlated than anyone inside the trusted circle realized. A guild works when its members' misfortunes are independent of each other: one tradesman's illness does not cause another's. It breaks when the shared exposure turns out to be common to the whole group at once — a mine collapse that kills half the local chapter's membership, an asbestos liability that hits every Lloyd's syndicate that wrote long-tail casualty business in the same twenty-year window, a wildfire season shaped by the same drought and the same wind pattern across every acre of a utility's service territory. The modern captive-insurance boom is not a rejection of the lesson Lloyd's Names paid for so dearly. It is, more often, an acknowledgment of it: a company forming a captive to hold a risk the commercial market has already told it, in the clearest possible language of a declined quote or an excluded peril, that it does not want to hold either.
