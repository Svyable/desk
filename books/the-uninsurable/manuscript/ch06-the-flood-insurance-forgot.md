# The Flood Insurance Forgot

Cori and Kerri Rigsby were sisters, both independent insurance adjusters working the Mississippi Gulf Coast in the fall of 2005, and both under contract to State Farm when they began noticing something in the claims files that didn't sit right. Hurricane Katrina had put a wall of storm surge across Biloxi and Gulfport that, in many neighborhoods, arrived alongside sustained winds strong enough to have torn roofs off on their own. Untangling which force did which piece of damage — the wind, covered under an ordinary homeowners policy, or the water, excluded from that policy and instead covered, if at all, by a separate federal flood policy — should have been a technical, if difficult, engineering question. The Rigsby sisters came to believe it had become something else: that engineers' findings were being altered, and that adjusters were being directed to attribute damage to flood whenever there was any water on the property at all, regardless of what the wind had done first, because the federal government would pay for flood damage while State Farm would have to pay for wind damage out of its own reserves. A jury later found that State Farm had defrauded the National Flood Insurance Program by billing it $250,000 for damage to a Biloxi home that a wind claim, not a flood claim, should have covered. State Farm eventually settled roughly 640 Mississippi wind-damage disputes for about $80 million and separately paid the federal government $100 million in restitution over its handling of Katrina flood claims.

The particular cruelty of the wind-versus-water dispute is that it turned a program built to help disaster victims into a target that private insurers had an incentive to shoot claims toward. The National Flood Insurance Program was never supposed to be an adversary sitting behind a homeowner's own insurer, waiting to be billed. It was supposed to be a backstop for a risk the private market had already tried, and failed, to hold — and by 2005, wind insurers had discovered that steering damage onto that backstop's books was sometimes cheaper for them than paying it themselves. Katrina put more homeowners through years of these disputes than any storm before it. Seven years later, a different storm would reveal that the fraud problem inside the flood program wasn't limited to insurers arguing about which policy applied. It extended to insurers deciding, on the flood program's own claims, how much damage had actually occurred at all.

Superstorm Sandy struck the mid-Atlantic coast in October 2012, and in its aftermath, thousands of homeowners along the New York and New Jersey shore filed claims under the National Flood Insurance Program, administered on the ground not by federal employees but by private insurance companies operating under a decades-old arrangement called Write Your Own. Under that program, established in 1983, a private insurer sells the federal flood policy under its own brand, collects the premium, adjusts the claim, and pays it out of an account funded by the U.S. Treasury — acting, in the government's own language, as FEMA's fiscal agent rather than as a risk-bearer in its own right. It is an unusual hybrid: private companies handling the customer relationship and the claims process for a product whose underlying risk sits entirely on the federal government's balance sheet. After Sandy, homeowners and their attorneys began comparing notes and discovered that the engineering firms these Write Your Own insurers had hired to assess flood damage had, in a number of documented cases, altered the actual field engineers' findings before those findings ever reached the homeowner or FEMA — reducing damage estimates, and therefore claim payouts, after the person who inspected the house in person had already written down something larger. More than 1,600 homeowners eventually filed suit, and roughly 1,200 of those cases ended up consolidated in federal court in Brooklyn, where a magistrate judge described some of the litigation conduct as "reprehensible gamesmanship." An engineering firm and one of its former employees eventually pleaded guilty to fraud. FEMA reopened thousands of Sandy claims for review and ultimately paid out tens of millions of dollars in additional money to homeowners whose original assessments, it turned out, had never reflected what their own hired engineers actually found.

What makes the Sandy engineering scandal worth dwelling on, more than a decade later, is what it reveals about incentives inside a system built on the assumption that the insurer administering a claim and the party ultimately paying for it are the same actor. In ordinary private insurance, an insurer that lowballs its policyholders faces the market discipline of losing customers, along with regulatory scrutiny and, in bad-faith cases, litigation exposure that comes directly out of its own capital. Under Write Your Own, the insurer processing a flood claim was spending the federal government's money, not exclusively its own, while still earning a fee for administering the policy — a structure in which minimizing payouts protected the insurer's own operating margin on a program where the downside risk of underpaying belonged mostly to homeowners and, eventually, to FEMA's reputation, not to the company's own reserves. Nobody designed that incentive on purpose. It emerged from stacking a federal risk pool on top of a private servicing layer, and it took a hurricane, and years of litigation, to surface.

The flood program did not begin as a story about fraud, and it is worth remembering what problem it was actually invented to solve. Private insurers sold flood coverage in the United States as far back as the 1890s, and kept selling it through the 1920s, until the Mississippi River flooded catastrophically in 1927 — one of the worst natural disasters in American history — and insurers who had underwritten flood risk discovered, in the way State Farm's descendants would rediscover about hurricanes seven decades later, that flood losses are not statistically independent across policyholders the way a fire in one house is independent of a fire in the house next door. When a river floods, it floods every house along its banks in roughly the same week, and it tends to flood the same low-lying, historically flood-prone places over and over across the decades, meaning the people most likely to buy flood coverage are disproportionately the people most likely to need it — the textbook condition insurers call adverse selection. Insurers concluded flood could not be profitably pooled at a price ordinary households could pay, and by the 1930s the private flood insurance market in the United States had essentially disappeared. It stayed gone for four decades, during which the federal government paid for flood recovery the way it paid for most disasters: through ad hoc, after-the-fact congressional relief, a mechanism that grew steadily more expensive as the country built more housing along more rivers and coastlines, and that offered homeowners no incentive whatsoever to build somewhere safer or higher, since the money would show up eventually regardless.

Congress created the National Flood Insurance Program in 1968, prompted less by any single flood than by the accumulating cost of that ad hoc relief system, sharpened by the 1964 Alaska earthquake and by Hurricane Betsy in 1965, the first hurricane in American history to cause more than a billion dollars in damage. The new program had two stated goals that, in retrospect, sat in some tension with each other from the start. It was meant to make flood insurance available at all, since the private market wouldn't sell it, and it was meant to use that availability as leverage — communities that wanted their residents to be able to buy flood insurance had to adopt floodplain maps and enforce basic building standards for new construction in flood-prone zones, discouraging further development in harm's way. The insurance was the carrot; the mapping and building requirements were supposed to be the mechanism that kept the program from simply subsidizing more people to build in flood zones over time.

The subsidy came anyway, deliberately, for a specific category of housing: buildings constructed before their community's first flood map was drawn, known as "pre-FIRM" structures, for Flood Insurance Rate Map. Congress reasoned that it wasn't fair to charge full actuarial rates to someone whose house had been built decades before any map told them they were in a flood zone, so those properties were allowed to buy coverage at rates well below what their real flood risk would have cost — a deliberate, openly acknowledged subsidy, not a pricing error. That subsidy persisted for decades, and it persisted specifically for the properties statistically most likely to flood again, since pre-FIRM housing sits, by definition, in the oldest and often lowest-lying developed areas. The predictable result was a small subset of properties that flooded, collected, rebuilt in the same spot, and flooded again. FEMA's own data on this is unambiguous: properties with two or more paid losses — what the agency calls repetitive loss properties — made up only about 2 to 4 percent of NFIP policies in various counts taken over the years, yet accounted for something like a third to nearly half of all claims dollars the program has ever paid out, depending on the period measured and exactly how the count is drawn. A Government Accountability Office analysis using one FEMA dataset put unmitigated repetitive loss properties at roughly 2.5 percent of policies but 48 percent of claims dollars; an earlier analysis covering 1978 through 2015 found repetitive-loss policyholders made up 3.8 percent of filers and accounted for about 35.5 percent of claims and 30.5 percent of claim payments. The two counts use different definitions and time windows and don't agree precisely, but they agree on the shape of the problem: a small number of the same houses, flooding over and over, absorbing a wildly disproportionate share of a program meant to spread flood risk broadly.

This is moral hazard in close to its purest textbook form, and it is worth being precise about what that phrase does and doesn't mean here. It doesn't mean the individual homeowners in a repetitive-loss house were behaving irrationally, or that most of them wanted to be flooded again. Most were doing exactly what a reasonable person does with cheap, available insurance: staying where they already lived, rebuilding what had already been damaged, and paying a premium that did not reflect what a fully priced policy would have charged them to keep doing it. The hazard sat one level up, in the incentive structure the subsidy created and in the absence, for a very long time, of any effective mechanism to counteract it — no meaningful cap on how many times a subsidized property could be rebuilt with NFIP money, and, until relatively recently, no serious buyout program at a scale that could move large numbers of the worst-repeating properties out of harm's way altogether. FEMA has run voluntary buyout programs for years, but a 2020 GAO review found the pace and scale of those acquisitions still left the program's fiscal exposure largely intact.

The bill for all of this arrived in three unmistakable installments. Hurricane Katrina, in 2005, produced more than $16 billion in NFIP claims payouts, the largest the program had ever seen up to that point and enough on its own to push the program into permanent structural debt to the U.S. Treasury, since the NFIP had never been designed, and had never collected premiums, to fund a loss on that scale. Superstorm Sandy, in 2012, added nearly $9 billion more. Hurricane Harvey, in 2017, added roughly $11 billion, and did so in the same catastrophic year as Hurricanes Irma and Maria, pushing the program's total borrowing against the Treasury to its statutory limit of $30.425 billion that September. Congress responded a month later by canceling $16 billion of that debt outright — the first time in the program's history it had ever forgiven any of it, a tacit admission that the debt as structured could never realistically be repaid out of premium income — and the program still owed roughly $20.5 billion three years after that write-off, a figure that has moved only modestly since: $22.525 billion as of early 2025, after FEMA exercised its Treasury borrowing authority again to cover claims from the 2024 hurricane season, leaving under $8 billion of headroom against the statutory cap. Since the program's inception, Louisiana and Texas policyholders alone have collected an estimated $35.6 billion of the roughly $69.4 billion the NFIP has paid out nationwide — just over half the program's entire claims history concentrated in two states, which is either an argument that flood risk really is that geographically concentrated in the United States, or an argument that the program has spent fifty years subsidizing development in exactly the places it should have been discouraging it, or, most plausibly, some of both at once.

FEMA has tried, more than once, to close the gap between the flood program's rates and the risk they're supposed to cover, and each attempt has run into the same wall: the people who would pay more live in real congressional districts and vote in real elections. The most serious modern attempt, a rate-setting overhaul called Risk Rating 2.0, took effect in October 2021 and represented the first fundamental change to how the NFIP priced individual properties since the program began — moving from a rating system based mostly on a property's flood-zone designation and elevation certificate toward one incorporating property-specific variables like distance to water, flood frequency, and the cost to rebuild a particular structure. In principle, this was actuarial modernization of the sort actuarial science had been demanding of the flood program for decades. In practice, because full actuarial rates for the highest-risk properties would in many cases have meant premium increases of many multiples overnight, Congress had already capped how fast NFIP premiums could rise under the Homeowner Flood Insurance Affordability Act — statutory limits generally holding annual increases to 18 percent for most primary residences, with a faster 25-percent-per-year glide path for non-primary residences, business properties, and the severe repetitive-loss properties responsible for so much of the program's claims history. Those caps mean that even a home Risk Rating 2.0 correctly assesses as needing a rate five or ten times higher than it currently pays will not reach that true rate for many years, if a rate cap that Congress can also choose to extend indefinitely doesn't intervene first. The gap between what the map says a house is worth insuring at and what the statute allows it to actually be charged is, in its own quiet way, a second subsidy layered on top of the first — smaller, more gradual, but built into the same program for the same underlying reason: full pricing produces bills that are, for a meaningful number of American households, simply larger than what people believe they should have to pay to keep living where they already live.

There is a second, less dramatic form of institutional fragility running underneath all of this, and it shows up not in a single storm but in the calendar. The NFIP's five-year statutory reauthorization expired at the end of fiscal year 2017 — the same year as Harvey, Irma, and Maria — and Congress has never passed another multi-year reauthorization since. Instead, lawmakers have kept the program alive through a long chain of short-term extensions: thirty-five of them and counting between the end of 2017 and early 2026, on top of another seventeen extensions between 2008 and 2012 alone. Five times since 2017, and four times in the earlier stretch, the extensions arrived too late and the program's authority to write new policies or renew existing ones actually lapsed, sometimes for a few days, once for weeks. A lapse doesn't cancel existing coverage outright, but it stops the NFIP from issuing new policies or increasing coverage limits, which in practice freezes home sales in flood-prone areas — during one ten-day lapse in 2010, an estimated 1,400 home closings a day were delayed or canceled nationwide because buyers in mapped flood zones couldn't get the flood coverage their mortgage lenders required to close. A program built to give households in flood-prone places a predictable, government-backed alternative to a private market that wouldn't insure them has itself spent most of the last two decades operating without predictability of its own, reauthorized in increments too short for anyone — homeowners, insurers, mortgage lenders, or FEMA's own long-range planners — to build much on beyond the next deadline.

The gap the program was built to close, meanwhile, remains enormous by any measure. FEMA's own estimates put flood insurance uptake at only around 4 percent of U.S. households nationally, and even within the highest-risk mapped zones, where mortgage lenders on federally backed loans are legally required to demand flood coverage, roughly half of the flood losses those properties can expect to suffer are estimated to go uninsured; outside the mapped high-risk zones, where no purchase requirement applies and homeowners often simply don't believe they're at risk, an estimated three-quarters or more of exposed single-family homes carry no flood policy at all. Some of that gap is affordability, the same tension the rate caps were built to manage. Some of it is the plain fact that FEMA's flood maps, redrawn on an irregular schedule and built on historical rainfall and river-flow data that a warming climate is steadily rendering less predictive, simply miss a great deal of the flooding that actually occurs — the 2017 Harvey rainfall over Houston, more than 60 inches in places, fell largely outside the mapped Special Flood Hazard Area that supposedly defined where flood insurance was required, leaving tens of thousands of flooded Houston-area homeowners to discover, in the worst possible week, that the line on the map had not been where the water actually went. A private flood insurance market has begun to reemerge in the years since Risk Rating 2.0 took effect, with several carriers now writing flood coverage outside the NFIP at prices that in some cases undercut the federal program for lower-risk properties — a modest, market-based echo of the private flood insurance that existed before 1927, now competing again at the margins the federal pool has priced itself out of, while the federal program keeps the properties the private market still won't touch.

None of this is a story about a program run by fools. The people who built the NFIP in 1968 correctly diagnosed a real market failure — flood losses concentrate too tightly along rivers and coastlines, and among the same repeat properties, for a private insurer relying on independence across its policyholders to price the risk at a level households can afford — and they built, in response, exactly the kind of institution this book keeps returning to: a pool assembled by government because private capital had already tried and declined to assemble one on its own. The debt to the Treasury, the repetitive-loss concentration, the Write Your Own fraud exposure, and the statutory rate caps are not separate failures. They are what happens when that kind of pool is asked to do two things Congress has never fully reconciled — price flood risk honestly enough to remain solvent, and keep flood insurance affordable enough that people already living in flood-prone homes can still afford to buy it — without ever being given the third thing that might have made both possible at once: a serious, well-funded, and politically durable program to move the worst-exposed properties somewhere the water isn't going to keep finding them. Every other fix is a way of managing that unresolved tension a little longer. It is not a way of resolving it.

The flood program's oldest promise — that a household living somewhere the private market wouldn't insure could still buy coverage, at a price it could afford, backed by the full faith of the federal government — has been kept for more than fifty years now, through Katrina and Sandy and Harvey and every ordinary flood season in between, and that continuity of coverage is a genuine, non-trivial achievement worth stating plainly. What has not been kept, and was arguably never actually promised in the fine print, is that the government's checkbook was a substitute for the pooling logic insurance is supposed to run on. A pool works when the many pay a little so the unlucky few don't pay everything. The NFIP, across its history, has often worked the other way: the same relatively few properties, flooding again and again in the same low ground, drawing disproportionately on a fund the broader public — as taxpayers, if not as flood-zone policyholders — ultimately backstops. That is not pooling in the actuarial sense. It is transfer, dressed in an insurance policy's clothing, and the $20-billion-plus still owed to the Treasury is the running total of how much of that transfer has never actually been paid back by anyone.
