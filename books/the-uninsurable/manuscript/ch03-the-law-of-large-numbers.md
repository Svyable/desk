# The Law of Large Numbers

In 1687, a pastor and amateur naturalist named Caspar Neumann, living in the Silesian city of Breslau, began sending records to the Royal Society in London. Neumann was a Lutheran clergyman, not a mathematician, but the data he had assembled, with the kind of patience that only a small city's parish records can provide, concerned something that would outlast any sermon he preached: the births and deaths of the town's population, tabulated by age, year over year, for a five-year stretch in the 1680s. Breslau kept unusually thorough vital records for a European city of its size, and Neumann, working with the philosopher Gottfried Leibniz as an intermediary, forwarded the tables to London in the hope that they might interest the Society's members. They reached Edmond Halley, the astronomer who would later have a comet named after him, and Halley did something with them that nobody had quite managed to do before: he built a table showing, for a hypothetical group of people all born in the same year, how many of them could be expected to still be alive at each subsequent age. He published it in 1693 under the title "An Estimate of the Degrees of the Mortality of Mankind," and in the same paper he did something that mattered even more than the table itself. He used it to calculate what a fair price would be for selling someone an annuity — a contract promising to pay a fixed sum every year for the rest of that person's life — based on how old the buyer was when the contract began.

Halley was not working from nothing. Three decades earlier, in 1662, a London draper and shopkeeper named John Graunt had published a slim book called Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality, working from the weekly death records London parishes had been compiling since the plague years of the sixteenth century. Graunt had no formal training in mathematics, but he noticed patterns in the raw counts that nobody had thought to look for: mortality was heavily concentrated among children under six, certain diseases recurred with a regularity that suggested underlying causes rather than random misfortune, and it was possible, working purely from the aggregated bills, to construct a rough table showing how many people out of a hypothetical starting population of one hundred would still be alive at each successive age. It was the first life table ever built from real observational data, and it made Graunt, a tradesman with no university post, a fellow of the Royal Society on the strength of a single small book. What Graunt lacked, and what Halley supplied thirty years later using Caspar Neumann's more precise and complete Breslau records, was the ability to turn a rough demographic curiosity into an instrument precise enough to price a financial contract. Graunt showed that mortality had structure. Halley showed what that structure was worth in pounds and shillings.

That calculation is worth sitting with, because it is the moment the pricing of the future stopped being a matter of custom or guesswork and became a matter of arithmetic. Annuities had existed for centuries before Halley, sold by governments and wealthy individuals as a way to raise cash immediately in exchange for a lifetime income stream, and for most of that history they had been priced with something close to indifference to the buyer's age: a sixty-year-old and a twenty-year-old might be offered annuities at nearly the same rate, even though the younger buyer, statistically, was likely to collect payments for decades longer. This was not a minor oversight. Governments financed wars by selling annuities at flat rates regardless of age, and shrewd buyers — often, in practice, healthy young people bought on behalf of by wealthier patrons, or annuities purchased on the lives of children who had already survived the highest-risk years of infancy — could extract enormous value from a pricing system that had no mechanism for telling the difference between a good risk and a bad one. Halley's table gave, for the first time, a mathematical basis for pricing that difference. A younger life was worth a lower annual rate for the same purchase price, because it was expected, on average, to draw payments for longer. Halley recommended his approach to the English government as a fairer and more sustainable way to price its own annuity sales. The government, by most accounts, ignored him for the better part of a century, continuing to sell annuities at flat, age-blind rates that would have struck any reader of his paper as an open invitation to arbitrage. The mathematics existed. The institutions built to use it did not yet exist, and the gap between the two would take decades to close.

The man usually credited with closing that gap, at least for private insurance rather than government finance, is a mathematician named James Dodson, and his motivation for doing so was personal and, in its way, a little bitter. In 1755, Dodson applied for life insurance and was turned down, purely on the grounds of his age — he was over forty-five, and the insurer in question priced all policyholders at a single flat premium regardless of how old they were when they bought in, which meant it simply refused to accept anyone whose age made that flat rate unprofitable. Dodson, who understood Halley's mortality tables as well as anyone alive, set out to prove that an insurer did not need to turn away older applicants at all, so long as it charged each applicant a premium that reflected his actual mortality risk at the age he purchased the policy — building on Halley's mortality tables to develop the level-premium system that has structured life insurance ever since, in which a policyholder pays a premium calculated to be larger than what his current mortality risk alone would require in the early years of a contract, banking the surplus to help cover the years, decades later, when his mortality risk will be much higher than the premium alone could fund. Dodson died in 1757, five years before his ideas were put into commercial practice, when a group of backers used his work to found the Equitable Life Assurance Society in London in 1762 — the world's first mutual insurer to price life insurance on an actuarial basis, charging different applicants different premiums according to their age and using reserves accumulated from younger, lower-risk years to fund claims in later, higher-risk ones. The framework the Equitable pioneered became, in the description of its own historians, the basis on which essentially all subsequent life assurance would be built.

None of this — not Halley's table, not Dodson's level-premium system, not the Equitable's reserves — would have meant anything without a separate piece of mathematics being worked out, mostly independently, by a Swiss mathematician named Jacob Bernoulli. Bernoulli spent roughly two decades, beginning around 1684, developing what he called his "golden theorem," published only after his death in 1713 in a book titled Ars Conjectandi. The theorem, later renamed the law of large numbers by the French mathematician Siméon Poisson in 1837, is not difficult to state, though it took real ingenuity to prove rigorously for the first time: if you repeat an uncertain trial — a coin flip, a die roll, the survival or death of one person over one year — a large number of times, and if each repetition is independent of the others and drawn from the same underlying probability, then the average outcome across all those repetitions will converge, with increasing reliability as the number of repetitions grows, toward the true underlying probability. Flip a fair coin ten times and you might see seven heads. Flip it ten thousand times and you will see something extremely close to five thousand. Bernoulli understood immediately that this had implications well beyond gambling; he explicitly proposed extending probabilistic reasoning into "civil, moral, and economic affairs," anticipating almost exactly the uses that demography, insurance, and eventually every branch of applied statistics would make of his theorem over the following three centuries.

Put Bernoulli's theorem next to Halley's mortality table and Dodson's premium structure, and the entire machinery of actuarial science falls into place. An insurer cannot know, for any single policyholder, whether that particular person will die this year. What Bernoulli's theorem promises is that the insurer does not need to know that. If the insurer sells enough policies — enough independent trials, in Bernoulli's language — the total number of deaths among its policyholders in a given year will settle close to the number the mortality table predicts, even though every individual death remains, considered alone, essentially unpredictable. The single most important word in that sentence is "independent." Bernoulli's proof, and every actuarial calculation built on top of it since, assumes that each policyholder's fate is statistically unrelated to every other policyholder's fate — that whether one person dies this year tells you nothing whatsoever about whether the person insured on the next line of the ledger will die this year too. When that assumption holds, pooling works exactly as advertised: uncertainty at the level of the individual dissolves into predictability at the level of the group, and an insurer can set a premium today with real confidence in what its claims will look like on average, years or decades into the future. When that assumption fails — when death, illness, fire, or storm start striking many policyholders for the same reason, at the same time — the entire structure of the calculation breaks down, not gradually but suddenly, because the mathematics that makes pooling reliable simply does not apply to correlated events in the same way.

The level-premium idea Dodson worked out is simple enough to illustrate without much mathematics, and the illustration explains why the Equitable's competitors initially regarded it as almost reckless. Consider a person who buys a policy at thirty and expects, based on a mortality table, to face a rising chance of death every year thereafter. A "natural premium," priced fresh each year to match that year's actual mortality risk, would start low and climb steadily, becoming quite expensive by the time the policyholder reached seventy or eighty — precisely the years when a fixed retirement income made the largest premiums hardest to afford. Dodson's level premium instead charged the same amount every year for the life of the policy, an amount higher than the true mortality cost in the early years and lower than it in the later years, with the insurer investing the early overpayments into a reserve fund that would grow, compounding over decades, to cover the gap between premium and true cost once the policyholder reached old age. This was actuarially sound only if the insurer could reliably estimate, decades in advance, how many of its policyholders would still be alive at each future age to draw down that reserve — which is to say, it depended entirely on the law of large numbers holding across a very long time horizon, not just a single underwriting year. The Equitable's early administrators kept meticulous records precisely because they understood that the whole structure would only work if the mortality experience of their actual policyholders continued to track the table it had been built on.

The discipline built around checking that assumption, year after year, eventually became a formal profession in its own right. In July 1848, roughly fifty men who held actuarial posts at British insurance companies met in London and voted into existence the Institute of Actuaries of Great Britain and Ireland, with a government actuary named John Finlaison elected as its first president. The organization existed to do exactly the kind of institutional maintenance that Dodson and Halley's insights required but that no individual company could be trusted to perform rigorously on its own: comparing a company's actual mortality experience against its assumed tables, sharing data across firms so that no single insurer's table rested on too small or too idiosyncratic a sample, and developing shared standards for how large a reserve a responsible insurer needed to hold against future claims. It was granted a royal charter in 1884, by which point actuarial science had matured from Halley's one-man calculation into a credentialed profession with its own examinations, its own accumulated body of practice, and its own institutional memory of exactly the kinds of mistakes — mortality tables built on too small a population, reserves calculated without enough margin for error, assumptions about independence quietly imported from one line of business into another where they did not hold — that could turn Bernoulli's elegant theorem into a company's ruin if it were applied carelessly.

The most vivid confirmation of what happens when independence fails came a little over two centuries after Bernoulli's theorem was published, and it struck an industry that had, by then, spent generations refining its actuarial confidence into something approaching a science. The influenza pandemic that swept the world beginning in 1918 killed, by most modern estimates, somewhere in excess of thirty million people globally in well under a year — a mortality event that unfolded across a period so compressed, and across a population so widely dispersed, that it defeated the ordinary logic of life-insurance mortality tables almost completely. Those tables, built over more than a century on the assumption that any given year's deaths among a large, geographically scattered population of policyholders would be driven by a relatively stable, independent mix of causes — old age, accident, ordinary disease, spread out across the calendar and across the map — had no mechanism for anticipating a single respiratory virus killing policyholders by the hundreds of thousands within a period of weeks, all around the same time, for the same underlying reason. One American company, the National Life Insurance Company of Vermont, offers an unusually precise illustration of just how lopsided the shock was: internal figures cited by historians show the company's 1918 death claims driven by influenza reducing its reserves by roughly 660,000 dollars, against a reduction of only about 196,000 dollars attributable to combat deaths from the First World War, a conflict that was, at that exact moment, still being fought. A single virus, moving through a correlated, interconnected human population over a period of months, cost that one insurer more than three times what an entire world war cost it in the same year. Life insurers across the country responded in the aftermath by raising prices for new policies, particularly among companies whose existing books of business had been hit hardest, treating the higher price itself as a crude form of insurance against the possibility that this had not been a once-in-a-generation anomaly but a preview of a risk the old mortality tables had never been built to capture: not scattered, independent death, but death arriving in waves.

The pandemic of 1918 was an extreme case, and it is tempting to file it away as a historical curiosity — a once-a-century event that ordinary actuarial practice can reasonably treat as an outlier and move past. But the same underlying failure, the quiet collapse of the independence assumption, recurs constantly in less dramatic and more localized forms throughout the history of insurance, and modern researchers who study catastrophic and systemic risk in the insurance sector have spent decades trying to formalize exactly how much it changes the underlying mathematics. When claims are genuinely independent, insurers can hold relatively modest capital reserves against a large pool of policies, because the pooling itself does most of the work of managing risk; one widely used benchmark suggests that an insurer covering a hundred similar policies with independent claims might need capital reserves on the order of one and a half times the expected loss to remain solvent with high confidence. Introduce correlation into that same pool of claims — make it more likely that if one policyholder suffers a loss, others nearby will too, whether because of a hurricane, a wildfire, a shared software vulnerability, or a virus — and the required capital reserve does not rise gradually. It rises sharply, sometimes by an order of magnitude, because the insurer is no longer diversifying away individual uncertainty; it is instead holding a leveraged bet on a single shared outcome, dressed up, on paper, as a hundred separate policies. Economists and actuaries who study the failure of catastrophe insurance markets have identified this dynamic as one of the central reasons private insurance so often struggles to cover the risks people most want protection against — pandemics, floods, systemic financial shocks, region-wide natural disasters — precisely because those are the risks for which the independence assumption that makes ordinary insurance affordable is least likely to hold.

There is a second, quieter way the law of large numbers can fail to deliver the comfort it promises, distinct from correlation and easy to confuse with it. Bernoulli's theorem guarantees that an average converges to the true underlying probability as the number of independent trials grows. It says much less about how large any single loss within that average might be. A life insurer's individual claims are bounded in a fairly narrow way — one human life is worth, at most, one death benefit — but a property or catastrophe insurer's claims are not bounded in the same way at all: a single hurricane, earthquake, or wildfire can produce a loss on one policy that is a thousand times larger than the average loss the rest of the portfolio experiences in an ordinary year. Statisticians call a distribution with this property heavy-tailed or fat-tailed, meaning that extreme outcomes, while individually rare, are not rare enough to be safely ignored, and that the ordinary average calculated from past experience can understate the true risk by a wide margin precisely because the sample has not yet included one of the very large, very infrequent events the underlying distribution allows for. This is a genuinely separate problem from correlation, and both can be present at once, compounding each other: a wildfire is both a correlated event, striking many neighboring properties for the same underlying reason on the same afternoon, and a heavy-tailed one, capable of producing a single season's losses far outside the range that recent history alone would suggest. An insurer can build a mortality table as rigorous as Halley's and a level-premium structure as sound as Dodson's, and still misprice a catastrophe risk badly, not because its arithmetic is wrong but because its historical sample simply has not yet lived through the tail of the distribution it is trying to estimate.

It is worth being precise about what this does, and does not, mean, because the temptation is to conclude that pooling is a kind of trick that works only when nothing important is actually at stake. That overstates the case. Pooling works extraordinarily well, most of the time, for an enormous range of genuinely independent risks: the ordinary accidents, illnesses, house fires, and deaths that make up the overwhelming majority of insurance claims in any given year really do behave, in aggregate, close enough to the way Bernoulli's theorem predicts that insurers can and do price them with real confidence, year after year, in a business that has quietly underwritten most of the developed world's economic risk-taking for three centuries. The problem is not that the law of large numbers is wrong. The problem is that it is conditional, and the condition — independence — is not a fact that can simply be assumed to hold just because a portfolio of risks looks diversified on paper. Halley could not have known, sitting in London in 1693 with Caspar Neumann's Breslau records in front of him, that the mathematics he was pioneering would eventually be applied to fire risk in San Francisco, to hurricane risk in Florida, to ransomware attacks moving through shared software, or to wildfires racing across a drought-stressed California hillside. He also could not have known that in every one of those later applications, the same quiet question his own table implicitly answered for individual human mortality — are these outcomes actually independent of one another, or only pretending to be? — would resurface, in a new costume, as the single most consequential thing an insurer could get wrong.

That is the inheritance actuarial science left behind: not a guarantee that risk can always be pooled away, but a precise, mathematically rigorous description of exactly the one circumstance under which it can be, and exactly what happens the moment that circumstance quietly stops applying. Three centuries of subsequent history, much of it recounted in the chapters that follow, amounts to a long, expensive, and still-ongoing education in how easy it is to mistake the appearance of independence for the real thing, right up until a shared cause — a fault line, a storm system, a piece of vulnerable code, a virus, a flame moving from block to block — reveals that it was never quite as independent as the mortality table, or the underwriter, or the model, had assumed.
