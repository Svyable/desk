# Chapter 17 — The Protection Gap

By the last week of August 2022, roughly a third of Pakistan was underwater. Monsoon rains that year fell at several times their normal volume across Sindh and Balochistan, swollen further by glacial melt in the north, and the flooding that followed killed more than 1,700 people and displaced on the order of 33 million more — close to the population of Canada. Pakistan's own post-disaster needs assessment, produced with the United Nations and the World Bank, put total damage and economic losses above $30 billion. Cotton fields, wheat stores, brick homes, irrigation canals, livestock, roads, and clinics went under together, in the same weeks, across an area larger than the United Kingdom. And when the water receded and the counting began, the insurance industry's contribution to the recovery was, by any measure, close to nothing: independent estimates put the insured share of that $30 billion at under one percent. Pakistan's overall insurance penetration — total premiums as a share of GDP — sits at around 0.7 percent, among the lowest in the world. The 2022 floods were not a story about an insurance system that failed. They were a story about a country where, for the vast majority of families and farms hit by the water, there had never been an insurance system there to fail.

Swiss Re Institute, the reinsurance giant's research arm and the closest thing the global industry has to an official scorekeeper, has a name for the space between what a disaster actually costs and what insurance actually pays: the protection gap. It sounds like a piece of bureaucratic jargon, and in some ways it is, but the concept it describes is close to the animating idea of this entire book. Every dollar of loss that is not covered by an insurance payout, a government transfer, or someone's savings has to be absorbed somewhere — by a family selling off the last of its livestock, by a government running an emergency deficit, by an NGO redirecting funds meant for something else, or, most often, simply by nobody, in the form of a house that never gets rebuilt and a farm that never quite recovers its prior yield. The protection gap is not a rounding error in the global economy. It is the visible edge of the pooling machine this book has been describing — the place where the mechanism runs out.

The scale of that edge, in recent tallies, is not small. Swiss Re Institute's sigma research put the global natural catastrophe protection gap at roughly $395 billion in 2024 and $424 billion in 2025, defining the gap as the portion of catastrophe losses that ultimately went uninsured. A separate reckoning of 2024's disasters, drawn along slightly different lines, put total economic losses that year at $318 billion, of which 57 percent — about $181 billion — was never covered by any policy. The two numbers do not reconcile cleanly, because they are not measuring quite the same thing: one is an estimate of unmet protection need across the full range of catastrophe exposure in a given year, the other a tally of losses from disasters that actually occurred, counted against the insurance that actually paid out for them. Swiss Re's broader "resilience index," which tries to capture the share of overall protection need that insurance currently satisfies, put that share at 27.3 percent in the most recent reading — up two points from 25.3 percent a decade earlier, which counts as meaningful progress in an industry that measures change in single percentage points over ten-year spans. None of these figures should be confused with an even larger number that sometimes travels under the same name in press coverage: Swiss Re also tracks a combined global protection gap across mortality, health, and natural catastrophe risk together, which has been reported north of $1.8 trillion. That figure describes a much wider problem — the world's underinsurance against death, illness, and disaster combined — and conflating it with the roughly $400 billion natural-catastrophe-only gap, as some summaries do, overstates how much of the shortfall is specifically about storms and floods. The honest description is narrower and still enormous: on the order of half of what the world loses to natural catastrophe in a given year is never insured at all, and that share has been remarkably stable for decades even as the dollar totals climb.

For most of the twentieth century, the standard explanation for that gap was straightforward and mostly correct: insurance markets simply had not reached most of the world's population. Building an actuarial industry requires capital, regulatory infrastructure, a functioning court system to enforce contracts, and enough historical loss data to price a policy — none of which arrives quickly or cheaply, and none of which low-income countries have had much reason or ability to prioritize ahead of clean water, education, or basic infrastructure. That explanation still accounts for most of the gap in Pakistan, in much of sub-Saharan Africa, and across large parts of South and Southeast Asia, where 80 to 90 percent of catastrophe losses typically go uninsured not because insurers assessed the risk and declined it, but because there was barely a market there to begin with. But a second, newer, and in some ways more unsettling version of the protection gap has opened up inside the world's most mature, most heavily capitalized insurance markets — not because insurance never arrived, but because it is now actively leaving.

California is the clearest American case. In March 2024, State Farm General — the state subsidiary of the country's largest homeowners insurer — notified regulators it would not renew roughly 72,000 policies across the state, citing wildfire and, in some cases, fire-following-earthquake exposure that its own underwriting had identified as excessive. State Farm was not acting alone or first. Allstate, Farmers, USAA, and more than a dozen smaller carriers had already paused writing new homeowners policies or issued their own waves of non-renewals across California since 2021, and in company filings State Farm was explicit that it was targeting not just parcels that had already burned but ones that merely sat in zones its models flagged as carrying "significant concentration" of fire risk, whether or not a fire had ever reached them. This is worth sitting with, because it inverts the folk understanding of how insurance retreat happens. Nobody was punished for a fire that already occurred on their property. Homeowners were being dropped for what a catastrophe model said was likely to happen to their neighborhood eventually, based on vegetation, slope, building density, and wind patterns that had not changed in decades but that insurers were, for the first time, actually able to model at the level of an individual parcel.

That capability is itself the story. For most of the history of American property insurance, wildfire risk was priced the way a lot of catastrophe risk was priced before modern catastrophe modeling existed: coarsely, by county or by broad zone, smoothing over the difference between a house on a ridge surrounded by dry chaparral and a house two miles away on an irrigated lot near a fire station. That coarseness was, in its own way, a form of pooling — it meant homeowners in genuinely higher-risk spots were being cross-subsidized by homeowners in lower-risk spots within the same rating territory, whether either group knew it or not. Improved satellite imagery, LIDAR-derived vegetation data, and machine-learning models trained on burn histories have made that cross-subsidy visible and, from an insurer's perspective, avoidable. Once a company can price — or refuse to price — a specific parcel rather than a whole county, the economic logic of staying in the cross-subsidizing pool evaporates. The same forensic clarity that improved catastrophe models were supposed to bring to underwriting turned out to also be the tool that let insurers walk away from exactly the customers who most needed the pool to hold.

This dynamic has acquired an uncomfortable name in recent housing and insurance research: bluelining, a deliberate echo of the mid-twentieth-century practice of redlining, in which mortgage lenders and the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation drew red boundaries around minority neighborhoods and refused to lend inside them, regardless of any individual borrower's creditworthiness. The parallel is not perfect — redlining was explicitly racial and enforced through maps drawn by a federal agency, while wildfire and flood non-renewals are driven by climate and geographic data that is, on its face, race-neutral. But researchers at the Consumer Federation of America and at Yale's Livable City Lab have documented that the same neighborhoods marked hazardous under redlining maps eight decades ago disproportionately overlap with the neighborhoods facing the highest flood and heat exposure today, a legacy of where investment was withheld and where cheaper, lower-lying, or industrially adjacent land was left for those who had no other choice. When a modern catastrophe model draws a new boundary around a "high-risk" zone and an insurer declines to renew everyone inside it, the practical effect on a historically disinvested neighborhood can look very much like the old practice wearing a new set of inputs, whatever the underwriter's actual intent. Whether that overlap reflects deliberate discrimination, an accident of geography and history, or some combination the industry has not fully reckoned with is a live and contested question; what is not contested is that the boundary itself is becoming sharper, more automated, and less visible to the people standing inside it, which is precisely the opposite of what a functioning insurance market is supposed to do.

Britain took a different route to the same underlying problem, and the contrast is instructive. Rather than let insurers simply exit flood-prone postcodes the way American carriers have exited wildfire-prone zip codes, the UK insurance industry, working with the government, created Flood Re in 2016: a not-for-profit reinsurance fund that every home insurer selling policies in the country is required to pay into, currently raising around £160 million a year through a levy spread across the entire market. An insurer that takes on a high-flood-risk customer can pass that customer's flood exposure to Flood Re at a fixed, discounted price tied to the property's council tax band, which lets the insurer charge the homeowner something closer to an ordinary premium instead of a risk-reflective one. It is, in effect, a deliberately engineered cross-subsidy — every policyholder in the country, including the ones who will never see a flood, pays a small amount into a pool that keeps coverage affordable for the minority who live somewhere the water regularly reaches. Flood Re's own architects built the scheme with an explicit expiration date in mind, intending it as a bridge to a future in which flood defenses and land-use planning have reduced risk enough that the market can price flood cover honestly without needing the subsidy at all. Whether that transition actually happens on schedule, or whether Flood Re simply becomes a permanent fixture the way California's FAIR Plan outgrew its original brief as a narrow, temporary safety net, is an open question the scheme's own reviewers have raised. But the underlying choice it represents — spread the cost of a shrinking pool across everyone rather than let the market sort itself by exit — is a live alternative to the American pattern, not a hypothetical one, and it has kept flood insurance available in England in a way that plain market retreat has not managed to do for wildfire in California.

California's regulators have tried to answer the retreat with a bargain rather than a mandate. Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara's Sustainable Insurance Strategy, finalized through a series of regulations enforced starting in 2024, allows insurers for the first time to use forward-looking catastrophe models — rather than only historical loss data — in setting their rates, something California had barred for decades under Proposition 103's rate-review rules. In exchange, insurers who want to use those models have to commit to writing more policies in wildfire-distressed areas, including expanded participation in the California FAIR Plan, the state's insurer-of-last-resort program originally designed as a narrow, temporary backstop and now, after the January 2025 Los Angeles fires pushed a fresh wave of nonrenewals its way, carrying a policy count and total exposure far beyond anything its designers imagined in the 1960s. The logic of the trade is coherent enough — insurers get to price risk more accurately, and in exchange they have to keep insuring some of the risk their own models tell them to avoid — but it is also, structurally, an admission that the private market alone will not hold this pool together without a regulatory thumb on the scale, which is a theme this book will return to directly in its next chapters.

Retreat is not the only response available once a risk map gets sharp enough to make a location's exposure undeniable. The other response, slower and far more disruptive to the people involved, is managed retreat: the deliberate, planned relocation of a community away from the hazard rather than an attempt to keep insuring or rebuilding in place. The United States has one federally funded example that is often, and not quite accurately, described as the nation's first: the resettlement of Isle de Jean Charles, a narrow strip of Louisiana bayou in Terrebonne Parish that has lost more than 98 percent of its land to erosion and subsidence since the 1950s, shrinking from roughly 22,000 acres to around 320. In 2016, the state won $48.3 million in federal Community Development Block Grant disaster-recovery funds — part of a larger $92 million national competition — to relocate the island's remaining residents, most of them members of the Biloxi-Chitimacha-Choctaw and Isle de Jean Charles Band of Biloxi-Chitimacha, to a new site inland. It was the first resettlement of an entire American community funded end-to-end by the federal government in response to climate-driven land loss, and it took roughly nine years to move fewer than forty households. Reporting published in 2025 by Inside Climate News, the Louisiana Illuminator, and the Center for Public Integrity, revisiting the project nearly a decade after the grant was announced, found a process marked by delayed construction, disputes over who actually qualified as a resident entitled to a new home, tension between the tribal community and the state agency running the relocation, and a persistent sense among some families that promises made in 2016 were only partially kept. None of that reporting concluded the resettlement was a failure exactly — most families did, eventually, get a new home on higher ground — but it landed closer to a warning than a template: even with federal money, a sympathetic press, and a decade of lead time, moving a few dozen families off land the ocean is visibly reclaiming turned out to be organizationally brutal. Scaling that same process to the millions of Americans living in coastal and floodplain zones that current NOAA and FEMA mapping identifies as high-risk is not obviously a matter of finding more money. It may be a matter the country's institutions, as currently built, are not actually equipped to do at scale, however necessary the underlying idea.

The federal government's more modest and more established retreat tool is the voluntary buyout: a homeowner sells a flood-prone property to a state or local government, usually with FEMA hazard-mitigation grant funding behind it, and the land is cleared and deed-restricted as permanent open space. More than 40,000 such buyouts have taken place across the United States since the program's modern form emerged in the 1990s, run through state initiatives like New Jersey's Blue Acres program and New York's post-Hurricane-Sandy NY Rising program alongside FEMA's own grant channels. Academic reviews of these programs, including work published in Science Advances and in floodplain-management journals, have found real risk-reduction benefits — a bought-out home cannot flood again, and the reclaimed land can sometimes buffer neighboring properties too — but they have also found the buyouts persistently concentrated in socially vulnerable communities and inconsistently accessible to the lower-income and minority homeowners who often need them most, since navigating a multi-year grant application while still living in a flood-damaged house is its own kind of means test. Managed retreat, in other words, works technically. It struggles logistically, financially, and politically, and forty thousand buyouts over three decades is a rounding error against the scale of American coastal and floodplain exposure that current mapping describes.

Return, for a moment, to Pakistan, because the two halves of the protection gap — the developing-world gap born of markets that never formed, and the developed-world gap now opening as markets deliberately withdraw — are not actually separate stories once you look at what happens after the loss lands. In both cases, the money that would have come from an insurance payout still has to come from somewhere, and in both cases it mostly comes from the government, whether that government is Pakistan's finance ministry absorbing a fiscal shock equivalent to roughly ten percent of GDP, or California's state-run FAIR Plan absorbing hundreds of thousands of newly homeless policies its private-market architects never intended it to carry, or the United States Congress periodically forgiving billions of dollars of accumulated debt in the National Flood Insurance Program because the alternative is letting the program simply stop paying claims. The protection gap does not describe an absence of risk. It describes an absence of a specific, invented technology — pooled, priced, transferable insurance — for spreading a risk that has not gone anywhere. Somebody still owns the flooded field in Sindh and the burned lot in the Sierra foothills. The only question the gap answers is who, in the end, gets stuck holding it: the family with the field, the taxpayer funding the recovery ministry, the policyholders left in a shrinking risk pool paying for a public insurer's growing losses, or, more often than any of these tidy categories suggests, some uneven and largely accidental mixture of all three, sorted less by fairness than by who happened to be standing closest when the water came up or the fire crossed the ridge.

The reason any of this matters beyond the balance sheets of insurers and the fairness of who gets covered is that the protection gap turns out to have a measurable macroeconomic signature, not just a moral one. Economists studying disaster recovery across dozens of countries and thousands of events have found that a disaster causing damage equal to one percent of a country's GDP tends to shave roughly a quarter of a percentage point off that quarter's growth rate when the losses are effectively uninsured, and roughly two-thirds as much when even a modest quarter of the losses are covered by insurance payouts that arrive quickly and get spent on reconstruction rather than waited on through a slower public appeals process. Insurance, in other words, is not simply a mechanism for making an individual family whole after a fire or a flood. At a national scale it functions as a demand stabilizer, pushing money into the local economy for rebuilding materials, contractor wages, and replacement goods at precisely the moment a disaster has otherwise knocked spending into reverse. Countries with thin insurance markets do not just suffer larger individual losses; they suffer slower, longer recoveries at the level of the whole economy, a fact regulators at the International Association of Insurance Supervisors and researchers at the European Central Bank have both flagged as a reason to treat the protection gap as a matter of financial stability rather than only consumer protection. India's own insurance regulator has put a number on its version of the problem: by its own estimate, only around 15 to 20 percent of the country's catastrophe losses are covered by insurance, a gap the regulator itself has publicly called one of the largest in the world in dollar terms even though India's economy, unlike Pakistan's, is large and diversified enough to absorb the shock without a full-blown fiscal crisis each time. Sovereign-level pooling arrangements have tried to close a sliver of this gap from the top down — the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility, formed in 2007 after Hurricane Ivan devastated Grenada, pays participating governments within one to two weeks of a qualifying hurricane or earthquake, using the same parametric logic this book examined in the context of African drought insurance — but a facility that pays a government tens of millions of dollars still leaves the individual farmer, shopkeeper, and homeowner to rebuild on their own, because sovereign parametric cover was never designed to reach that far down.

What makes the current moment different from earlier decades of protection-gap statistics is not the existence of the gap — it has been documented, named, and worried over by actuaries and development economists for as long as Swiss Re has been publishing sigma reports — but the direction it is moving in wealthy countries that had, until recently, been closing it. For most of the postwar period, insurance penetration in the United States, Western Europe, and Japan rose steadily, decade over decade, as incomes grew and markets matured, even as the developing world's gap stayed stubbornly wide. California's non-renewal wave, and the smaller but similar retreats now visible in Florida's homeowners market, Louisiana's coastal parishes, and parts of coastal Australia, mark the first sustained instance in generations of rich-country insurance penetration for a major peril moving the other way — not because people got poorer or insurers got less sophisticated, but because the sophistication itself, applied honestly to a warming climate and decades of building in harm's way, produced an answer the market did not want to underwrite at any price a homeowner could actually pay. A protection gap born of poverty is a problem with a reasonably clear, if difficult, remedy: build markets, extend capital, subsidize premiums until penetration catches up with development. A protection gap born of a mature market looking hard at the numbers and voluntarily stepping back is a different and harder kind of problem, because it suggests the gap is not a temporary market failure waiting to be fixed. It may be the honest price of a risk that has simply outgrown the pool built to hold it.
