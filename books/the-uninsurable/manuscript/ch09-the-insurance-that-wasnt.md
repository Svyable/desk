# The Insurance That Wasn't

On the afternoon of September 15, 2008, as Lehman Brothers filed for the largest bankruptcy in American history, the three major credit rating agencies were finishing a different piece of work. Moody's, Standard & Poor's, and Fitch had spent the weekend watching American International Group's stock lose more than half its value and listening to a company insist it merely had a liquidity problem, not a solvency one. By that evening all three had downgraded AIG below the AA- threshold written into thousands of contracts the company had signed over the previous decade. The downgrade did not, by itself, cost AIG a dollar. What it did was trigger a set of clauses buried in credit default swap agreements that let AIG's trading partners demand collateral — cash, posted immediately, to cover the falling value of instruments AIG had agreed to make good on if they ever went bad. Calls for collateral that had stood at roughly $23 billion three days earlier jumped to $32 billion. AIG did not have it. It could not borrow it, because the same downgrade that triggered the calls had cut off its access to the short-term credit markets it relied on to fund itself day to day. A company with $1 trillion in assets, one of the largest insurers on earth, was going to run out of cash within hours.

The next day, September 16, the Federal Reserve announced an $85 billion loan to AIG in exchange for a warrant for 79.9 percent of the company's equity — the government, in practice, taking over the insurer to keep it from collapsing. Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson and Fed Chairman Ben Bernanke had concluded that letting AIG fail would be worse than letting Lehman fail, because AIG's obligations ran through the balance sheets of nearly every major bank on Wall Street and much of the banking system in Europe. Robert Willumstad, who had been AIG's chief executive for barely three months after replacing the long-serving Martin Sullivan, was informed by government officials that he would be replaced as a condition of the rescue. Over the following months the $85 billion loan proved not to be enough. By early 2009 the government's total commitment to AIG, through loans, asset purchases, and equity, had grown to $182 billion — a figure that made AIG, for a time, the largest corporate rescue in the history of American finance, larger than the government's exposure to Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or any bank.

What had actually gone wrong at AIG was not, for the most part, the insurance business that had made the company famous. AIG sold property coverage, casualty coverage, life insurance, and specialty lines in more than 130 countries, and that business — regulated, reserved, examined by state insurance commissioners the way regulated insurers are — continued to function through the crisis roughly as it was supposed to. The hole was somewhere else: a unit called AIG Financial Products, headquartered in Wilton, Connecticut, with a trading desk in London's Mayfair district that at its peak employed a few hundred people and generated, in some years, more than $2 billion in profit for a company with hundreds of thousands of employees worldwide. AIGFP was run from 2001 to early 2008 by Joseph Cassano, a Brooklyn-born executive who had joined the unit at its founding in 1987 and eventually earned tens of millions of dollars a year overseeing it. Its core business, by the mid-2000s, was writing credit default swaps — a financial contract that functioned, in every way that mattered to the buyer, like an insurance policy, except that it was not called insurance, was not regulated as insurance, and was not backed by anything resembling the reserves an insurance regulator would have required.

A credit default swap is not a complicated idea once it is stripped of its jargon. One party — call it the protection buyer — holds a bond, or a pool of bonds bundled into a security, and worries it might default. The protection buyer pays a regular premium to a second party, the protection seller, in exchange for a promise: if the underlying bond defaults, the seller will make the buyer whole. That is the entire structure of a fire insurance policy, with "the building burns down" replaced by "the bond defaults." AIGFP was, by the middle of the last decade, one of the largest protection sellers in the world, and the securities it was insuring were increasingly not ordinary corporate bonds but multi-sector collateralized debt obligations — pools of mortgage-backed securities, sliced and reassembled, that Wall Street had spent years persuading rating agencies, and itself, were diversified enough to be safe. AIGFP's swap book covering these instruments grew from about $17 billion in notional exposure in 2005 to roughly $78 billion by 2007, part of a broader derivatives portfolio that ran into the hundreds of billions of dollars. Cassano, testifying years later before the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, maintained that his unit's underwriting had not grown reckless, that it had simply grown large, and that the swaps his team wrote were priced using models that had never, in the historical record available to them, produced a loss.

On August 5, 2007, more than a year before the Fed's rescue, Cassano told investors on a conference call that it was "hard for us, without being flippant, to even see a scenario within any kind of realm of reason that would see us losing $1 in any of those transactions." He added that his unit saw "no dollar of loss associated with any of that business." AIG's chief executive at the time, Martin Sullivan, told the same call that Cassano's confidence let him sleep "a little bit easier at night." That same month, Goldman Sachs had already begun demanding collateral from AIG over the falling value of the mortgage securities its swaps covered — an early version of the same mechanism that would nearly sink the company thirteen months later. By the time AIG filed its annual report the following February, it disclosed $11.5 billion in unrealized losses on the swap portfolio Cassano had described, seven months earlier, as essentially riskless. The gap between those two figures is not best explained by dishonesty. It is better explained by a model that had priced correlation as though it barely existed, built by people who had never lived through a year in which it did.

That last claim was, in a narrow sense, true, and it is the reason AIGFP's collapse belongs in the same story as every other chapter of this book. The models treated each CDO tranche as a distinct risk, diversified by geography, by originator, by the vintage year of the underlying mortgages. In practice nearly all of them were exposed to a single common factor: the assumption that U.S. home prices, considered in the aggregate, do not fall very much, and certainly do not fall everywhere at once. When that assumption failed, starting in 2007 and accelerating through 2008, it failed simultaneously across nearly the entire portfolio, because the loans underneath the securities had been originated under the same lax standards, in the same national housing bubble, in the same brief window when a mortgage broker's incentive to make a loan and a rating agency's incentive to bless the resulting security pointed in the same direction. AIGFP had written what looked, from a distance, like a diversified insurance book. It was in fact a concentrated bet on the proposition that American housing was a stable, uncorrelated national asset class — the same proposition an actuary would recognize, with some alarm, as the independence assumption dressed up in the language of structured finance.

None of this happened without warning, exactly, though the warning came a decade early and from a different direction than the housing market. In 1998, Brooksley Born, then chair of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, pushed to open a regulatory inquiry into the fast-growing, largely invisible market in over-the-counter derivatives, credit default swaps among them. Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan, Treasury Secretary Robert Rubin, and SEC Chairman Arthur Levitt opposed her publicly and successfully, arguing that regulation would drive the business overseas and that the market's sophisticated participants did not need the protection. Congress obliged with a moratorium and, two years later, with the Commodity Futures Modernization Act itself. Levitt would say years afterward, after the crisis had made the argument moot, that opposing Born had been a mistake. The derivatives market Born wanted to examine in 1998 was already large; the credit default swap market alone would grow, over the following decade, from a niche product into one with a notional value estimated in the tens of trillions of dollars, traded bilaterally between banks and dealers with no central registry recording who owed what to whom. When Lehman Brothers failed a day before AIG's downgrade, dealers spent weeks simply trying to determine the net amount owed on the credit default swaps written against Lehman's own debt, because no single party — not the dealers, not the regulators, not the rating agencies — had ever been required to keep a complete map of the market. AIG was the largest single node in that map to nearly fail. It was very likely not the only one that could have.

The deeper problem was not that AIGFP's judgment about housing turned out to be wrong. Insurers are wrong about things regularly; that is what reinsurance, capital reserves, and regulatory oversight exist to absorb. The deeper problem was that nothing built into the structure of the credit default swap market required AIGFP to behave like an insurer at all. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000, passed with bipartisan support and signed by President Clinton in the final weeks of his term, explicitly excluded credit default swaps from regulation as either futures contracts or insurance products, and it swept away a body of older common-law doctrine — the requirement, going back centuries, that a party buying protection on an asset actually hold an insurable interest in that asset, lest the contract amount to a wager rather than a hedge. Under the old rule, you could only insure a house you owned, a life you had a stake in, a cargo you had a claim on; the rule existed precisely to prevent people from betting on catastrophes they had every incentive to cause or root for. Credit default swaps, freed from that requirement, could be — and increasingly were — bought by parties who owned none of the underlying bonds at all, simply speculating on default. And because the CFMA also stripped state insurance regulators of any claim to oversee the product, no one required AIGFP to set aside reserves against the possibility that its swaps might eventually have to pay out.

This is the fact worth sitting with, because it is easy to state and strange to absorb: a regulated insurance company selling a homeowner's policy in New Jersey is required, under state law and industry practice built up over more than a century, to hold statutory reserves and risk-based capital calculated against the plausible scenarios in which its policies might all have to pay at once — a hurricane season, an earthquake, a wildfire year. An insurance regulator can examine the company's books, restrict its dividends, and in the worst case seize it before policyholders are left unprotected. None of that apparatus applied to AIG Financial Products, because AIGFP was legally a derivatives dealer, not an insurer, even though the economic substance of what it did — accepting a premium today in exchange for a promise to cover a loss tomorrow — was indistinguishable from insurance to everyone except a regulator's org chart. AIGFP held essentially no capital against its swap book, on the theory, embedded in its own risk models, that the underlying securities were investment-grade and diversified enough that large simultaneous losses were not a realistic scenario worth reserving against. The company was, in the most literal sense available, running an uninsured insurance company, selling protection against a risk it had not priced correctly and had no legal obligation to reserve for even if it had priced it correctly.

The collateral mechanism made the danger sharper rather than softer. Many of AIGFP's swap contracts required it to post collateral not only when the underlying CDOs actually defaulted, but whenever independent pricing services marked those CDOs down in value, and whenever AIG's own credit rating fell. Both triggers activated in 2007 and 2008, well before most of the underlying mortgages had technically defaulted, because markets had already concluded the mortgages would default eventually and priced the securities accordingly. AIG found itself owing tens of billions of dollars in collateral against losses that had not yet been realized and might never fully be realized, to counterparties who had every legal right to demand the cash immediately. By June 30, 2008, its trading partners had called $15.78 billion in collateral and AIG had managed to post $13.24 billion of it. By September 12, the total called had climbed to $23.44 billion. The rating downgrades three days later did not create a new problem so much as detonate one that had been assembled, contract clause by contract clause, over the preceding several years.

Among the counterparties waiting on the other side of those swaps were some of the largest banks in the world — Goldman Sachs, Société Générale, Deutsche Bank, Merrill Lynch, and others — who had used AIGFP's protection to make mortgage exposure on their own books look hedged and, therefore, to hold less capital against it than they otherwise would have had to. This was, in its own way, the appeal of the whole arrangement to everyone involved before it broke: banks got to treat risky assets as safe because AIG had "insured" them; AIG got to book steady premium income against a risk its models said would almost never materialize; and regulators on both sides of the transaction were satisfied, because each was looking at only half the picture. The Federal Reserve's rescue of AIG was, from this angle, less a rescue of an insurance company than a rescue of the counterparties who had quietly outsourced their own capital cushion to a firm that had none. When the New York Fed later set up a vehicle called Maiden Lane III to buy the distressed CDOs directly from AIG's trading partners in exchange for canceling the swaps, Goldman Sachs received roughly $14 billion, Société Générale about $16.5 billion, Deutsche Bank about $8.5 billion, and Merrill Lynch about $6.2 billion — each made whole at 100 cents on the dollar, funded substantially by public money, a resolution that Congress's oversight panel and outside critics alike would spend years calling a backdoor bailout of Wall Street routed through AIG's books.

There is a version of this story that ends with villains, and Cassano was cast, not without reason, as its central one; he was investigated for years by the Justice Department and the SEC, though no criminal charges were ultimately brought, and the SEC's 2011 settlement with him involved no admission of wrongdoing. But the more durable lesson has less to do with any individual's judgment than with what the episode revealed about the boundary between insurance and finance — a boundary that had always been more a matter of legal label than economic substance, and that the market had, over a decade, learned to arbitrage. Insurance regulation exists because insurance is a promise made today about a payment due on some future contingent date, and every incentive in the industry pushes toward writing more of that promise now and worrying about the reserve later. State insurance law answers that incentive with capital requirements, guaranty funds, and examiners who can walk into a company's offices and ask to see the numbers. When a nearly identical promise is repackaged as a derivative and routed through a subsidiary regulated, if at all, by an office with a few dozen examiners for the entire financial system — AIGFP's primary regulator was the Office of Thrift Supervision, an agency better suited to overseeing savings and loan associations than a global derivatives book — the promise survives, but the discipline does not.

The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, convened by Congress to produce an official account of what had happened, put the regulatory failure in blunt terms in its 2011 report: AIG's collapse was possible because the deregulation of over-the-counter derivatives, credit default swaps chief among them, had "eliminated effective regulation of insurance," including any requirement that sellers hold capital or margin against the promises they were making. The commission found that AIG had engaged in a form of regulatory arbitrage, deliberately locating its swap-writing business in London and placing the whole enterprise under the supervision of the Office of Thrift Supervision, an agency built to examine savings and loan associations, not a multinational derivatives book running into the hundreds of billions of dollars. The OTS, the commission concluded, lacked the expertise and the staff to understand what it was supervising and did not meaningfully try. No single regulator — not the OTS, not the SEC, not the Fed, not the New York State Insurance Department that oversaw AIG's actual insurance subsidiaries — had a complete view of AIGFP's exposure, because no single regulator had jurisdiction over the entire company at once. The gap was not an oversight in the ordinary sense. It had been built, deliberately, by a decade of legislative choices that treated the swap market as too sophisticated, too private, and too important to Wall Street's balance sheets to bear the same scrutiny applied to a homeowner's fire policy.

Congress's answer, in the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, was to try to close exactly that gap: most standardized swaps would now have to be cleared through central counterparties that collect margin from both sides in real time, rather than relying on bilateral collateral agreements that could be renegotiated, delayed, or simply overwhelmed; large derivatives dealers would face capital requirements more comparable, in spirit, to what a bank or an insurer already carried; and a new Financial Stability Oversight Council was given the power to designate companies as "systemically important," subjecting the largest and most interconnected non-bank financial firms to Federal Reserve supervision whether or not any state called them an insurance company. AIG itself was designated systemically important in 2013 and fought for years, ultimately successfully, to have that designation lifted, arguing that a shrunken, chastened AIG posed no comparable threat. The Treasury and the Federal Reserve, for their part, eventually recovered all $182 billion committed to the rescue and then some — a combined profit to taxpayers of roughly $22.7 billion by the time the government sold its last AIG shares in December 2012, a fact AIG's defenders would cite for years afterward as evidence the bailout had been, in the end, a sound trade rather than a giveaway. It was both: taxpayers were made whole and then some, and AIG's counterparties were made whole immediately and in full, while the mechanism that had put all of them at risk in the first place — a promise to absorb correlated losses, sold by an entity with no obligation to reserve against them — remained, a decade and a half later, only partially rebuilt into something a regulator could see coming.

The insurance industry likes to describe its own function, when it describes it at all, in fairly dry language: the pooling of independent risks, priced actuarially, backed by reserves calibrated to a worst plausible year. What AIG Financial Products demonstrated, at a scale that reordered the balance sheet of the United States government for the better part of a year, is what happens when that same promise is made by an entity operating entirely outside the apparatus built over a century and a half to keep the promise honest. The swaps AIGFP wrote were not fraudulent, in the sense that lawyers use the word; they were priced, on paper, by people who believed their own models, models that treated a national housing bubble as a well-diversified basket of local risks. But a promise to pay in a bad year, made by a party with no capital set aside for that year and no regulator with the authority to make it set capital aside, is not really insurance at all. It is a bet dressed in insurance's clothing, and like any bet placed by someone who does not expect ever to have to pay it, it can be made in nearly unlimited size right up until the day the bill actually comes due — at which point it turns out the bill was never AIGFP's alone to pay. It was Goldman Sachs's, and Société Générale's, and eventually, for a while, everyone's who filed a federal tax return in the year the government decided that the alternative to a rescue was worse than the rescue itself.
