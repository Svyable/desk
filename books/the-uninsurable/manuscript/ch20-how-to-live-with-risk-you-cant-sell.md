# Chapter 20 — How to Live With Risk You Can't Sell

The Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety, an industry-funded research group that spends its budget doing things individual insurers rarely have time to do — building full-scale test structures and setting them on fire, deliberately, under controlled and repeatable conditions — published a finding a few years ago that is easy to state and somewhat harder to sit with. A home built with four specific wildfire-hardening features together — a Class A fire-rated roof, noncombustible exterior siding, double-paned windows, and enclosed rather than open eaves — had roughly a 54 percent likelihood of surviving a wildfire without damage. A home with only one of those four features had a 36 percent likelihood. The difference between those two numbers is not produced by insurance, by government policy, by a catastrophe bond, or by any of the elaborate financial machinery this book has spent nineteen chapters describing. It is produced by roofing material, window glazing, and the geometry of a roofline, decided years before any fire arrives, by a builder or homeowner who may never think about insurance at all while making the choice. IBHS's researchers have found a similarly blunt physical fact at ground level: a five-foot buffer of noncombustible material immediately surrounding a house's foundation — no mulch, no dry vegetation, no woodpile stacked against the siding — measurably reduces the chance that wind-driven embers, which start the overwhelming majority of home losses in a wildfire, ever find enough fuel next to the structure to ignite it. Five feet. Not a levee, not a firebreak spanning a whole hillside, just the width of a narrow garden path kept clear and boring on purpose.

This book has spent its length describing an elaborate, centuries-old, and increasingly strained set of institutions built to answer one question: who pays when something goes wrong. It would be a disservice to that whole argument to end by suggesting the answer is simply to build better roofs and call it a day — home hardening reduces wildfire loss, it does not eliminate it, and nothing in IBHS's research implies that a defensible five-foot buffer would have saved a home directly in the path of the firestorm that leveled Paradise, California in 2018 or Lahaina in 2023, where wind speeds and fuel loads overwhelmed distinctions of that kind entirely. But the finding is worth opening this final chapter with, because it points at something the book's closing argument needs and that no amount of financial engineering can substitute for: some risks respond to being made physically smaller in ways no repricing, pooling, or transfer scheme can match, and a civilization that has spent the last three centuries getting extraordinarily good at inventing new ways to spread risk around has had correspondingly less practice at the older, less glamorous work of simply making the risk itself smaller before asking who should pay for what remains.

That imbalance is not an accident of institutional focus; it follows from what insurance is actually good at. A market that prices and pools risk is, by design, a market that treats the underlying hazard as a fixed input and competes on how efficiently to distribute the cost of it. Insurers spend enormous sums on catastrophe models that describe where wildfires, floods, and storms are likely to strike, and almost nothing, historically, on reducing how often those events occur or how much damage they do once they arrive — not because insurers are shortsighted, but because reducing the hazard itself is mostly outside an insurer's control, resting instead with building codes, land-use planning, forest management, and millions of individual property owners' choices about siting, materials, and upkeep. This has begun to change, unevenly and under pressure, as the earlier chapters of this book's final section have already documented: California's regulatory bargain now explicitly credits insurers for pricing in the benefits of home hardening and prescribed fire, and the same catastrophe models that drove insurers out of the riskiest zip codes are, in their newer versions, built to reward measurable mitigation with lower premiums rather than simply redlining the whole area regardless of what an individual homeowner has done to their own structure. The FAIR Plan and its counterparts still function as backstops for correlated risk the private market won't hold, but the newer generation of wildfire policy is trying, deliberately, to make fewer houses need that backstop in the first place, by changing what gets built and how, rather than only changing who pays once it burns.

Florida offers an older and, by now, better-documented version of the same argument, running through wind rather than fire. When Hurricane Andrew tore through Miami-Dade County in August 1992 with sustained winds near 165 miles per hour, it destroyed more than 25,000 homes and caused roughly $27 billion in damage, and the forensic engineering reports that followed found something more damning than simply bad luck: post-storm inspections attributed a large share of the destruction to buildings that failed at their windows and doors first, letting wind pressure into the structure and blowing roofs off from the inside, in a county whose own building code had, on paper, been considered among the nation's strongest before the storm exposed how loosely it was actually enforced. The reforms that followed — a statewide Florida Building Code adopted in 2002, and a Miami-Dade and Broward standard stricter still, requiring impact-rated windows or shutters, minimum five-eighths-inch plywood roof decking nailed rather than merely stapled down, and testing regimes that expose materials to wind-borne debris before they can be certified for use — did not change how insurers price a hurricane. They changed how many homes need a payout in the first place. Engineering assessments after subsequent storms have consistently found post-2002 construction surviving hurricane-force winds at dramatically higher rates than older housing stock built to the pre-Andrew code, sitting on the same streets, facing the same storm. Three decades on, the lesson embedded in Miami-Dade's rebuilt skyline is the same one IBHS's fire researchers reached from the opposite coast: a code written and actually enforced before the disaster arrives does more to determine who needs a claim paid than any innovation in how the claim itself gets priced or pooled afterward.

The wildland-urban fire strategy that produced these standards took decades to arrive at, and it is worth a brief accounting of how wrong an earlier, confident consensus turned out to be, because the correction contains a lesson about humility that applies well beyond forestry. For most of the twentieth century, American fire policy, led by the US Forest Service's long-standing practice of suppressing essentially every wildfire as quickly as possible, treated fire itself as the enemy to be eliminated rather than a natural process to be managed. That policy succeeded, for decades, at suppressing small fires — and in doing so allowed dead wood, dry brush, and dense undergrowth to accumulate across millions of acres of forest and chaparral that had, for thousands of years before European settlement, burned periodically and lightly enough to keep that fuel load in check. A 1963 advisory report to the Secretary of the Interior, led by the biologist A. Starker Leopold and commissioned to address wildlife management in the national parks, argued for something that ran directly against that entrenched practice: that periodic fire was not a threat to be suppressed on sight but a natural process the parks had been actively, harmfully denying their own ecosystems, and that controlled burning was, in the report's own words, the cheapest and most natural tool available for managing the land. Most scientists at the time, along with the general public, had spent decades absorbing the opposite lesson, that any wildfire was an emergency and any deliberately set one an act of recklessness, and the recommendation was not adopted quickly; the National Park Service formally made it policy only in 1968, and parks including Sequoia and Kings Canyon spent the years after experimenting with letting lightning-caused fires burn under controlled conditions and setting prescribed fires of their own. By the time land managers widely accepted the trade they had actually been making — fewer small fires purchased at the cost of eventual, much larger and more destructive ones, once a fire inevitably escaped containment in an overloaded forest — much of the American West was already carrying decades of accumulated fuel that no single fire season, however aggressively burned, could safely clear all at once, and the acreage burned deliberately and safely each year still lags far behind what forestry researchers say would be needed to fully unwind that backlog. The shift toward deliberate, if still incomplete, prescribed burning amounts to an institutional admission that the original strategy had inverted cause and effect: treating every fire as a loss to be prevented had made the eventual, unavoidable fires larger and more correlated across the landscape rather than smaller and more contained, which is close to the same mistake this book found running through Lloyd's LMX spiral and AIG's credit-default-swap book — mistaking the temporary absence of a visible loss for the absence of risk, when the risk was simply accumulating, unpriced and unmanaged, until it broke all at once.

Cyber insurance has stumbled into a comparable role faster and more explicitly than property insurance ever did, largely because software failure is cheaper to observe and correct than a forest's fuel load. Multi-factor authentication — a second login step beyond a password, on a phone or a hardware key — has gone in the space of a few years from a security best practice recommended by IT departments to something close to a mandatory condition of coverage: most cyber insurers now require it on email, remote access, and administrator accounts before they will write a policy at all, and the claims data explains why insurers stopped treating it as optional. One major cyber insurer's own 2024 analysis of denied ransomware claims found that 82 percent of the denials involved a policyholder who had not fully implemented multi-factor authentication, making its absence the single most common reason a claim went unpaid; a large broker's survey of new cyber applications the same year found 41 percent rejected outright on first submission, with missing multi-factor authentication and weak endpoint protection the two most frequent causes. Underwriting has shifted correspondingly, from questionnaires that took a company's word for its own security posture to requests for actual screenshots, configuration exports, and vendor attestations that verify the controls are really in place. This is, in effect, private industry acting as a de facto safety regulator, using the price and availability of coverage rather than statute to push an entire economy's security practices toward a baseline the market has concluded it can no longer profitably insure around — an echo of Merrill's insurance-funded electrical testing laboratory from a much earlier chapter of this book's history, minus even the pretense that the underlying motive is anything other than the insurer's own loss ratio.

None of this, cyber hygiene or defensible space or fire-rated roofing, closes the harder question this book's final chapters have been circling: what a person, a company, or a government is supposed to do about the portion of risk that adaptation cannot reach — the neighborhood built decades ago on a floodplain nobody would permit today, the coastal town whose entire economy depends on land the sea is visibly reclaiming, the software dependency too deeply embedded in the economy to simply diversify away, the AI system already making decisions faster than any liability framework has been built to govern. For that residual category, the honest answer this book has been building toward is neither triumphant nor especially comfortable: sometimes the correct response to an uninsurable risk is not a better insurance product, a cleverer pooling mechanism, or a larger government backstop, but the plain acceptance that the risk has to be carried directly, consciously, and without the illusion of having transferred it to someone else. A retiree in Florida who chooses to keep an older home rather than pay for the wind-mitigation retrofit that would lower her premium has made a decision to self-insure that risk, whether she thinks of it that way or not. A hospital system that joins a group captive rather than pay a commercial cyber insurer's asking price has made the same decision at industrial scale. A family that takes a federal buyout rather than rebuild on a floodplain has made it by accepting the loss outright instead of gambling on the next storm missing them. None of these choices make the underlying risk disappear. What they change is whether the person carrying it knows, clearly and in advance, that they are the one carrying it — which turns out to matter enormously, because the households and institutions that get hurt worst by an uninsurable risk are rarely the ones who consciously decided to bear it. They are the ones who assumed, often reasonably, based on decades of a market that used to price the risk cheaply and reliably, that someone else already had.

That distinction — between a risk knowingly retained and a risk unknowingly abandoned — is where the honest reading of this book's title has to land. "Uninsurable" sounds, on first hearing, like a failure: a market breaking down, an industry shirking its obligations, a promise quietly reneged on. Sometimes that is exactly what it is, and this book has documented real cases of insurers using improved information asymmetrically, retreating from neighborhoods that overlap uncomfortably with decades-old redlining maps, or writing exclusions so broad that a policyholder who thought they had coverage discovers, mid-crisis, that they never really did. But sometimes — more often, this book's evidence suggests, than the discourse around insurance retreat tends to admit — "uninsurable at an affordable premium" is simply the price system finishing an honest calculation and reporting the answer nobody wanted: that a specific plot of land, a specific software dependency, a specific category of automated decision-making, has become, given what is now known about it, genuinely too likely to produce a large loss to spread across a pool of premium payers without either bankrupting the pool or charging a price indistinguishable from simply saving up for the loss directly. A price is a forecast, as this book has argued from its opening chapters onward, and a forecast that keeps saying the same uncomfortable thing, from every insurer independently, in every renewal cycle, using increasingly granular and difficult-to-dispute data, stops being a market failure to be corrected and starts being information worth acting on rather than arguing with.

The forestry version of that same reversal took longer to arrive and required overturning a much more entrenched orthodoxy. A 1963 advisory report to the Secretary of the Interior, commissioned to address wildlife management in national parks and led by the biologist A. Starker Leopold, argued for something that ran directly against decades of federal practice: that periodic fire was not a threat to be suppressed on sight but a natural process the parks had been actively, harmfully denying their own ecosystems, and that controlled burning was, in Leopold's own description, the cheapest and most natural tool available for managing the land. The report's recommendations were not adopted lightly or immediately — most scientists at the time, along with the general public, had spent decades absorbing the message that any wildfire was an emergency and any deliberately set fire an act of recklessness — but the National Park Service formally adopted the Leopold Report's approach as policy in 1968, and parks including Sequoia and Kings Canyon began experimenting with letting lightning-caused fires burn under controlled conditions and setting prescribed fires of their own soon after. It took the better part of a century, several catastrophic escaped fires, and a substantial body of ecological research to move federal land policy from treating fire exclusively as a disaster to be prevented toward treating a controlled amount of it as a form of prevention in its own right, and the acreage burned deliberately and safely each year still lags far behind what forestry researchers say would be needed to fully unwind a century of accumulated fuel. The larger point survives the incompleteness of the correction: an institution can spend decades confidently doing exactly the wrong thing, for reasons that felt obviously correct at the time, and the eventual fix often turns out to be something closer to letting a natural, bounded version of the very hazard being guarded against occur on a schedule humans can control, rather than waiting for it to occur on a schedule nature chooses instead.

Acting on it does not mean retreating into fatalism, and nothing in the record this book has assembled supports the idea that risk, once judged uninsurable, is therefore unmanageable. It means treating the signal the way a good captain treats a barometer rather than the way a gambler treats a losing streak: not as an insult to be overcome through sheer persistence, but as information about a world that has changed and now requires a different set of choices than the ones that used to work. Diversification, at every scale from a household's investments to a nation's economy, remains one of the oldest and most reliable answers, precisely because it is the one strategy that does not depend on finding someone else willing to hold the risk — it works by making sure no single loss, however large, falls entirely on one place, one industry, or one balance sheet. Building codes and defensible space work by shrinking the loss itself rather than redistributing it. Captives and self-insurance work by making the decision to retain risk deliberate and reserved-for rather than accidental and unfunded. Managed retreat, however slow and institutionally painful the Isle de Jean Charles experience showed it to be, works by removing people from a hazard's path entirely rather than continually re-pricing their exposure to it. None of these is a universal substitute for insurance, and none of them is available equally to everyone — a wealthy corporation can form a captive far more easily than a family living paycheck to paycheck can afford a wind-mitigation retrofit, and that inequality is itself one of the harder problems a book like this one can describe honestly but cannot resolve on the page. What all of them share is a refusal of the premise that every risk must, eventually, find a pool willing to hold it, and an acceptance that the search for one can itself become a kind of denial, more comfortable in the moment than the alternative, and considerably more expensive in the years it takes to discover the pool was never really going to hold.

There is an image worth closing on, and it is a small one, chosen because it resists the kind of tidy summary this book has tried throughout to avoid. Somewhere in California right now, on a lot rebuilt after a wildfire took the house that stood there before, there is very likely a five-foot ring of gravel or bare, raked earth running around a new foundation, put there not because a law required it in every jurisdiction — building codes vary, and enforcement varies further — but because an insurer's underwriting checklist, or a homeowner's own hard-earned caution, insisted on it. It is not a dramatic image. It will not be photographed for a magazine cover, and no bond trader will ever price a security against it. It is simply a small, physical, permanent decision to make one property slightly less likely to burn, made by someone who understood, correctly, that no contract signed after the fact would matter nearly as much as the five feet of cleared ground put there before the next fire arrived. The whole machinery this book has traced — the coffeehouse underwriters, the actuaries, the catastrophe bond investors, the reinsurers stacked on reinsurers, the state backstops straining under policies they were never meant to hold at this scale — exists because that kind of small, upfront, unglamorous prevention will never cover every risk a modern economy generates, and someone still has to answer for what it doesn't reach. But the five feet of cleared ground is a reminder that the answer to "who pays" is not always a financial one, and that the oldest, least fashionable tool in this entire book — simply making the danger smaller before asking anyone to price it — has never actually gone out of style. It only stopped being the first thing anyone thought of, once enough clever ways were invented to avoid thinking about it at all.
