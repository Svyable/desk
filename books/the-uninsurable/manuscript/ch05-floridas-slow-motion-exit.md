# Florida's Slow-Motion Exit

On July 12, 2023, Farmers Insurance told the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation that it was leaving the state entirely — not scaling back, not tightening underwriting, but withdrawing its name from the market altogether. The move affected about 100,000 policies covering homes, cars, and umbrella liability. Farmers had already stopped writing new home policies in Florida five months earlier. Now it was done taking chances on the ones it already had. Two days later, AAA announced it would non-renew a "small percentage" of its Florida homeowners policies, citing, in almost identical language, the need "to manage risk and catastrophe exposure." Bankers Insurance, Lexington, and United Insurance Holdings had already gone before them. Farmers was, by most local counts, the fourth or fifth national or regional carrier to head for the exits inside of a year.

None of these companies said Florida had become uninsurable. That would have been bad for business and probably wasn't true in the narrow sense — someone, at some price, would still write a policy on almost any house that hadn't already burned or washed away. What they said, in the careful language of corporate withdrawal, was that they could no longer make the numbers work. The premiums they could charge, constrained by state regulators and homeowners' ability to pay, no longer covered the reinsurance they had to buy to stay solvent, the litigation costs they had to budget for, and the storms that kept arriving on schedule. Florida hadn't stopped being insurable. It had stopped being insurable at a price most insurers were willing to charge and most people were able to pay, which in the business of pooling risk amounts to nearly the same thing.

The state had seen this movie before, and the fact that it had seen it before is itself the story. Florida's home insurance market has been in a state of near-permanent triage since Hurricane Andrew tore through South Dade County in August 1992, and the mechanisms Florida built to manage that first crisis are now most of what's failing in this one.

Andrew made landfall on August 24, 1992, as a Category 5 storm, and it did something reinsurers and homeowner insurers had not fully priced for: it destroyed an enormous number of structures in a small, dense area almost simultaneously. Insured losses came to roughly $17 billion at the time — more than double what catastrophe models of the era had projected for a storm of that path and strength. Eleven insurance companies were driven into insolvency. Others simply stopped writing new homeowners policies in the state, and some tried to cancel or non-renew existing ones on a scale large enough that Florida's legislature imposed a moratorium to stop it. The private market, in other words, had already demonstrated once that it could not absorb a Florida hurricane on its own, and the state had already had to build a substitute.

That substitute arrived in two pieces. The Florida Windstorm Underwriting Association covered wind damage for coastal residents who couldn't get it elsewhere; the Florida Residential Property and Casualty Joint Underwriting Association covered residents shut out of the broader homeowners market. In 2002, the legislature merged the two into a single entity: Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, a nonprofit, tax-exempt insurer of last resort owned by the state. Citizens was explicitly designed not to compete with private insurers — its rates were supposed to be at or above the private market's, so that it would only take on the customers nobody else wanted, and would shrink as the private market recovered. For its first two decades, it mostly behaved that way, ballooning after storms and bad market years and shrinking as private insurers came back in.

Then it stopped shrinking. Citizens had 552,340 policies in force in February 2021. By September 2023, it had 1.4 million — making it, for a while, the largest property insurer in the state of Florida, a distinction the entity was invented explicitly to avoid. The growth was not evidence that Floridians had suddenly discovered a government bargain. It was evidence that the private market underneath Citizens was giving way, one non-renewal notice at a time, and that homeowners with nowhere else to go were landing in the one place required by law to take them.

The reasons the private market gave way are worth taking one at a time, because they compound rather than simply add.

The first is reinsurance — the insurance that insurers themselves buy to survive a bad year. A Florida homeowners insurer, particularly the smaller, Florida-only carriers that make up a large share of the market, cannot plausibly hold enough capital on its own balance sheet to pay claims after a major hurricane; instead it buys reinsurance coverage, largely from global reinsurers in Bermuda, London, and Europe, that will absorb losses above a certain threshold. After Hurricane Ian struck the state's southwest coast in September 2022 — the second-costliest natural disaster in U.S. history behind Katrina, with insured losses estimated by different modelers at somewhere between $50 billion and $65 billion — global reinsurers reassessed Florida risk from top to bottom. At the January 2023 reinsurance renewal, the traditional date on which most annual contracts reset, catastrophe reinsurance rates rose more than 37 percent worldwide, the largest single-year increase since 1992, and closer to 50 percent for buyers concentrated in the United States. Retrocession — the reinsurance that reinsurers themselves buy to lay off their own risk, a layer most people have never heard of and which matters enormously to this story — rose by an average of 50 percent, with some layers up 90 percent, partly because a meaningful slice of the capital backing those contracts was suddenly "trapped": tied up in loss reserves from Ian and unavailable to write new business. Florida-focused insurers, already thin on capital, faced a reinsurance bill that had roughly doubled in a single renewal cycle, and they had no real alternative supplier to shop it to. Reinsurance is a global market; Florida hurricane risk is one line item reinsurers can simply decline to write, and in 2022 and 2023, many of them did.

The second is litigation, and this is the piece of the Florida story that has no real analogue in California's wildfire crisis or the flood program discussed elsewhere in this book — a distinctly Florida invention that turned ordinary claims disputes into an industry of their own. For years, Florida represented a share of U.S. homeowners insurance claims in the high single digits nationally but accounted for a wildly disproportionate share of the lawsuits filed over those claims — figures cited by state regulators and the industry put it at roughly 7 to 9 percent of claims nationwide against, at various points, 70 to 80 percent of homeowners insurance lawsuits. The mechanism that produced this gap was called assignment of benefits, or AOB. Under an AOB agreement, a homeowner facing a leaky roof or water damage could sign over their claim, in full, to a contractor or public adjuster, who would then negotiate — or sue — the insurer directly, with no further need for the homeowner's participation and, critically, in Florida, no financial risk to the contractor's law firm if the case lost, thanks to a one-way attorney-fee statute originally written in 1893 to protect ordinary policyholders from being bullied by big insurance companies. That statute let a policyholder's attorney recover full fees from the insurer if they won even a nominal amount above the insurer's initial offer, while the insurer could never recover its own fees if it won. It had made sense as consumer protection for a century. Paired with AOB and a fast-growing roofing-and-litigation industry that discovered how profitable this pairing could be, it became a machine for generating lawsuits regardless of the merits of the underlying claim, because filing suit was nearly free and settling was often cheaper than fighting. Roofing contractors going door to door after any storm, offering a "free roof inspection," became a recognizable Florida ritual; state regulators and insurers both pointed to a wave of these claims, some for damage so minor it wouldn't have been claimed at all a decade earlier, as a major driver of underwriting losses even in years without a major hurricane.

The mechanics of an actual insurer failing are worth walking through once, because "insolvency" can otherwise sound like an abstraction. United Property & Casualty Insurance Company, a Florida-based carrier that had posted underwriting losses of more than $35 million in most years going back to 2017, was carrying around 135,000 Florida policies when Hurricane Ian hit in September 2022. Its losses from the storm came in well above what it had reserved and reinsured for. State regulators determined in February 2023 that if every asset the company held were liquidated immediately, it still would not be enough to pay every claim UPC owed, which is the statutory definition of insolvency, and the Florida Department of Financial Services placed the company into receivership the same month. Its remaining policies were either transferred to another carrier, Slide Insurance, which took over about 72,000 of them, or canceled outright, with the displaced homeowners routed into Citizens by default. The Florida Insurance Guaranty Association, itself funded by assessments on other Florida insurers, stepped in to cover claims UPC could no longer pay. UPC was one of six Florida property insurers pushed into receivership in 2022 alone, and one of roughly a dozen since 2018. Every one of those failures worked the same way: a company's actual claims came in above what its capital and reinsurance could absorb, and the shortfall didn't disappear, it moved — to a receiver, to the guaranty association, to the policyholders left holding canceled coverage, and eventually to Citizens.

The legislature tried to fix this twice in quick succession, which is itself a sign of how badly the first attempt failed. Senate Bill 76 in 2021 imposed new disclosure and contracting requirements around AOB and roof claims. It didn't work, or didn't work fast enough — Florida's property insurers as a group kept posting large underwriting losses through 2021 and 2022, and Demotech, a rating agency whose financial-strength ratings many mortgage lenders require of the insurers backing the homes they finance, put seventeen Florida insurers on notice for a possible downgrade in mid-2022, ultimately downgrading several outright. A downgrade below the minimum rating required by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac can effectively end a company's ability to write mortgage-eligible homeowners policies overnight, which is its own kind of correlated failure: a company doesn't have to run out of money to become unable to operate, it only has to lose a letter grade. In December 2022, in a special legislative session, lawmakers passed Senate Bill 2-A, eliminating one-way attorney fees for property claims and banning new AOB agreements for residential property losses altogether. In March 2023, House Bill 837 went further, repealing the one-way fee statute across most of Florida's civil litigation, not just insurance, and narrowing the circumstances under which a plaintiff's attorney could recover fees to cases involving a formal offer of judgment or a proven bad-faith verdict.

By most measures available so far, the litigation reforms worked, at least on their own narrow terms. New lawsuits against property insurers fell sharply through 2023 and 2024. Whether that reduction reflects less genuine fraud and abuse, or simply fewer claims being pursued at all — including some legitimate ones that a homeowner without a lawyer now has less leverage to fight for — is a question state regulators, plaintiffs' attorneys, and insurers still answer very differently, and it is worth noting that a policyholder-side account of these reforms describes a system that no longer gives ordinary homeowners much recourse against a lowball offer, while an insurer-side account describes a system finally freed from manufactured litigation. Both things can be true in different individual cases, and the data available publicly does not yet resolve, cleanly, how much of the pre-reform litigation was fraud, how much was legitimate disputes that happened to be economically rational to bring only because fees were one-way, and how much of the post-reform decline represents homeowners with real damage simply giving up.

What is measurable is the state's exposure, and it has been shrinking. Citizens Property Insurance Corporation's policy count peaked around 1.4 million in September 2023 and has fallen since, dropping below one million by December 2024 and continuing downward into 2025, driven substantially by a formal "depopulation" program that pays private insurers to take blocks of Citizens policies back into the voluntary market, more than 428,000 of them since January 2024 alone. Several new or reentering carriers — smaller, Florida-domiciled companies rather than national names — began writing again in 2024 and 2025, encouraged by the litigation reforms and by a new reinsurance backstop the legislature created alongside them, and state officials pointed to Citizens's shrinkage as evidence the market was healing.

It is worth sitting with what "healing" means here, because the word implies a return to some prior condition of health that Florida's insurance market may never actually have possessed. Citizens exists because the state's homeowners, collectively, present a catastrophe exposure that private capital has repeatedly proven reluctant to hold at a price ordinary households can pay — first after Andrew, again through the 2000s hurricane seasons, and again now. Every time Citizens shrinks, it does so by moving policies onto the balance sheets of insurers that are, in nearly every case, smaller and less diversified than the national carriers that have been leaving, meaning the same total hurricane exposure gets redistributed across companies with thinner capital cushions rather than eliminated. And Citizens itself is not actually insulated from the risk it's shedding. Under Florida law, if Citizens ever faces a deficit large enough to threaten its own solvency after a major storm — larger than its accumulated surplus and its own reinsurance and bond financing can cover — it is authorized to levy "emergency assessments," a surcharge that can be charged not just to Citizens policyholders but to nearly every property and casualty insurance policyholder in the state, including people who have never bought a Citizens policy in their lives, layered on top of their existing premiums for years until the deficit is retired. Florida tried this once, modestly, after the 2004 and 2005 hurricane seasons. The mechanism has never been triggered at a scale commensurate with a truly catastrophic, Andrew-or-larger hurricane hitting a dense metropolitan area directly, which is the scenario Florida's coastal population growth has been quietly making more expensive every year since 1992, storm activity held constant.

That population growth is its own multiplier and it rarely gets named as one, because it produces no headline of its own. Florida's population grew from roughly 13 million at the time of Hurricane Andrew to more than 23 million today, and a large share of that growth has occurred in exactly the coastal counties most exposed to hurricane storm surge and wind — Lee County, where Ian made landfall, grew by roughly 40 percent between 2010 and 2020 alone. A storm identical in path and intensity to Andrew or Ian, striking the same coastline forty years from now, will not produce identical insured losses; it will produce far larger ones, because there is simply more insured value sitting in the storm's path each year than there was the year before. Modelers try to account for this "exposure growth" explicitly, but every model is calibrated against a past that had fewer houses in harm's way than the present does, which means the loss estimates going into every reinsurance renewal are, in a sense, always slightly behind the world they're supposed to describe.

There is also the state's own catastrophe fund sitting underneath all of this, quietly bearing risk that rarely makes the news precisely because it's designed to be boring. The Florida Hurricane Catastrophe Fund, created in 1993 in direct response to Andrew, is a tax-exempt trust fund that sells mandatory reinsurance to Florida insurers at rates below what the private market would charge, financed by premiums, by the fund's own investment income, and — if a storm or storms ever exhaust both — by its own authority to issue bonds and levy an emergency assessment on almost the same statewide base of policyholders that backs Citizens. The fund exists because the 1992 experience showed that private reinsurance capacity alone would not reliably show up for Florida at an affordable price, which was true again in 2023, which is either proof the fund is doing exactly the job it was built for or a sign that the underlying problem it was built to paper over in 1993 was never actually solved, only subsidized. Both readings appear in the professional literature on the fund, and neither is unreasonable.

Citizens itself, meanwhile, has spent more than a decade quietly becoming one of the largest single sponsors in the catastrophe bond market described later in this book — not out of any particular enthusiasm for financial engineering, but because it needed somewhere else to put the risk it was accumulating and the capital markets were willing to take a slice of it. Since first sponsoring a bond in 2012 under the name Everglades Re, Citizens has come back to that market year after year; in 2025 it priced a $1.525 billion issuance that was, at the time, the largest single catastrophe bond ever sold, and it entered 2026 with more than $2 billion of its roughly $2.8 billion total risk-transfer program sitting in bonds bought by pension funds, hedge funds, and specialized reinsurance-linked-securities investors rather than by a traditional reinsurer. Every one of those investors is, in effect, a small, willing, well-diversified holder of a slice of a hurricane that has not happened yet. It is one of the more genuinely encouraging developments in this whole story, because it means Florida's hurricane risk is no longer resting on the balance sheets of a handful of thinly capitalized regional carriers alone. It is also not a solution to the underlying problem, only a wider and more sophisticated way of distributing it — the bonds pay out on a schedule and under trigger conditions that were themselves modeled against a hazard that keeps getting recalibrated upward as the state's coastline fills in.

What all of these mechanisms share — Citizens, the FHCF, the depopulation program, the reinsurance renewal cycle, even the litigation reforms — is that none of them creates a single new dollar of capital to absorb a hurricane. Each one is a way of moving the same underlying exposure to a different party: from a national insurer's balance sheet to a smaller Florida carrier's; from the private reinsurance market to a state fund; from Citizens's own book to a private insurer that took a depopulation transfer and a state incentive payment to do it; from a plaintiff's attorney's contingency fee to an insurer's loss ratio and back again, depending on which year's legislation you're standing in. The total hurricane risk sitting over the Florida peninsula on any given August afternoon does not shrink because a bill passed in Tallahassee. It only gets reassigned, at a price someone else agreed, for now, to accept.

The reinsurance market itself illustrates how fast that price can move in both directions, and how little of the underlying physics changes when it does. By mid-2025, barely two years after the hardest renewal in a generation, global reinsurers were reporting the opposite problem: too much capital chasing too little demand for property catastrophe risk, driven partly by a run of comparatively mild hurricane seasons and partly by a surge of new capacity from catastrophe bonds and other capital-markets vehicles, discussed at length elsewhere in this book. Rates fell at the January 2025 renewal, fell again in the June and July mid-year renewals that matter most for Florida-focused reinsurance buyers, and kept falling into the January 2026 renewal, with property catastrophe pricing down somewhere between 15 and 20 percent year over year by most broker estimates — a remarkably fast reversal for a market that had just finished calling 2023 the hardest in a generation. None of this reflects a reassessment of how much wind and storm surge a Category 5 hurricane will eventually deliver to a barrier island in Lee County. It reflects capital availability, investor risk appetite, and a few storm seasons that happened to miss the most exposed places. Reinsurance pricing tracks recent memory and available capital more reliably than it tracks the physical hazard sitting offshore, which is a fact easy to miss when rates are falling and impossible to miss when they aren't.

Florida's insurance commissioner and legislature will likely describe the market's current trajectory — falling Citizens enrollment, new companies entering, softer reinsurance pricing — as a recovery, and in the narrow, technical sense that fewer homeowners are being non-renewed this year than three years ago, they will be right. But recovery from what, exactly, is the harder question, and it is one the state has now answered the same way twice: not by making Florida's coastline less exposed to hurricanes, which nothing short of retreat could do, and not by making the total cost of that exposure smaller, which nobody has figured out how to do either, but by finding new parties willing to hold pieces of it for a while — smaller domestic insurers, a state catastrophe fund, capital-markets investors buying reinsurance-linked bonds, homeowners themselves through higher premiums and higher deductibles, and, if the mechanisms designed to prevent it ever actually fail in a storm large enough, every property-casualty policyholder in the state through an emergency assessment none of them individually agreed to. The risk has not gone anywhere. It has just been circulating, since 1992, in search of someone new to hold it, and Florida's slow-motion exit from the ordinary private insurance market — company by company, legislative session by legislative session — has mostly been the sound of that search continuing.
