# The Psychology of the Underinsured

On July 4, 2025, catastrophic flooding along the Guadalupe River in central Texas killed more than a hundred people, many of them children at a summer camp along the water, in a disaster the region's own emergency planners had modeled and warned about for years. The direct economic damage came to somewhere between $18 billion and $22 billion. In the counties hit hardest, take-up of federal flood insurance was estimated at or below 5 percent of households — meaning that in a stretch of Texas Hill Country known locally, and in FEMA's own flood maps, as flash-flood alley, nineteen out of every twenty homeowners had no flood coverage at all when the water came. This was not an isolated failure of a single community to read its own risk. Across the United States, only about 4 percent of households carry a flood insurance policy of any kind, federal or private, even though flooding is the single most frequent and costliest natural disaster the country faces. Homes sitting well outside FEMA's officially mapped flood zones fare even worse: one 2025 analysis found that 77 percent of at-risk properties outside those zones carry no flood coverage whatsoever, and a Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia study estimated that roughly 70 percent of the nation's annual flood losses — some $17.1 billion a year — go uninsured, absorbed instead by homeowners, by federal disaster aid, or simply by whoever happens to be standing in the water when it recedes.

Howard Kunreuther has spent a career trying to explain why. A professor at Wharton and co-director of its Risk Management and Decision Processes Center, Kunreuther began studying flood insurance take-up in the 1970s and has returned to the same basic puzzle across five decades and a shelf of subsequent disasters: people and firms routinely decline insurance against low-probability, high-consequence events even when the coverage is, by any reasonable financial calculation, worth buying — priced fairly, sometimes subsidized, occasionally close to free at the margin — and even in places where the disaster in question has already happened once before. The puzzle is sharper than ordinary risk-aversion would predict. Standard economic theory says people should be somewhat more willing to insure against a catastrophic loss than an ordinary one, precisely because catastrophic losses are the ones that can bankrupt a household outright; the utility of avoiding ruin is supposed to be worth more, at the margin, than the utility of avoiding a routine expense. What Kunreuther and his collaborators found, again and again, was closer to the opposite. Homeowners are aware, in the abstract, that an earthquake or a flood could destroy their house. They buy the coverage anyway only in the narrow window right after one already has.

Kunreuther's earliest work on earthquake insurance in California, dating to a 1978 study, described a pattern that has held up strikingly well in the decades since: homeowners recognize that a major earthquake could be destructive, but because a destructive one is rare on any individual property in any individual year, most simply set the risk aside — until an earthquake actually strikes nearby, after which insurance purchases spike for a year or two and then decay back toward the baseline as the memory fades. That pattern shows up in the current numbers just as clearly as it did in his original surveys: as of the mid-2020s, only about 13 percent of California homeowners carry earthquake insurance, in a state where the hazard is not exactly a secret. A 2019 study building directly on Kunreuther's framework modeled what it would take to get near-universal earthquake coverage in California and found essentially two paths, both extreme: either a widespread, credible belief that a major earthquake was imminent, or a reduction in average annual premiums by a factor of more than six, from roughly $980 to $160 in 2015 dollars. Short of either condition, the study concluded, most homeowners would keep behaving exactly as they had been — which is to say, not buying it.

The explanation Kunreuther and his longtime collaborator Robert Meyer eventually settled on, laid out at length in their 2017 book The Ostrich Paradox, is not that people are behaving irrationally in some sweeping, dismissible sense. It's that a specific and recurring set of ordinary cognitive habits, each individually sensible in the situations where human judgment first evolved to use them, systematically misfires when applied to rare, catastrophic risk. Meyer and Kunreuther describe six such biases. Myopia is the tendency to weigh near-term costs — this year's premium, due right now — more heavily than a benefit that might not materialize for a decade or might never materialize at all. Amnesia is how quickly the vividness of a past disaster fades from memory, so that a flood insurance policy bought in the panicked weeks after a hurricane gets quietly dropped a few renewal cycles later, once the water damage has been repaired and the memory has dulled into something abstract again. Optimism is the built-in tendency to believe that bad outcomes are somewhat less likely to happen to oneself specifically than to the statistical average — the “it won't happen to me” reasoning that shows up, nearly verbatim, in interview after interview with uninsured flood victims. Inertia is the pull of the default option, the tendency to let an existing policy lapse through simple non-renewal rather than through any considered decision, or conversely to never initiate coverage in the first place because doing so requires an active choice nobody was forced to make. Simplification is the habit of screening out complexity — ignoring a probability altogether rather than weighing it, treating an event either as impossible or as certain rather than holding a small percentage in mind at all. And herding is judging one's own risk by what the neighbors are doing: if none of the houses on the street carry flood insurance, that absence itself reads, wrongly, as evidence that the risk must not be very high.

None of these biases require a person to be careless or foolish. They are, if anything, mostly efficient rules of thumb operating outside the domain they were built for. A short time horizon is a reasonable way to manage most everyday financial decisions, most of which really do resolve within a year or two. Letting recent, vivid memory guide caution is a reasonable strategy against most of the hazards a person actually encounters day to day, which tend to recur on a timescale memory can track. The trouble is that a Gulf Coast hurricane, a California earthquake, or a hundred-year flood does not recur on a timescale that ordinary memory or ordinary financial planning is built to track, and a mind built for the recurring and the near-term consistently underweights the rare and the catastrophic — not because the person doing the underweighting is unintelligent, but because the machinery making the judgment was built for a different distribution of risk than the one actually being faced.

The National Flood Insurance Program has spent decades running an unplanned, nationwide experiment in exactly this dynamic, because Congress made flood coverage mandatory for federally backed mortgages on homes in designated high-risk zones, and then discovered how porous that mandate actually is in practice. Enforcement of the mandatory-purchase requirement has always been uneven — lenders are supposed to verify coverage at the time a loan is written and to force-place a policy if a homeowner lets one lapse, but the Government Accountability Office has documented for years that this enforcement mechanism catches only a fraction of the households it's meant to reach, and coverage frequently lapses quietly in the years after a mortgage closes, once the loan officer who checked the box has moved on and nobody is left checking again. A GAO review found that a significant share of properties in mapped high-risk flood zones — with a mortgage requiring coverage under federal law — simply did not have it. Layer onto that enforcement gap the amnesia problem Kunreuther describes, and the result is a program whose mandatory core keeps eroding for the same behavioral reasons that make the voluntary portion of the market so thin: memory fades, premiums feel like a near-term cost against a distant and uncertain benefit, and a homeowner who has gone five years without a flood starts to experience the premium bill as pure loss rather than as insurance against a risk that hasn't gone anywhere.

FEMA's own attempt to correct the National Flood Insurance Program's chronic mispricing has produced, almost as a side effect, a live demonstration of how tightly underinsurance is bound up with cost as well as psychology. Risk Rating 2.0, the overhaul FEMA implemented starting in 2021, was designed to end decades of underpricing by tying premiums to each property's actual, individually assessed flood risk rather than to the coarse flood-zone categories the program had relied on for years — a change insurers and economists had wanted for a long time, on the reasonable theory that risk nobody prices honestly is risk nobody manages well. The reform worked, in the narrow sense that it made prices more accurate. It also worked exactly the way price theory would predict for a good many households: a study published in the Journal of Catastrophe Risk and Resilience found that in the years after the change, new NFIP policy sales fell somewhere between 11 and 39 percent and renewals fell 5 to 13 percent, with the declines concentrated most heavily among the households facing the steepest premium increases, and disproportionately in lower-income areas — meaning that honest pricing, however necessary, pushed a meaningful share of exactly the households most exposed to flood risk and least able to absorb an uninsured loss out of the coverage altogether. Kunreuther's biases explain why people underinsure even when a policy is affordable and subsidized; Risk Rating 2.0 shows what happens when the psychological tendency to defer a near-term cost against a distant risk meets a price that has simply gotten too high for a stretched household budget to bear, and the two effects compound rather than offset each other.

The same underinsurance pattern shows up in businesses, not just households, and Kunreuther's research agenda has always treated firms as a parallel case rather than a separate one — his earliest published work on the economics of underinsurance, dating to the late 1960s and continuing through a 1984 paper on the causes of underinsurance against natural disasters, drew directly on how corporations, not just individuals, chronically failed to buy catastrophe coverage that a rational cost-benefit calculation would recommend. The pattern has proven durable. Surveys of small and midsized firms conducted well into the 2020s have found that the overwhelming majority believe themselves prepared for a disaster — as high as 94 percent in one U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation study — while barely a quarter actually have a written disaster plan in place, and among small businesses that go on to experience an actual disaster loss, a large majority turn out to have carried the wrong kind of coverage, or not enough of it, for the specific damage that occurred: property coverage without business-interruption coverage, for instance, leaving a firm able to repair its building but not to survive the months of lost revenue before the doors reopen. One estimate put the share of small businesses found to be underinsured, after the fact, at roughly three in four. The mismatch is the institutional version of the same six biases Meyer and Kunreuther describe in homeowners — a business owner's planning horizon is dominated by this quarter's cash flow in the same way a household's is dominated by this month's budget, and the low annual probability of a disaster gets discounted for exactly the same reasons, just wearing a different set of financial statements.

There is a second, less intuitive half of Kunreuther's research program, developed largely with the economist Mark Pauly, and it complicates the tidy story of people simply failing to protect themselves: the same population that systematically underinsures against catastrophic, low-probability losses often does the opposite when the loss in question is vivid, immediate, and small. Extended warranties on household appliances are a familiar example — a product that actuarially makes little sense for most buyers, since the expected payout is well below the premium once the seller's margin is included, and yet one that a large share of consumers buy anyway, apparently because the prospect of an unexpected repair bill on a specific, recently purchased object feels more vivid and more personally threatening than an abstract, larger risk spread across an uncertain future. Trip-cancellation insurance and flight insurance sold at the airport gate work on similar psychology: the loss being insured against is concrete, recent in the buyer's mind because they are standing at the gate about to fly, and easy to picture in specific and personal detail, which appears to make people willing to pay a premium loaded well above the actuarially fair price for coverage against a risk that, calculated honestly, is fairly small. Kunreuther and Pauly have argued that this is not a separate phenomenon from catastrophic underinsurance but the same distortion running in both directions: risk perception here is being driven by the vividness and immediacy of a loss rather than by its actual probability and magnitude, and vividness happens to run in the opposite direction from severity in a great many of the situations people actually face. The earthquake that could destroy the house is abstract, distant, and easy to set aside; the airline losing a piece of luggage next Tuesday is concrete, near, and easy to imagine in detail — so the small, vivid risk gets overinsured and the large, abstract one gets underinsured, in defiance of what a purely rational calculation of expected loss would recommend in either direction.

Researchers working in this tradition have also tested whether the biases can be nudged rather than merely diagnosed, with mixed but not hopeless results. Presenting a rare hazard's probability cumulatively over a longer horizon — not "a 1 percent chance this year" but "roughly a 26 percent chance over the life of a 30-year mortgage" — has shown some ability to increase stated interest in flood insurance in experimental settings, apparently because it converts an easy-to-dismiss annual number into a harder-to-ignore lifetime one, closer to how the risk actually accumulates. Comparable experiments testing simple defaults — automatically enrolling people in coverage and requiring an active choice to opt out, rather than requiring an active choice to opt in — draw on the same inertia Meyer and Kunreuther describe as a bias, but attempt to redirect it toward protection instead of neglect. Results across these interventions have been genuinely mixed; one widely cited experiment specifically testing for a bias toward low-deductible insurance plans, using controlled scenarios with well-specified probabilities and losses, found no strong systematic effect in the predicted direction, a reminder that behavioral interventions validated in one context do not automatically generalize to every insurance decision, and that the underlying biases interact with each other in ways a single nudge often cannot fully counteract. Researchers have also drawn a distinction between "nudges," which quietly steer a decision by changing the default or the framing without requiring the person to understand why, and "boosts," which instead try to improve someone's own capacity to reason about the risk — teaching them, in effect, how to think about a cumulative thirty-year probability rather than simply defaulting them into a policy they never examined. A comparative experimental study testing both approaches on flood insurance demand found each could move stated purchase intentions, but neither reliably closed the gap on its own, which is roughly what a researcher steeped in six separate, mutually reinforcing biases should expect: a single well-designed intervention aimed at one bias, whether by nudge or by boost, still leaves five others standing.

Enforcement failures compound the psychology rather than existing apart from it. The mandatory-purchase requirement attached to federally backed mortgages in high-risk flood zones exists precisely because lawmakers understood, even without the language of behavioral economics, that voluntary uptake alone would leave the program's target population badly underinsured; the Government Accountability Office has nonetheless found, across several reviews spanning decades, that lenders inconsistently verify coverage at origination and rarely follow through on force-placing a policy when an existing one lapses, so that a legal requirement written specifically to override amnesia and inertia ends up relying, in practice, on exactly the kind of ongoing institutional vigilance that amnesia and inertia are equally good at eroding over time.

What makes the underinsurance problem more than a private matter of individual miscalculation is what happens after the disaster the household failed to insure against actually arrives. An uninsured homeowner whose house is destroyed by a flood or an earthquake does not simply absorb the loss alone; in the developed world's political economy, the loss migrates outward; toward emergency federal disaster assistance, toward state relief funds, toward charitable giving, toward the diminished tax base and depressed property values of a devastated community, and often toward the eventual argument, made in the aftermath of nearly every major American disaster in recent memory, that a federal or state government should simply make affected homeowners whole regardless of whether they carried a private policy. That expectation, once it becomes reliable enough for households to plan around, becomes its own behavioral hazard on top of the six Meyer and Kunreuther named — a kind of anticipated bailout that quietly lowers the perceived cost of skipping the premium in the first place, since the household correctly senses that the government, however reluctantly, tends to show up afterward regardless. Individual underinsurance, multiplied across a flood plain or a seismic zone and then backstopped by the expectation of public relief, stops being a personal miscalculation and starts looking like a mechanism that reliably transfers catastrophic private risk onto public balance sheets, one uninsured household and one disaster declaration at a time — not through any single decision by any single actor, but through millions of small, individually reasonable choices to underweight a risk that a well-functioning market was, in principle, standing by to price and to pool.

The uncomfortable implication of Kunreuther's five decades of research is that this outcome cannot be fixed simply by better information. Homeowners in flash-flood alley were not, for the most part, ignorant of the fact that they lived in a flood-prone region; Texas's own hazard maps and emergency planners had been sounding warnings about exactly that stretch of the Guadalupe for years before the July 2025 flood arrived. California homeowners are not, by and large, unaware that they live in earthquake country. The information has largely been available. What has been harder to supply is a set of incentives and defaults robust enough to overcome myopia, amnesia, optimism, inertia, simplification, and herding operating all at once, on a risk that by its very nature shows up rarely enough that none of those biases ever gets fully corrected by direct experience before the next long, quiet, insurance-free stretch of ordinary years sets back in.

This is also why the underinsurance problem resists the fix that usually works for an ordinary market failure, which is simply telling people the truth more loudly. A campground operator along the Guadalupe River in 2025 did not need a better flood map; central Texas already had one of the more sophisticated flash-flood warning histories in the country, built and rebuilt after previous disasters. A California homeowner in a moderate seismic zone does not typically doubt that the ground can move. What both are missing is not information but some mechanism, operating at the moment the annual premium notice arrives rather than at the moment the television crews arrive, that makes the abstract and statistically rare outcome feel as real, for thirty seconds, as the concrete and certain cost of the check. Kunreuther's decades of research amount, in the end, to a fairly narrow and fairly humbling finding: that mechanism has been much harder to build, and much harder to make durable across the long quiet years between disasters, than anyone administering one of these programs originally expected it to be.
