# The Reinsurance of Reinsurance

At 10 p.m. on July 6, 1988, a gas leak on the Piper Alpha platform, 120 miles off the coast of Aberdeen in the North Sea, ignited an explosion that killed 165 of the 226 men aboard and two rescuers, and turned one of the world's largest offshore oil production platforms into a column of fire that burned for three weeks before it finally collapsed into the sea. It remains, decades later, the deadliest offshore oil disaster in history. It was also, in a much narrower sense that has nothing to do with the lives lost, an insurance event that took years to finish happening, because nobody who priced the original policies on Piper Alpha had a clear view of where the risk they were writing actually ended up.

The initial insured loss from Piper Alpha came to roughly $1.4 billion, itself the costliest man-made catastrophe the insurance industry had recorded up to that point. By the time all the claims worked their way through the market, the figure being cited had grown past $16 billion — more than ten times the initial estimate — and the reason for that growth had almost nothing to do with new information about the platform itself. It had to do with a market structure called the London Market Excess of Loss spiral, or LMX spiral, in which Lloyd's syndicates had spent years selling each other excess-of-loss reinsurance on marine and energy risk, syndicate A laying off part of its exposure to syndicate B, which laid off part of that to syndicate C, which sold protection back to syndicate A on some entirely different piece of business, until a single dollar of underlying loss from one exploding platform could pass through the same relatively small pool of Lloyd's capital five, ten, or more times on its way through the web, with a fee extracted and a fresh layer of uncertainty added at every step. Nobody had designed the spiral on purpose, and nobody sitting inside any single syndicate could see the whole shape of it from where they sat. Each underwriter saw only the slice of risk directly in front of them and could reasonably believe they had diversified it by passing part of it along. What they had actually done, collectively, was recirculate the same North Sea disaster through the same market again and again until a bad outcome had been quietly transformed into a catastrophic one, for reasons that showed up on nobody's balance sheet until the checks started arriving.

The men and women who underwrote those Lloyd's syndicates were, for the most part, doing something insurers had done for three centuries by then, since long before anyone called it reinsurance: finding someone else willing to take a slice of a risk too large to hold alone. Lloyd's itself traces its name to a coffee house on Tower Street in the City of London, opened by a man named Edward Lloyd sometime in the mid-1680s, where sailors, ship captains, and merchants gathered to trade shipping news, and where Lloyd began keeping his customers informed with a hand-copied, and later printed, sheet of intelligence about which ships had sailed, which had arrived, and which had been lost. Marine underwriters found the coffee house a convenient place to gather and write policies on the ships and cargoes the assembled patrons were discussing, and over the following century that informal gathering evolved into a formal subscription market: individual underwriters, and later syndicates of underwriters backed by pooled capital, each taking a slice, or "line," of a given risk, so that no single underwriter's ruin depended on any single ship going down. That subscription structure — many capital providers, each taking a manageable fraction of a risk too large for any one of them — is the same basic idea reinsurance runs on today, just scaled up from ships to hurricanes, earthquakes, and exploding oil platforms.

The mechanics of that subscription market, remarkably, still operate on close to the same principle today, even though the paperwork has moved from a coffee-stained sheet of parchment to a digital platform. A broker representing a client with an unusually large or strange risk — a satellite launch, a major construction project, the product liability of a global pharmaceutical company — approaches a specialist underwriter to act as "lead," who negotiates the core terms and premium rate and then agrees to accept a defined percentage of the total exposure. The broker then carries those leader's terms around the rest of the market, on a document still called a slip, and other underwriters, the "followers," each mark their own smaller percentage on it until the whole risk is fully subscribed — 100 percent of it accounted for, but spread across however many syndicates and companies it took to get there, none of them alone on the hook for the whole loss if the satellite fails to reach orbit or the platform explodes. It is the same idea Edward Lloyd's coffee-house patrons were practicing on wooden ships three centuries ago, updated for launches and pharmaceuticals: no single underwriter has to be right about the entire risk, only about the fraction they chose to carry.

Reinsurance itself, the practice of an insurance company buying its own insurance, developed alongside marine underwriting and matured into its modern form in continental Europe in the second half of the nineteenth century, for reasons that mirror almost exactly the argument running through this book. Swiss Re was founded in Zurich in December 1863, prompted directly by the Great Fire of Glarus two years earlier, a blaze that destroyed most of a Swiss town and demonstrated to the insurers who had covered it just how badly a single local insurer's capital could be overwhelmed by a single local catastrophe. Munich Re followed in 1880, licensed by the Bavarian government and founded by a small group led by Carl Thieme, who began operations with a staff of four. Both companies existed to solve the same structural problem Lloyd's subscription market had already been solving for shipping: an insurer that has sold policies against fire, or storm, or any other peril concentrated in one place needs somewhere else to lay off part of that risk, so that one bad year in one town doesn't wipe out a company that has served thousands of ordinary customers well for decades. Reinsurance is, in that sense, simply insurance for insurance companies — the same pooling logic, one level up the chain, taken on by institutions large and diversified enough across geography and peril to absorb a loss that would ruin any single primary insurer standing beneath them.

Retrocession, the mechanism at the center of the Piper Alpha spiral, is that same logic taken up one further level still. A reinsurer, having taken on slices of catastrophe risk from primary insurers all over the world, faces exactly the same problem the primary insurers faced in the first place: too much risk concentrated in too few hands, this time at a much larger scale. So reinsurers buy their own reinsurance — retrocession — from other reinsurers, spreading a portion of what they've already absorbed even further across the global market. In principle, this ought to make the whole system more resilient, distributing catastrophic risk across an ever-widening base of capital until no single failure anywhere can bring down the chain. In practice, as Piper Alpha showed, retrocession can also do the opposite, if the same relatively small circle of reinsurers keeps selling protection to and buying protection from each other rather than from genuinely independent, outside capital — because at that point the "spreading" isn't actually diversifying anything. It's routing the same underlying loss through the same pool of capital repeatedly, extracting fees and adding delay each time, until a market that looks diversified from any single participant's vantage point turns out, from above, to be dangerously concentrated after all. This is the same illusion of independence that recurs throughout this book in different costumes — homes that seemed uncorrelated until the wildfire found all of them the same afternoon, mortgages that seemed uncorrelated until the same housing market fell everywhere at once. At Lloyd's in the late 1980s, it wore the shape of an excess-of-loss spiral that nobody inside the market could fully trace.

Piper Alpha was not, in fact, the single event that broke Lloyd's; it was one bad year layered onto several others in a run of catastrophes and long-tail liability claims — hurricanes, and a slow-emerging wave of asbestos-related bodily-injury and industrial-pollution claims tracing back to American policies written decades earlier, some to the 1930s — that together produced roughly £8 billion in losses at Lloyd's between 1988 and 1992 alone, ultimately closer to £16 billion by the time the full reckoning was done, spread across some 33,500 Names, the individual investors who had traditionally backed Lloyd's syndicates with their personal wealth under unlimited liability. What that crisis, and the market reforms that eventually followed it in the 1990s, means for the individual people who bore those losses is a story this book returns to later. What matters here is narrower: Lloyd's spent the following decade rebuilding itself around corporate capital, capped liability, and centralized oversight — a body now called the Lloyd's market, overseen by managing agents who run individual syndicates on behalf of members, the majority of that capital now supplied by insurance groups, listed companies, and institutional investors rather than by individuals risking their own homes. Fifty-seven managing agents ran syndicates at Lloyd's as of the end of 2025, and in 2021 the market even opened a formal channel, called London Bridge, for pension funds and hedge funds to supply capital directly into Lloyd's underwriting through insurance-linked structures much like the catastrophe bonds described in the previous chapter — a market that nearly collapsed under the weight of concentrated, poorly diversified capital in 1992 now actively courting the same kind of broad, arm's-length capital that saved the catastrophe bond market after its own near-death experience that same decade.

The instrument reinsurers actually sell comes in a handful of basic shapes, and the difference between them matters enormously for how correlated risk gets handled once a bad year arrives. A proportional, or pro rata, treaty has the reinsurer taking a fixed percentage of every policy in a given book of business and a matching percentage of the premium, sharing gains and losses in lockstep with the primary insurer from the first dollar. An excess-of-loss treaty, the non-proportional structure at the heart of the LMX spiral, instead has the reinsurer paying only once the primary insurer's own losses cross a specified threshold, and only up to a further limit above that — cheaper for the primary insurer to buy in an ordinary year, since the reinsurer collects a premium for coverage it may never have to pay out, but capable of concentrating catastrophic losses at exactly the layer where multiple reinsurers, each holding a slice of that same excess layer, all get hit by the same event simultaneously. Treaty reinsurance, whichever structure it uses, covers an entire class of business automatically; facultative reinsurance, by contrast, is negotiated risk by risk, for the unusual, oversized, or hard-to-categorize exposure that doesn't fit neatly under an existing treaty. None of these structures is inherently safer than the others. Each is a different way of deciding, in advance, exactly which losses get shared and at what threshold — and each, as Piper Alpha demonstrated, can hide a concentration of correlated risk behind what looks, contract by contract, like ordinary diversification.

There is a further layer to how Lloyd's specifically manages the concentration risk that nearly destroyed it, and it is worth naming even briefly, because it shows an institution designing rules in direct response to its own worst historical failure rather than simply hoping not to repeat it. Since the reforms of the 1990s, Lloyd's has operated under central oversight — a Council and, functionally, an executive franchise board — that sets minimum standards for every syndicate operating under the Lloyd's name, requires each syndicate to hold capital against a centrally modeled view of its realistic disaster exposure rather than merely its own internal estimate, and maintains a shared Central Fund that every syndicate contributes to and that exists specifically to pay valid claims if an individual syndicate cannot. That central layer is itself a form of internal reinsurance, a pool sitting above all the individual pools, built for exactly the scenario the LMX spiral disguised for years: a loss circulating and compounding faster than any single participant could see coming. It does not eliminate the possibility of another spiral-like blind spot forming somewhere in a market this large and this interconnected. It does mean that the market, having been nearly destroyed once by a correlation nobody could see from inside their own syndicate, built a permanent, funded mechanism whose entire job is to look at the market from above.

Bermuda's rise as a reinsurance capital tells the same story from the capital-formation side, in three distinct waves that map with almost mechanical precision onto the industry's worst years. After Hurricane Andrew in 1992 devastated Florida and strained reinsurance capacity worldwide, a wave of new reinsurers — including firms like RenaissanceRe and Partner Re — set up on the island specifically to write the catastrophe risk that existing capital had just proven reluctant to hold, drawn by Bermuda's light-touch regulation, favorable tax treatment, and proximity to both the U.S. and London markets. After the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks produced the largest insured loss the industry had ever recorded up to that point, a second wave — industry veterans nicknamed it the Class of 2001, and it included firms like Arch Capital, Axis Capital, and Endurance Specialty — raised some $8.5 billion in fresh capital and set up shop on the same island for the same reason. After Hurricanes Katrina, Rita, and Wilma battered the Gulf Coast in 2004 and 2005, a third wave, the Class of 2005, attracted roughly $13.3 billion more, including firms like Validus, Lancashire, and Ariel Re, with nearly $5 billion of that new capital arriving specifically through reinsurance sidecars — the short-term, single-purpose vehicles, discussed in the previous chapter, that let outside investors take a direct slice of a specific reinsurer's book for a single underwriting cycle. Each wave followed the same script: a catastrophe exposes a gap in existing capacity, prices spike to reflect the newly visible risk, and fresh capital rushes in to capture the higher returns those prices offer, eventually competing the price back down until the next catastrophe repeats the cycle. This is the underwriting cycle in its purest form — hard markets following soft ones, soft markets following hard ones, driven less by any new understanding of the physical hazard than by how much capital happens to be chasing it in a given year, and by how recently that capital was last frightened.

The most recent turn of that cycle followed a now-familiar pattern. Hurricane Ian's roughly $50 to $65 billion of insured losses in September 2022, arriving on top of a war in Ukraine, forty-year-high inflation, and rapidly rising interest rates, pushed the January 2023 reinsurance renewal to what brokers and rating agencies called the hardest property-catastrophe market in a generation: global rate increases above 37 percent, U.S.-specific increases nearer 50 percent, and retrocession rates up an average of 50 percent, with some layers rising as much as 90 percent, driven partly by the same trapped-capital dynamic that squeezed catastrophe bond investors in the same period. Global reinsurance capital, measured across both traditional balance-sheet capacity and the alternative capital markets described in the previous chapter, reached a record $663 billion by the end of 2025, and both AM Best and S&P Global maintained stable outlooks on the sector heading into 2026 even as they described pricing as having passed its peak, with combined ratios — the standard measure of underwriting profitability, where a figure below 100 means an insurer collected more in premium than it paid out in claims and expenses — expected to run in the mid-90s through 2026 and returns on equity in the low-to-mid teens, comfortably above most reinsurers' cost of capital. Munich Re, Swiss Re, and Hannover Re, the "big four" continental European reinsurers alongside SCOR, posted a combined €11 billion in earnings for 2024, with Munich Re's non-life reinsurance combined ratio improving to 77.3 from 85.2 the year before — genuinely excellent underwriting results, the kind that historically precede exactly the capital inflow, and the eventual softening, that Bermuda's history of "classes" would predict.

The clearest modern illustration of why retrocession exists at all, and of exactly how it can fail, arrived not from a storm or an explosion but from a virus. When COVID-19 shut down large parts of the global economy in 2020, thousands of businesses with ordinary commercial property policies discovered that many of those policies included business-interruption coverage, and many of their owners believed, reasonably enough, that a government-ordered shutdown of their restaurant or shop should qualify as the kind of interruption that coverage was meant to address. Insurers overwhelmingly disagreed, arguing the coverage required physical damage to the property, and the resulting disputes ran through courts on nearly every continent for years. But the more fundamental problem, one that separate coverage disputes could never fully resolve either way, was structural rather than contractual: the top twenty global reinsurers absorbed something on the order of $12 billion in COVID-related losses, a real but almost trivial sum next to the trillions of dollars in global economic loss the pandemic actually produced, because a pandemic is close to the purest possible case of correlated risk — every business in every country affected in roughly the same weeks, by the same cause, with no geographic diversification anywhere on Earth able to offset it the way a well-spread book of hurricane or earthquake risk normally can. Reinsurers and retrocessionaires exist to average an unlucky region's bad year against a lucky region's good one. A genuinely global, simultaneous shock leaves nothing on the other side of that average to draw on, which is exactly why analysts at the Geneva Association and elsewhere concluded, in the pandemic's immediate aftermath, that pandemic economic-interruption risk sits closer to fundamentally uninsurable than almost anything the industry had tried to price before — not because insurers lacked capital, but because the entire retrocession chain described in this chapter depends on losses being spreadable, and a shock this simultaneous simply refuses to spread.

None of this cycle, hard or soft, changes how much a hurricane, an earthquake, or an exploding oil platform actually costs when it happens. What it changes is who is willing to hold the risk beforehand and at what price, which is a statement about available capital and recent memory more than a statement about physical hazard. A reinsurer in a hard market is not pricing a storm that has become more dangerous; it is pricing the same storm at a moment when fewer competitors are willing to underwrite it and its own capital feels scarcer than it did two years earlier. A reinsurer in a soft market is not pricing a storm that has become safer; it is pricing the same storm at a moment when abundant capital, several quiet seasons, and competitive pressure have combined to make underwriters willing to accept a thinner margin for the same exposure. The retrocession layer sitting behind both kinds of market — the reinsurance of reinsurance, the least visible link in a chain that runs from a homeowner's premium payment all the way up to a syndicate at Lloyd's or a treaty desk in Zurich — exists to spread whatever risk survives all the layers beneath it across the widest base of capital anyone can find. Piper Alpha showed what happens when that spreading turns out to be an illusion, circulating the same loss through the same narrow circle of capital until a bad year became a catastrophic one. The industry's answer, in the decades since, has not been to eliminate the spiral's basic logic — risk still gets passed from insurer to reinsurer to retrocessionaire, one layer at a time, exactly as it did in 1988 — but to widen the base at every layer as far as it can be widened: more reinsurers, on more continents, backed by more kinds of capital, including, increasingly, capital that has nothing whatsoever to do with the insurance industry at all. Whether that widening is wide enough, the next genuinely correlated catastrophe — one large enough, or strange enough, to find the hidden edges of the modern market the way Piper Alpha found the hidden edges of the old one — will be the one to actually test.
