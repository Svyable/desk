## Falsification note for Chapter 15

The chapter's central claim — that underinsurance against catastrophic risk is driven substantially by systematic cognitive biases rather than simple unavailability or unaffordability of coverage — would be overstated if it ignored the Risk Rating 2.0 evidence the chapter itself cites, which shows that price alone, independent of any bias, pushes meaningful numbers of households out of coverage; the honest position is that psychology and affordability interact and are difficult to fully separate in the available data, not that psychology alone explains the gap. Some of the specific behavioral-economics findings summarized here (the low-deductible bias experiment, the boosts-versus-nudges study) come from single studies or small experimental literatures rather than large, replicated consensus bodies of evidence, and behavioral results in this field have a documented history of failing to replicate or generalize across contexts — a caution the chapter states directly. Kunreuther's earthquake-insurance and flood-insurance research is heavily U.S.-focused, and take-up patterns, subsidy structures, and mandatory-purchase regimes differ enough across countries that the specific percentages and dynamics described should not be assumed to generalize to non-U.S. insurance markets without separate verification.
