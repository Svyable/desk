## Falsification note for Chapter 5

The chapter's central claim — that Florida's insurance mechanisms redistribute hurricane risk rather than reduce it — would be undercut if a future direct hit on a dense metropolitan area (Miami, Tampa) were absorbed without triggering Citizens' emergency-assessment authority, without further insurer insolvencies, and without a renewed reinsurance rate shock; no storm since Andrew has yet tested the system at that scale under the post-2022 reforms, so the claim rests on structural exposure rather than a realized worst case. The litigation-reform narrative is also contested: insurer-side sources attribute falling lawsuit counts to reduced fraud, while policyholder advocates argue the same reforms simply removed leverage from homeowners with legitimate but hard-to-prove claims, and the publicly available data as of this writing does not cleanly separate those two effects. Finally, the chapter should not be read as claiming private insurers are irrational or predatory in leaving Florida; the reinsurance and litigation cost increases they cite are independently documented by regulators and rating agencies, and their withdrawals track those costs closely.
