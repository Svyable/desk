# Research Brief — The Uninsurable

## Thesis under test

Insurance is the oldest working technology for turning an unpredictable individual loss into a predictable collective cost. It depends on a quiet assumption: that the past is a usable guide to the future, and that enough of the risks in the pool are independent of one another that a bad year for one policyholder is not a bad year for everyone at once.

Climate change is breaking both halves of that assumption at the same time, faster in some regions than insurers, regulators, lenders, or homeowners have adjusted to. The working thesis:

> Private insurers are the first institution forced to reprice climate risk honestly, because a mispriced policy shows up on their own balance sheet within a year. When they reprice by raising rates or refusing to write policies, the risk they used to absorb does not disappear — it is transferred, in sequence, to state-backed "insurer of last resort" plans, to mortgage lenders and the government-sponsored enterprises that buy their loans, to municipal tax bases and the bond markets that fund them, and eventually to taxpayers and future residents who never chose to hold it and often do not know they do.

The book should be able to survive being wrong. If private markets adapt — through better catastrophe modeling, parametric products, building-code reform, and honest rate-setting — and the last-resort pools shrink rather than grow, that is real evidence against the thesis and the manuscript should say so plainly, chapter by chapter, rather than explaining it away.

This is not a book that argues climate change is uniquely catastrophic on its own terms; it is a book about the financial plumbing that decides who absorbs a loss once the weather has already happened, and about what changes when that plumbing gets rerouted from a diversified private market toward concentrated public backstops.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Withdrawal:** Are private insurers actually reducing new-policy writing and non-renewing existing policies in specific U.S. states, at what scale, and over what years — verified against state insurance-department filings, NAIC data, and insurer disclosures, not just news headlines?
2. **Last-resort growth:** Is exposure (total insured value, policy count) in state FAIR plans, Citizens-type insurers, and other residual markets actually growing, and how does that growth compare to the private market it is replacing?
3. **Reinsurance repricing:** Did global reinsurance rates and terms actually tighten after specific catastrophes (Ian, the 2025 Los Angeles fires, etc.), verified against broker renewal reports (Guy Carpenter, Aon, Gallagher Re) rather than anecdote?
4. **Alternative capital:** Is the catastrophe bond / insurance-linked securities market actually growing as a share of global reinsurance capacity, and who is buying it?
5. **Map accuracy:** Do FEMA's official flood maps understate flood risk relative to independent modeling (First Street Foundation, Army Corps studies), and by how much, with the methodological limits of each source stated honestly?
6. **Mortgage/GSE transmission:** Is there real evidence — peer-reviewed or Federal Reserve/FHFA working-paper evidence, not just plausible narrative — that climate-exposed mortgage risk is migrating toward Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac as banks originate and then sell loans?
7. **Municipal transmission:** Are credit-rating agencies (Moody's, S&P) actually incorporating climate/insurance-availability risk into municipal bond ratings, with real methodology documents and real downgrade or outlook examples?
8. **Migration paradox:** Is population still net-migrating toward high physical-risk, high-amenity regions (Florida, the Southwest) despite rising insurance costs, and what does the demographic and real-estate data show about whether insurance cost is yet suppressing that migration?
9. **International comparison:** How do other systems — the UK's Flood Re, Australia's cyclone reinsurance pool, the EU's insurance protection gap work (EIOPA), and developing-world parametric insurance (CCRIF, African Risk Capacity) — handle the same repricing problem differently, and what do their outcomes suggest about the menu of real policy options?
10. **Agricultural parallel:** Does the federal crop insurance program show the same last-resort dynamic — rising subsidized taxpayer exposure as weather losses rise — as home insurance, as a second, older case of the same mechanism?

## Falsification and counter-evidence to actively preserve

- Insurers exiting a state is not proof of an unmanageable crisis; it can be a rational response to rate regulation that keeps premiums below actuarially fair levels (this is the explicit argument insurers themselves make in California, and it deserves to be represented fairly, not as a strawman).
- Reinsurance and primary rates can harden temporarily after a catastrophe and soften again within a few underwriting cycles; a multi-year rate spike is not automatically a permanent repricing.
- Catastrophe bond investors are not proof of market failure — some economists argue insurance-linked securities are exactly the diversification mechanism that should absorb correlated risk, moving it to global capital markets that can bear it, which is closer to success than failure.
- Migration toward risk can be explained by factors other than mispriced insurance: job markets, taxes, retirement preferences, and housing supply constraints elsewhere.
- Some regulatory reforms (Florida's 2022–23 litigation reforms, California's 2024 Sustainable Insurance Strategy) are explicitly designed to fix the underlying problem and have produced measurable results (new insurer entries, slowing non-renewal rates) that should be reported even where they complicate the thesis.
- FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 is a genuine attempt at more accurate pricing, not merely a burden shifted onto homeowners; the backlash to it is itself evidence of how unprepared households were for honest pricing, which cuts in more than one direction.
- Parametric insurance is not a costless solution — basis risk (a payout trigger firing without matching real damage, or real damage occurring without a trigger firing) is a real and documented limitation, not a footnote.

## Sourcing hierarchy

1. Primary: state insurance-department filings and bulletins, NAIC data, FEMA/NFIP program data, Federal Reserve/FHFA/GAO reports, SEC filings and earnings disclosures, court records and legislative text, insurer and reinsurer investor materials, catastrophe-modeling firm and broker renewal reports (Swiss Re sigma, Munich Re NatCatSERVICE, Aon, Gallagher Re, Guy Carpenter), academic and working-paper research (NBER, Federal Reserve working papers, peer-reviewed journals).
2. Contemporaneous reporting from outlets with a record of accuracy on this beat (Reuters, AP, Bloomberg, the Wall Street Journal, the New York Times, ProPublica, ICT/Inside Climate News, and regional outlets close to the events described), used for events, quotations, and figures the primary sources do not carry directly.
3. Books and long-form scholarship (for example Rebecca Elliott's *Underwater* on flood insurance) may inform framing and should be cited by name when drawn on, never paraphrased into the text as if the idea originated here.
4. No invented statistics, no invented quotations, no composite or invented named individuals. Where a real name and event cannot be verified, describe the pattern generically ("a homeowner in the Pacific Palisades," "a small Florida carrier") rather than inventing a person.
5. Every chapter's research note records source, date accessed, and what specific claim it supports, in the shared ledger schema: `id, year, author_or_institution, title, source_type, book_use, url`.

## Chapter map (20 chapters)

1. The Day the Rate Filing Came Due — cold open on the January 2025 Los Angeles firestorm and the California FAIR Plan's exposure, framing the book's question.
2. What Insurance Is For — the history and mechanics of risk pooling, from marine insurance and Lloyd's coffee house to the law of large numbers and actuarial science.
3. Weather That Won't Diversify — why catastrophic, spatially correlated risk breaks the diversification assumption insurance depends on, and why "stationarity" (the past predicting the future) is the quiet assumption climate change removes.
4. California Leaves the Building — State Farm, Allstate, and the wave of non-renewals; Proposition 103 rate regulation; the insurers' own account of why they left.
5. The Insurer of Last Resort — the California FAIR Plan's mechanics, exposure growth, and the assessment mechanism that spreads its losses back onto every other policyholder.
6. Florida's Slow-Motion Collapse — Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, small-insurer insolvencies, Hurricane Ian, and the 2022 litigation reforms.
7. The Reinsurance Squeeze — how global reinsurance capital reprices after catastrophes, and what that means for the insurers who depend on it.
8. Betting on the Weather — the catastrophe bond and insurance-linked securities market, and the pension funds and hedge funds now underwriting hurricanes directly.
9. The Map Was Wrong — FEMA flood maps, their limits, Risk Rating 2.0, and the private risk-modeling firms filling the gap.
10. The Impossible Math of Flood Insurance — the National Flood Insurance Program's history, debt, and repetitive-loss problem.
11. Fire Season Everywhere — the Marshall Fire, Lahaina, and the wildland-urban interface spreading insurance non-renewal beyond the traditionally understood fire belt.
12. Who Moves Toward the Risk — the migration paradox of population growth in high-risk, high-amenity regions, and what it says about whether price signals are working.
13. The Mortgage That Outlives the Insurance — forced-placed insurance, lender risk, and the evidence on climate risk migrating into the mortgage market and the GSEs.
14. Stranded on the Coast — municipal finance, property tax bases, and the early signs of climate and insurance-availability risk showing up in credit ratings.
15. Managed Retreat — Isle de Jean Charles, post-Sandy buyout programs, and the politics of paying people to leave.
16. The Regulator's Dilemma — insurance commissioners caught between rate suppression and insurer solvency, and California's 2024 regulatory reform.
17. Parametric Insurance and the Developing World — CCRIF, African Risk Capacity, and index-based insurance as a different design answer to the same problem.
18. Global Reckonings — the UK's Flood Re, Australia's cyclone pool, and the EU's insurance protection gap.
19. The Farm Bill Nobody Reads — the federal crop insurance program as an older, larger version of the same last-resort dynamic.
20. Pricing What We Can't Avoid — synthesis: what an honest repricing looks like, who bears the transition, the real policy menu, and what would make this book's thesis wrong.
