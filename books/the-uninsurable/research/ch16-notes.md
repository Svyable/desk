## Falsification note for Chapter 16

The chapter's central claim — that parametric insurance solves speed and moral hazard while leaving correlation risk fully intact — could be overstated if newer multi-variable and graduated-trigger products (mentioned but not exhaustively surveyed here) prove more effective at narrowing basis risk than the Malawi case suggests; the chapter treats 2016 as illustrative of a structural tendency, not as proof that all parametric products remain equally crude a decade later. The Malawi dispute itself is genuinely contested: ARC rejected ActionAid's harshest conclusions and maintains it worked with Malawian technical experts on the model, so the chapter's account should be read as describing a real and well-documented failure of fit between index and loss, not as a settled finding of institutional negligence on ARC's part. Figures on CCRIF and ARC payouts and capital structure come from the institutions' own press materials in most cases, which is appropriate for factual claims about payout amounts and dates but means independent, adversarial financial audits of these structures are not the primary source used here. The chapter's claim about parametric insurance "migrating" into U.S. and UK consumer markets rests on company self-descriptions (FloodFlash, StormPeace) rather than independent market-penetration data, since comprehensive third-party figures on this still-small market segment were not located.
