## Falsification note for Chapter 1

The chapter's central claim — that marine insurance at Lloyd's coffee house represents a genuinely new technology of pooling, distinct from older risk-sharing devices like general average and bottomry — rests on a distinction (loss-sharing after the fact vs. ex-ante risk transfer to uninvolved third parties) that most historians of insurance would accept, but the line between "insurance" and its precursors is drawn differently by different scholars, and some would push the origin of true marine insurance earlier, to fourteenth-century Genoa or Barcelona, rather than crediting Lloyd's with inventing the underlying mechanism at all. Lloyd's is best understood as the place a particular, highly durable, individually-underwritten version of marine insurance was refined and institutionalized in England, not as the site of insurance's absolute invention. The 1780 convoy disaster and the 1906-adjacent framing of "correlation" as a period concept are the author's own analytical framing, applying a modern statistical vocabulary retroactively to explain why the loss was so severe; eighteenth-century underwriters would not have used the word "correlation," and the claim that they understood the mechanism "instinctively" is an inference from behavior (diversifying across many ships) rather than a documented statement of theory. The chapter would be overstated if it implied Lloyd's underwriters had a rigorous, examinable understanding of independence; the more defensible claim is that market practice embodied the principle before anyone could prove it mathematically.
