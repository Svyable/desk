## Falsification note for Chapter 11

The chapter's central empirical claim — that reimbursing ransom payments may increase attack incentives — is presented as a live, contested debate rather than a settled finding, because no rigorous causal study establishes how much marginal ransomware activity insurance-funded payments actually generate; the ~30-35% figure for ransom payments made without insurer involvement (cited to undercut the strongest version of the incentive claim) comes from industry/trade reporting rather than a peer-reviewed study and should be treated as an approximate, self-reported industry figure. The chapter's claim that underwriting standards have modestly improved policyholder security is directionally supported by AM Best and NAIC data on the market's return to profitability alongside declining ransomware frequency among insured firms, but total ransomware losses (including uninsured victims, ransoms never publicly disclosed, and downstream costs like reputational harm) are not fully captured in any single dataset, so claims about the ecosystem's overall trajectory should be read as provisional. Exact dollar and percentage figures for CNA's ransom payment, Colonial's insurance-limit consumption, and 2021 cyber premium growth vary somewhat across the secondary sources consulted (a common pattern in this space, since insurers rarely confirm payment amounts on the record); the chapter uses the most commonly repeated figures across independent outlets and flags in the text, where relevant, that estimates vary.
