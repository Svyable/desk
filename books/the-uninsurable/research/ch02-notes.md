## Chapter 2 source trail — The City That Insurance Couldn't Save

- USGS, "1906 San Francisco Earthquake" historical event pages. Confirms the 5:12 a.m. origin time on April 18, 1906, the roughly 296-mile rupture along the San Andreas Fault, and modern magnitude estimates (7.9, with values up to 8.25 historically proposed). https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/events/1906calif/18april
- Wikipedia, "1906 San Francisco earthquake." Corroborates death-toll revision from an original estimate near 700 to modern estimates exceeding 3,000, more than 28,000 buildings destroyed, and the approximate 98%-fire/2%-shaking split in cause of building loss. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1906_San_Francisco_earthquake
- Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I), "The San Francisco earthquake of 1906: An insurance perspective." Source for the estimate that insured losses were around $235 million, with only about $180 million actually paid, and background on the disaster's effect on insurer solvency. https://www.iii.org/article/the-san-francisco-earthquake-of-1906-an-insurance-perspective
- Insurance Journal, "Lloyd's sees 1906 San Francisco earthquake as a turning point" and Cal Academy of Sciences summary. Source for the alternative, widely cited figure of 243 insurers paying roughly $225 million in claims — exceeding 47 years of accumulated U.S. fire-insurance industry profit — and total economic loss estimates near $500 million (about 1.8% of 1906 U.S. GDP). https://www.insurancejournal.com/magazines/mag-features/2006/05/08/69889.htm
- Insurance Museum, "Insurance history snippet: The 1906 San Francisco Earthquake." Source for the account of German insurers resisting claims in home courts, four German insurers abandoning the U.S. market, the "Earthquake Commission" formed by German and Swiss reinsurers to standardize an earthquake exclusion clause, and the roughly 20 insurers (American, German, Austrian) driven into insolvency. https://insurance.museum/insurance-history-snippet-the-1906-san-francisco-earthquake
- Cited historical accounts of Cuthbert Heath and the Lloyd's response (via Lloyd's own history pages, Wikipedia's "Cuthbert Heath" entry, and secondary insurance-history sources). Source for Heath's instruction to "pay all of our policyholders in full, irrespective of the terms of their policies," the estimated $50 million-plus cost to Lloyd's, and the claim that the episode transformed Lloyd's American reputation. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuthbert_Heath
- Fireman's Fund Insurance Company historical records (OAC finding aid) and company-history summaries. Source for the figures on Fireman's Fund's roughly $11.2 million in estimated claims against about $7 million in assets, the settlement of about 8,600 claims paid half in cash and half in stock, and the $300-per-share stockholder assessment. https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8th8sd2
- Pillsbury Winthrop Shaw Pittman LLP / Robert A. James, "Six Bits or Bust: Insurance Litigation over the 1906 San Francisco Earthquake and Fire." Source for the characterization of post-1906 insurance litigation as substantially resolved through negotiated compromise rather than clean judicial rulings on the earthquake-versus-fire causation question. https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/six-bits-or-bust-insurance-litigation-over-the-1906.html
- Reporting summarizing post-1906 reinsurance history (Hartford-led London negotiations for catastrophe-accumulation reinsurance) and the emergence of anti-concurrent-causation policy language, drawn from Insurance Museum and Triple-I retrospectives on the disaster's institutional legacy.
- NAIC, "Catastrophe Models (Property)" and general secondary summaries of catastrophe-modeling history. Used to support the more cautious, honest claim that formal computerized catastrophe modeling is a post-1992 (Hurricane Andrew) development, not a direct product of 1906, while 1906 nonetheless demonstrated the specific failure mode — geographic accumulation defeating apparent portfolio diversification — that later catastrophe models were built to address. https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/catastrophe-models-(property)
- Secondary historical accounts of Amadeo Giannini and the Bank of Italy after the 1906 earthquake (company-history and popular-history sources). Source for the account of Giannini removing the bank's gold and securities by wagon, the disputed amount saved (accounts range from about $80,000 to about $2 million), and his improvised wharf-side lending operation.

## Falsification note for Chapter 2

The total-loss and insured-loss figures for 1906 vary by tens of millions of dollars depending on the source and the years/companies counted, and the chapter deliberately preserves that disagreement rather than picking one number as authoritative; a reader should not treat any single dollar figure in this chapter as a precise, uncontested fact. The claim that 1906 "seeded" later catastrophe modeling is explicitly qualified in the text — formal, computerized catastrophe modeling is a post-1992 development following Hurricane Andrew, and crediting 1906 with directly causing that later industry would overstate the historical connection. The account of insurer behavior (English/Lloyd's paying vs. some German insurers resisting) reflects the dominant narrative in English-language insurance histories, most of which are written from an American or British vantage point; a fuller accounting would need to weigh German-language sources and the German insurers' own record of the disputes, which this chapter does not have direct access to. The framing of civic boosters downplaying "earthquake" in favor of "fire" is a widely noted historical observation but is necessarily an inference about collective motive rather than a documented, single-author admission of intent.
