## Falsification note for Chapter 7

The chapter credits USAA's 1997 Residential Re transaction as the deal that made the catastrophe bond market real, while noting that earlier AIG (1992) and Hannover Re (1994) transactions have their own claims to being "first"; this is a genuine, unresolved disagreement among market historians rather than a settled fact, and the chapter's emphasis on USAA reflects that deal's outsized influence on subsequent market structure rather than strict chronological priority. The claim that catastrophe bonds "widen who stands behind the risk" would be weakened if a sufficiently large catastrophe ever triggered defaults or forced fire-sales across enough bonds simultaneously to threaten the pension funds and institutional investors holding them — a systemic scenario the market has not yet faced, since even Hurricane Ian's losses, while real, were absorbed without broader financial contagion. The strong 2023–2024 returns cited are a function of a specific post-Ian hard-pricing window and should not be read as the asset class's typical or sustainable return; by the chapter's own account, pricing was already softening as of 2025–2026 as capital competed the spread back down.
