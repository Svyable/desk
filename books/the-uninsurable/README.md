# The Uninsurable

Insurance is one of civilization's stranger inventions: a promise that a stranger's bad luck can be turned into everyone's small, predictable expense. For most of its history, that promise has depended on a quiet mathematical assumption — that the disasters being pooled together are, for the most part, unrelated to one another. A house fire in one town says almost nothing about the odds of a house fire in another. Spread the risk across enough independent households, and the group as a whole becomes almost perfectly predictable even though no individual household is.

That assumption is failing in more places at once than it has before. A warming climate makes wildfire, flood, and hurricane losses in the same region arrive together instead of one at a time. A handful of cloud platforms and security vendors now sit underneath so much of the economy that one bad software update can generate claims from airlines, hospitals, and banks simultaneously. A new generation of AI systems is being deployed faster than anyone has worked out who is liable when one of them causes real harm. In each case, the same pattern repeats: a risk that insurers priced as if it were scattered turns out to be concentrated, and when it breaks, it breaks everywhere at once.

This book is a history of that pattern, told through the people, companies, and disasters that exposed it — from a seventeenth-century coffeehouse on the Thames to the software update that grounded flights around the world in July 2024. It asks a question that gets more urgent every year: when a risk becomes too correlated for private insurance to carry, who ends up holding it — the homeowner, the taxpayer, the shareholder, or, increasingly, nobody at all?

## Editorial principles

1. **Start with a real, dated event.** Every chapter opens with named people, real numbers, and a specific place before naming the underlying mechanism.
2. **Explain the machinery, not just the drama.** Actuarial, legal, and financial mechanisms are described precisely enough that a reader could explain them to someone else.
3. **Prefer primary sources.** Regulatory filings, court records, government data, and insurer or reinsurer disclosures are preferred over secondary summaries wherever they were accessible.
4. **Keep disagreement visible.** Where credible sources disagree on a loss estimate or a causal claim, the disagreement is reported rather than resolved by fiat.
5. **Track the asymmetry.** Who is protected and who is exposed are frequently different people; the book follows both.
6. **Look for what would make each chapter's claim false or overstated.** Boundary conditions and counter-evidence are recorded in the research notes, not argued away.
7. **End with the mechanism intact, not a slogan.** Chapters build toward an insight the reader can apply, not a bulleted list of lessons.

## Continuity motifs

- **Pooling is an invented technology, not a natural fact.** Someone had to build the institutions that let strangers share losses before the idea could feel ordinary.
- **Independence is the whole trick.** Insurance works because most disasters are unrelated to each other; it breaks when disasters start happening for the same underlying reason at the same time.
- **Correlation hides until the day it doesn't.** A risk can look diversified for decades and then reveal, all at once, that it was never really spread out.
- **Risk cannot be destroyed, only moved.** Every chapter follows the same loss as it is passed from a household to an insurer, from an insurer to a reinsurer, from a reinsurer to a bondholder, or from all of them to a government — and asks where it stops.
- **A price on risk is a forecast.** When the forecast is wrong, the price was a promise nobody meant to break, and someone has to absorb the difference.
