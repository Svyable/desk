How Pricing Algorithms Learned to Collude Without Talking, and What It Means for the Price of Everything.

The working thesis is that American and European antitrust law were built around a single, century-old assumption: that fixing a price requires an agreement, that an agreement requires communication, and that communication leaves behind evidence — a recording, a document, a witness — a regulator can eventually find. Pricing software has not repealed that assumption everywhere, but it has opened a wide and rapidly growing space in which a small number of sellers, pooling nonpublic data through a shared vendor, can arrive again and again at prices no one of them could have set alone without appearing to gouge — without a single phone call, email, or meeting a prosecutor could ever subpoena.

The book traces that argument from Robert Crandall's taped 1982 phone call proposing a fare-fixing scheme to a rival airline, through George Stigler's 1964 theory of oligopoly and Robert Axelrod's prisoner's-dilemma tournaments, into a landmark 2020 laboratory experiment in which self-interested, non-communicating pricing algorithms taught themselves to sustain supracompetitive prices, and out into the real-world litigation this century's version of that experiment has produced: the Justice Department's case against the rent-pricing software vendor RealPage, a federal circuit split over a shared casino-hotel pricing algorithm, a wave of municipal and state bans on algorithmic rent-setting, and the European Union's parallel, guidance-based response. It closes with the strongest arguments that this framing overstates the evidence, a return to Friedrich Hayek's account of what a price is supposed to do, and an open question about what happens once AI agents start negotiating on both sides of the transaction at once.

Every case in this book is real, dated, and drawn from court filings, government records, published economics research, and contemporaneous journalism. Where a claim depends on active litigation, the book says so; where a finding comes from a laboratory simulation rather than a documented real-world effect, the book keeps the two distinct. The strongest skeptical case against the book's own thesis gets a full chapter, not a paragraph.
