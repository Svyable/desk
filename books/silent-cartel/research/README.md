# Research Brief

## Thesis under test

*Silent Cartel* asks whether the legal system's century-old model of price-fixing — an agreement, reached through communication, that leaves behind evidence of a meeting of minds — still describes how coordinated, supracompetitive pricing actually happens.

The strong, easy version of the book's claim would be that all algorithmic pricing is secretly collusive. That version is false and the book does not make it. The version the evidence actually supports is narrower and more durable:

> Under specific, identifiable conditions — a small number of sellers, a standardized product or service, and a shared mechanism that pools nonpublic pricing or capacity data across nominal competitors and returns a price recommendation most of them follow — pricing software can produce outcomes that function like a cartel's, without any individual seller ever communicating with another about price. Existing antitrust law, built around proving a human agreement, struggles to reach this pattern cleanly, producing genuine, unresolved disagreement among courts, regulators, and economists about how to respond.

The book should survive contact with contrary evidence. Where algorithmic pricing adoption did not raise prices (partial adoption in the German gasoline market), where courts declined to find liability (Gibson v. Cendyn), and where serious economists dispute the real-world magnitude of the phenomenon, the book preserves that evidence rather than arguing it away.

## Core empirical tests

1. **Historical antecedent:** Did pre-AI shared pricing infrastructure (ATPCO, NASDAQ quoting conventions) already demonstrate that non-communicating coordination is possible using nothing more than a shared observation channel?
2. **Laboratory mechanism:** Do self-interested, non-communicating pricing algorithms with no instruction to collude actually learn supracompetitive, reward-and-punishment strategies under controlled conditions?
3. **Field replication:** Does real-world algorithmic pricing adoption produce measurable margin or price effects outside the laboratory, and under what specific market conditions does the effect appear or fail to appear?
4. **Legal traction:** Has this pattern actually produced enforceable legal consequences (settlements, injunctions, verdicts), and how consistent is that legal treatment across jurisdictions?
5. **Regulatory response:** What have legislatures, city councils, and competition regulators in the United States and European Union actually done in response, and how well has each response actually worked once implemented?
6. **Counter-evidence:** What are the strongest arguments that the entire "algorithmic collusion" framing overstates a narrower, more conditional, and more benign phenomenon?

## Falsification and counter-evidence

Every chapter looks for evidence against the central argument.

- The German gasoline study found collusive margin effects only under near-universal local adoption — evidence the phenomenon is conditional, not automatic.
- The Ninth Circuit's Gibson v. Cendyn ruling found a broadly similar fact pattern to Cornish-Adebiyi legally insufficient, directly splitting from the Third Circuit.
- Serious economists (Jay Ezrielev; contributors to Truth on the Market and the Mercatus Center) have argued specific court rulings adopted flawed reasoning, or that the underlying laboratory literature overstates real-world applicability.
- Revenue management has a genuine, well-documented efficiency case (filled seats, lower average fares, reduced vacancy) that predates and is distinct from the collusion-facilitating mechanism this book focuses on.
- San Francisco's and other cities' enforcement records show that passing a prohibition does not, by itself, demonstrate the underlying conduct stopped.

## Evidence lanes

### I. Legal and historical antecedents
Robert Crandall/Braniff (1982); the electrical-equipment cartel (1961); NASDAQ market-maker odd-eighth avoidance (Christie & Schultz, 1994); ATPCO consent decree (1994); Interstate Circuit v. United States (1939); Theatre Enterprises v. Paramount (1954); Bell Atlantic v. Twombly (2007).

### II. Game theory and economics of tacit collusion
George Stigler, "A Theory of Oligopoly" (1964); Robert Axelrod's prisoner's-dilemma tournaments and *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984); the folk theorem; Chamberlin and Sweezy on oligopoly interdependence.

### III. Algorithmic pricing: laboratory and field evidence
Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò & Pastorello, "Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion" (AER, 2020); Assad, Clark, Ershov & Xu, "Algorithmic Pricing and Competition: Empirical Evidence from the German Retail Gasoline Market" (JPE, 2024); the companion AEA P&P adoption-identification paper; critical replies including "The Illusion of Collusion."

### IV. Litigation
United States v. Topkins (2015) and related Amazon Marketplace poster-pricing pleas; United States and Plaintiff States v. RealPage, Inc. (M.D.N.C., filed 2024); In re RealPage, Inc., Rental Software Antitrust Litigation (MDL 3071, M.D. Tenn.); Gibson v. Cendyn Group (D. Nev.; 9th Cir. 2025); Cornish-Adebiyi v. Caesars Entertainment, Inc. (D.N.J.; 3d Cir. 2026); Meyer v. Kalanick (S.D.N.Y.; 2d Cir. 2017).

### V. Regulation and legislation
Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act (S.3686, 118th Congress; S.232, 119th Congress); San Francisco Rent Ordinance §37.10C and successor city ordinances; California, New Jersey, and New York state bans; EU Horizontal Cooperation Guidelines (2023); NAIC Price Optimization White Paper (2015) and state insurance bulletins; Colorado SB21-169; FTC Surveillance Pricing 6(b) Study.

### VI. Price theory and philosophy
Arthur Pigou, *The Economics of Welfare* (1920); Friedrich Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945); Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, "Fairness as a Constraint on Profit Seeking" (1986).

## Source discipline

- Prefer primary sources: court filings, government press releases and consent decrees, agency reports, bill text, and original academic papers.
- Where only secondary reporting or law-firm client memos were reachable (this book's research was conducted primarily via web search rather than direct court-record access), the source-ledger records that limitation explicitly.
- Distinguish an allegation in a complaint, a finding in a settlement, and a holding in a judicial opinion; never present one as another.
- Distinguish a laboratory or simulation result from a documented real-world effect.
- Record the vintage of every regulatory or litigation-status claim; several remained actively moving as this book was completed in the second half of 2026.
- Never invent a quotation, statistic, or attribution. Where a number could not be independently confirmed with confidence, the manuscript uses qualitative language ("a substantial settlement," "a widely cited figure") rather than a fabricated precise number.
- Preserve counter-evidence and minority legal outcomes rather than selecting only the cases that fit the thesis.
