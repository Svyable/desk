# Pumps in Germany

Germany's decision to house its fuel-price transparency mandate inside the Bundeskartellamt itself, rather than in a separate consumer-protection or energy-regulatory agency, turns out to matter for this chapter's argument in a way worth flagging before turning to the study's substance. Competition authorities, unlike ordinary consumer-protection regulators, bring an institutional disposition already calibrated to ask exactly the kind of question this book has centered throughout: not simply whether consumers can see prices clearly, but whether the resulting transparency serves competition or undermines it. That institutional pedigree likely explains why German researchers and regulators alike were positioned to notice and study the algorithmic-adoption pattern this chapter documents relatively quickly once commercial repricing software began spreading through the market around 2017, compared with, for instance, the multi-year gap between RealPage's software reaching wide adoption and Heather Vogell's 2022 reporting finally surfacing its effects to public attention in the American housing market, examined in chapter eight. A transparency mandate built and housed inside a competition regulator, rather than a generic consumer-affairs office, carries with it a kind of institutional vigilance for exactly the inversion this chapter describes — transparency captured by sellers rather than serving buyers — that a differently sited agency might have been slower to notice or slower to prioritize investigating.

Every serious empirical claim about algorithmic collusion in the real world eventually runs into the same practical problem: real markets do not announce, the way Calvano's laboratory experiment did, exactly when the algorithms switched on. A researcher who wants to know whether real pricing software produces real supracompetitive prices needs an industry where enough sellers adopted the technology, at different but identifiable times, that their behavior before and after adoption can be compared — and, ideally, an industry that already generates enormous quantities of granular, public, high-frequency price data, because that is the only kind of data fine enough to detect the subtle shifts an algorithm's arrival might cause. Germany's retail gasoline market turned out, almost by accident of an entirely unrelated policy decision, to be close to a perfect natural laboratory for exactly this question.

The accident began with a 2011 sector inquiry by the Bundeskartellamt, Germany's federal competition authority, into suspiciously synchronized fuel-price movements at the country's petrol stations — a suspicion that predates, and has nothing directly to do with, the algorithmic-pricing software this book is concerned with, but that produced the infrastructure later researchers would use to study it. The inquiry led to a new law creating the Markttransparenzstelle für Kraftstoffe, the Market Transparency Unit for Fuels, which began operating in December 2013 and required every public gas station in the country to report each price change for regular and premium gasoline and diesel to the government within five minutes of the change taking effect. The stated purpose was consumer protection in the most direct sense: the collected data would feed comparison apps and websites, letting German drivers see, in close to real time, which nearby station had the cheapest fuel before they decided where to pull in, and the underlying price data would also let the Bundeskartellamt itself watch for the kind of suspicious synchronization the original 2011 inquiry had flagged. By the time researchers went looking, the transparency unit was collecting real-time price data from more than fourteen thousand stations across the country — a comprehensive, publicly documented record of exactly the kind Stigler's 1964 theory says a would-be cartel needs and, before this law existed, almost never had this cheaply.

Sometime around 2017, commercial software vendors began offering German gas station operators automated repricing tools built to consume this same government transparency data — the identical, publicly reported price feed created to help consumers comparison-shop — and use it to set each station's own prices automatically, adjusting continuously in response to what competitors nearby were charging. This is the detail worth sitting with before any of the study's findings: the infrastructure a regulator built explicitly to make the market more transparent for consumers, in the hope of disciplining prices downward through easier comparison shopping, turned out to be equally available, at the same cost, to software built to help stations coordinate their prices upward. Hayek's argument about prices carrying dispersed information, examined at length in this book's closing chapters, assumed that better information flow serves the market's collective interest by helping buyers and sellers find each other efficiently. The German fuel-transparency mandate is a case study in how quickly that assumption can invert once the same information becomes cheaply available to algorithms whose interest is not efficient matching but sustained, mutual, tacit restraint.

The choice of retail gasoline as this chapter's central case study, rather than some other European market with comparably rich transparency data, deserves a brief word of justification before turning to the specific findings, because fuel pricing carries a symbolic and political weight in most countries that few other consumer goods share. Gasoline prices are among the most closely watched, most frequently discussed retail prices in almost any national economy, prominently displayed on tall roadside signs precisely because governments have long treated fuel-price volatility as a matter of legitimate public concern, tied closely to inflation politics, transportation costs, and household budgets in a way that, say, the price of a wall poster or a hotel minibar item never approaches. That civic prominence is part of why Germany's Bundeskartellamt built the Market Transparency Unit for Fuels in the first place, and part of why the resulting natural experiment, once academic economists thought to study it carefully, produced findings with an unusually direct and immediate relevance to millions of ordinary consumers' daily lives — a relevance this book's other, less visible case studies, however consequential in aggregate, do not always carry with quite the same immediate, personally felt weight.

It helps, before turning to the study's results, to picture what this actually looks like from behind the wheel, because retail gasoline is a market ordinary people encounter directly, in a way most of this book's other case studies are not. Drive along a commercial strip in a mid-sized German town with three competing stations within sight of each other, and the prices posted on each station's tall roadside sign, readable from a moving car, will very often be identical to the cent, or will differ by only a cent or two, and will change several times over the course of a single day — not because the underlying cost of crude oil or refined fuel is genuinely shifting hour to hour, but because each station's software is watching the other two and adjusting continuously in response. Before the Market Transparency Unit and the repricing tools that grew up around its data feed, a station manager checking a competitor's price meant physically driving past it, or sending an employee to look, a few times a day at most; the posted price stayed put for hours because updating it was a manual, low-frequency task assigned to a person with a dozen other things to do that day. The algorithmic version of the same job runs continuously, costlessly, and with a precision no human employee driving past a rival's sign could ever match, checking and adjusting far more often than the underlying economics of buying and reselling fuel could plausibly require on cost grounds alone.

The margins at stake, while they sound trivially small in isolation, matter more than the raw numbers first suggest, because retail fuel is one of the thinnest-margin businesses in ordinary consumer commerce. A station's markup over its wholesale cost of fuel is often a matter of single-digit cents per liter, with the bulk of a pump price made up of crude-oil cost, refining, distribution, and — in most European countries, Germany very much included — heavy fuel taxation that the station operator never sees as profit at all. Against that backdrop, an algorithmic pricing effect that raises a station's own margin by even a small fraction of a cent per liter, sustained continuously across every liter a busy station sells in a day, compounds into a meaningful shift in the station's bottom line — and, mirrored across every station in a market where the software has produced the same effect, a meaningful and largely invisible transfer from every driver who fills a tank in that market to the stations selling to them. No individual driver would ever notice a cent-per-liter shift as anything other than ordinary price fluctuation, the kind fuel prices have always displayed for reasons connected to crude-oil markets and refinery outages. That invisibility is, in fact, part of what makes this chapter's case study so useful for the rest of the book: the German gasoline market did not require years of contested antitrust litigation, a state attorney general's investigation, or an aggrieved renter's class-action lawsuit to surface. It required four economists, a natural experiment created by an unrelated consumer-protection law, and a dataset patient enough to let a structural break in pricing behavior speak for itself.

The United States has no direct equivalent to Germany's Market Transparency Unit for Fuels, and no comparably clean natural experiment has emerged from American gas stations as a result, though the underlying commercial software business is very much present on this side of the Atlantic as well — companies selling automated fuel-pricing tools to convenience-store chains and independent station operators have been a fixture of the industry for years, marketed on the same promise RealPage used for apartment rents: let the software watch the competition and set the optimal price continuously, rather than leaving the decision to a station manager's twice-daily glance at the signs across the street. American antitrust regulators have occasionally scrutinized consolidation in this specific software niche — the Federal Trade Commission has, at various points, reviewed proposed mergers among fuel-pricing-technology vendors under the same general concern about concentrated pricing infrastructure that runs through this entire book — but the absence of a mandatory, government-run, five-minute real-time price feed like Germany's has made the kind of precise, structural-break natural experiment Assad, Clark, Ershov, and Xu were able to run considerably harder to replicate using American data alone. This is worth noting not as a criticism of American regulators, who cannot study data that does not exist, but as a caution for how this book's evidence should be weighed: the clearest empirical proof that real-world algorithmic pricing produces measurable, adoption-dependent margin effects comes from a market that happens to have unusually rich public data, which means the true scope of the phenomenon in markets with less transparent pricing — most of the American economy, most of the time — is close to unknowable with the same precision, and is very likely underestimated rather than overestimated by the visible cases this book is able to document in detail.

A team of four economists — Stephanie Assad of Queen Mary University of London, Robert Clark and Daniel Ershov at HEC Montréal, and Lei Xu — recognized the opportunity this created and set out to measure what actually happened once German gas stations started adopting the new software. Their central methodological problem was the one this chapter opened with: no public registry recorded which stations had adopted algorithmic pricing or when, because the software vendors, unsurprisingly, treated their client lists as confidential. Assad, Clark, Ershov, and Xu solved this with a clever statistical workaround, examining each station's own pricing behavior over time for a "structural break" — a sudden, sustained shift in how frequently and how finely a station changed its prices, the kind of shift that plausibly marks the moment a human station manager, who might update prices once or twice a day, was replaced by software capable of updating prices dozens of times in an afternoon. Most of the breaks they detected clustered suspiciously close to 2017, the same period independent reporting placed the German market's algorithmic-pricing software becoming widely available, giving the researchers reasonable confidence that they were tracking real adoption rather than statistical noise.

Their results, eventually published in the *Journal of Political Economy* in 2024 after several years circulating as a widely cited working paper, delivered a finding more nuanced, and in some ways more analytically interesting, than a simple story of software making prices go up everywhere it touched. Adoption of algorithmic pricing raised margins — but only at stations that were not local monopolies, and, more strikingly, only in markets with two or three competing stations when every single competing station in that local market had adopted the software. A station using the new pricing tool in a market where its rivals were still setting prices the old-fashioned way saw little to no margin benefit from the software on its own; the effect appeared only once the whole small cluster of local rivals had switched over, at which point margins in that market rose in a way the researchers could trace directly to the timing of the last holdout's adoption. This is, in miniature, an almost perfect real-world confirmation of the theoretical apparatus this book built in chapter two: a "cartel," tacit or otherwise, is only as strong as its ability to monitor every relevant participant, and a market where one seller out of three is still operating on old information is a market where Stigler's detection problem remains unsolved, no matter how sophisticated the other two sellers' software has become.

The critical-mass finding is, if anything, the most important single result in this entire book for readers trying to calibrate how worried to be, because it cuts directly against the simplest and most alarming version of the algorithmic-collusion story — the version in which any algorithm, deployed anywhere, mechanically raises prices the moment it goes live. The German data says something more specific and, properly understood, more useful: algorithmic pricing does not automatically produce collusive outcomes; it produces them only under conditions that echo, almost exactly, the conditions Stigler identified sixty years earlier as the prerequisites for any form of tacit coordination, human or artificial — a small number of sellers, a standardized product, and, crucially, near-universal participation in whatever mechanism allows rivals to observe and respond to each other. Remove any one of those ingredients, and the German evidence suggests the collusive effect weakens or disappears, exactly as it did in Calvano's own robustness checks when the number of simulated competitors increased.

The detection method itself deserves a moment of appreciation as a piece of applied economic craftsmanship, because it illustrates just how indirect the evidence for algorithmic collusion in the wild usually has to be, compared with the clean, directly observable setup Calvano's team enjoyed inside a computer. Assad, Clark, Ershov, and Xu could not simply ask software vendors for a client list, and vendors had no legal obligation to disclose one; instead, the researchers treated each station's own historical price series as a kind of fingerprint, looking for the exact moment its pattern of price changes shifted from the coarse, infrequent, somewhat sticky behavior typical of a manager glancing at a competitor's sign once or twice a day to the fine-grained, high-frequency, closely tracking behavior typical of software checking and responding to rivals many times within a single day. A station that had genuinely adopted algorithmic pricing would show a visible jump in how often its price changed and how tightly those changes tracked nearby competitors, occurring at one specific, identifiable point in its multi-year price history rather than drifting gradually — the statistical signature of flipping a switch rather than a slow evolution in management style. That the researchers were able to locate these switches with enough confidence to build a credible before-and-after comparison, using nothing but publicly reported price data and careful statistics, is itself a small demonstration of the same principle running through this entire book: the evidence for what algorithms are doing to competition rarely arrives as a confession. It has to be inferred, patiently, from the shape the data leaves behind — precisely the kind of circumstantial, pattern-based case that antitrust law, built for a century around direct evidence of agreement, is only beginning to learn how to credit.

There is a second, related finding buried in the same body of research, published by an overlapping set of authors in a companion paper focused specifically on the technical challenge of identifying adoption, that deserves mention because it speaks directly to the enforcement puzzle raised at the end of the previous chapter. If economists studying a market as data-rich as German retail gasoline — fourteen thousand stations, government-mandated five-minute price reporting, years of clean historical data — still had to resort to indirect statistical inference, hunting for structural breaks in pricing frequency, just to figure out which stations were using algorithmic pricing at all, then a regulator trying to build a real-time enforcement case against a specific company's pricing software, in a market with far less comprehensive public data than Germany's fuel-transparency system provides, faces a genuinely difficult evidentiary problem. The German researchers had the luxury of years of hindsight and a uniquely rich, government-collected dataset; a prosecutor deciding in real time whether to bring a case rarely has either.

It is worth returning, briefly, to the irony this chapter opened with, because it recurs throughout this book in different forms and deserves to be named plainly rather than left as background texture. The Bundeskartellamt built its fuel-price transparency mandate for reasons that were, on their own terms, entirely sound and squarely aligned with what a century of competition policy has generally believed about markets: more visible prices help consumers shop around, and comparison shopping disciplines sellers who might otherwise charge more than the market would bear. Nothing about that logic was wrong. What the Assad-Clark-Ershov-Xu study demonstrates is that the same transparency, once algorithmic pricing tools became cheap and widely available, could be captured and redirected to serve exactly the opposite function — not helping consumers discipline sellers, but helping sellers discipline each other, using data a government agency collected and published for the public's benefit. San Francisco's algorithmic rent-pricing ban and the RealPage settlement's restrictions on real-time data, both examined later in this book, can be read as belated attempts to solve, through different legal tools, precisely the problem the German case reveals in its purest, most data-rich form: that transparency is not an unqualified good in a market where the parties reading the data are software rather than people, and where the software's only instruction is to make more money for whoever owns it, with no concept of the consumer-protection purpose the transparency was originally built to serve.

Austria, Germany's neighbor and a useful point of comparison, tried a different regulatory answer to a similar underlying worry, and its experience is worth setting alongside the German case because the two countries chose almost opposite tools to solve what is, at bottom, the same problem. Rather than simply mandating transparency and hoping competition authorities could police whatever emerged from it, Austria's energy regulator, E-Control, imposed a direct constraint on the pricing behavior itself: for roughly fifteen years, Austrian filling stations have been permitted to raise fuel prices only once per day, at a fixed time around midday, while remaining free to cut prices at any moment they choose. The rule, periodically extended by the Austrian government and most recently renewed through 2028, was designed less as an antitrust remedy than as a straightforward consumer-protection measure aimed at the intraday price swings that had frustrated Austrian drivers — but it has the secondary effect, whether or not Austrian regulators framed it this way at the time, of directly attacking the same monitoring-and-response speed that Stigler's theory identifies as the fuel any tacit-coordination mechanism runs on. A pricing algorithm that can only raise its station's price once a day, at a publicly known time, has far less room to run the kind of continuous, rapid reward-and-punishment cycle that Calvano's Q-learning agents discovered on their own in an unconstrained simulated market. German policymakers debating fuel-price volatility in the years after the Assad-Clark-Ershov-Xu results circulated have periodically floated adopting a similar once-daily rule of their own, an implicit acknowledgment that Germany's pure-transparency approach, valuable as it has been for consumer price comparison, may not by itself be sufficient to blunt the algorithmic dynamics the transparency helped enable.

Germany's regulators, to their credit, did not simply shrug at this finding once it reached them. The Bundeskartellamt has continued monitoring the fuel market closely in the years since the Assad-Clark-Ershov-Xu results circulated, and European competition authorities more broadly have cited the German gasoline case repeatedly in their own subsequent guidance on algorithmic pricing risk, discussed at greater length in chapter sixteen's account of how Brussels has approached this problem differently than Washington has. But no German regulator has moved to dismantle the Market Transparency Unit itself, because the underlying consumer-protection benefit — letting an ordinary driver find the cheapest nearby pump before committing to it — remains real and remains popular, and dismantling the system would throw out a genuine public good along with the collusion risk it inadvertently enabled. That tension, between the real benefits of price transparency and the real risk that the same transparency becomes fuel for tacit algorithmic coordination once enough sellers plug into it, has no clean resolution in the German case or in any of the cases that follow in this book. It is, instead, the central design problem every regulator examined in the second half of this book has had to grapple with, in one form or another: how to keep a market transparent enough to work for the people buying in it, without making it transparent enough to let the people selling in it stop competing at all.
