# The Strip and the Boardwalk

The economics of a casino-hotel room carry one further wrinkle worth naming before turning to the litigation itself, because it distinguishes this chapter's case studies from the ordinary hotels examined in chapter seven's account of yield management's spread beyond airlines. A casino-hotel's room revenue is only one part, and often not even the largest part, of a property's total profitability; casino operators have long understood that a guest occupying a room is also a guest positioned to spend money on the gaming floor, at restaurants, and at shows, meaning room pricing decisions in this specific industry have always been entangled with a property's broader calculation of a given guest's total expected value to the house — a metric the industry calls theoretical win, estimating how much a specific guest is statistically likely to lose at the tables over the course of their stay. This entanglement means casino-hotel pricing algorithms, including Cendyn's Rainmaker, plausibly draw on an even richer, more individually granular data profile than RealPage's apartment-focused software does, incorporating not just occupancy and competitor pricing but a specific guest's own gambling history and predicted spending — raising, alongside the hub-and-spoke collusion theory this chapter focuses on primarily, an additional individualized-pricing dimension closely related to the surveillance-pricing phenomenon examined in chapter twelve, layered on top of the collusion concern rather than separate from it.

Casino-hotels sell rooms the same way airlines sell seats and apartment landlords sell leases — a fixed, perishable inventory that generates zero revenue the moment a night passes with a room unsold — which makes them, unsurprisingly, some of the most sophisticated and most aggressive adopters of the revenue-management discipline this book traced back to Robert Crandall's 1985 fare war. The specific software at the center of this chapter, Rainmaker, built its reputation over more than two decades as a dedicated hotel and casino revenue-management platform before Cendyn, a broader hospitality-technology company, acquired it in August 2019, and the product's core function reads as a close cousin of everything RealPage's YieldStar was built to do for apartment landlords: pool operating data across client properties, forecast demand, and recommend a price for every room, every night, adjusted continuously as booking patterns shift.

Two nearly identical lawsuits, filed against different casino-hotel operators in different states using the same underlying software vendor and an almost identical legal theory, produced, within about a year of each other, two federal appellate rulings that flatly contradict one another — a genuine circuit split, in the technical legal sense, on a question central to this entire book: whether a shared revenue-management algorithm, pooling nonpublic pricing and occupancy data across nominally competing properties, can support an antitrust claim under Section 1 of the Sherman Act even without any direct communication between the competing hotels themselves. The first case, filed against a group of casino-hotel operators along the Las Vegas Strip and captioned Gibson v. Cendyn Group, alleged that the defendant hotels had knowingly shared commercially sensitive occupancy and pricing information with Cendyn's Rainmaker platform, which used that pooled information to generate pricing and vacancy recommendations the hotels then followed closely enough, and consistently enough, to produce room rates above what genuine competition among the properties would have generated.

The financial stakes underlying both lawsuits are worth quantifying briefly, because casino-hotel room revenue, unlike apartment rent, fluctuates with tourism cycles in a way that makes any single year's damages estimate considerably harder to pin down than the steadier, more predictable rental-housing market this book examined in chapters eight and nine. Las Vegas and Atlantic City together host tens of millions of hotel-room-nights annually across the properties named in Gibson and Cornish-Adebiyi, meaning even a modest, single-digit-percentage inflation in average room rates, sustained across the multi-year period both complaints allege, would translate into aggregate consumer harm easily reaching into the hundreds of millions of dollars — a scale that helps explain why both sets of defendants, despite the material differences in outcome this chapter documents, chose to litigate the underlying legal theory as far as each circuit's own appellate process would allow, rather than settling quickly the way several of the RealPage landlord defendants ultimately did.

Both cases arrived in court against the backdrop of a pleading standard the Supreme Court had tightened considerably in 2007, and understanding that standard is essential to understanding why these particular factual details — timing of contracts, compliance percentages — carried so much weight in courts that never reached a trial on the actual merits. In Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, the Court held that an antitrust plaintiff alleging a conspiracy under Section 1 could not simply plead that competitors behaved in parallel ways and ask a court to infer an agreement; the complaint had to allege enough specific factual detail to make an illegal agreement plausible, not merely possible, before the defendant would even be required to open its files to discovery. Twombly transformed antitrust litigation practice nationwide, making the initial complaint — the document a plaintiff files before ever seeing a single internal company document — far more consequential than it had been under the more permissive pleading rules that governed cases like Interstate Circuit decades earlier. Both Gibson and Cornish-Adebiyi were resolved, at the appellate level that mattered most, entirely on the question of whether the plaintiffs' initial complaint cleared this plausibility bar — meaning the cases turned not on what Cendyn's software actually did, proven through depositions, expert testimony, and produced documents, but on what the plaintiffs' lawyers were able to allege, and support with publicly available circumstantial detail, before any of that evidence-gathering process had even begun. This is a detail easy to lose in summaries of the two cases, and important to hold onto: the circuit split described in this chapter is not, strictly speaking, a disagreement about whether Cendyn's software actually facilitated a cartel. It is a disagreement about how much a plaintiff has to allege, in a filed complaint, before a federal court will let them try to find out.

The United States District Court for the District of Nevada dismissed the Gibson complaint with prejudice in May 2024, and its reasoning is worth walking through carefully because it became the template the Ninth Circuit would go on to affirm the following year. The district court found that the plaintiffs had failed to satisfy the "concerted action" requirement that sits at the core of every Section 1 claim examined throughout this book, emphasizing three specific factual gaps in the complaint: the defendant hotels had each contracted with Cendyn at different times, rather than joining the arrangement as part of any coordinated, simultaneous decision; the hotels had not shared their sensitive pricing information directly with one another, only with Cendyn as an intermediary; and, perhaps most significantly for the court's reasoning, none of the hotels had committed, contractually or otherwise, to actually accepting Rainmaker's recommendations — each retained, at least on paper, full discretion to set its own prices regardless of what the software suggested. On appeal, a panel of the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the dismissal in August 2025, rejecting the plaintiffs' argument that the hotels' common, voluntary use of shared revenue-management software, standing alone, was sufficient to plead an illegal conspiracy under a hub-and-spoke theory. The Supreme Court subsequently declined to hear a further appeal, letting the Ninth Circuit's ruling stand as governing law across the large western judicial circuit that includes Nevada, California, and several other states — a geography that happens to include most of the country's major casino-resort markets outside of Atlantic City and a handful of tribal gaming jurisdictions.

The second case tells almost exactly the same story with a different set of defendants, a different bridge over a different body of water, and a strikingly different ending. Cornish-Adebiyi v. Caesars Entertainment, Inc. was filed against a group of Atlantic City casino-hotel operators, again alleging a conspiracy built around Cendyn's Rainmaker software, again describing a hub-and-spoke arrangement with Rainmaker as the hub and the competing casino-hotels as the spokes. The factual allegations in the New Jersey case pushed further than Gibson's had, in ways that would prove decisive: the plaintiffs alleged that the Atlantic City defendants fed non-public, real-time room-pricing and occupancy data into Rainmaker, that the software pooled this data across the competing properties to generate coordinated pricing recommendations, and — the specific factual detail that distinguishes this case most sharply from its Nevada predecessor — that the hotels followed those recommendations approximately 90 percent of the time, a compliance rate high enough, the plaintiffs argued, to demonstrate that the "independent discretion" Cendyn's software nominally preserved was, in practice, a legal fiction rather than a meaningful business reality. The United States District Court for the District of New Jersey initially dismissed this complaint too, largely tracking the Nevada court's reasoning about the absence of direct inter-hotel communication and formal commitment to follow recommendations.

On appeal, though, the Third Circuit Court of Appeals reversed the dismissal on July 29, 2026, reviving the case and, in doing so, breaking cleanly from the Ninth Circuit's approach to the identical underlying legal question. The Third Circuit's opinion focused heavily on the 90-percent compliance figure and on the specificity of the plaintiffs' allegations about real-time data pooling, reasoning that a sufficiently high, sufficiently consistent rate of adherence to a shared algorithm's recommendations could itself constitute the kind of "plus factor" evidence this book's earlier chapters traced back to Interstate Circuit v. United States in 1939 — evidence beyond mere parallel conduct that could support a plausible inference of concerted action, even without a document showing direct hotel-to-hotel communication. Legal commentary that followed the ruling was near-unanimous in describing it as the first federal appellate decision to squarely credit a hub-and-spoke theory of algorithmic price-fixing on facts this similar to Gibson's, making it, as several antitrust practice groups put it in client memos published within days of the decision, the most significant appellate ruling on algorithmic pricing conspiracies issued anywhere in the country to that point. Law firms that had spent the preceding two years advising corporate clients that the Ninth Circuit's Gibson decision offered a reasonably safe harbor for shared revenue-management arrangements — provided the software preserved nominal client discretion and clients had not adopted it in visible lockstep — reversed course within days of the Cornish-Adebiyi opinion, issuing updated guidance warning that any company relying on shared algorithmic pricing software, in any industry, should now expect meaningfully higher litigation risk if its adoption pattern and its clients' compliance rate with the software's recommendations looked anything like what the Third Circuit had found sufficient. The abruptness of that shift in professional advice is itself a useful barometer of how unsettled this area of law remains: the standard corporate playbook for managing legal risk around shared pricing software changed meaningfully within a single news cycle, on the strength of one appellate panel's reading of one set of facts.

Picture, for a moment, what any of this looks like from the perspective of someone simply trying to book a hotel room for a weekend in either city, because the abstraction of hub-and-spoke doctrine can obscure how directly this dispute touches an experience millions of travelers have every year. A visitor comparing room rates across five or six casino-hotels along the Las Vegas Strip, or along the Atlantic City Boardwalk, for the same weekend, sees what looks like a normal, competitive market — different brands, different room categories, prices that shift by the day and sometimes by the hour as the date approaches. What neither lawsuit disputes, because both sets of defendants have acknowledged using Cendyn's software as a matter of undisputed fact, is that a meaningful share of those seemingly independent prices were generated, at least in part, by the same underlying model, fed by data the properties would never have shared directly with each other and would never have disclosed to the shopper comparing their listings. Whether that shared origin makes the resulting prices the product of an illegal conspiracy, or simply the predictable and lawful result of a competitive industry adopting the best available forecasting technology, is precisely the question on which the Ninth and Third Circuits have now split — a split a traveler pricing out a weekend trip has no way to see, and no way to know applies differently depending on which coast they happen to be visiting.

The practical consequence of this split is genuinely strange, and it is worth sitting with the strangeness rather than rushing past it, because it captures something true about the state of American antitrust law at the exact moment this book was written that no single case, on its own, could convey as clearly. A casino-hotel operator running the identical Rainmaker software, ingesting the identical kind of pooled, nonpublic competitor data, and following the software's recommendations at a similarly high rate, faces a meaningfully different legal reality today depending on which side of the country the property sits on. A hotel on the Las Vegas Strip, within the Ninth Circuit's jurisdiction, can point to controlling appellate precedent holding that this fact pattern does not, without more, support a Section 1 claim. A hotel on the Atlantic City Boardwalk, within the Third Circuit's jurisdiction, now faces the opposite: controlling precedent holding that a comparable fact pattern can proceed past a motion to dismiss and into discovery, where a plaintiff might eventually obtain the kind of internal documents, code, or communications that could make or break the underlying conspiracy claim on the actual merits. Two circuits, one statute, one broadly similar software product, two opposite rules — the textbook definition of the kind of split federal appellate circuits periodically produce and that the Supreme Court exists, in part, to eventually resolve.

Whether the Supreme Court will actually take up the question remains genuinely uncertain as this book goes to print. The Court's 2025 denial of certiorari in Gibson closed off one obvious vehicle for resolving the split, but that denial came before the Third Circuit's Cornish-Adebiyi decision created the split in the first place, and the Court's own practice generally favors granting review once a genuine, well-developed disagreement between circuits has emerged rather than when only one circuit has spoken. Cendyn and the Atlantic City defendants retain the option of seeking further review of the Third Circuit's decision, either through a request for rehearing before the full circuit or a subsequent petition to the Supreme Court, and antitrust litigators tracking the case have widely predicted that some form of further appellate review is likely, given the stakes for an entire industry watching to see which circuit's rule will eventually govern nationally.

There is a deeper analytical lesson embedded in the specific factual distinction that separated the two cases, one that connects directly back to the theoretical machinery this book built in its earliest chapters and that deserves to be named explicitly. The Ninth Circuit's Gibson opinion and the Third Circuit's Cornish-Adebiyi opinion did not disagree, at bottom, about whether hub-and-spoke conspiracies can exist in principle — both panels accepted, at least implicitly, that Interstate Circuit's doctrine remains good law and could in principle apply to a modern algorithmic pricing arrangement. What separated the two rulings was a judgment call about how much circumstantial evidence — a "plus factor," in the antitrust jargon this book has used throughout — is enough to nudge parallel conduct across the line from ordinary competitive behavior into an inferable, sanctionable agreement. A 90-percent compliance rate with a shared algorithm's recommendations struck the Third Circuit panel as substantial enough evidence of the "meeting of minds" underlying doctrine to survive a motion to dismiss; a comparable, though somewhat less precisely quantified, pattern of algorithm-following struck the Ninth Circuit panel as still consistent with genuinely independent business judgment. Both readings are legally defensible applications of the same underlying doctrine to genuinely ambiguous facts, which is precisely why serious, careful federal judges, working from the same statute and broadly similar records, could reach opposite conclusions without either side committing an obvious legal error — a state of affairs the next several chapters of this book will show recurring across nearly every domain where algorithmic pricing and antitrust law have collided so far.

The gaming industry's own regulatory structure adds a further layer of complexity worth noting before turning to Cendyn's defense, because casino-hotels operate under a degree of state-level scrutiny most ordinary hotels do not. Nevada's Gaming Control Board and New Jersey's Casino Control Commission both maintain extensive oversight authority over the casino-hotel properties named in Gibson and Cornish-Adebiyi respectively, authority that in principle extends to reviewing business practices well beyond the gaming floor itself. Neither regulator has, as of this book's writing, brought an independent enforcement action against Rainmaker's pricing mechanism specifically, a restraint that stands in some contrast to state insurance commissioners' comparatively swift action against price optimization, examined in chapter fourteen, and that may reflect the narrower statutory mandate gaming regulators generally operate under — chartered to police the integrity of gambling operations and the financial fitness of casino operators, not, in most states' regulatory schemes, general consumer pricing practices in the hotel rooms attached to the casino floor. That gap between an industry's heavy regulatory presence in one domain and its comparative freedom in an adjacent one recurs throughout this book: airlines are intensively regulated for safety but only lightly for pricing conduct beyond antitrust law; insurers are intensively regulated for pricing but face little sector-specific oversight of the algorithms underlying their claims-denial decisions, examined in chapter fourteen. A regulator's statutory mandate, this book's cases suggest again and again, rarely maps cleanly onto the full range of ways a modern, software-mediated business can actually affect the people who rely on it.

Cendyn itself has maintained, throughout both pieces of litigation, essentially the same defense RealPage advanced in the case examined two chapters earlier: that Rainmaker offers pricing recommendations rather than binding instructions, that participating hotels retain genuine discretion to disregard those recommendations, and that a software vendor cannot be held liable for an antitrust conspiracy simply because a large number of its clients happen to find its recommendations useful enough to follow most of the time. That defense carries real intuitive force — plenty of lawful business software, from accounting platforms to inventory-management systems, gets used by the overwhelming majority of clients in the overwhelming majority of the ways its vendor recommends, without anyone treating high adoption of good advice as evidence of a criminal conspiracy. The unresolved question both circuits were really wrestling with, underneath the technical pleading-standard analysis, is where exactly the line sits between a genuinely useful piece of software that happens to be popular and a piece of software whose usefulness depends specifically on its ability to coordinate nominally independent competitors' behavior in a way none of them could lawfully coordinate directly. Neither the Ninth Circuit nor the Third Circuit purported to answer that question in the abstract; each answered it only for the specific complaint in front of it, which is exactly why the same underlying software can now be lawful in one circuit's eyes and plausibly unlawful in another's, pending whatever the Supreme Court eventually decides to do with the disagreement its own 2025 denial of certiorari in Gibson helped set in motion.
