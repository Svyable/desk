# Legislators Catch Up

The federal antitrust reform landscape Klobuchar's bill entered was already, by early 2024, unusually crowded with proposed legislation targeting different aspects of digital-era market concentration, a context worth sketching briefly because it explains why one more antitrust bill, however well-tailored to the specific problem this book documents, faced such long odds of actually reaching a floor vote. The same Congress that received the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act was simultaneously considering separate bills addressing app-store competition, journalism's bargaining power against dominant tech platforms, and rail and shipping consolidation, each with its own coalition of sponsors, its own competing industry lobbying effort, and its own claim on the antitrust subcommittee's limited hearing calendar. Congressional antitrust reform of any kind had, by this period, become a genuinely difficult legislative category to advance regardless of a given bill's underlying merit, caught between industry opposition well-funded enough to slow nearly any individual proposal and a broader legislative environment in which comprehensive antitrust modernization had not passed Congress in any meaningful form for decades. Klobuchar's bill's failure to reach a vote reflects that broader gridlock at least as much as it reflects any specific weakness in the bill's own drafting.

Senator Amy Klobuchar of Minnesota, who has chaired or served as ranking member of the Senate Judiciary Committee's antitrust subcommittee across several Congresses, introduced the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act on January 30, 2024 — seven months before the Justice Department would file its own complaint against RealPage, making Klobuchar's bill an early, prescient legislative response to a problem regulators and litigants were still in the process of documenting through case-by-case enforcement. The bill, co-sponsored by Senators Ron Wyden, Dick Durbin, Richard Blumenthal, Mazie Hirono, and Peter Welch, took direct aim at the specific evidentiary gap this book's earlier chapters have shown recurring across nearly every algorithmic-pricing case: the difficulty of proving an "agreement" in the traditional Sherman Act sense when the actual coordination happens inside software that no human directly instructed to collude.

The bill's central operative provision was, by the standards of federal antitrust legislation, unusually direct: it would make it unlawful for any person to use or distribute a pricing algorithm that uses, incorporates, or was trained using a competitor's nonpublic data. That single sentence, if enacted, would have reached directly into the mechanism at the heart of the RealPage, Cendyn, and ATPCO cases examined throughout this book, sidestepping the hub-and-spoke pleading battles that produced the Ninth and Third Circuits' contradictory rulings in chapter ten by making the underlying conduct — training or operating a pricing algorithm on pooled, nonpublic competitor data — illegal on its own terms, without requiring a plaintiff to first prove that a shared algorithm's output amounted to an inferable conspiracy under decades-old, judge-made doctrine. The bill defined "pricing algorithm" broadly enough to capture the full range of technology this book has examined, describing it as any computational process, including one derived from machine learning or other artificial-intelligence techniques, that processes data to recommend or set a price or other commercial term affecting interstate commerce — a definition written specifically to avoid the kind of narrow, technology-specific loophole that might let a sufficiently novel piece of software escape the statute's reach simply by using a different technical architecture than whatever the bill's drafters had specifically in mind.

It is worth situating Klobuchar's bill against the specific enforcement gap chapter nine's account of the RealPage settlement identified, because the legislation was drafted with apparent awareness of exactly that gap even though it predated the settlement itself by more than a year. The RealPage consent judgment achieved its data-freshness and geographic-granularity restrictions only after the government spent roughly two years building and litigating a hub-and-spoke theory under existing law, a slow, resource-intensive process that produced a remedy binding on one company for seven years. Klobuchar's bill, by contrast, would have made the underlying conduct — training or operating a pricing algorithm on nonpublic competitor data — illegal by statute for every company in every industry simultaneously, the moment the bill took effect, without requiring the government to individually investigate, negotiate with, and litigate against each vendor one at a time the way it had to with RealPage. That structural difference, a general prophylactic rule versus a case-by-case common-law remedy, is precisely the trade-off this book's seventeenth chapter identified as separating the European Union's guidance-based approach from the American litigation-driven one, and Klobuchar's bill represents, in this sense, an attempt to import something closer to the European model into American federal statute — an attempt that has not yet succeeded, but that remains, as this chapter's next section shows, very much alive in the next Congress.

Beyond the core prohibition, the bill proposed two further mechanisms worth noting because they address problems this book's earlier chapters identified as genuine practical obstacles to enforcement. It would have created a formal audit tool for antitrust enforcers, giving the Justice Department and the Federal Trade Commission clearer statutory authority to compel disclosure of a pricing algorithm's training data and underlying logic — addressing, directly, the detection problem chapter six's account of the German gas-station study illustrated so starkly, where even sophisticated academic economists had to rely on indirect statistical inference rather than direct access to determine which sellers had adopted algorithmic pricing at all. And it proposed enhanced transparency requirements, aimed at making it easier for regulators, and potentially competitors and consumers, to determine when a given price had been influenced by pooled competitor data rather than by a seller's own independent analysis.

The bill's legislative fate, as of this book's writing, illustrates a pattern common to a great deal of proposed federal antitrust legislation regardless of its underlying merit: introduction, committee attention, and eventual stall, without ever reaching a floor vote in either chamber of the 118th Congress. Klobuchar and her cosponsors reintroduced a nearly identical version, the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act of 2025, in the 119th Congress, a reintroduction that itself signals the bill's sponsors judged the underlying problem important enough to keep pressing even after an initial legislative cycle produced no enacted result. Legal commentary published shortly after the bill's original introduction was notably skeptical of its prospects and, in some cases, of its underlying design; one widely circulated analysis from the law firm DLA Piper, published under the pointed headline "A swing and a miss?", argued that the bill's broad definitions risked sweeping in large amounts of entirely legitimate, procompetitive uses of shared or aggregated market data alongside the genuinely collusion-facilitating practices this book has documented, echoing the same "don't throw out the legitimate baby with the collusive bathwater" tension that has recurred throughout this book's account of revenue management, insurance price optimization, and personalized retail pricing alike.

While the federal legislative effort stalled, San Francisco moved with a speed and decisiveness federal lawmakers could not match, becoming, in the words of its own Board of Supervisors, the first city in the country to ban algorithmic rent-setting outright. Legislation adding a new section to the city's Rent Ordinance passed the Board of Supervisors in September 2024 and took effect the following month, prohibiting the sale or use within San Francisco of any "algorithmic device" — defined as a system that performs calculations using nonpublic competitor rent or occupancy data for the purpose of recommending a rent or vacancy-management decision to a landlord — with civil penalties reaching up to $1,000 per violation, alongside potential liability for damages, restitution, and attorneys' fees in private litigation. Supervisor and then-Board President Aaron Peskin, the ordinance's chief sponsor, described the measure explicitly as an attempt to move faster than either the federal government or the state legislature, framing San Francisco's own acute, chronically documented housing-affordability crisis as justification for local action on a national problem that federal and state lawmakers had, to that point, only begun to study.

The choice to legislate a specific prohibition, rather than rely on existing antitrust doctrine litigated case by case, reflects a deliberate strategic judgment worth naming explicitly, because it responds directly to the evidentiary problem this book's earlier chapters have documented at length. A tenant suing under the Sherman Act, as chapter ten's account of the Twombly pleading standard explained, has to allege specific facts making an illegal agreement plausible before a court will even grant discovery — a genuinely difficult bar to clear without first seeing the internal data and communications discovery alone can surface. A municipal ordinance banning "algorithmic devices" outright, by contrast, does not require any tenant, prosecutor, or city attorney to prove an agreement existed at all; it only requires proving that a landlord used a device meeting the ordinance's statutory definition, a considerably narrower and more mechanical factual question that sidesteps the entire hub-and-spoke pleading battle this book's tenth chapter showed splitting the Ninth and Third Circuits. This is the ordinances' genuine legal innovation, whatever their practical enforcement record has looked like in practice: they convert a hard-to-prove state-of-mind question, was there an agreement, into an easier-to-prove factual question, was this specific tool used — at least in principle, if a city or a tenant ever actually brings the case.

San Francisco's first-mover status carried real symbolic and legal significance — press coverage at the time noted, with evident local pride, that the city had managed to enact a targeted ban on the specific conduct underlying the RealPage litigation before the Justice Department itself had even filed its complaint, let alone reached the November 2025 settlement examined in chapter nine. But the ordinance's practical bite, in the roughly two years since its passage, has turned out to be considerably softer than its symbolic significance might suggest. Reporting published in the *San Francisco Standard* in August 2026 — timed, notably, to roughly two years after the ordinance's enactment — found that the law had not produced a single civil lawsuit against a San Francisco landlord in the entire period since it took effect, with neither the City Attorney's office nor the San Francisco Anti-Displacement Coalition, a tenant-advocacy organization that had championed the ordinance's passage, able to identify a single enforcement action brought under the new law. Rents in the city, meanwhile, had continued rising over the same period, driven by the broader forces — chronic housing scarcity, sustained demand pressure from the region's technology industry — that predate and extend well beyond any single pricing algorithm, complicating any simple before-and-after story about the ordinance's effect on the underlying affordability crisis it was written to address.

The entire wave of local ordinances this chapter and the next one document came startlingly close to being wiped off the books nationwide in a single stroke, through a legislative maneuver that had nothing to do with rent, housing, or antitrust law on its face. House Republicans, drafting the sprawling tax and budget package that would come to be known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, inserted a provision imposing a ten-year moratorium on state and local enforcement of essentially any law regulating artificial-intelligence models or automated decision systems, with only narrow exceptions for measures that facilitated AI deployment rather than restricted it. The House passed its version of the bill, containing the moratorium, on May 22, 2025. Legal analysts covering the housing sector specifically flagged the provision's likely effect on the exact ordinances examined in this chapter and the next: a blanket, decade-long federal preemption of state and local AI regulation would very plausibly have swept up San Francisco's rent-algorithm ban, Philadelphia's, Berkeley's, and every other city ordinance this book documents, along with California, New Jersey, and New York's statewide prohibitions, none of which would have survived a provision written to bar states and localities from regulating "automated decision systems" of any kind for a full decade.

Senator Elizabeth Warren and a group of colleagues wrote directly to RealPage in the midst of the legislative fight, pressing the company on its own lobbying spending and pointedly connecting that spending to the House provision's potential effect on exactly the state and local protections this book's fifteenth and sixteenth chapters describe — an unusually direct, publicly documented instance of a Senate office alleging that a specific defendant in an active piece of federal antitrust litigation was simultaneously lobbying Congress to preempt an entirely separate category of state and local laws targeting its own core product. More than a hundred advocacy organizations, spanning consumer-protection, housing, and civil-liberties groups, organized to oppose the moratorium provision as the bill moved toward the Senate, and the provision's ultimate fate turned out to hinge on a dramatic, early-morning Senate vote: the moratorium was stripped from the Senate's version of the bill before final passage, preserving, for the time being, the entire patchwork of local and state ordinances this book has spent two chapters documenting.

That the moratorium failed is genuinely significant, and this book does not want to understate the outcome simply because the underlying threat did not ultimately materialize. But the episode is worth dwelling on precisely because of how close it came to succeeding, and because it illustrates a structural vulnerability this book's account of municipal and state-level regulation has not yet had occasion to name directly: every ordinance examined in chapters fifteen and sixteen, however carefully drafted, however broadly adopted across dozens of jurisdictions, remains legally subordinate to federal law, and a single provision inserted into an unrelated, must-pass piece of budget legislation came within one Senate floor vote of erasing the entire regulatory apparatus this book has spent two chapters documenting, in one stroke, with no separate vote ever taken on the housing-specific question at all. Nothing prevents a similar provision from being reintroduced in a future legislative session, and nothing in this book's account of the moratorium's 2025 defeat should be read as evidence that the underlying vulnerability has been permanently resolved.

The gap between San Francisco's ordinance and its enforcement record deserves careful interpretation rather than dismissal, because several distinct explanations are plausible and this book cannot definitively adjudicate among them with the evidence currently available. It is possible that the ordinance succeeded in a genuinely preventive way — that landlords quietly stopped using algorithmic pricing tools banned under the new law, or that software vendors serving the San Francisco market recalibrated their products to comply, with the resulting absence of enforcement actions reflecting successful deterrence rather than toothless drafting. It is equally possible, and the *Standard*'s reporting leaned toward this reading, that the ordinance has simply gone unenforced — that the city lacks the investigative resources or political will to proactively audit landlords' software use, that affected tenants lack the practical means or legal sophistication to identify a Rent Ordinance violation and bring a private claim, or that the underlying evidentiary problem this entire book has traced back to Stigler's monitoring theory in chapter two — the difficulty of proving what pricing mechanism a specific landlord actually used — applies just as forcefully to a city ordinance's civil enforcement as it does to a federal antitrust complaint. Whichever explanation is closer to the truth, San Francisco's experience stands as a genuinely important data point for the wave of similar legislation examined in the next chapter: passing a ban is, on its own, no guarantee that the underlying conduct the ban targets will actually stop, or that anyone will ever be positioned to prove that it has.

Klobuchar's own committee position gives her legislative effort a durability the previous chapters' more scattered regulatory episodes generally lack, and it is worth closing this chapter by noting why that institutional detail matters. A senator who chairs or holds the ranking position on the subcommittee with direct jurisdiction over antitrust legislation controls, in practice, a considerable amount of the agenda-setting power that determines whether a bill like the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act ever receives a committee hearing, let alone a floor vote — a form of institutional leverage neither San Francisco's Board of Supervisors nor the European Commission's Directorate-General for Competition possesses in quite the same way. That leverage has not, as this chapter has already documented, been sufficient to move the bill to passage across two full congressional sessions, a reminder that committee position alone cannot overcome the broader legislative gridlock that has characterized federal antitrust reform efforts across both political parties for much of the past two decades. But it has been sufficient to keep the bill alive, reintroduced rather than abandoned, carried forward by the same core group of cosponsors across the transition from the 118th to the 119th Congress — a slower, less satisfying form of legislative persistence than a single dramatic floor vote would provide, but one that keeps the underlying issue formally before Congress rather than allowing it to disappear entirely from the federal legislative agenda, ready to be picked back up the moment a future RealPage-scale scandal, or a future Delta-style controversy, creates the political momentum a purely technical antitrust bill has so far struggled to generate on its own.
