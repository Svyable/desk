# Your Health, Priced by Algorithm

The McCarran-Ferguson Act's own history is worth a brief accounting, because the statute's origin explains why insurance ended up on such a different regulatory track than nearly every other industry examined in this book. Congress passed the Act in 1945 in direct response to a Supreme Court decision, United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Association, which had held for the first time that insurance transactions crossing state lines constituted interstate commerce subject to federal antitrust law, upending a long-standing assumption that insurance was purely a matter of state regulatory concern. The insurance industry, alarmed at the prospect of federal antitrust scrutiny displacing the state-based regulatory system it had operated under for generations, lobbied hard for federal legislation preserving state primacy, and McCarran-Ferguson was the result: a statute granting insurance a limited but significant exemption from federal antitrust law specifically to the extent the industry remained regulated by state law and did not involve boycott, coercion, or intimidation. That eight-decade-old compromise, reached for reasons that had nothing to do with algorithmic pricing or artificial intelligence, is the reason this chapter's regulatory story runs through state insurance commissioners and the NAIC rather than through the Federal Trade Commission or the Justice Department's Antitrust Division, the two agencies that have dominated nearly every other chapter in this book.

Insurance regulation operates under a different constitutional and statutory framework than almost any other industry examined in this book, and that difference turns out to matter enormously for how quickly and how decisively American regulators moved against a close cousin of the surveillance-pricing practices examined in chapter twelve. Since a landmark 1945 federal law called the McCarran-Ferguson Act, the business of insurance in the United States has been regulated primarily by individual state insurance commissioners rather than by federal antitrust or consumer-protection agencies, and most state insurance codes contain a specific, explicit statutory standard that has no real analogue anywhere else in American commercial law: an insurance rate must not be excessive, must not be inadequate to cover the insurer's actual costs, and — the phrase that matters most for this chapter — must not be "unfairly discriminatory." That third standard, baked into state insurance law for the better part of a century, gave American insurance regulators a statutory hook to act against personalized, elasticity-based pricing years before the Federal Trade Commission had even opened its own surveillance-pricing inquiry into ordinary retail commerce.

Insurance's three-part rate standard — not excessive, not inadequate, not unfairly discriminatory — is itself worth pausing on, because it represents a considerably more sophisticated regulatory instrument than the blunter prohibitions this book has examined in the housing and hospitality sectors. Where San Francisco's ordinance, examined in chapter fifteen, simply bans a defined category of software outright, insurance rate regulation asks a state commissioner to affirmatively evaluate whether a specific proposed rate is actuarially sound, a standard that requires an ongoing, technically sophisticated regulatory relationship between insurer and regulator rather than a one-time compliance determination. Insurers file proposed rates for state approval on a recurring basis in most states, giving regulators routine, structural visibility into pricing methodology that a municipal rent-ordinance enforcer, dependent on tenant complaints or investigative journalism to even learn a violation might be occurring, simply does not have built into their regulatory process at all.

The specific practice that triggered this regulatory response acquired a technical name, price optimization, and it is worth defining precisely because the definition itself explains why regulators treated it as categorically different from ordinary, actuarially sound insurance rating. Traditional insurance pricing sets a policyholder's premium based on that policyholder's assessed risk of loss — driving record, age, location, type of vehicle or property, claims history, and similar factors with a demonstrated statistical relationship to how likely and how costly a future claim is. Price optimization, as insurance regulators and actuaries came to define and criticize it, added a second, entirely different variable into the pricing model: price elasticity of demand, meaning a statistical estimate of exactly how large a premium increase a specific policyholder would tolerate before shopping for a cheaper competitor, derived from data about that policyholder's apparent price sensitivity, loyalty patterns, and likelihood of comparison-shopping — a variable, crucially, with no relationship whatsoever to the policyholder's actual risk of filing a claim. Two policyholders with an identical driving record, an identical vehicle, and an identical claims history could, under a price-optimized model, be charged different premiums simply because the insurer's data suggested one of them was statistically less likely to notice or act on the difference.

The parallel to the surveillance-pricing debate examined two chapters earlier is close enough that it deserves to be stated explicitly: insurance price optimization is, in substance, the same first-degree price discrimination this book traced back to Arthur Pigou's 1920 taxonomy, applied specifically to a policyholder's willingness to pay rather than to any measure of the underlying risk the policy is actually meant to price. What differs is the speed, decisiveness, and legal clarity of the regulatory response. Maryland's insurance commissioner issued a bulletin in October 2014 declaring that price optimization, as the practice was then being marketed to insurers by a growing number of specialized analytics vendors, resulted in unfairly discriminatory rates under Maryland law and directing insurers doing business in the state to stop the practice. Ohio followed in January 2015, issuing its own bulletin with nearly identical reasoning and an identical directive. Montana's insurance division issued an advisory memorandum in September 2015 reaching the same conclusion, and Connecticut followed with its own bulletin that December, directing property and casualty insurers to strip out any price-optimization technique that could not be justified on a straightforward, cost-based actuarial foundation. By the time the National Association of Insurance Commissioners, the coordinating body for state insurance regulators, formally adopted its own Price Optimization White Paper in November 2015, individual states had already been acting unilaterally for over a year, and legal trackers monitoring the issue would eventually count price optimization as having been formally addressed, through some combination of bulletins, advisory memoranda, and outright statutory bans, in eighteen separate American jurisdictions.

The contrast with the FTC's surveillance-pricing inquiry, examined at length in chapter twelve, could hardly be sharper, and the reasons for the contrast are instructive rather than incidental. The Federal Trade Commission, investigating personalized pricing in ordinary retail commerce, had to work within the general framework of federal antitrust and unfair-and-deceptive-practices law — a framework that, as chapter thirteen explained through the Robinson-Patman Act's narrow business-to-business focus, was never specifically designed to reach an individual consumer's personalized price at all, leaving the agency to rely on a comparatively blunt 6(b) study authority and, ultimately, an unfinished investigation whose final report had still not been published by the second half of 2026. State insurance commissioners, by contrast, were working within a statutory framework that had, for nearly a century, explicitly directed them to police "unfair discrimination" in rate-setting as a core, undisputed part of their regulatory mandate — giving them, in effect, a pre-built legal weapon that federal retail regulators simply did not have available, and that they were able to deploy within roughly two years of price optimization first becoming a subject of serious industry and regulatory discussion.

Insurance regulation's second major intervention against algorithmic pricing arrived through a different mechanism and targeted a related but distinct concern: not price optimization's use of elasticity data specifically, but the broader risk that algorithms and artificial-intelligence models, trained on large external datasets, might reproduce or amplify discrimination against legally protected groups even without any insurer intending that outcome. Colorado's Senate Bill 21-169, signed into law in July 2021, became the first legislation of its kind in the country, requiring insurers that use external consumer data and information sources — a broad category encompassing everything from social-media activity and consumer purchasing data to the kind of behavioral signals the FTC would later document in its own surveillance-pricing study — along with any algorithm or predictive model built on that data, to affirmatively test and demonstrate that their systems were not producing unfairly discriminatory outcomes based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, sexual orientation, disability, gender identity, or gender expression. The law reached across the full span of an insurer's business, from marketing and underwriting through pricing, claims management, and reimbursement, and imposed a genuinely unusual affirmative burden: rather than waiting for a regulator or a plaintiff to prove discriminatory intent or effect after the fact, Colorado required insurers themselves to build and document a quantitative testing framework proving their own systems' fairness on an ongoing basis, with life insurers facing a compliance deadline requiring documented progress reports as early as June 2024.

The specialized analytics vendors whose products triggered the original 2014 and 2015 regulatory bulletins deserve a brief accounting on their own terms, because they occupy a structural position in the insurance industry closely analogous to the one RealPage and Cendyn occupy in housing and hospitality, examined at length earlier in this book. Companies built specifically to sell price-optimization software to insurers marketed their products using language that, read against RealPage's own "outperform the market" pitch quoted in chapter eight, sounds almost interchangeable: tools designed to help an individual insurer capture additional premium revenue by better estimating which policyholders would tolerate a rate increase without shopping elsewhere, sold as a sophisticated technical upgrade over cruder, purely risk-based rating models. The parallel is not coincidental. It reflects the same underlying commercial logic driving every case examined in this book: once the technical capacity exists to estimate an individual customer's specific tolerance for a price increase, a software vendor can build a profitable business selling that capacity to any industry with enough customers, enough data, and enough repeat pricing decisions to make the investment worthwhile — apartment leases, hotel rooms, insurance premiums, or airline seats, the underlying commercial opportunity looks remarkably similar from the vendor's side of the transaction, regardless of which regulated industry ultimately deploys it.

It would be a mistake to read the insurance industry's regulatory experience as evidence that price discrimination concerns are easy to solve once the right statutory framework exists; the underlying tension between actuarial precision and consumer fairness has proven durable even within a regulatory system built specifically to police it. Insurers have continued to argue, both publicly and in the extensive comment process that preceded the NAIC's 2015 white paper, that a meaningful amount of what regulators labeled price optimization was, in the industry's own view, difficult to cleanly distinguish from legitimate, sophisticated actuarial modeling — that willingness to pay and risk of loss are not always as separable in practice as the regulatory bright line assumes, since factors correlated with a policyholder's price sensitivity, such as how actively they shop for alternative coverage, can also carry a genuine, if imperfect, statistical relationship to underlying risk. The American Academy of Actuaries submitted formal comments during the NAIC's white-paper drafting process pushing back against an overly broad prohibition, warning that regulators risked banning legitimate, cost-justified pricing refinements alongside the genuinely elasticity-based practices the white paper was meant to target, a version of the same "don't throw out the legitimate baby with the collusive bathwater" concern this book's earlier chapters raised about revenue management more broadly.

The insurance industry's experience with usage-based and telematics-driven auto insurance adds a further layer of complexity worth acknowledging before this chapter closes, because it shows a form of highly individualized, algorithmically driven pricing that has grown enormously popular with both insurers and many consumers, without triggering the same "unfair discrimination" objection that price optimization drew. Programs that track an individual driver's actual braking patterns, mileage, time of day, and driving speed through a smartphone app or a plugged-in device, adjusting that specific driver's premium based on their own measured behavior rather than broad demographic proxies like age or ZIP code, represent an extraordinarily granular, individualized form of algorithmic pricing — arguably far more personalized than anything RealPage's software does to a renter's monthly bill. Yet telematics-based pricing has drawn comparatively little of the regulatory hostility price optimization attracted, precisely because the data being used bears a direct, transparent, and easily defended relationship to the actual risk being priced: a driver who brakes hard less often and drives fewer late-night miles genuinely does file fewer claims, on average, than one who does not, preserving the actuarial link between data and risk that price optimization's critics argued elasticity-based pricing had severed. The distinction insurance regulators drew, in other words, was never simply "more data is worse" or "algorithms are dangerous." It was a considerably more precise, and more defensible, distinction between data that measures risk and data that measures only how much a customer can be persuaded to pay — the same distinction, stated in insurance-specific language, that this book's earlier chapters drew between legitimate yield management, keyed to a seller's own costs and genuine scarcity, and pooled, competitor-derived pricing power keyed to nothing but what the market would bear.

A further wrinkle in the health-insurance market, distinct from both price optimization and the nH Predict coverage-denial controversy examined below, involves the algorithms insurers use to determine which providers to include in a plan's network and at what negotiated reimbursement rate — a set of decisions that shapes a patient's actual out-of-pocket costs just as directly as a premium does, even though it rarely gets discussed using the same "pricing algorithm" language this book has applied throughout to rent, hotel rooms, and airfare. Network-adequacy and reimbursement-rate algorithms increasingly draw on shared, cross-insurer benchmarking data supplied by specialized healthcare-data vendors, a structural arrangement that echoes, in a quieter and less publicly scrutinized corner of the health-insurance market, the same pooled-intermediary pattern this book's central chapters documented in housing and hospitality. Whether that specific practice has drawn the kind of sustained regulatory attention RealPage's rent-pricing software attracted is, as of this book's writing, considerably less clear, in part because reimbursement-rate data is commercially proprietary in ways that make the kind of investigative reporting Heather Vogell conducted against RealPage in chapter eight harder to replicate against a healthcare-data vendor whose clients are insurers and hospital systems rather than the individual consumers who might otherwise organize public pressure the way RealPage's tenants eventually did.

Health insurance specifically has produced a related but analytically distinct algorithmic controversy, one worth including in this chapter even though it concerns coverage decisions rather than the price-setting mechanisms this book has focused on elsewhere, because it shows the same underlying dynamic — a private company's proprietary algorithm substituting for individualized human judgment at enormous financial and personal stakes — operating on the claims-payment side of insurance rather than the premium-setting side. UnitedHealth Group's subsidiary NaviHealth developed and deployed an algorithm called nH Predict, used to estimate how much post-acute care, such as skilled nursing or in-home rehabilitation, a Medicare Advantage patient was likely to need after a hospital stay. A class-action lawsuit filed in November 2023, drawing heavily on an investigation published by the health-news outlet STAT, alleged that the algorithm's predictions carried an extraordinarily high error rate — calculated, according to the complaint, from the fact that roughly 90 percent of denied claims that patients or their families appealed were ultimately reversed, either through UnitedHealth's own internal appeals process or before an administrative law judge — and that the company had set internal targets pressuring case-management employees to keep actual patient rehabilitation stays within one percent of whatever length the algorithm had predicted, regardless of a treating physician's own clinical judgment about how much care a specific patient actually needed.

UnitedHealth's defense, consistent with a pattern this book has documented across nearly every algorithmic controversy it examines, drew a careful, technically bounded distinction: the company maintained that nH Predict was never used to make coverage decisions directly, describing it instead as a planning tool meant to inform providers and families about the kind of assistance a patient might need, with actual coverage determinations resting on the specific terms of each member's health plan and on formal criteria set by the federal Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. A federal magistrate judge in Minnesota was sufficiently unpersuaded by that framing to order UnitedHealth, over the company's objection, to produce a broad range of internal documents concerning the algorithm's actual use and its relationship to real coverage denials, allowing the litigation to proceed into a discovery phase that, as with the RealPage and Cendyn cases examined earlier in this book, promised to surface far more about how the system actually operated in practice than either side's public statements had revealed. Whether nH Predict constitutes an unlawful business practice under the relevant Medicare Advantage regulations, a question distinct from and considerably narrower than the antitrust theories this book's central chapters examine, remains for the courts handling that specific litigation to resolve. What the case demonstrates for this book's broader argument is that the anxiety animating both the RealPage tenant and the UnitedHealth Medicare beneficiary is, at bottom, the same anxiety: a decision that used to involve direct, accountable human judgment — what should this apartment rent for, how much rehabilitation care does this specific patient need — now runs first through a proprietary statistical model whose internal logic the affected person has no practical way to inspect, challenge, or even fully discover before the decision has already been made and its consequences are already being lived.

That distinction, sharpened by a century of insurance-specific statutory language most other industries never developed, offers a template this book's later chapters will return to directly: not a blanket suspicion of algorithmic pricing wherever it appears, but a specific, defensible test — does this price track a genuine, cost-based, risk-based, or scarcity-based justification, or does it track only what the algorithm has learned a particular buyer, or a particular pool of buyers, can be made to accept? Insurance regulators reached for that test almost by statutory instinct, a full decade before RealPage's tenants, Cendyn's casino guests, or Delta's air travelers had the vocabulary, or the legal standing, to ask the same question about the industries governing their own rent, their hotel rooms, and their airfare.
