# The DVD That Cost $22.74 or $26.24

The dot-com era Amazon operated in during its original 2000 pricing experiment is worth remembering for context, because the company's underlying financial pressures at the time shaped why an ambitious dynamic-pricing test might have looked especially attractive to its leadership. Amazon had gone public in 1997 and, like most internet retailers of the period, was operating at a substantial loss, under intense pressure from investors and analysts to demonstrate a credible path toward profitability following the broader dot-com sector's collapse earlier in 2000. A pricing strategy capable of extracting even modestly more revenue per transaction, across a customer base numbering in the millions, represented an obvious and mathematically appealing lever for a company under exactly that kind of margin pressure — a commercial logic that recurs, in different form, in nearly every personalized or algorithmic pricing initiative examined throughout this book, from RealPage's promise to help landlords "outperform the market" to Delta's pursuit of individualized fares two and a half decades later.

In September 2000, an Amazon customer curious about a DVD's price did something that would become an ordinary act of consumer self-defense within a decade but was, at the time, a slightly unusual bit of technical fussiness: he deleted the cookies his browser had stored, the small files that let a website recognize a returning visitor, and reloaded the page. The listed price for the same DVD dropped from $26.24 to $22.74. Word of the discrepancy spread among Amazon shoppers comparing notes online, and journalists picked up the story within days, forcing Amazon to acknowledge publicly what its customers had already begun piecing together for themselves: the company had been running an experiment in dynamic, personalized pricing, using a shopper's browsing history, apparent purchase patterns, and other identifying signals to adjust the price the same product showed different visitors.

Amazon's public response followed a pattern this book's later chapters will show repeating across every subsequent personalized-pricing controversy examined in this book, from Orbitz to Delta: a swift, categorical denial paired with a technical clarification that fell well short of a full account of what had actually happened. Jeff Bezos personally stated that Amazon had "never tested and never will test prices based on customer demographics," a carefully worded denial that left open, without directly addressing, whether the company had tested prices based on other data that was not, strictly speaking, demographic — browsing behavior, prior purchase history, or the specific combination of signals that had produced the $26.24-versus-$22.74 discrepancy in the first place. The company's official account described the episode as a random price test rather than an individually targeted one, and, whatever the precise mechanism, Amazon moved quickly to make the affected customers whole: the company refunded 6,896 customers an average of $3.10 each, a modest total of roughly $21,378, small enough in absolute dollar terms that the settlement reads less as meaningful restitution than as a calculated, inexpensive way to end a public-relations problem before it grew any larger.

The scale of the backlash, disproportionate to the modest dollar amounts actually at stake for any individual customer, is the real subject of this chapter, and it connects directly to a body of behavioral-economics research that predates the Amazon episode by more than a decade and explains, with unusual empirical precision, exactly why consumers react to price discrimination with an intensity that a purely rational, self-interested calculation of the dollars involved would not seem to justify. Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Richard Thaler published a paper in 1986 in the *American Economic Review* titled "Fairness as a Constraint on Profit Seeking: Entitlements in the Market," built around a series of telephone surveys asking ordinary people to judge the fairness of specific pricing scenarios. The most famous of these scenarios described a hardware store that had been selling snow shovels for fifteen dollars, and that raised the price to twenty dollars the morning after a large snowstorm. Asked to rate the action as very unfair, unfair, acceptable, or completely fair, roughly 82 percent of respondents rated the price increase either unfair or very unfair — a striking result given that, from the standpoint of pure supply-and-demand economics, raising a price in response to a genuine, unexpected surge in demand is exactly the textbook-correct response a rational, profit-maximizing, and arguably even a socially efficient seller should make, since a higher price would ration the limited supply of shovels toward the buyers who needed them most urgently rather than letting the first customers through the door buy up the entire stock at the old price.

The 1986 study's methodology deserves a moment of its own attention, because the telephone-survey format Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler chose was itself a deliberate methodological choice that later behavioral economists have generally regarded as ahead of its time. Rather than asking trained economists or business students to evaluate the hardware-store scenario, the researchers surveyed ordinary members of the public by phone, on the theory that a norm of fairness governing real commercial behavior had to be measured against the intuitions of the people actually participating in that commerce, not against the specialized reasoning of an audience already trained to think about pricing in terms of supply, demand, and allocative efficiency. That methodological choice produced a result with unusual staying power: the 82 percent figure has been replicated, with broadly similar results, in follow-up studies conducted in different countries and different decades since the original 1986 paper, suggesting the underlying fairness intuition is not a peculiarity of early-1980s American telephone-survey respondents but something closer to a stable, cross-culturally recurring feature of how people evaluate commercial transactions generally.

Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler's explanation for this gap between economic efficiency and perceived fairness turned on a concept they called dual entitlement, and it is worth explaining carefully because it supplies the psychological mechanism underneath every pricing controversy examined in this chapter. Ordinary people, their survey data suggested, believe both buyers and sellers are entitled to the terms of a reference transaction — the price that has prevailed in the recent past, absent some change in the seller's own underlying costs. A seller is entitled to pass along a genuine increase in cost; raising the price of umbrellas because a wholesaler raised the price the store pays for umbrellas struck survey respondents as perfectly fair, because the seller's own entitlement to their previous profit margin was being preserved rather than expanded. But a seller is not entitled, in most respondents' moral accounting, to exploit a sudden increase in market power — a snowstorm creating desperate demand, a temporary shortage, a captive customer with no alternative — to extract additional profit beyond what the previous transaction had implicitly promised, even though nothing about that behavior violates any law and even though, again, the resulting higher price might allocate the scarce good more efficiently than holding the price steady would. The snowstorm did not raise the hardware store's costs. It raised the store's leverage over its customers, and using that leverage, rather than a genuine cost increase, to charge more is precisely the distinction ordinary economic reasoning tends to treat as morally neutral and ordinary consumer psychology tends to treat as a violation worth remembering, resenting, and, when the opportunity arises, punishing through negative word of mouth, canceled purchases, or, in Amazon's case, direct and immediate public exposure.

It is worth pausing on precisely why Amazon's episode generated the intensity of backlash it did, because the mechanics of discovery matter as much as the underlying practice for understanding the dual-entitlement framework this chapter is built around. The customer who deleted his cookies and watched the price drop by three dollars and fifty cents did not discover a large financial injury; three dollars and fifty cents on a twenty-six-dollar DVD is a trivial sum by almost any reasonable measure of consumer harm. What he discovered, and what spread rapidly once other shoppers began comparing notes, was proof that the price Amazon displayed was not a fixed, objective fact about the DVD's value, but one of several numbers Amazon had available to show him, selected specifically because its models predicted he, personally, would accept it without complaint. That discovery is corrosive to a different kind of trust than a simple overcharge would be — not trust that a company charges a fair price, but trust that the price displayed means the same thing to every customer looking at the identical page, a background assumption so basic to how retail commerce has operated for most of its modern history that most shoppers had never previously had occasion to question it at all.

Orbitz's 2012 discovery, reported first by the *Wall Street Journal*, applied a version of the same personalized-pricing logic to an entirely different signal: rather than a shopper's specific browsing history or purchase pattern, Orbitz's system used the operating system of the device a customer was browsing from, steering visitors using Apple's Mac computers toward more expensive hotel options than visitors using Windows PCs. Orbitz's own internal data, according to the *Journal*'s reporting, showed that Mac users spent between twenty and thirty dollars more per night on hotels than PC users booking through the same site, and were roughly 40 percent more likely to book a four- or five-star property — a genuine, measurable behavioral difference between the two groups of customers that gave Orbitz's targeting a real statistical foundation rather than an arbitrary or discriminatory pretext. Orbitz executives, defending the practice once it became public, insisted the company was not showing the identical room at different prices to different users, only steering different users toward different options within the same search results, a distinction the company argued made the practice a form of personalization rather than price discrimination in any objectionable sense — the same kind of carefully bounded, technically accurate but incomplete denial Amazon had offered twelve years earlier and Delta would offer thirteen years later.

The dual-entitlement framework helps explain why the Orbitz story, despite involving a smaller and more indirect form of differentiation than Amazon's direct price testing, still generated significant public discomfort once it became widely reported. A Mac user comparing notes with a Windows-using friend and discovering that the friend had been shown cheaper options for an identical trip experiences something structurally similar to the hardware-store customer told a neighbor bought the same snow shovel for five dollars less an hour earlier: not necessarily a financial catastrophe, but a violation of an implicit entitlement to be treated, at minimum, the same way as any other customer walking through the same virtual door. That the targeting criterion in Orbitz's case, device type rather than a browsing cookie, was arguably even less directly tied to any specific individual's identity than Amazon's original approach did little to soften the reaction, because the psychological injury the Kahneman-Knetsch-Thaler research identifies does not depend on how the targeting was technically accomplished. It depends on the discovery, however it arrives, that a price presented as if it were the market's single objective answer was in fact one of several answers the seller had available, selected specifically because the algorithm predicted this particular customer would accept it.

The behavioral-economics literature following Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler's original 1986 study has continued probing exactly which features of a pricing scenario trigger the strongest fairness backlash, and later research has generally confirmed a pattern directly relevant to every controversy this chapter documents: transparency about the mechanism, not merely the existence of price variation itself, is what determines how consumers react. A senior discount or a matinee ticket price, both instances of the third-degree price discrimination this chapter's opening pages described through Pigou's original taxonomy, draws little to no public objection precisely because the criterion is disclosed openly and applied to a category the customer can identify about themselves in advance. What triggered outrage in Amazon's 2000 controversy, Orbitz's 2012 controversy, and Delta's 2025 controversy alike was not price variation as such, but the discovery that variation was happening covertly, based on data signals the customer had no way of knowing were being collected or used, and that the company in each case had denied or minimized once the practice came to light rather than disclosing it as an open, named part of its pricing model from the start. This distinction between disclosed and covert discrimination is, in a sense, this chapter's own version of the diagnostic test chapter eighteen proposed for algorithmic collusion more broadly: not whether a price varies, but whether the person paying it has any way of knowing why.

Part of why every company examined in this chapter has been able to rely on denial and quiet retreat, rather than a legal defense argued on the merits, is that American law gives them remarkably little reason to fear a courtroom over this specific practice. The Robinson-Patman Act of 1936, the primary federal statute governing price discrimination in the United States, was written to protect small businesses from being undercut by suppliers who gave large competitors preferential wholesale pricing — a business-to-business concern rooted in the Depression-era anxiety that chain stores like the Great Atlantic & Pacific Tea Company were using their buying power to squeeze independent grocers out of existence. The statute's protections run to business customers purchasing goods for resale, not to the individual consumer standing at the end of the supply chain deciding whether to buy a DVD, book a hotel room, or purchase an airline ticket; a retailer that charges two different individual shoppers two different prices for the identical consumer product, absent some other applicable civil-rights or consumer-protection statute triggered by discrimination based on a legally protected category, has historically faced very little exposure under federal antitrust law specifically because Robinson-Patman was never designed to reach that transaction at all. This is the quiet legal backdrop against which every controversy in this chapter has played out: Amazon, Orbitz, and Delta each faced fierce public and political criticism, but none faced anything resembling the sustained federal antitrust litigation RealPage and Cendyn confronted in the chapters just before this one, because personalized pricing aimed at individual consumers, however uncomfortable it makes those consumers feel, sits largely outside the specific legal machinery this book has spent its middle chapters examining.

Europe has begun moving toward a different answer, though slowly and not yet in finished form as this book goes to print. The European Commission has been developing a Digital Fairness Act, following an extensive "fitness check" review of existing EU consumer-protection law that specifically flagged personalized pricing and personalized advertising, tied to tracking and behavioral profiling, as practices capable of exploiting consumer vulnerabilities when deployed without adequate transparency. The Commission's own timeline, as of this book's writing, pointed toward a formal legislative proposal in the final quarter of 2026, with the broader legislative process expected to continue into 2027 before any final rules take effect — a substantially more deliberate, precautionary regulatory posture than the reactive, scandal-driven pattern of denial and retreat that has characterized the American response to every controversy this chapter documents. Whether the eventual Digital Fairness Act actually reaches dynamic and personalized pricing with binding force, or whether the practice gets narrowed out of the final legislative text the way politically contentious provisions so often are during the EU's own lengthy negotiation process, remained an open question at the time this book was finished, and is a question chapter sixteen returns to directly in its broader account of how European and American regulators have diverged in handling the entire subject this book covers.

This is the thread that connects Amazon's 2000 controversy and Orbitz's 2012 controversy directly to the FTC's surveillance-pricing study and Delta's 2025 controversy examined in the previous chapter, and it is worth stating plainly as this chapter closes: the specific data signals available to sellers pursuing personalized pricing have grown almost unrecognizably more granular and more comprehensive across the quarter-century separating Amazon's early, relatively crude cookie-based experiment from the mouse-movement and cart-abandonment tracking the FTC documented in 2025, but the underlying psychological reaction those experiments trigger, once discovered, has remained remarkably constant. Every company examined in this chapter and the previous one has responded to public discovery of its personalized pricing with some version of the same defensive pattern: a categorical denial framed narrowly enough to be technically true, followed, where the practice was confirmed rather than successfully denied, by a swift retreat rather than a public defense of the underlying economic logic. No major consumer-facing company examined in this book's research has ever attempted the argument an economics professor might actually make in the practice's defense — that first-degree price discrimination, taken to its efficient extreme, could in principle let a seller serve more total customers profitably, including price-sensitive customers who would otherwise be priced out entirely by a single, uniform price set high enough to cover the seller's costs across the whole customer base. That argument may well be correct as a matter of economic theory. It has never once, in the quarter-century of controversies this chapter surveys, proven to be a commercially survivable argument to make in public, a pattern that says less about the underlying economics than about the durability of the fairness intuition Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler measured with such precision four decades ago, and that no amount of increasingly sophisticated targeting technology has managed to dissolve.
