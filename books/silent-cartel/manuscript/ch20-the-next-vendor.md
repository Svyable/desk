# The Next Vendor

This closing substantive chapter carries a different kind of evidentiary burden than the eighteen chapters preceding it, and it is worth being explicit about that shift before proceeding. Every case study from chapter one through chapter eighteen describes something that already happened, verifiable against court filings, published research, or contemporaneous journalism this book's research trail documents in detail. This chapter, by contrast, is necessarily more speculative, extrapolating from the patterns those earlier chapters established toward a technology still actively forming as this book was being written. That shift in evidentiary footing deserves to be marked clearly rather than allowed to blend into the same confident, declarative register the rest of this book has earned through its sourcing discipline; where the preceding chapters said what happened, this chapter can only responsibly say what the preceding chapters' own logic suggests is likely, plausible, or worth watching for, and it tries to flag that distinction at each turn rather than letting speculation borrow the authority of documented fact.

Every case examined in this book, from Robert Crandall's 1982 phone call through the RealPage settlement's twelve-month data-freshness restriction, shares a structural feature worth naming explicitly as this book turns toward its close: in each case, the algorithm doing the pricing was working on behalf of a seller, helping landlords, hotels, or airlines extract more revenue from a comparatively less-informed pool of buyers. The technology this book's final substantive chapter examines threatens to invert that structure entirely, and the inversion raises questions no court, regulator, or piece of legislation surveyed in this book has yet had to confront directly.

Agentic artificial-intelligence systems — AI models capable of taking multi-step actions on a user's behalf, rather than simply answering questions or generating text — had, by the time this book was being written in 2026, begun moving from research demonstrations into genuine commercial deployment, including in the specific domain of shopping and price comparison this book has spent its central chapters examining. An AI agent acting on a consumer's behalf, rather than a landlord's or an airline's, could in principle perform exactly the kind of exhaustive, continuous, superhuman price-monitoring this book has shown pricing algorithms performing for sellers throughout its earlier chapters — scanning every available apartment listing in a target neighborhood, tracking every hotel's rate changes hour by hour, waiting for the precise moment an airline's yield-management system opens its cheapest fare bucket — and using that monitoring not to help one seller extract more from many buyers, but to help one buyer extract a better deal from many sellers. Consumer advocates and some antitrust scholars examining this emerging trend have described it, with cautious optimism, as a potential counterweight to the seller-side algorithmic pricing power this book has spent its middle chapters documenting: if RealPage gives landlords a superhuman ability to monitor and respond to the rental market, perhaps a consumer-facing AI agent could give renters a comparably superhuman ability to monitor and respond right back.

The commercial reality of this transition, as of this book's writing, has moved considerably more haltingly than the more breathless forecasts around "agentic commerce" suggested it would just a year or two earlier, and the stumbles are worth documenting because they complicate any simple narrative of imminent AI-agent dominance over ordinary shopping. OpenAI launched a feature called Instant Checkout inside ChatGPT in 2025, built on what it called an Agentic Commerce Protocol, letting users complete a purchase directly inside a chat conversation rather than clicking through to a retailer's own website. The feature struggled from the start with data quality — pricing and inventory information pulled through web scraping proved unreliable enough that OpenAI retired Instant Checkout entirely in March 2026, roughly six months after launch, with only about thirty merchants ever live on the system. Walmart's own internal measurement, reported alongside the feature's retirement, found that purchases completed inside ChatGPT converted at roughly a third the rate of an ordinary click-through to Walmart's own website, even though ChatGPT was simultaneously driving roughly double the rate of genuinely new customers compared to traditional search — a split result suggesting AI chat interfaces were proving effective at product discovery, introducing shoppers to options they might not otherwise have found, while remaining considerably less effective, at least in this first generation of the technology, at the actual mechanics of completing a trustworthy transaction.

The underlying ambition has not disappeared, even though this particular implementation stumbled; it has simply moved to different technical protocols and different corporate sponsors. Google and Shopify jointly developed a more comprehensive successor called the Universal Commerce Protocol, intended to span a shopper's entire journey from initial product discovery through post-purchase support rather than only the checkout moment OpenAI's feature had focused on, and Perplexity launched its own competing "Instant Buy" feature pursuing a similar ambition. McKinsey's own analysts, in projections that should be read with the same caution this book has applied to every other industry forecast cited across its chapters, estimated agentic commerce could generate between three and five trillion dollars in annual transaction volume by 2030 — a figure that, whatever its ultimate accuracy, signals how seriously the major technology companies building these systems are treating the underlying opportunity, regardless of any single product's early stumbles. By February 2026, OpenAI reported that ChatGPT was fielding roughly fifty million shopping-related queries a day, and Amazon's own AI shopping assistant, Rufus, had reportedly reached some three hundred million users, figures that describe an enormous and rapidly growing volume of AI-mediated shopping activity even in the absence of any single, dominant, fully matured agentic-checkout product.

That optimistic framing deserves genuine scrutiny rather than uncritical acceptance, because the same theoretical apparatus this book built in chapter two to explain how seller-side algorithms learn to collude applies with very little modification to a population of buyer-side agents operating in the same market. If enough renters in a given city eventually delegate their apartment search to AI agents built by the same handful of vendors — a plausible commercial outcome, given how concentrated the current market for large AI models already is among a small number of major technology companies — those agents could, in principle, discover their own version of the reward-and-punishment strategies Calvano's Q-learning agents found on their own in chapter five, this time coordinating among buyers rather than sellers, potentially suppressing prices below a genuinely competitive level in ways that could harm landlords just as unfairly as pooled seller-side algorithms have harmed tenants. Antitrust law, built as this book has shown throughout around policing seller-side coordination that raises prices against buyers, has comparatively little developed doctrine addressing buyer-side algorithmic coordination that might suppress prices against sellers, a genuine asymmetry in the existing legal framework that the rise of consumer-facing AI agents seems likely to test directly within the next several years.

American enforcers were, as this book was being finished, already signaling how they intend to approach this next wave, and their stated posture offers a useful bridge between the case-by-case litigation this book has documented in its earlier chapters and the more anticipatory, general-principle approach chapter seventeen showed European regulators favoring. Daniel Glad, an Acting Deputy Assistant Attorney General in the Antitrust Division, delivered remarks at an antitrust conference in May 2026 under the title "Old Crime, New Code," previewing how the division intends to bring criminal enforcement to bear on algorithmic and AI-enabled pricing conduct going forward. His core formulation, quoted widely in the legal press that covered the speech, distilled this entire book's central argument into a single prosecutorial principle: a company cannot do with the knowing use of an algorithm what it could not lawfully do through a simple, old-fashioned agreement accomplishing the identical result. Gail Slater, the Assistant Attorney General heading the division more broadly, had struck a similar note the previous August, telling an audience that she expected the department's algorithmic-pricing investigations to grow in number as adoption of shared pricing tools continued spreading across new industries, and warning companies to conduct genuine due diligence on the algorithms and data inputs they share with competitors, rather than assuming that automation itself supplied any kind of legal shelter.

The Federal Trade Commission and the Justice Department took a further, more structural step in February 2026, jointly opening a public inquiry into updating the two agencies' guidance on collaborations among competitors — a foundational document dating back to 2000, written for a economy of joint ventures and research partnerships that looked nothing like the data-sharing arrangements, AI training collaborations, and shared foundation-model dependencies increasingly common by the mid-2020s. The inquiry's own framing acknowledged directly the concern this chapter has spent its opening pages describing: that companies relying on the same shared AI tools might find themselves facilitating collusion among competitors without any of them ever entering into an express agreement to do so, precisely the hub-and-spoke-without-a-spoken-word pattern this book has traced from ATPCO's advance-dated fare filings through RealPage's pooled lease data to the still-hypothetical foundation-model scenario this chapter has been sketching. Whether that updated guidance, once finalized, manages to draw a workable line between genuinely dangerous algorithmic hubs and the ordinary, beneficial data-sharing collaborations that power a great deal of legitimate innovation — the same line this book's chapter eighteen showed serious skeptics arguing existing doctrine already struggles to draw with precision — will only become clear once the guidance is actually published and, inevitably, tested against the next generation of pricing technology this chapter has tried to anticipate.

A second, more immediate concern following directly from the RealPage and Cendyn litigation examined in chapters nine and ten involves what happens to hub-and-spoke doctrine once the "hub" is not a dedicated, industry-specific revenue-management vendor like RealPage or Cendyn, but a general-purpose foundation model licensed by a small number of major AI developers and used, in some form, across a huge range of unrelated industries simultaneously. If a handful of landlords, a handful of hotels, and a handful of airlines all end up building their pricing tools on top of the same underlying foundation model — not because they coordinated with each other, but because that model happens to be the best, cheapest, or most widely available option on the market — the resulting concentration could, in principle, produce exactly the kind of shared-hub coordination Interstate Circuit's 1939 hub-and-spoke doctrine was designed to reach, but spread across industries that share no obvious commercial relationship and that would never, under any previous antitrust framework, have been analyzed together at all. Legal scholars have only begun sketching what a hub-and-spoke theory would look like when the hub is a general-purpose AI model rather than an industry-specific pricing tool, and no court, as of this book's writing, had yet been asked to resolve the question directly — an unresolved frontier this book can identify clearly without being able to predict how it will ultimately be settled.

The regulatory tools this book has documented across its second half offer some genuine basis for cautious hope, even against these harder, less settled future scenarios, because several of them were built with a flexibility that does not depend on knowing in advance exactly what technology will eventually need restraining. The RealPage settlement's twelve-month data-freshness rule and its ban on sub-state geographic pricing granularity, examined in chapter nine, target the underlying mechanism — rapid, granular, pooled monitoring — rather than any specific piece of software, meaning a similar remedy could, in principle, be adapted to a foundation-model-based pricing tool built by an entirely different kind of company than RealPage. San Francisco's ordinance and its many municipal successors, examined in chapters fifteen and sixteen, define an "algorithmic device" functionally, by what it does with nonpublic competitor data, rather than by naming any specific product, a drafting choice that should, in principle, extend to whatever the next generation of pricing tools turns out to look like. Klobuchar's stalled federal bill, examined in chapter fifteen, defined "pricing algorithm" broadly enough to include any computational process derived from machine learning or artificial-intelligence techniques, anticipating, at the drafting stage in early 2024, a category of technology considerably more advanced than what most of this book's central case studies had actually deployed by the time the bill was introduced.

There is a further, stranger possibility worth naming before this chapter closes, one that follows from the buyer-side agent scenario sketched earlier but pushes it a step further than most current commentary has been willing to go. If both sellers and buyers in a given market eventually deploy AI agents built on overlapping foundation models, licensed from the same small handful of frontier AI developers, the resulting market could in principle feature algorithmic sellers and algorithmic buyers negotiating against each other, with both sides ultimately drawing on correlated, if not literally identical, underlying training and reasoning capabilities. Game theorists have not yet produced anything close to Calvano's clean, replicated laboratory result for this specific configuration — seller agents and buyer agents, built on shared or overlapping model families, negotiating repeatedly against each other rather than only against fellow sellers — and this book resists the temptation to predict an outcome the relevant research has not yet actually tested. What this book can say with more confidence, based on everything the preceding nineteen chapters have documented, is that every previous generation of pricing technology examined in this book arrived at its most consequential, most legally contested behavior only once deployed at real commercial scale, in real markets, well after the underlying capability first became technically available — RealPage's YieldStar existed for two decades before ProPublica's reporting made its actual market effect legible, and Calvano's Q-learning result took a controlled laboratory setting to demonstrate cleanly. There is no particular reason to expect agentic AI commerce to defy that pattern, and considerable reason, drawn from this book's entire preceding argument, to expect regulators to be several years behind whatever the technology actually ends up doing once it reaches the scale RealPage and Cendyn's products eventually reached.

Whether that forward-looking drafting proves adequate once agentic AI systems, foundation-model-based pricing tools, and whatever comes after them actually arrive at the scale this book's central case studies have already reached is a question no one, including the regulators, litigators, and legislators whose work fills this book's middle chapters, can answer with genuine confidence today. What this book's evidence does support, with considerably more confidence, is a prediction about the shape any future controversy is likely to take, because that shape has now repeated itself with remarkable consistency across every technology this book has examined, from ATPCO's advance-dated fare filings in 1994 to RealPage's pooled lease data three decades later: a genuinely useful technology, built to solve a real forecasting or matching problem, gets adopted widely enough that its aggregate effect on a market becomes visible only in hindsight; journalists or academic economists document the pattern using painstaking, often indirect statistical methods, because no company voluntarily discloses that its product functions as a coordination mechanism; regulators and legislators respond slowly, unevenly, and initially with tools calibrated for an older technology; and courts, asked to apply a century-old statute to genuinely novel facts, split, hedge, and take years to settle on an answer that the technology itself may have already outrun by the time the answer finally arrives. Nothing in the specific technology examined in this chapter suggests that cycle is about to break. Everything in this book's first nineteen chapters suggests it is, if anything, about to run faster than it ever has before.

One structural feature of the emerging agentic-commerce landscape deserves a final, closer look before this chapter closes, because it bears directly on the buyer-side collusion concern raised earlier: the sheer concentration of the underlying infrastructure. Unlike the revenue-management software market this book examined in chapter seven, which supports multiple genuinely competing vendors — RealPage, Cendyn's Rainmaker, IDeaS, Duetto, and others each building broadly comparable but independently engineered products — the foundation-model layer powering the current generation of AI shopping agents is dominated by a strikingly small number of companies capable of training and operating models at the necessary scale, a concentration driven by the enormous capital costs of frontier AI development rather than by any deliberate anticompetitive strategy on any single company's part. That concentration matters enormously for this chapter's central concern, because Stigler's own 1964 theory, examined in chapter two, predicts that fewer independent decision-makers make coordination easier to sustain, whether the decision-makers in question are gas stations, casino-hotels, or, in this chapter's forward-looking scenario, the small number of firms whose models increasingly mediate an enormous share of both sides of an enormous share of consumer transactions. A market structure this concentrated at the infrastructure layer, even without any single company's software directly resembling RealPage's pooled-data model, may turn out to recreate Stigler's classic collusion-friendly conditions by a different, more indirect route than any case examined earlier in this book — not through a dedicated pricing vendor serving one industry, but through a general-purpose intelligence layer serving nearly every industry at once.
