# A Theory of Oligopoly

Stigler's own career, worth a brief note before turning to his 1964 argument directly, embodied a broader intellectual tradition at the University of Chicago that treated markets with considerable respect but never with naive faith that competition was automatically self-sustaining. Stigler would go on to win the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1982, cited specifically for his pioneering studies of industrial structures, the functioning of markets, and the causes and effects of public regulation — a body of work that, taken as a whole, resists easy political categorization. Stigler is remembered, accurately, as a foundational figure in the Chicago School's general skepticism toward heavy-handed antitrust enforcement, having argued in other, equally influential work that regulation is often captured by the very industries it is meant to constrain, producing outcomes that serve incumbent firms rather than consumers. That same skepticism toward naive regulatory intervention makes "A Theory of Oligopoly" a more interesting source for this book's argument, not a less interesting one: a scholar with every intellectual incentive to downplay the risk of tacit collusion instead built, with unusual rigor, the clearest available explanation of exactly the mechanism by which it becomes possible — a body of theory this book has found more durable and more analytically useful than work from scholars starting from more overtly interventionist premises.

George Stigler did not set out, in 1964, to explain how software would one day fix prices without an agreement. He set out to answer a much narrower and, at the time, more embarrassing question: why do cartels keep falling apart? Economists had spent decades treating collusion as a simple matter of willpower — a handful of firms agree to hold prices high, and the agreement holds until greed or a new entrant breaks it. Stigler, publishing "A Theory of Oligopoly" in the *Journal of Political Economy*, thought this missed the actual mechanics of the problem. A price-fixing agreement, he argued, is not really a promise; it is closer to a monitoring problem. Every member of a cartel has a private incentive to secretly undercut the agreed price and grab extra sales, and every member knows every other member has that same incentive. The cartel survives only as long as its members can detect cheating quickly enough, and punish it credibly enough, to make cheating not worth the trouble. Stigler's real subject was not greed. It was information — specifically, how expensive it is for a group of rivals to figure out, in something close to real time, whether one of their number has quietly started giving customers a better deal.

This turns out to be the single most important idea in this book's entire argument, so it is worth sitting with it before moving on. A group of competitors does not need to sit in a hotel room and shake hands to sustain prices above what a competitive market would produce. All they need is a reliable way to observe each other's behavior and a credible way to retaliate against a defector. Stigler worked through the statistical mechanics of this in surprising detail for a 1964 paper: a firm trying to catch a secretly discounting rival has to distinguish an actual price cut from ordinary, innocent variation in sales — a rival might sell more this month simply because demand shifted, not because they cheated — and the fewer firms there are in an industry, the more concentrated the buyers, and the more standardized the product, the easier that statistical detection problem becomes. A market with two sellers of an identical, frequently purchased good is, on Stigler's logic, one of the easiest markets in the world to tacitly collude in, because any change in one seller's sales is almost certainly explained by the other seller's price. A market with two hundred sellers of slightly different products, sold to buyers who rarely reveal what they paid, is one of the hardest, because the statistical noise drowns out the signal. Stigler had, without knowing it, written down the exact list of conditions that make a market vulnerable to algorithmic pricing eight decades later: few sellers, a standardized product, and — critically — some shared mechanism for observing what everyone else is charging in close to real time. A pricing algorithm that pools nonpublic rent, occupancy, or fare data across every subscriber in a metro area is, in Stigler's own terms, a detection technology. It solves, mechanically and near-instantly, the exact monitoring problem Stigler identified as the main obstacle to sustained collusion.

Stigler was building on an older and, at the time, controversial strand of economic theory that had emerged out of the Great Depression, when economists were first trying to make sense of industries — steel, cigarettes, gasoline — where a small number of large firms seemed to charge suspiciously similar prices without any of them formally agreeing to. Edward Chamberlin, at Harvard, and later Paul Sweezy, working independently, described this as a form of recognized "mutual dependence": in a market with only a few sellers, each firm understands that cutting its price will provoke a matching cut from its rivals, wiping out any gain, while raising its price alone will simply hand market share to competitors who hold steady. Rational sellers in that situation, Chamberlin argued, will tend to behave as if they had a formal agreement, purely out of self-interested calculation, without ever needing one — a phenomenon economists began calling tacit collusion, to distinguish it from the explicit, spoken kind that had put executives in Philadelphia hotel rooms and, eventually, in a Philadelphia courtroom. Sweezy's contribution, the "kinked demand curve," tried to explain a related puzzle: why oligopoly prices often seem sticky, resistant to change in either direction, even as costs shift underneath them. His answer was that a firm expects rivals to match a price cut, discouraging price wars, but expects rivals to ignore a price increase, discouraging unilateral hikes — leaving prices stuck at whatever level the market had already settled into, a level that need not have anything to do with what a truly competitive market would produce.

None of this theory, on its own, was illegal, and courts were careful — are still careful — to keep it that way. In 1954, the Supreme Court decided *Theatre Enterprises, Inc. v. Paramount Film Distributing Corp.*, a case brought by a suburban Baltimore movie theater that could not obtain first-run films because every major distributor, without any apparent communication among them, insisted on giving first-run rights only to downtown theaters. The theater owner argued that this uniform behavior was circumstantial proof of an illegal agreement. The Court disagreed, holding that parallel business behavior is not itself sufficient evidence of a conspiracy, because — in language later courts would cite for decades — businesses are permitted, even expected, to take note of what their competitors are doing and act accordingly. "Conscious parallelism," the resulting doctrine came to be called: firms that independently watch each other and independently reach the same conclusion are behaving exactly as a competitive market intends them to, and the law cannot punish that resemblance to collusion without some further evidence that the parallelism was coordinated rather than merely observed. Stigler, Chamberlin, and Sweezy had, between them, described a mechanism by which markets could produce cartel-like prices through nothing more than rational self-interest and careful observation — and American law had, at almost the same moment, drawn a line declaring that rational self-interest and careful observation, by themselves, were not a crime. The gap between what the economists showed was possible and what the courts were willing to punish is the gap this book's later chapters live inside.

Game theory supplied the last piece of the theoretical machinery, and it arrived from an unlikely direction: not from economists studying cartels at all, but from a political scientist trying to understand why nuclear-armed superpowers sometimes cooperate. In the late 1970s, Robert Axelrod, at the University of Michigan, organized a computer tournament built around the prisoner's dilemma, the classic game in which two players each choose to cooperate or defect without knowing the other's choice in advance, and in which defecting is always individually rational in a single round even though mutual cooperation would leave both players better off than mutual defection. Axelrod's innovation was to run the game repeatedly between the same two players, round after round, so that a strategy could build a reputation and respond to a rival's past behavior — turning a game about a single, isolated decision into a game about an ongoing relationship. He invited game theorists from around the world to submit computer programs, encoded strategies that would play the repeated game against every other submission, and the results were tallied. The winner, both in Axelrod's first tournament and again in a second, larger one he ran the following year with entries reacting to what had worked before, was the simplest program submitted: tit for tat, written by the mathematician and psychologist Anatol Rapoport, which did nothing more than cooperate on the first move and then copy whatever its opponent had done on the previous move. No threats, no memory beyond one round, no complicated modeling of the opponent's intentions — just an immediate, transparent willingness to reward cooperation and punish defection, one round at a time.

Axelrod's finding, published in his 1984 book *The Evolution of Cooperation*, was that mutual cooperation between self-interested actors with no ability to communicate, no enforceable contract, and no central authority could nonetheless emerge and stabilize, provided the interaction repeated often enough and the participants cared enough about future rounds relative to the present one. Economists already had a formal version of this result, known as the folk theorem — so called because, unlike most results in economics, no single person is credited with discovering it; the insight circulated informally among game theorists for years before anyone bothered to publish a rigorous proof. The folk theorem shows that in a game repeated indefinitely, with players sufficiently patient about future payoffs, almost any outcome that leaves every player at least as well off as their worst-case punishment — including an outcome that looks exactly like a price-fixing cartel — can be sustained as a stable equilibrium, enforced entirely by the credible threat that any deviation will trigger retaliation in future rounds. No communication is required for the folk theorem to apply. No agreement is required. All that is required is repetition, patience, and the ability to observe and respond to what the other player just did — precisely Stigler's monitoring problem, restated in the language of game theory, and precisely the three ingredients a modern pricing algorithm has in genuinely inhuman abundance: it interacts with its rivals' prices thousands of times a day, it has effectively no impatience because it is not a person waiting to get paid, and it can observe a rival's price change within seconds rather than the weeks or months it might take a human sales manager to notice a pattern in the noise.

Stigler's theory got its most spectacular real-world confirmation almost thirty years after he published it, in a market that had nothing to do with apartments, airlines, or gasoline: the over-the-counter stock market run by the National Association of Securities Dealers, known as NASDAQ. In May 1994, two finance professors, William Christie of Vanderbilt and Paul Schultz of Ohio State, published a study noticing something odd in NASDAQ's price quotes. Stock prices at the time were still quoted in fractions of a dollar rather than decimals, and market makers were technically free to post any eighth-of-a-dollar increment they liked — an eighth, a quarter, three-eighths, and so on. Christie and Schultz found that for a large group of actively traded stocks, including Apple, Amgen, and Microsoft, market makers almost never quoted the "odd" eighths — an eighth, three-eighths, five-eighths, seven-eighths — sticking overwhelmingly to even quarter-dollar increments instead. The practical effect was to keep the minimum spread between what a market maker would pay and what it would charge stuck at a quarter of a dollar rather than the eighth of a dollar the exchange technically allowed, effectively doubling the built-in cost of trading for every investor on the other side of those trades. No market maker had to tell another market maker to avoid odd eighths. Each one simply observed that everyone else was avoiding them, and understood — exactly as Stigler's theory predicted — that quoting an odd eighth first would only invite the others to match it, eroding a spread that was, as long as everyone held the line together, comfortably wider than competition alone would have produced.

The publication of the Christie-Schultz study did what Stigler's monitoring theory said good monitoring should do: it broke the pattern almost immediately. Within days of the paper appearing, odd-eighth quotes on the stocks it named jumped sharply and spreads on those stocks fell by nearly half, a natural experiment that economists would later cite as unusually clean evidence that the pre-1994 pattern really had been sustained by nothing more than mutual, unspoken restraint rather than any cost-based justification. The Securities and Exchange Commission and the Justice Department's Antitrust Division both opened investigations; recorded phone calls, subpoenaed as part of the inquiry, reportedly captured traders at rival firms explicitly warning each other against quoting odd eighths, turning what had started as a purely statistical anomaly into something closer to old-fashioned, spoken-word collusion once investigators started listening. By 1998, thirty-seven Wall Street brokerages had agreed to a $1.03 billion class-action settlement — at the time the largest civil antitrust settlement in American history — and two dozen of the firms separately agreed, as part of a Justice Department consent decree, to start tape-recording their traders' phone calls, a remedy that reads, in hindsight, like a direct institutional acknowledgment of the same lesson Robert Crandall's taped call had already taught a decade earlier: if the collusion is going to happen over the phone, the fix is to put a tape recorder on the phone. Nobody, in 1998, thought to ask what would happen once the pricing decisions stopped being made by traders on a phone at all — once the quoting convention that Christie and Schultz had caught by hand, using nothing more than published trade data and a statistics package, became something a machine could learn to reconstruct on its own, at a speed and scale no professor with a dataset of historical quotes could ever have observed it happening in real time.

It would be tidy, and wrong, to say that Stigler and Axelrod predicted algorithmic pricing. Neither man was writing about computers setting prices, and neither lived to see the cases this book examines — Stigler died in 1991, Axelrod is a political scientist who spent most of his career on questions of international cooperation and conflict, not corporate pricing. What they did, working from opposite ends of the problem — one asking how cartels survive, the other asking how cooperation arises without a central authority — was describe, with unusual precision for economics of that era, the exact conditions under which non-communicating rivals can settle into a mutually beneficial, cartel-like equilibrium through nothing more than repeated observation and calculated response. They described the theory of the phenomenon roughly sixty and forty years, respectively, before anyone built a machine efficient enough to execute it at scale, in real markets, continuously, without the fatigue, impatience, or moral hesitation that had always limited how far a human sales manager was willing to take Stigler's logic on their own. The economists who would eventually test whether an artificial intelligence could rediscover tit-for-tat on its own, without being told the strategy existed, are the subject of chapter five, and their result is close to the thing this chapter has been building toward: computer programs with no instruction to cooperate, no ability to talk to each other, and no concept of fairness or law, converging, again and again, on strategies that look remarkably like Rapoport's simple, forty-year-old, prize-winning move — cooperate, and punish whoever breaks the peace.

None of this theory implies that tacit collusion is easy, automatic, or even likely in most markets, and the discipline's own skeptics have spent decades making that case, for reasons this book takes seriously rather than treating as a footnote. The folk theorem's guarantee of sustainable cooperation depends on players being patient enough to value future payoffs nearly as much as present ones, on the game continuing indefinitely with no clear end, and on players being able to observe each other's behavior with reasonable accuracy — three conditions that hold cleanly in a laboratory model and far less cleanly in a real industry where firms enter and exit, products differ in ways that make prices hard to compare directly, demand shifts for reasons that have nothing to do with rival behavior, and a market with two firms behaves very differently from a market with twenty. Add players, and Stigler's own logic works against collusion rather than for it: more sellers means more possible sources of an unexplained sales increase, which means slower, noisier detection of cheating, which means a weaker deterrent against it. The economist Jean Tirole, whose 1988 textbook *The Theory of Industrial Organization* became the standard graduate reference on precisely this literature, was characteristically careful to frame tacit collusion as a possibility oligopoly theory could not rule out rather than an outcome it could reliably predict; real markets, Tirole and others after him have stressed, are full of the kind of complexity — differentiated products, fluctuating costs, uncertain demand, buyers who can walk away — that make Stigler's clean detection problem much harder to solve in practice than a two-firm, one-product model on a page suggests. This caution matters for everything that follows in this book, because it is exactly the caution that gets lost when a news article calls a piece of pricing software a "collusion machine": the theory says collusion becomes more achievable as monitoring gets cheaper and more precise, not that cheap monitoring guarantees collusion regardless of every other feature of the market it's dropped into.

What Stigler, Chamberlin, Sweezy, and Axelrod could not have anticipated was how radically technology would eventually collapse the costs their theories treated as fixed. Detecting a rival's secret price cut, in Stigler's model, required statistical inference from noisy sales data, a slow and imperfect process for a mid-century manufacturer. A modern software platform can simply see the rival's price directly, updated continuously, because the rival is often using the same vendor, the same dataset, or a competing product scraping the same public listings. Sustaining a punishment strategy, in Axelrod's tournament, required a computer program executing a simple rule across a few hundred simulated rounds. A production pricing algorithm executes an analogous rule across millions of real transactions, learning and adjusting between each one, with no lunch break, no quarterly earnings pressure to cut a deal for short-term volume, and no human discomfort at the idea of quietly punishing a competitor who steps out of line. The theory in this chapter was never the hard part. The hard part, historically, was building something patient enough, well-informed enough, and tireless enough to actually run the strategy the theory always said would work. The first attempt did not require anything as sophisticated as artificial intelligence — it required nothing more exotic than a shared database that let every airline in the country watch every other airline's proposed fares within hours instead of weeks, and it arrived a full three decades before anyone thought to teach a computer to set prices on its own. The next chapter is about that database, and about a Justice Department lawsuit that treated it, correctly, as Stigler's monitoring problem solved by wire rather than by algorithm.
