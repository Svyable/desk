# Everyone Gets Their Own Price

The cart-abandonment and mouse-movement tracking the FTC documented deserve a moment of plain explanation for readers unfamiliar with the underlying web-analytics technology, because the mechanics reveal how far the relevant data collection extends beyond what most consumers would guess they are exposing. Modern retail websites routinely embed tracking code that records not only which pages a visitor viewed and which items they purchased, but granular behavioral signals most visitors never consciously register generating at all: how long a cursor lingers over a particular price before moving on, whether a visitor scrolled past a product without stopping, which items were added to a cart and then removed before checkout, and how a visitor's browsing pace on a given day compares with their own historical pattern or with a broader population of similar visitors. None of this tracking requires a visitor to create an account, log in, or knowingly submit any personal information at all; it operates continuously, passively, and largely invisibly, through the ordinary act of browsing a retail website the way a shopper would browse a physical store's shelves. The FTC's finding that companies were using exactly this class of signal to individualize pricing represents, in a very literal sense, the logical extension of Amazon's crude 2000-era cookie-based experiment, examined in the next chapter, into a considerably richer and more comprehensive behavioral profile than a quarter-century-old dynamic-pricing test could have drawn on.

Every case examined so far in this book involves a version of the same basic structure: multiple nominally competing sellers, pooling information through a shared intermediary, converging on a single price that every buyer in a given market ends up facing. This chapter turns to a related but conceptually distinct phenomenon, one that does not require multiple sellers at all, and that raises different economic and ethical questions even though it draws on much of the same underlying data and technology. Surveillance pricing, as regulators and researchers have come to call it, is the practice of a single seller charging different prices to different individual customers for the identical product, based not on the customer's location, timing, or purchase volume in the traditional sense, but on granular, often highly personal data about who that specific customer is and what the seller's models predict that customer is willing to pay.

The economics of price discrimination are old, well-understood, and, in their classical form, not inherently sinister; the British economist Arthur Cecil Pigou laid out the standard taxonomy in his 1920 treatise *The Economics of Welfare*, distinguishing what he called first-degree, second-degree, and third-degree price discrimination in terms every economics student since has learned. Third-degree discrimination, the most familiar variety, charges different prices to different identifiable groups — a student discount, a senior discount, a matinee movie ticket priced below an evening showing — using a broad, easily verified category rather than information about any specific individual. Second-degree discrimination lets customers sort themselves by choosing among different versions or quantities of a product — a bulk discount, a premium airline cabin, a "buy two, get one free" offer — without the seller needing to know anything about who is buying. First-degree discrimination, sometimes called perfect price discrimination, is the theoretical extreme: a seller who somehow knows exactly what each individual customer is willing to pay, and charges precisely that amount to every customer, extracting the maximum possible revenue from every single transaction. Pigou treated first-degree discrimination largely as a textbook limiting case, useful for teaching the underlying theory but essentially unachievable in practice, because no real seller before the era of comprehensive digital data collection could plausibly know each individual customer's precise willingness to pay.

What changed, in the years leading up to this book's writing, is that first-degree price discrimination stopped being a textbook abstraction and became, for a growing number of sellers, a genuinely pursuable commercial strategy — not perfect, in the economist's idealized sense, but far closer to it than anything Pigou could have imagined in 1920. The Federal Trade Commission formalized its own investigation into this shift in July 2024, invoking its authority under Section 6(b) of the FTC Act to compel eight companies to turn over detailed information about how they develop and sell what the agency termed surveillance-pricing products and services — tools that let retailers and other sellers adjust prices for individual consumers based on precise location, browsing history, purchase history, device type, demographic profile, and other real-time behavioral signals. Six of the eight companies ordered to respond ultimately provided the material the study would rely on: Mastercard, the consulting firm Accenture, the pricing-software vendors PROS, Bloomreach, and Revionics, and the consultancy McKinsey & Company — a client list that itself reveals something about how far surveillance pricing has penetrated ordinary commerce, spanning payment-network infrastructure, general management consulting, and dedicated retail pricing technology rather than being confined to any single, easily isolated industry niche.

The FTC released a preliminary account of its findings in January 2025, in the form of a staff research summary rather than the comprehensive final report the 6(b) process is typically designed to produce, and the timing of that release turned out to matter as much as its content. The findings themselves were striking on their own terms: the agency's staff reported that companies were drawing on a remarkably granular range of behavioral signals to set individualized prices, extending well beyond the kind of location or browser data most consumers might already suspect retailers track, down to signals as fine-grained as how a shopper's mouse moved across a webpage and which specific items a shopper had added to and then abandoned in an online cart before checking out — behavioral exhaust that most consumers generate constantly and unknowingly, and that the study found companies were actively capturing and feeding into pricing models designed to estimate, transaction by transaction, exactly how much a given individual was likely to accept paying.

The release landed at an unusually fraught political moment for the underlying investigation, and understanding that timing is essential to understanding why the study's ultimate fate looks so different from the RealPage and Cendyn litigation examined in earlier chapters. The FTC had opened its 6(b) inquiry under Chair Lina Khan, whose tenure was broadly associated with an aggressive, consumer-protection-forward approach to antitrust and data-privacy enforcement; her successor, Andrew Ferguson, became chairman on January 20, 2025, mere days after the preliminary research summary was published, and the two Republican commissioners who would go on to lead the agency under the new administration, Ferguson and Melissa Holyoak, issued a dissenting statement objecting to the manner and timing of the release, accusing the outgoing Democratic majority of what they characterized as slowly leaking selective findings rather than completing the careful, comprehensive final report the 6(b) process was designed to produce. That comprehensive final report, as this book was being finished in the second half of 2026, had still never been published; Ferguson's FTC had effectively left the underlying market study incomplete, even while insisting, in subsequent public statements, that the agency remained attentive to the underlying concern.

The partisan dimension of this particular regulatory reversal deserves to be stated plainly, because it complicates a narrative running through several of this book's other chapters in which algorithmic-pricing concern reads as a broadly bipartisan phenomenon. Chapter sixteen documented a legislative wave spanning cities and states with genuinely different political character, and chapter nine's account of the RealPage settlement drew criticism from progressive advocates without becoming a partisan flashpoint in the way the underlying litigation itself did not obviously split along party lines. Surveillance pricing's federal regulatory trajectory has followed a more conventionally partisan arc: opened and initially pursued under a Democratic FTC chair, then substantially slowed, though not entirely abandoned, under her Republican successor, with the Republican commissioners' own January 2025 dissenting statement framing the concern explicitly in terms of process and administrative propriety rather than disputing the underlying factual findings about mouse-movement tracking and cart-abandonment data. Whether that partisan pattern holds for the algorithmic-collusion cases examined elsewhere in this book, or whether surveillance pricing specifically proved more susceptible to a change in FTC leadership because it never generated the kind of concrete, bipartisan-friendly enforcement action a completed RealPage-style settlement provides, is a distinction future scholars of this period will likely find worth examining more carefully than this book, writing in the middle of the story rather than after its conclusion, is yet positioned to resolve.

Ferguson's own public position on surveillance pricing evolved in a direction that illustrates a broader pattern this book has traced across several of its regulatory chapters: rhetorical concern persisting even as the specific enforcement mechanism shifts or stalls. In April 2026, Ferguson told reporters that agency staff were continuing to examine the issue and directed staff to explore whether the commission should issue a formal policy statement addressing when sellers using highly personalized pricing ought to be required to disclose that practice to the consumers paying it. Weeks later, on April 14, 2026, the FTC issued an Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking covering several related consumer-pricing concerns simultaneously — total price disclosure requirements, so-called "junk fee" transparency, personalized-pricing disclosure, and protections against unauthorized recurring billing — folding the surveillance-pricing question into a broader rulemaking process rather than pursuing it as a freestanding priority. An investigative piece published in *The American Prospect* in August 2026, examined against the same timeline, argued bluntly that despite the agency's continued rhetorical commitment to addressing surveillance pricing, its actual enforcement record had produced essentially no concrete action against the practice by the time of publication — a critique that echoes, in a different regulatory context, the same disappointment tenant advocates voiced about the RealPage settlement's lack of teeth in chapter nine.

The clearest public test of how ordinary consumers would react to a major company openly discussing individualized pricing arrived not through the FTC's own investigation but through Delta Air Lines, in a controversy that unfolded largely in real time over the course of 2025 and offers a useful, concrete case study to set against the FTC's more abstract, staff-level research summary. Delta executives told investors on an earnings call that the airline had begun testing AI-driven pricing on a small share of tickets — roughly 1 percent in 2024, expanding toward a stated goal of 20 percent of fares by the end of 2025 — describing the early results as "amazingly favorable." Glen Hauenstein, Delta's president, told investors in November that the system's ambition was to arrive at "a price that's available on that flight, at that time, to you, the individual," language that, read alongside the FTC's own definition of surveillance pricing, drew an immediate and pointed reaction once it reached a wider audience beyond the investor call it was originally delivered to.

Three Democratic senators — Ruben Gallego, Mark Warner, and Richard Blumenthal — sent Delta a formal letter in July raising both data-privacy and price-discrimination concerns, writing that the airline's individualized pricing practices would likely mean fare increases calibrated to each individual consumer's personal "pain point," at a moment when, in the senators' framing, American families were already struggling with the broader cost of living. Delta's public response was an unambiguous denial that stood in some tension with its own executives' earlier investor-facing language: the company stated flatly that it had never used, tested, or planned to use any fare product that targets customers with individualized offers based on their personal information. Senator Gallego was openly skeptical of the reconciliation, telling reporters that "Delta is telling their investors one thing, and then turning around and telling the public another," and pointing to a Delta executive's own comments to investors describing an AI system capable of pricing tickets based on what a given passenger appeared willing to pay for premium products layered on top of a base fare. Representatives Jesús García and Jerry Nadler led a group of House colleagues in a parallel demand for answers directly from Delta's chief executive, extending the same congressional pressure this book has already documented, in chapter eight, shaping the RealPage investigation into a second industry entirely.

The Delta episode is instructive less for its ultimate factual resolution — which remained genuinely contested, with the airline's public denials sitting awkwardly beside its own executives' earlier statements to Wall Street — than for what it reveals about the gap between the FTC's careful, technical staff research and the far more visceral, immediate response ordinary consumers have when a familiar brand appears to be discussing charging them a personally calibrated price. The FTC's January 2025 research summary, dense with references to mouse-movement tracking and cart-abandonment signals, generated relatively modest mainstream news coverage outside specialized legal and privacy trade press. Delta's earnings-call language, four words long — "to you, the individual" — generated national headlines, congressional letters, and a direct corporate denial within weeks. The difference is not that Delta's practices were necessarily more extensive or more troubling than the six companies the FTC had already examined; it is that a familiar consumer brand, discussing its own pricing plans in its own executives' own words, makes an abstract regulatory concern concrete and personal in a way a government research summary about unnamed enterprise software vendors rarely manages to do on its own.

It is worth being precise about how surveillance pricing relates to, and differs from, the algorithmic-collusion cases that occupy most of this book, because conflating the two phenomena — as popular commentary frequently does — obscures rather than clarifies what each one actually threatens. Every case examined in chapters eight through eleven involves multiple sellers converging, through a shared mechanism, on a single price that every buyer in a given market faces roughly equally; the underlying antitrust harm is that a market that should have many independent price-setters effectively has one, and that concentration of pricing power raises the price everyone pays. Surveillance pricing inverts this structure entirely: a single seller, with no need to coordinate with any competitor at all, charges different prices to different buyers of the identical product, based on individualized data about who each buyer is. The economic harm, to the extent regulators and consumer advocates argue there is one, is not that competition has been suppressed in the aggregate — a surveillance-pricing seller may face plenty of genuine competition from rival sellers — but that the seller has become so good at estimating each individual buyer's specific willingness to pay that ordinary consumers lose the practical ability to know whether the price in front of them reflects the actual market or reflects, instead, a model's private judgment about how much that particular person, given their particular browsing history, income signals, and purchasing patterns, can be persuaded to accept.

It is worth noting, too, that the eight companies the FTC originally compelled to respond under its 2024 order were not selected at random, and the specific mix reveals something about how deeply surveillance-pricing infrastructure had already penetrated ordinary commerce by the time regulators formally took notice. Mastercard's inclusion is particularly telling: a payment-network company sits at a structural chokepoint through which an enormous share of all retail transactions already flow, giving it visibility into purchasing patterns that individual retailers, however sophisticated their own data collection, could never independently assemble on their own. McKinsey and Accenture's inclusion, as general management consultancies rather than dedicated software vendors, suggests surveillance-pricing capability was being built into much broader digital-transformation engagements these firms sell to retail clients across many sectors simultaneously, rather than existing only as a narrow, specialized product category. The FTC's selection of these six respondent companies functioned, in effect, as an informal map of where personalized-pricing capability actually resides in the American economy: not concentrated in a single identifiable industry the way RealPage dominates apartment revenue management, but distributed across payment infrastructure, general consulting, and specialized software vendors serving retailers across an enormous range of unrelated product categories.

That distinction matters enormously for how each phenomenon should be regulated, and it is a distinction this book's remaining chapters try to preserve carefully rather than collapse into a single undifferentiated worry about "AI and prices." A remedy built for algorithmic collusion — restricting how much nonpublic competitor data a pricing tool can ingest, the core mechanism behind the RealPage settlement examined in chapter nine — does essentially nothing to address surveillance pricing, because surveillance pricing requires no competitor data at all; it runs entirely on data the seller already has about its own individual customers. A remedy built for surveillance pricing — mandatory disclosure that a price has been personalized, the direction Ferguson's FTC gestured toward in its 2026 rulemaking — does little to address algorithmic collusion, because disclosing that a price was personalized says nothing about whether that price was also, independently, inflated by coordination among competing sellers pooling data through a shared vendor. A renter facing a RealPage-influenced price and a shopper facing a surveillance-priced hotel booking are experiencing two different failures of the same underlying trend — pricing decisions migrating from human judgment, exercised transparently and roughly uniformly across a customer base, to software making individualized or coordinated decisions largely invisible to the person paying the resulting bill — and treating the two failures as interchangeable, as much popular and even some regulatory commentary tends to, risks producing remedies calibrated for the wrong problem.

The uncomfortable overlap between Delta's dynamic-pricing ambitions and the legitimate revenue-management tradition traced in chapter seven deserves acknowledgment before this chapter closes, because it shows how easily the line between the two can blur even for a sophisticated airline with forty years of yield-management experience behind it. Airlines have always priced seats differently based on booking timing, fare class, and route-specific demand, exactly the legitimate practice Robert Crandall pioneered in 1985; nothing about that history is new or troubling on its own. What alarmed the senators writing to Delta was the prospect of a further, qualitatively different step — pricing keyed not to when a ticket is purchased or which fare class it belongs to, information equally available to every purchaser making the same choice, but to who, specifically, is doing the purchasing, using data about that individual's personal browsing behavior, financial profile, or demonstrated price sensitivity that no other customer buying the identical seat on the identical flight would see reflected in their own price. Delta's public denial insisted the airline had not crossed that line; its critics in Congress remained unconvinced the distinction was being honored in practice. Resolving that specific factual dispute is beyond what public reporting can settle conclusively as this book goes to print, but the shape of the argument — is this legitimate, decades-old yield management, or is it something categorically new wearing yield management's respectable branding — is the exact same argument this book traced in chapter seven regarding RealPage's own evolution from a legitimate forecasting tool into a pooled-data pricing engine, applied now to a single seller's use of individual customer data rather than to a vendor pooling data across many competing sellers.

The next chapter turns from the regulatory and legal apparatus surrounding both phenomena to the psychology underneath them, examining what happens when ordinary consumers actually discover, as they periodically do, that the price they were charged differed from the price charged to someone else for the identical product — a discovery that has produced some of the sharpest, most immediate public backlash of any episode in this book's entire history, well before RealPage, Cendyn, or the FTC's own surveillance-pricing inquiry existed.
