# The Copycat Cities

The specific timing of this legislative wave, concentrated so heavily within an eighteen-month window between September 2024 and early 2026, is itself worth remarking on before this chapter turns to the individual cities. Local legislation of this kind, on ordinary matters unrelated to a live national news cycle, typically moves at a considerably slower pace, often taking years to travel from initial proposal to committee hearing to final council vote in any single jurisdiction, let alone across more than a dozen separate jurisdictions in close succession. The speed of this particular wave suggests the underlying political conditions were unusually favorable: a housing-affordability crisis already dominating local political discourse in cities across the country gave elected officials an immediately available, emotionally resonant villain in RealPage's software, at exactly the moment the Justice Department's own parallel investigation was generating a steady stream of national headlines that made the underlying practice legible to local officials and their constituents without requiring each individual city council to conduct its own independent investigation into a genuinely technical subject.

San Francisco's September 2024 ordinance, examined in the previous chapter, did not stay a solitary experiment for long. Within roughly eighteen months, a cluster of American cities across the country had passed some version of the same basic prohibition, and three states had gone further, banning algorithmic rent-setting statewide rather than leaving the question to individual municipalities. Tracking this wave in detail matters for this book's argument because it shows something the RealPage litigation and San Francisco's own uneven enforcement record cannot show on their own: a genuine, broad-based, cross-partisan political consensus that pooled, nonpublic-data-driven rent algorithms cross a line ordinary dynamic pricing does not, even as the practical mechanics of actually stopping the practice remain, city after city, strikingly difficult to pin down.

The specific drafting choices individual cities made, even while broadly following San Francisco's template, reveal a genuine policy-learning process worth noting rather than treating this wave as a single uniform ordinance copied and pasted a dozen times over. Some cities defined "nonpublic competitor data" more broadly than San Francisco's original language, aiming to close loopholes local advocates had identified after watching how RealPage's lawyers characterized the company's own data practices in the Berkeley litigation examined later in this chapter. Others attached their prohibition to existing rent-stabilization or just-cause eviction ordinances already on the books, reasoning that folding the new prohibition into an existing, previously litigated regulatory framework might provide more durable legal footing than standing the new rule up as an entirely freestanding ordinance vulnerable to the kind of First Amendment challenge RealPage would eventually bring against Berkeley. This variation across jurisdictions, while it complicates the goal of a single, predictable national standard this chapter's closing section discusses, also functions as a genuine natural experiment in ordinance design, one whose comparative results — which drafting approach actually produces enforceable, litigation-resistant law — remained too early to assess with confidence as this book was being completed.

Philadelphia moved second, passing its own ordinance in October 2024, roughly a month after San Francisco, with language that traced San Francisco's model closely: a prohibition on algorithm-driven revenue-management tools that rely on nonpublic competitor data to recommend residential rental prices. Berkeley, San Diego, and Minneapolis followed in the first several months of 2025, each city codifying a similar prohibition into its own municipal code — Berkeley's ordinance taking effect in March 2025, San Diego's in late February, Minneapolis's in late March — a pace of adoption that accelerated noticeably through the spring of that year as city councils in one jurisdiction after another cited the others' example as precedent for their own local action. Seattle joined the wave in June 2025 with a notably sharper financial penalty than San Francisco's original ordinance, setting a fine of $7,500 for each individual instance of a landlord using an algorithm-based pricing service, a sevenfold increase over San Francisco's $1,000-per-violation ceiling that suggests later-adopting cities were consciously trying to give their own versions more enforcement teeth than the pioneering ordinance had turned out to carry. Jersey City, Providence, Santa Monica, Hoboken, Portland, Spokane, Santa Ana, Evanston, and King County, Washington rounded out a list that, by the time this book was finished, spanned the entire continental United States and several genuinely different regional housing markets, urban cores and smaller cities alike.

The multifamily housing industry's own trade association, the National Apartment Association, mounted a sustained, national opposition campaign against this entire legislative wave, publishing its own recurring analysis tracking the spread of what it consistently characterized as ill-conceived legislation that would prohibit housing providers from lawfully using ordinary data-analytics technology to make routine pricing and vacancy decisions. The association's public materials framed the issue, consistently, as a matter of preserving legitimate business efficiency against what it portrayed as legislative overreaction to a mischaracterized business practice — an institutional echo, delivered at trade-association scale, of the same defense RealPage itself has mounted directly in its Berkeley litigation and its response to the Justice Department's complaint. Whether the NAA's advocacy meaningfully slowed the legislative wave this chapter documents is difficult to measure with precision, given how many cities ultimately passed some version of the prohibition despite the association's sustained opposition; what the advocacy campaign does demonstrate clearly is that the industry itself, unlike some of the more genuinely contested technical questions examined elsewhere in this book, has treated the entire wave of local ordinances as a unified threat worth a unified, well-resourced, national response, rather than as a scattered collection of unrelated local disputes.

The state-level response arrived somewhat later but carried considerably broader legal force, since a statewide ban reaches every municipality within a state's borders rather than requiring city-by-city adoption. California and New Jersey enacted statewide prohibitions, and New York followed in a step advocacy groups and legal commentators described as especially significant given the state's size and the prominence of its rental markets: Governor Kathy Hochul signed legislation, formally designated Senate Bill S.7882, making New York the first state in the country to prohibit the use of algorithmic pricing tools specifically in residential rent-setting, a law firms tracking the antitrust landscape characterized in analyses published shortly after signing as signaling a broader, more assertive posture toward state-level antitrust enforcement generally, extending well beyond the housing sector alone.

The sheer geographic and political breadth of this legislative wave is worth pausing on, because it cuts against a simple story in which concern about algorithmic rent-setting divides neatly along the partisan lines that shape so much of contemporary American housing policy. Cities as different in political character and housing-market structure as San Diego, Minneapolis, Jersey City, and Spokane all passed some version of the same underlying prohibition within roughly a year of each other, suggesting the underlying concern — that a shared piece of software pooling nonpublic data across landlords can produce collusive-feeling outcomes regardless of a given city's rent-control regime, housing supply, or local political culture — resonated broadly enough to clear very different local legislative processes with comparatively little of the sustained partisan opposition that characterizes most contested housing policy in the United States. Advocacy organizations like Local Progress, a network of progressive local elected officials that has tracked and encouraged the spread of these ordinances explicitly, treated the pattern as evidence of a genuinely national grassroots movement, with individual city councils learning directly from one another's legislative language and enforcement design rather than each reinventing the underlying policy independently.

Berkeley's own experience, which unfolded in full public view over the course of 2025, supplies the clearest and most concrete illustration available anywhere in this book of exactly how badly the balance of legal resources can tilt against a city trying to enforce one of these ordinances against a well-capitalized corporate defendant. RealPage itself sued Berkeley in April 2025, months before the city's own ban was even scheduled to take effect, arguing that the ordinance violated the company's First Amendment rights by illegally preventing it from communicating data-driven pricing recommendations to its own clients — reframing the entire dispute, in a single legal filing, from an antitrust and consumer-protection question into a free-speech question, a reframing this book's earlier chapters have not had occasion to examine but that follows a well-established pattern in First Amendment litigation, where commercial speech doctrine has periodically been used to challenge regulations that restrict what a business may communicate to its own customers. RealPage's complaint characterized the underlying factual premise of Berkeley's ordinance as resting on "misinformation," directly disputing the causal story this book's chapters eight and nine have laid out at length regarding how the company's software actually operates and what effect it has on rents.

Berkeley, a city of roughly 120,000 people already confronting a budget shortfall reported at around $27 million heading into its 2025 fiscal year, faced a stark choice: fund a potentially expensive, multi-year First Amendment defense against a company with the financial resources of a $10.2 billion private-equity-backed enterprise, or quietly step back from enforcing its own recently passed law. The City Council chose the latter, voting unanimously in June 2025 to postpone the ordinance's effective date by a full year rather than proceed into litigation Berkeley's own budget situation made difficult to sustain. Local reporting on the decision was direct about the underlying dynamic: the city was not conceding that its ordinance was legally deficient, but was making a pragmatic judgment that a small municipality's limited legal budget could not comfortably absorb a prolonged fight against a defendant with RealPage's resources, regardless of how the underlying legal merits might eventually shake out if the case were ever fully litigated. Berkeley holds the distinction, as this book was being finished, of being the first city RealPage chose to sue directly over one of these ordinances, out of a field that by then included more than a dozen similar local bans — a strategic choice that industry observers read as an attempt to establish a chilling legal precedent against the entire wave of copycat legislation examined in this chapter, by targeting the jurisdiction least able to afford defending itself, rather than San Francisco, whose ordinance had already been in effect the longest and had already drawn the most public attention.

The Berkeley episode reframes the enforcement puzzle this chapter and the previous one have been documenting in a genuinely important way. San Francisco's own toothless enforcement record, examined in chapter fifteen, could plausibly be attributed to limited investigative resources, limited tenant awareness, or simple bureaucratic inertia — mundane, unglamorous explanations for why a law on the books had not yet produced a single lawsuit. Berkeley's experience suggests a more pointed possibility: that the underlying corporate defendants these ordinances target are not merely difficult to catch violating the law, but are actively and strategically willing to spend meaningful legal resources fighting the laws themselves before they ever have the chance to be enforced against anyone, using exactly the kind of well-funded, sophisticated litigation this book's central chapters have shown RealPage deploying against the Justice Department itself. A municipal ordinance that a determined, well-resourced defendant can simply sue into indefinite postponement, without ever needing to prevail on the underlying merits, offers considerably less practical protection to the tenants it was written to help than its supporters' celebratory press releases, issued at the moment of passage, generally suggested it would.

Local Progress and similar advocacy networks tracking this legislative wave have generally framed each new city's adoption as evidence of accelerating momentum, a framing that is not wrong on its own factual terms — more cities did pass ordinances in 2025 than in 2024 — but that this book's account of San Francisco's and Berkeley's actual experiences suggests deserves a more measured reading than a simple momentum narrative provides. Passing an ordinance and meaningfully constraining the underlying conduct that ordinance targets are, this chapter and the one before it have shown repeatedly, two distinct achievements that do not automatically travel together, and a genuinely honest accounting of this legislative wave's significance has to hold both the real, cross-partisan political consensus this chapter has documented and the real, largely unresolved enforcement gap in view simultaneously, rather than letting the first fact stand in for the second.

That said, the specific enforcement weaknesses San Francisco's experience revealed in the previous chapter are not unique to San Francisco, and early reporting on some of the other cities in this wave has begun to surface similar patterns, even though most of the newer ordinances had not yet been in effect long enough, as this book was finished, to generate the kind of two-year retrospective the *San Francisco Standard* was able to produce. Several of the newer ordinances explicitly attempted to learn from San Francisco's apparent enforcement gap by building in sharper penalties, as Seattle's $7,500-per-violation figure illustrates, or by creating more explicit investigative authority for city attorneys rather than relying primarily on private tenant litigation to surface violations. Whether those design changes will translate into a meaningfully different enforcement record than San Francisco's remains, as of this book's writing, an open empirical question rather than a settled one — the newest ordinances in this wave were barely a year old when this chapter was drafted, too recent for the kind of enforcement-record retrospective this book has been able to apply to San Francisco's considerably older law.

New York's statewide law deserves slightly closer attention than the city-level ordinances this chapter has otherwise treated as a group, because its scale and its timing make it the single most consequential piece of legislation examined in this chapter after San Francisco's original ordinance. Signed by Governor Hochul after the wave of city-level bans had already demonstrated, across more than a dozen jurisdictions, that some version of this prohibition could pass without triggering the kind of sustained industry defeat the National Apartment Association's opposition campaign had presumably hoped to produce, New York's law extends the prohibition across an enormous and famously expensive rental market, reaching millions of tenants who would otherwise have depended on a patchwork of city-specific rules that, as this chapter has already shown, do not reliably extend even across a single metropolitan region's own municipal boundaries. Law firms advising corporate clients on compliance treated the New York law's passage as a genuine inflection point, signaling that state legislatures, not merely progressive city councils, were now willing to enact this specific prohibition — a broader base of legislative support than the ordinance's critics, including RealPage's own litigation strategy against Berkeley, had perhaps anticipated when the wave began with a single California city council vote in September 2024.

A deeper structural problem underlies nearly every ordinance in this entire wave, one this book's earlier chapters have already surfaced in a different context and that deserves to be named explicitly here rather than left implicit. Every city and state ban examined in this chapter targets a single geographic jurisdiction, prohibiting the sale or use of algorithmic pricing tools within that specific city's or state's boundaries. RealPage, Cendyn, and every other revenue-management vendor examined in this book operate as national businesses, serving landlords across dozens of metropolitan areas and, in RealPage's case specifically, subject to a federal consent decree, examined in chapter nine, that already restricts certain practices nationwide regardless of any individual city's own ordinance. A landlord operating buildings in both San Francisco and a neighboring, unregulated suburb faces genuinely different legal obligations block by block, a patchwork that mirrors, in miniature, the federal circuit split this book documented in chapter ten between the Ninth and Third Circuits' contradictory rulings on the Cendyn casino-hotel litigation. Whether this jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction approach will eventually converge toward a uniform national standard, whether through the kind of comprehensive federal legislation Senator Klobuchar's stalled bill proposed, through further litigation eventually producing binding nationwide precedent, or through software vendors themselves simply adopting the most restrictive jurisdiction's rules as a de facto national baseline rather than maintaining dozens of separate, jurisdiction-specific product configurations, is a question this book's closing chapters return to directly, because the answer will shape how much of the entire regulatory apparatus surveyed across this chapter and the one before it ultimately amounts to more than a patchwork of well-intentioned, unevenly enforced local experiments.

A final comparison worth drawing, before this chapter closes, sets the entire American local-ordinance wave against the insurance-regulation experience examined in chapter fourteen, because the contrast is instructive about what makes a prohibition actually bite rather than merely exist on paper. State insurance commissioners moved against price optimization using bulletins and advisory memoranda backed by a century-old statutory framework that already gave them clear authority to police "unfair discrimination" in rate-setting, operating within a regulatory apparatus that already included routine rate filings, actuarial review, and an established institutional relationship between insurers and their regulators that made compliance monitoring a normal, expected part of doing business rather than an exceptional enforcement action. The cities examined in this chapter, by contrast, are attempting to police a comparatively obscure, largely invisible piece of backend software using ordinary municipal code-enforcement mechanisms — building inspectors, business-license reviewers, and city attorneys' offices — that were never designed with anything like a pricing algorithm in mind, and that generally have no routine, ongoing visibility into a landlord's internal software stack the way an insurance commissioner has routine visibility into a filed rate schedule. That institutional mismatch, more than any defect in the ordinances' actual drafted language, may be the single best explanation available for why insurance price optimization was substantially curtailed within about eighteen months of the first state bulletin, while San Francisco's rent-algorithm ban, examined in the previous chapter, produced no enforcement action at all across nearly two full years on the books.
