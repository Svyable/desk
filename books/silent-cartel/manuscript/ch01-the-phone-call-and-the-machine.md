# The Phone Call and the Machine That Replaced It

To understand why Crandall's call happened at all, it helps to understand the fare war that provoked it, because the underlying commercial pressure recurs, in different form, across nearly every case this book goes on to examine. Braniff, once one of the most storied names in American aviation, had expanded aggressively into new routes following the 1978 Airline Deregulation Act, betting that deregulation's promised competitive gains would reward the carrier bold enough to move fastest into newly opened markets. The bet went badly. By early 1982, Braniff was hemorrhaging cash, slashing fares on routes it shared with American in a desperate bid to fill seats and generate enough revenue to service its expansion-fueled debt, a strategy that was, from American's perspective, actively damaging both airlines' finances simultaneously without meaningfully growing the overall market for either of them. Braniff would file for bankruptcy within a year of Crandall's call, in May 1982, becoming one of the first major casualties of the very deregulation that had briefly seemed to promise expanded opportunity for exactly the kind of aggressive new competition it had attempted.

On the evening of February 1, 1982, Robert Crandall, the president of American Airlines, picked up the telephone and called Howard Putnam, the president of Braniff International, a rival carrier that was bleeding money in a fare war the two men's airlines had been waging out of Dallas–Fort Worth. Crandall did not know it, but Putnam was recording the conversation. What Crandall said next would end up quoted in a Department of Justice complaint, dissected in an antitrust casebook still assigned in law schools four decades later, and remembered, imperfectly, as the moment American aviation almost fixed its prices out loud.

"I think it's dumb as hell for Christ's sake, all right, to sit here and pound the s— out of each other and neither one of us making a f—ing dime," Crandall told Putnam, according to the transcript Braniff later supplied to federal investigators. He went further. "Raise your goddamn fares twenty percent. I'll raise mine the next morning." Putnam pushed back, and Crandall pressed again: "You'll make more money and I will too."

Putnam refused, on tape, and reported the call. The Justice Department sued American in February 1983, not for price-fixing — no agreement had actually been reached, and antitrust law generally requires one — but for attempted monopolization, arguing that Crandall's proposal amounted to an illegal invitation to collude even though Braniff had turned it down. American settled by agreeing to a consent decree barring Crandall from discussing prices with competitors for the rest of his tenure. He was not indicted. He was not fired. Three years later, in 1985, he was still running American, and Braniff, whose president had done the ethical and legal thing, was gone — liquidated after a bankruptcy the price war had helped cause. It is one of the more instructive footnotes in the history of American business: the executive who broke, or came very close to breaking, the law kept his job, and the one who turned him in still lost his company.

The Crandall call has survived in memory for a reason that has little to do with aviation economics and everything to do with evidence. For most of the twentieth century, price-fixing was, almost definitionally, a spoken-word crime. It required two or more human beings to communicate an intention to raise prices together, and it left behind exactly the kind of proof a courtroom is built to use: a recording, a note passed at a trade-association dinner, a hotel-room meeting later described by a cooperating witness. The infamous 1950s cartel of electrical-equipment manufacturers — General Electric and Westinghouse among them — that fixed prices on turbines and switchgear got caught partly because the conspirators, worried about being seen together, developed a code in which company representatives were assigned numbers instead of names for meeting invitations, a level of cloak-and-dagger effort that itself became evidence once investigators found it. Price-fixing left fingerprints because it required people to say the illegal thing to each other, and people are bad at saying illegal things without leaving a trace.

The electrical-equipment case is worth pausing on a moment longer, because it shows what the old system did when it worked. Executives from General Electric, Westinghouse, and roughly two dozen smaller manufacturers had spent much of the 1950s meeting secretly in hotel rooms across the country to divide up the market for turbine generators, circuit breakers, and switchgear sold to public utilities, using a rotation system nicknamed "the phase of the moon" because the schedule for who won which contract changed with a fixed calendar the conspirators had worked out among themselves. They used pay phones instead of office lines, met under assumed names, and destroyed documents after each meeting, precautions that read, in hindsight, less like ordinary business caution and more like a confession of what they already knew they were doing. It did not save them. Federal investigators pieced the conspiracy together from expense reports, hotel receipts, and eventually cooperating witnesses, and in February 1961 a federal judge in Philadelphia sentenced seven corporate executives to thirty days in prison — the first time in American history that businessmen from major corporations had actually been jailed for a Sherman Act violation, rather than merely fined. The case became a landmark precisely because it proved the system could work: enough people had to know about the plan, enough of them eventually talked or left a trail, and the trail was enough to convict.

The system's other landmark case, decided a generation before the electrical-equipment prosecutions, supplied the theory this book will need most in its later chapters, so it is worth introducing now. In 1939, the Supreme Court decided *Interstate Circuit, Inc. v. United States*, a case about movie-theater chains in Texas that had each separately agreed with film distributors to enforce minimum ticket prices, without any of the theater chains ever directly communicating with each other. The Court held that an illegal conspiracy could still exist, because each theater chain knew the distributors were making the identical demand of its rivals, and each chain's cooperation only made sense as part of a coordinated scheme. Antitrust lawyers call this a hub-and-spoke conspiracy: a central actor, the hub — in 1939, the film distributors; eight decades later, a pricing-software vendor — sits at the center of a wheel, striking similar arrangements with a set of competitors, the spokes, who never need to speak to one another because the hub is doing the coordinating on everyone's behalf. Interstate Circuit did not involve so much as a shared telephone line between the theater chains, let alone software. It is, nonetheless, the closest thing available in 1939 to a legal blueprint for what several of the cases in this book's second half will argue a modern pricing algorithm actually is: not a neutral tool two competitors happen to use in parallel, but a hub, coordinating spokes that never had to meet.

This book is about what happens when that requirement disappears — when the price two competitors both charge can end up identical, or near-identical, or reliably higher than either would have set alone, without either of them ever picking up a phone. It is not a book about a hypothetical future. Every case examined in the chapters that follow already happened, most of them in the years just before this one was written, and several of them are still working their way through federal courts as this sentence is being typed. A Texas software company is under a Justice Department settlement, its business model rewritten by court order, for building a tool that let landlords in the same city implicitly agree on rent without a single landlord calling another landlord. A pricing algorithm used by casino-hotels along the Las Vegas Strip and the Atlantic City Boardwalk has produced two contradictory federal appellate rulings within roughly a year of each other, one saying its effects amount to nothing a court can touch, the other saying the opposite. Economists working with nothing more exotic than the same reinforcement-learning technique that teaches a computer to play chess have shown, repeatedly, in controlled experiments, that software with no instruction to collude and no ability to communicate will nonetheless teach itself to charge more than a competitive market would allow, and will teach itself to punish any algorithm that tries to undercut it.

None of this required a conspiracy in the sense Crandall attempted with Putnam. That is the uncomfortable part. American antitrust law, going back to the Sherman Act of 1890, was built around a specific mental event: an agreement, a meeting of minds, two or more parties who understood themselves to be coordinating and chose to do it anyway. Judge Learned Hand, in a 1945 opinion that remains a foundational statement of the law, wrote that mere parallel conduct — two firms independently arriving at the same price because they are responding to the same market conditions — is not by itself illegal, because a market is exactly the kind of place where competitors are supposed to watch each other and react. Conscious parallelism, this is called: two rivals watching the same signals and landing in the same place, each one deciding independently that this is simply what a smart competitor does. The line the law has always tried to draw is between rivals independently reaching the same conclusion, which is legal, and rivals agreeing in advance to reach it together, which is not. That line assumed there would be two minds involved, and assumed, further, that those minds would leave some trace of having communicated.

What happens when the mind doing the pricing is not, technically, a mind at all, or is a piece of software trained on data from every landlord in a neighborhood, or is licensed from the same handful of vendors that serve entire industries? The University of Chicago economist and Nobel laureate George Stigler, writing in 1964 in an essay this book returns to in the next chapter, had already worked out the theoretical mechanics of how sellers could sustain higher-than-competitive prices without an explicit agreement, using nothing but the ability to observe and punish deviation. Stigler was writing about human oligopolists watching each other's public price lists. He had no way of knowing that, sixty years later, his logic would describe, almost exactly, what a family of algorithms trained by four Italian economists would do to each other inside a computer simulation with no market, no products, and no humans in the loop at all — a result this book examines closely in chapter five, because it is arguably the single most important piece of evidence in the entire debate over what pricing software is actually capable of on its own.

The word this book uses most often for the phenomenon — "collusion" — is doing real, contested work, and it is worth being honest about that from the first chapter rather than the last. Collusion, in ordinary legal usage, implies intent: an agreement, a plan, a shared understanding among parties who know what they are doing to a market and choose to do it anyway. Some of what follows in this book fits that definition comfortably. Some of it does not, and the disagreement over where to draw that line is not a footnote to the story; it is a great deal of the story. A poster seller on Amazon who programs a bot to match a co-conspirator's price, having already agreed by email to fix that price, is committing old-fashioned collusion with new-fashioned tools — a crime with a paper trail, examined in chapter four. A pricing algorithm that teaches itself, with no human instruction and no communication channel, to punish a rival that undercuts it, is doing something structurally similar in its effect on consumers but categorically murkier in the eyes of a judge trained to look for an agreement. Whether the second case deserves the same word, the same moral weight, or the same legal remedy as the first is a question this book takes seriously rather than assuming away, and chapter eighteen is devoted entirely to the strongest arguments that the word "collusion" is being stretched further than the evidence supports. A reader who wants the comfortable version of this story — a clean villain, a clean fix, a clean ending — should know now that this book does not think the comfortable version survives contact with the actual court record.

Start with the plainest, best-documented case, because it makes the mechanism unusually legible before the murkier cases complicate it. In October 2022, the investigative outlet ProPublica published an article by reporter Heather Vogell describing a piece of software called YieldStar, sold by a Texas company named RealPage, that had by then become the dominant revenue-management tool for large apartment landlords across the United States. RealPage's own marketing told prospective clients plainly what the software was for: "Find out how YieldStar can help you outperform the market 3% to 7%." Outperforming the market is an odd phrase for a tool that is, in theory, simply helping one landlord set a fair price for one building; markets, in the textbook telling, are supposed to be things landlords compete inside of, not things they outperform. The mechanism Vogell described was straightforward once laid out: competing property managers, who would never legally be permitted to call each other and compare notes on what they planned to charge next month, instead fed their occupancy levels, lease-renewal dates, and asking rents into the same software, which pooled that nonpublic information across an entire metro area and then told each landlord what to charge — landlords who, in ProPublica's reporting, accepted the software's recommendation the overwhelming majority of the time. A former RealPage executive told ProPublica the pricing data gave landlords insight into what competitors within a half-mile or a mile were charging, information no individual landlord could have lawfully obtained by asking directly. Three United States senators later wrote that they were concerned the arrangement amounted to, in their words, "a cartel to artificially inflate rental rates in multifamily residential buildings." In some Sun Belt neighborhoods that the Justice Department would go on to cite in litigation, RealPage-suggested prices governed between a third and two-thirds of an entire multifamily building stock; in one Seattle neighborhood ProPublica examined, ten separate, competing property managers were all using RealPage to set rent on 70 percent of the units.

The Justice Department, joined eventually by eight state attorneys general, sued RealPage in August 2024, alleging violations of Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act — the same century-old statute Robert Crandall had narrowly avoided being prosecuted under in 1983. The government later amended its complaint to add six of the country's largest landlords as co-defendants: Greystar, Cushman & Wakefield, Camden Property Trust, Cortland Management, Willow Bridge Property Company, and Blackstone's LivCor. By late 2025, RealPage had agreed to a seven-year settlement that did not admit wrongdoing but did rewrite how its software is allowed to operate — restricting it to pricing data at least twelve months old, barring real-time lease information, and prohibiting geographic pricing models built at a finer resolution than the state level. Two of the landlord defendants, Cortland and Greystar, settled as well. As this book was being written, the case remained active against the rest, and a separate suit brought by the Commonwealth of Kentucky was still moving through the courts on its own timeline. Nobody in this story taped a phone call. Nobody had to.

That is the shape of the argument this book is going to make across the chapters that follow, and it is worth stating plainly before the evidence arrives, in the spirit of a book that would rather tell a reader where it is going than let the thesis sneak up on them. A century of American competition law was built on the assumption that fixing a price requires an agreement, that an agreement requires communication, and that communication leaves a trace a regulator or a plaintiff's lawyer can eventually find. Pricing software has not repealed that assumption everywhere — the poster sellers on Amazon in chapter four communicated plenty, in writing, and got prosecuted for exactly the crime their grandfathers would have been prosecuted for. But pricing software has opened a wide and rapidly growing space in the middle: markets where every competitor uses the same handful of vendors, trains on overlapping pools of nonpublic data, and arrives, again and again, at prices no individual competitor could have set alone without appearing to gouge — prices that look, feel, and function like the output of a cartel, produced by parties who never spoke, using tools that were, in some cases, never told to do anything but maximize revenue.

Whether the law, the economics, and the ordinary human sense of fairness can catch up to that space is genuinely unresolved, and this book does not pretend otherwise. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals looked at almost exactly this fact pattern in a case involving Las Vegas casino-hotels and concluded, in 2025, that no antitrust claim survived; the Third Circuit, looking at a nearly identical fact pattern involving Atlantic City casino-hotels using the same software vendor, reversed a dismissal the following year and let the case proceed. Two federal appellate courts, applying the same statute to the same underlying technology within about twelve months of each other, reached opposite conclusions — a split this book examines in detail in chapter ten, because it is the clearest evidence available that American law genuinely does not yet know what to do with a cartel that never held a meeting.

A note on method before the argument gets underway. Every case in this book is real, dated, and drawn from primary sources where they were reachable — court filings, government reports, published economics papers, and contemporaneous journalism — rather than composite anecdotes assembled for narrative convenience. Where a claim depends on a lawsuit still in progress, this book says so and reports the claim as an allegation rather than an established fact; where an academic finding comes from a laboratory simulation rather than an observed real-world market, this book flags the distinction rather than letting the two blur together, because a great deal of the public conversation about "algorithmic collusion" has done exactly that blurring, and the confusion has made the topic easier to dismiss than the underlying evidence deserves. This book also tries to hold onto the cases that complicate its own thesis rather than filtering them out. Chapter six shows an empirical study of Germany's gasoline market finding that algorithmic pricing raised margins only when every competing station in a market adopted the software — a result that cuts against the idea that algorithmic collusion is easy or automatic, since it shows the effect collapsing whenever adoption is partial. Chapter eighteen goes further and gathers the strongest arguments, made by serious economists and not merely by industry lobbyists, that a meaningful share of what gets called algorithmic collusion in the press is closer to ordinary, lawful, demand-responsive pricing wearing a menacing new name. A reader owed an honest account of this subject is owed both halves of it: the genuine and growing evidence that pricing software can produce outcomes indistinguishable from a cartel, and the genuine uncertainty, still unresolved in the case law and the economics literature alike, about how often it actually does.

Robert Crandall died in 2024, having spent his later career as one of the airline industry's most quoted elder statesmen, his 1982 phone call recycled in business-school case studies as a cautionary tale about saying the quiet part out loud. The quiet part, this book argues, no longer needs to be said out loud at all. It can be trained into a model, licensed as a subscription, and delivered as a daily pricing recommendation that a landlord, a hotel manager, or a gas-station owner is free — and, the evidence increasingly suggests, functionally pressured — to accept. What follows is an attempt to take that machinery seriously: how it was built, what the economists who study it have actually found under laboratory conditions and in the field, which real markets it has already reshaped, what the law has managed to do about it so far, what the strongest skeptics of the whole "algorithmic collusion" framing get right, and what is likely to happen next, now that the conversation Robert Crandall started on a telephone in 1982 has migrated somewhere no wiretap can reach.
