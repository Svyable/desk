# Brussels Writes It Down

The institutional architecture of European competition enforcement differs from its American counterpart in a further structural respect worth explaining before this chapter turns to specific cases, because the difference shapes how quickly and how uniformly any given piece of guidance actually reaches the twenty-seven individual member states whose own national competition authorities share enforcement responsibility with Brussels. The European Commission's Directorate-General for Competition can issue guidelines and pursue enforcement actions with effect across the entire European Union, but day-to-day competition enforcement in any individual member state also depends heavily on that country's own national competition authority — Germany's Bundeskartellamt, already familiar from chapter six's account of the gas-station case, France's Autorité de la concurrence, and their counterparts across the rest of the bloc — each operating with a meaningful degree of independent enforcement discretion even while nominally bound by the same EU-wide legal framework. This two-tiered structure means the "Brussels writes it down" framing this chapter's title suggests is, in an important sense, only half the story: guidance written centrally in Brussels still depends on twenty-seven separate national enforcement bodies to translate that guidance into the kind of concrete investigations and penalties this book's American chapters have documented in such granular detail, a structural reality that likely contributes to the same guidance-versus-enforcement gap this chapter's closing sections examine at length.

European competition authorities arrived at concern over algorithmic pricing earlier than their American counterparts did, in at least one important formal sense, even though the enforcement actions and court rulings this book has spent its central chapters documenting have overwhelmingly been American. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, whose membership includes most of Europe's major economies alongside the United States, circulated an internal working paper titled "Algorithms and Collusion" in 2017, three years before Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò, and Pastorello's landmark laboratory result gave the theoretical concern its clearest empirical backing. Margrethe Vestager, the European Union's competition commissioner from 2014 to 2024, began publicly warning about the risk as early as a March 2017 speech in Berlin, telling an audience of competition regulators that companies "can't escape responsibility for collusion by hiding behind a computer program" — a formulation of the exact enforcement principle this book's American case studies would spend the following decade testing in federal courtrooms from Nevada to New Jersey.

It is worth noting that the OECD working paper predating both Vestager's warning and the EU's eventual guidelines was itself a genuinely multinational document, drafted with input from competition officials across member countries including the United States, meaning American antitrust officials had access to essentially the same early theoretical warning European regulators did, in 2017, three years before any regulator on either continent had concrete evidence like Calvano's laboratory result to act on. That both sides of the Atlantic received the same early warning but proceeded down such different institutional paths afterward — America toward the case-by-case litigation this book's central chapters document, Europe toward the guidance-based framework this chapter describes — reflects less a difference in how seriously each side took the underlying concern than a difference in the basic tools each legal system makes available to regulators confronting a genuinely novel problem before the first concrete violation has actually occurred.

The European Union's principal regulatory response arrived through a mechanism quite different from the case-by-case litigation this book has traced through the American legal system: rather than waiting for enforcement actions to build precedent one contested case at a time, the European Commission built algorithmic-pricing guidance directly into its own generally applicable competition-law framework. The Commission adopted revised Guidelines on Horizontal Cooperation Agreements, effective July 1, 2023, updating the EU's existing rules governing what kinds of cooperation and information-sharing among competitors are and are not permissible under Article 101 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, the EU's rough equivalent to Section 1 of the Sherman Act. The revised guidelines added an entire chapter addressing information exchange specifically in the context of algorithmic pricing tools, warning explicitly that algorithms "then become a device to facilitate collusion" — using language, "collusion by code," that legal commentators tracking the guidelines' publication seized on as a memorable, almost deliberately quotable encapsulation of the entire regulatory concern this book has spent its middle chapters documenting through individual American lawsuits.

The structural difference between the European and American approaches is worth drawing out explicitly, because it reflects a genuinely different regulatory philosophy rather than merely a difference in enforcement pace. American antitrust law, as this book's earlier chapters have shown repeatedly, develops primarily through litigation: a plaintiff or a government enforcer brings a specific case against a specific defendant, a court rules on the specific facts before it, and the resulting precedent — Theatre Enterprises, Interstate Circuit, Gibson, Cornish-Adebiyi — accumulates gradually, case by contested case, often producing genuine uncertainty and, as chapter ten documented at length, outright contradictions between different circuits interpreting the identical statute. European competition law, by contrast, operates substantially through ex ante regulatory guidance issued by the European Commission itself, giving companies a general rulebook to consult and comply with before any dispute ever reaches a court, rather than asking them to infer the boundaries of lawful conduct from a gradually accumulating, occasionally contradictory body of judicial precedent. The Commission's 2023 guidelines did not wait for a European equivalent of the RealPage litigation to work its way through years of contested proceedings before stating a general principle; they stated the principle directly, in advance, as guidance every company operating in the EU's single market was expected to consult before deploying a pricing algorithm at all.

Vestager's tenure itself, spanning a full decade as competition commissioner before her departure in 2024, gave the European Commission an unusual continuity of leadership on exactly this issue that the American regulatory landscape, examined throughout this book's other chapters, has struggled to match. The FTC's own posture on surveillance pricing, chronicled in chapter twelve, shifted meaningfully within months of a change in the agency's chairmanship, and the Justice Department's Antitrust Division has operated under leadership from Makan Delrahim through Jonathan Kanter to Gail Slater across the same period this book's central chapters span, each bringing a somewhat different enforcement philosophy to the same underlying docket of cases. Vestager, by contrast, held her position long enough to see the OECD's 2017 working paper, her own 2017 Berlin warning, Calvano's 2020 laboratory result, and the 2023 Horizontal Guidelines all unfold under a single, consistent institutional voice — a continuity advantage that may partly explain why the EU's guidance-based approach arrived at a clearly articulated general principle years before its American counterpart, even if, as this chapter has already shown, that early clarity has not yet translated into meaningfully faster concluded enforcement.

This is not to say the European approach has produced faster or more decisive real-world enforcement than the American pattern this book has documented at length — the evidence on that score remains genuinely mixed, and this book resists the temptation to declare one continent's regulatory philosophy simply superior to the other's. Linsey McCallum, the European Commission's Deputy Director-General for Antitrust, confirmed in July 2025 that Brussels was conducting multiple active inquiries into algorithmic pricing mechanisms across different sectors, language that, read carefully, describes ongoing investigation rather than completed enforcement action — a status not meaningfully further along, in practical terms, than the RealPage litigation had reached by a comparable point in its own American timeline, or than the FTC's still-unfinished surveillance-pricing study examined in chapter twelve. The German gas-station case examined in chapter six, despite occurring within EU jurisdiction and despite the Bundeskartellamt's own leading role in creating the underlying transparency infrastructure that made the Assad-Clark-Ershov-Xu study possible, did not produce a formal EU-level antitrust enforcement action against any specific software vendor or group of gas stations, remaining, to date, primarily an academic case study rather than a litigated legal precedent — a reminder that clear, well-documented economic evidence of algorithmic pricing effects and a formal legal finding of liability are two genuinely separate achievements, on either side of the Atlantic.

The most concrete illustration of the European Commission's enforcement machinery in action, as of this book's writing, involves a genuinely novel wrinkle worth setting apart from every other case examined in this book: regulators using artificial intelligence themselves, not to investigate a pricing algorithm directly, but to detect the kind of verbal price signaling this book traced back to Robert Crandall's 1982 phone call in the first place. In January 2024, the Commission conducted unannounced inspections — dawn raids, in the field's own terminology — at the premises of Michelin and several other major tire manufacturers, a move the Commission later disclosed had been triggered by an AI-assisted screening exercise that analyzed hundreds of thousands of the companies' public statements and earnings-call transcripts, hunting for language patterns consistent with price signaling among competitors, before a manual review of the screening's findings persuaded investigators the evidence justified a physical raid. Michelin challenged the Commission's inspection decision in the EU's General Court, and the resulting July 2025 judgment in Michelin v Commission became an important test of how much latitude regulators have to build an entire investigation's evidentiary foundation on an automated language-analysis tool applied to public corporate communications rather than the kind of internal documents and recordings this book's earlier chapters have shown driving American cases like the electrical-equipment prosecutions and the NASDAQ market-maker settlement.

The Commission did not stop at tires. Unannounced inspections using the same broad investigative posture followed in 2024 at companies active in financial derivatives and data-center construction, and in 2025 at firms in the non-alcoholic beverage sector — a spread across genuinely unrelated industries that suggests the Commission's AI-assisted screening tool, once built for one sector, was quickly generalized into a standing capability applied wherever the underlying language-pattern analysis flagged a plausible signal, rather than a bespoke tool built and discarded for a single tire-industry investigation. This pattern of broad, sector-agnostic deployment mirrors, on the enforcement side, the same general-purpose quality this book's twentieth chapter identified as the central risk on the collusion side: just as a single foundation model licensed across many unrelated industries could in principle become a single point of coordination spanning sectors that share no obvious commercial relationship, a single AI-assisted detection tool, once built, can just as readily be pointed at tires, derivatives, data centers, and soft drinks in quick succession, with comparatively little additional engineering required to extend its reach from one sector to the next.

The Michelin episode inverts, in a genuinely interesting way, the entire technological arms race this book has otherwise documented as running in one direction — sellers deploying increasingly sophisticated algorithms to coordinate prices, regulators scrambling to catch up using comparatively primitive, document-by-document investigative tools. Here, instead, a regulator got to the automation first, at least in this specific instance, using machine-learning text analysis to scan a volume of corporate communications — hundreds of thousands of earnings-call transcripts and public statements — that no team of human investigators could plausibly have read and cross-referenced by hand within any reasonable timeframe. Whether this kind of AI-assisted detection becomes a routine tool in the European Commission's enforcement kit, and whether American regulators, working within Twombly's considerably stricter pleading-standard constraints discussed in chapter ten, could deploy something similar without running into the same "how much can you allege before you've even seen the evidence" problem that shaped the Gibson and Cornish-Adebiyi litigation, remains an open and genuinely consequential question this book cannot resolve, but one worth flagging clearly: the same technology capable of learning to collude, this book's chapter five demonstrated at length, may turn out to be just as capable of learning to catch the humans who still, on occasion, collude the old-fashioned way.

Vestager's own framing of the problem, echoed and formalized in the 2023 guidelines she helped shape before leaving office in 2024, rested on a specific doctrinal move worth setting explicitly against the American conscious-parallelism doctrine this book traced through Theatre Enterprises in chapter two. European competition law under Article 101, like American law under Sherman Act Section 1, generally requires some form of concerted practice rather than merely parallel, independently arrived-at behavior; the EU's own case law has developed a body of doctrine, going back decades before algorithmic pricing existed, addressing when information exchange among competitors crosses from lawful market transparency into an unlawful concerted practice. The 2023 guidelines' innovation was to apply that pre-existing information-exchange doctrine explicitly and specifically to algorithmic pricing tools, reasoning that a company deploying a shared pricing algorithm cannot use the algorithm's automation as a shield against liability that would clearly attach if the identical information exchange and price coordination had been accomplished by human employees meeting in person — precisely the reasoning Judge Rakoff had reached for, using different language, in his 2016 ruling on Spencer Meyer's case against Uber, examined in chapter eleven, and precisely the reasoning the Justice Department's Jonathan Kanter invoked in his own remarks announcing the RealPage lawsuit, examined in chapter nine. Two continents, two different legal systems, and two regulatory bodies that rarely coordinate their enforcement priorities directly, converged independently on functionally the same core principle: a computer program does not get to inherit a legal immunity its human operators never had.

The United Kingdom, having left the European Union in 2020 and lost the direct benefit of Brussels's 2023 Horizontal Guidelines as a result, has pursued its own parallel track worth noting briefly for completeness, since it illustrates a third regulatory model distinct from both the American litigation-driven approach and the EU's guidance-driven one. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority has identified algorithmic pricing as an enforcement priority in its own right, drawing on powers under domestic UK competition law that closely mirror, but operate independently of, the EU framework Britain exited. Legal commentary tracking developments on both sides of the English Channel through 2025 grouped the UK and EU together as having simultaneously escalated algorithmic-pricing enforcement priority, even as the two regimes now operate under formally separate legal authorities following Brexit — a reminder that the underlying economic and technological pressures driving concern about algorithmic collusion do not respect the same jurisdictional boundaries that now separate British and European competition law from each other, any more than they respect the boundary between a Nevada casino and an Atlantic City one.

Where the two systems diverge most sharply, and where this book's closing chapters find the most genuine cause for both hope and skepticism about the road ahead, is in how each treats the relationship between guidance and enforcement. The EU's ex ante guidelines give companies clearer advance notice of what conduct crosses the legal line, in principle reducing the kind of genuine, good-faith uncertainty that has characterized much of the American litigation this book has surveyed — RealPage's lawyers, Cendyn's lawyers, and the various casino-hotel and landlord defendants examined throughout this book have each, at different points, argued in good faith that existing American antitrust doctrine simply did not clearly forbid what their software did, an argument considerably harder to sustain against language as explicit as the EU's own "collusion by code" framing. But clear advance guidance is not, on its own, enforcement, and the European Commission's active-inquiries status as of 2025, more than eight years after Vestager's original Berlin warning, suggests that translating a clearly stated legal principle into an actual, concluded enforcement action against a specific company remains just as slow and just as difficult in Brussels as it has proven to be in Washington — a convergence, in outcome if not in method, that runs through nearly every regulatory chapter this book has examined, from San Francisco's toothless rent ordinance to the FTC's unfinished surveillance-pricing report to the European Commission's own still-open inquiries into the exact phenomenon its own guidelines were written, years in advance, to prevent.

The European Parliament has periodically pressed the Commission for a more assertive posture than the Directorate-General for Competition's own cautious, inquiry-stage public statements have so far reflected, with individual members of Parliament from several political groupings raising formal written questions to the Commission about the pace of algorithmic-pricing enforcement, particularly with respect to the housing sector, where several EU member states have their own versions of the affordability pressures driving the American ordinances examined in chapters fifteen and sixteen. The Commission's own responses to these parliamentary questions have generally reiterated the 2023 guidelines' existing framework rather than announcing new, sector-specific measures, a pattern of institutional caution that European housing advocates have criticized in terms that echo, almost exactly, the disappointment American tenant advocates voiced about the RealPage settlement's lack of teeth in chapter nine. The parallel is worth naming because it suggests the gap between clearly stated legal principle and concretely felt enforcement is not a peculiarly American failing, rooted in some specific defect of Sherman Act doctrine or Twombly's pleading standard, but a more general pattern that recurs whenever a regulatory body confronts a genuinely novel, fast-moving technology using an institutional process built, on both sides of the Atlantic, for a slower and more deliberate era of commercial life.
