# The Posters on Amazon

The buy-box mechanism itself deserves brief explanation for readers unfamiliar with third-party e-commerce marketplaces, because understanding exactly how narrow and mechanical the competition for it is helps explain why price coordination proved so tempting, and so mechanically simple to automate, for the sellers this chapter examines. When multiple sellers list an identical product on Amazon Marketplace, only one seller's listing appears as the default, one-click purchase option most shoppers actually click, with Amazon's own algorithm selecting that default seller using a formula weighing price, shipping speed, seller performance metrics, and inventory availability. Losing the buy box does not mean losing the sale entirely, but it means losing the overwhelming majority of casual, one-click purchases, since few shoppers bother clicking through to see the full list of alternative sellers offering the identical item at a different price. This structure creates an unusually direct, almost mechanical link between price and sales volume that few other retail environments replicate as cleanly: a seller who can win the buy box by pricing a single cent below a competitor captures a hugely disproportionate share of that product's sales, and a seller who loses the buy box by pricing a single cent above a competitor loses nearly all of them — precisely the kind of tight, immediate, and continuously observable feedback loop that made automated repricing bots, whether lawfully deployed or bent toward the illegal conspiracy examined in this chapter, an almost irresistible tool for any seller competing in this specific environment.

David Topkins ran a small business selling wall posters through Amazon's Marketplace, the platform Amazon built to let independent sellers list products alongside its own inventory and compete for the "buy box," the single default purchase button Amazon displays to a shopper, which in practice goes to whichever seller offers the most attractive combination of price, shipping speed, and seller rating. Buy-box competition among poster sellers is about as close to pure price competition as retail gets: posters are commodity products, buyers rarely care which particular seller ships them, and the deciding factor in most transactions is simply which listed price is lowest. That structure is exactly the kind Stigler's theory identifies as easiest to monitor and easiest to collude in — a handful of sellers, a standardized product, and, on a platform like Amazon Marketplace, prices that are not merely observable but published to the public in real time, refreshed continuously, and available to anyone, competitor or customs authority, who cares to look.

Sometime around September 2013, Topkins and a group of competing poster sellers began doing something that would eventually make his case the answer to a trivia question in every antitrust conference panel about pricing software for the next decade: they agreed, in plain language, to fix prices on certain posters sold through the Marketplace, and then wrote code to make the agreement enforce itself. According to the Justice Department's account of the scheme, the conspirators divided responsibility for monitoring and matching. One company would program its pricing algorithm to search continuously for the lowest price offered by a seller outside the conspiracy — a genuine competitor, not part of the deal — and set its own price just below that outside seller's price, undercutting the legitimate competition while staying above the price the conspiracy had agreed to defend. A second conspiring company would program its own algorithm to track the first company's price and match it automatically. The result, running with no further human input once the code was deployed, was a set of poster prices that moved in lockstep, stayed above what open competition would have produced, and updated itself continuously without requiring a single additional phone call, email, or meeting after the initial agreement and the initial lines of code.

The Antitrust Division of the Justice Department announced Topkins's guilty plea in April 2015, describing it explicitly as the division's first criminal prosecution specifically targeting price-fixing conducted through e-commerce using pricing algorithms — a framing the department clearly intended as a warning shot to an industry that had, by the mid-2010s, seen an explosion of automated repricing tools built to help ordinary, law-abiding Marketplace sellers stay competitive without manually checking rivals' prices dozens of times a day. Topkins pleaded guilty to a single count of violating the Sherman Act and agreed to pay a $20,000 fine and cooperate with the government's ongoing investigation into the broader poster-pricing conspiracy, a modest penalty that reflected both the relatively small dollar volume of the specific scheme and prosecutors' interest in using Topkins's cooperation to reach the other participants rather than maximizing punishment against the first person to plead. William Baer, then head of the Antitrust Division, used the announcement to make a point that has been quoted in nearly every subsequent legal memo on this subject: price-fixing remains price-fixing, the department said in substance, whether the conspirators shake hands, exchange spreadsheets, or write software to do the shaking and exchanging for them. The method of execution does not change the legal character of the underlying agreement.

This is the sentence worth sitting with, because it is easy to read the Topkins case as evidence that algorithms are dangerous in some free-floating, technology-specific way, and that reading gets the case backward. Nothing about the Topkins conspiracy depended on any special capability of the algorithms involved. The code the conspirators wrote was, by the standards of modern pricing software, almost primitive — a simple rule-based system that searched for a reference price and applied a fixed offset, the kind of repricing logic that thousands of entirely legitimate Amazon sellers were running, and continue to run, without ever committing a crime. What made Topkins's conduct illegal was not the algorithm. It was the conversation that preceded it: an actual agreement, reached through actual communication among actual human beings, about what the algorithms would be told to do and why. Strip away the code entirely and imagine the same sellers agreeing by phone to check each other's prices manually every morning and adjust accordingly — the legal analysis does not change at all. The algorithm in the Topkins case was a delivery mechanism for an ordinary, old-fashioned, Sherman Act Section 1 conspiracy, not a novel legal category unto itself. It automated the execution of an agreement that was itself entirely conventional.

That distinction — automation of an agreement versus algorithmic production of collusive outcomes with no agreement at all — is the single most important conceptual fork in this entire book, and the Topkins case sits squarely on the easy side of it. Every automated repricing tool legitimately used across e-commerce, from small Amazon Marketplace operations to major retailers, performs some version of the same basic function Topkins's software performed: it watches competitors' publicly posted prices and adjusts a seller's own price in response, usually to undercut a rival and win the buy box, occasionally to hold a price steady rather than race to the bottom. Companies like Feedvisor and RepricerExpress built entire businesses in the 2010s selling exactly this kind of software as a service to ordinary sellers who had no interest in colluding with anyone and simply wanted to avoid manually checking hundreds of competitor listings every day. None of that is illegal, and nothing in the Topkins prosecution suggested otherwise; watching a public price and reacting to it is the definition of competitive behavior the Supreme Court protected in Theatre Enterprises three decades before Amazon Marketplace existed. What made the poster conspiracy a crime was the private agreement about what price to defend and how to defend it — an agreement that existed independently of, and prior to, any code — with the software serving only as the hands that carried out a decision the conspirators' minds had already made together.

The Justice Department's broader investigation, which the Topkins plea agreement described him as agreeing to assist, eventually reached other poster sellers and other product categories on Amazon Marketplace, producing a string of additional guilty pleas over the following two years involving sellers of items ranging from customized promotional products to other Marketplace commodity goods, each case following a similar pattern: an explicit agreement, reached through ordinary written or verbal communication, followed by algorithmic enforcement of that agreement through repricing software. Trod Limited, a United Kingdom company trading online as Buy 4 Less and several similar storefront names, pleaded guilty in August 2016 to participating in the same poster conspiracy Topkins had already admitted to, and the company's former director, Daniel Aston, pleaded guilty the following January and was eventually sentenced to six months in prison — a materially harsher outcome than Topkins's fine, reflecting Aston's larger role in orchestrating the scheme rather than any difference in the underlying legal theory. A year after that, in August 2017, the Antitrust Division announced a guilty plea from Zaappaaz Inc. and its president, Azim Makanojiya, for an unrelated but structurally identical conspiracy to fix prices on customized promotional products such as wristbands and lanyards sold online — proof that the poster case was not a one-time curiosity but the first documented instance of a pattern prosecutors would keep finding as they went looking for it, across product categories that shared nothing except a commodity good, a public marketplace, and sellers willing to let code enforce what they had already agreed to by other means. It is worth dwelling for a moment on how large and how ordinary the legitimate version of this technology had already become by the time Topkins pleaded guilty, because the scale of lawful repricing is what makes the illegal version so hard to spot from the outside. An entire commercial industry — companies selling software with names like Feedvisor, RepricerExpress, and Informed Repricer — had grown up specifically to serve the mundane, competitive reality of Amazon Marketplace selling: thousands of independent merchants, many of them small operations run by a handful of employees, competing continuously for a buy box that can flip to a different seller within minutes based on price alone. For a seller running a catalog of hundreds or thousands of products, manually checking competitor prices and adjusting listings by hand is not a viable business practice; automated repricing is simply what competing on the platform requires, in the same unglamorous way that a retail cashier requires a working register. Fee-for-service repricing tools of exactly this kind remain, industry reporting suggests, standard equipment for any Marketplace seller operating at meaningful volume, particularly those using Amazon's own fulfillment service, where buy-box eligibility makes price competition especially direct. None of that lawful infrastructure looks meaningfully different, from the outside, from the code Topkins and his co-conspirators deployed. The line between the two is invisible to an algorithm-detection tool, invisible to Amazon's own platform monitoring, and invisible to a casual observer glancing at two sellers' identical prices. It becomes visible only when an investigator gets access to the human record behind the code — the emails, the messages, the meetings — and finds, or fails to find, an agreement.

The specific product category the original conspiracy targeted, wall posters, is worth a moment's reflection, because its very mundanity underscores how broadly this kind of vulnerability extends across ordinary commerce. Posters are about as unglamorous a product as e-commerce offers: low unit cost, minimal brand loyalty, near-total substitutability between one seller's inventory and another's, and a purchase decision most buyers make in seconds based on price and image alone. That mundanity is precisely what made the category so susceptible to the scheme Topkins orchestrated, echoing Stigler's own observation, examined in chapter two, that standardized, low-differentiation products in concentrated markets are the easiest targets for sustained coordination. A reader tempted to think of algorithmic price-fixing as a phenomenon confined to high-stakes markets like apartment rent or hotel rooms should remember that the very first prosecuted instance of the modern era involved nothing grander than the price of a poster — a reminder that the underlying vulnerability this book documents scales down to the most ordinary corners of retail commerce just as readily as it scales up to a tenant's monthly rent.

Antitrust practitioners writing about the wave of cases in its immediate aftermath were careful to draw the same distinction this chapter draws, warning corporate clients not that algorithmic pricing itself was newly risky, but that using pricing software as a tool to execute an agreement reached through any channel — a phone call, a text message, a conversation at an industry trade show — carried exactly the same criminal exposure it always had, and that prosecutors now had both the technical sophistication and the stated institutional will to go looking for the digital fingerprints such schemes left behind: shared code repositories, matching algorithmic logic across supposedly independent competitors, timestamps on software deployment that lined up suspiciously well with a documented in-person meeting.

There is a small irony buried in the poster case that is easy to miss and worth drawing out explicitly, because it cuts against the intuitive assumption that algorithms make collusion harder to catch. A conversation, once it ends, leaves behind only what a witness remembers or what a recording captured; the words themselves disappear. Code does not disappear. It sits in a repository with a timestamp, a version history, and often the name or credentials of whoever wrote or last modified it, and two pieces of independently written software rarely converge on identical logic by coincidence in the way two independently truthful witnesses might happen to remember a conversation the same way. When investigators in the poster case and its successors went looking for evidence, they were not limited to the kind of testimony and recollection that made the electrical-equipment prosecutions of the 1960s such slow, painstaking work; they could examine the actual source code each conspirator's algorithm ran, compare its logic against the other conspirators' code, and, where the sellers had been sloppy enough to reuse or closely mirror each other's scripts, produce something close to a smoking gun far more durable than Howard Putnam's cassette tape. Software, in this narrow sense, made the Topkins conspiracy easier to prosecute than an equivalent conspiracy conducted entirely by phone would have been, not harder — a fact that should complicate any simple story in which algorithms are assumed to be a criminal's best friend. They can be. They can also be an unusually patient, unusually literal witness for the prosecution, incapable of forgetting or embellishing what it was told to do.

It is worth asking why a case this legally unremarkable — an old-fashioned conspiracy, prosecuted under a century-old statute, with software playing a purely mechanical role — became, and remains, one of the most frequently cited episodes in any serious discussion of algorithmic pricing and antitrust law. Part of the answer is simply chronological: the Topkins plea was among the first, if not the first, criminal case anywhere to use the word "algorithm" in its Justice Department press materials in connection with a price-fixing prosecution, which made it an easy, quotable reference point for a subject that reporters, regulators, and corporate compliance departments were all beginning to take seriously at almost exactly the same moment. But part of the answer is more substantive, and more useful for this book's purposes: the Topkins case gave courts, regulators, and legal scholars a clean, uncontroversial baseline case to reason from — the algorithmic-collusion equivalent of a control group — against which every subsequent, murkier case involving pricing software could be measured. Is this new case more like Topkins, where a human agreement clearly preceded and explained the algorithmic behavior? Or is it something categorically different, where the algorithmic behavior arose without any identifiable agreement at all? That question, asked explicitly or implicitly, runs through every chapter that follows in this book, and the Topkins case is the fixed point against which the harder cases have to be measured.

RealPage's own defense, when the Justice Department eventually sued it a decade after Topkins pleaded guilty, leaned directly on this distinction, and it is worth previewing the argument here so that later chapters can be read against it. RealPage's lawyers have argued, both in court filings and in public statements, that YieldStar and its successor products are fundamentally different from the Topkins scheme because no landlord using RealPage's software ever entered into an actual agreement with any other landlord about what to charge; each landlord independently chose to subscribe to a commercial software product, independently chose whether to accept any given price recommendation, and never communicated directly with a competitor about pricing at all. On the narrow, Topkins-derived reading of what makes algorithmic pricing illegal — was there a human agreement that the software merely executed? — RealPage's defense has real force, because the mechanism the Justice Department alleges is different in kind from the mechanism Topkins admitted to. Nobody has produced a RealPage-era equivalent of the Crandall tape or the Topkins email chain: no document, so far as the public record shows, in which a landlord using RealPage's software explicitly agreed with a competing landlord about what rent to charge. This absence is not a minor technicality in the government's case; it is the entire reason the RealPage litigation, and the parallel Cendyn casino-hotel litigation examined in chapter ten, have produced genuinely contested, sometimes contradictory rulings from federal judges applying the identical statute Topkins violated without any dispute at all. A prosecutor with an email chain like the one behind the poster conspiracy does not need a jury to understand game theory, monitoring costs, or the folk theorem; a prosecutor without one has to persuade a court that a hub-and-spoke arrangement, inferred from data-sharing architecture and suspiciously high rates of compliance with software-generated recommendations, is functionally equivalent to an agreement the old-fashioned law was built to punish. That is a substantially harder case to win, and its difficulty is precisely why RealPage's dispute with the government did not end, the way Topkins's did, in a quick guilty plea and a modest fine, but instead in years of contested litigation, an amended complaint adding six major landlords as co-defendants, and a negotiated settlement that reshaped how the software is allowed to operate going forward without ever resolving, in a way either side could claim as a clean legal victory, whether what RealPage built was a crime in the Topkins sense at all. What the government's RealPage complaint alleges instead, and what chapters eight and nine of this book examine at length, is closer to the hub-and-spoke theory introduced in chapter one by way of Interstate Circuit v. United States: a shared central actor, coordinating parallel behavior among competitors who never had to speak to each other directly, because the hub was doing the coordinating on their behalf, using their own pooled, nonpublic data as the raw material.

Topkins, in other words, is the easy case precisely because it still fits the old model cleanly: an agreement happened, in the ordinary human sense, and a court can find it if prosecutors look hard enough at emails, messaging logs, and deployment timestamps. The interesting, unresolved question this book exists to explore is what happens once the pattern of coordinated, supracompetitive pricing persists even when investigators dig through every email account, every text message, and every meeting calendar available to them, and find nothing that looks like an agreement at all — not because the conspirators were unusually careful, but because there may genuinely be no conspiracy in the traditional sense to find. That is the question the next chapter takes up directly, using a piece of research that did something no antitrust investigation ever could: it built two pricing algorithms from scratch, gave them no instructions beyond "maximize your own profit," placed them in a simulated market with each other, and watched, with the investigators' equivalent of a security camera running the entire time, to see whether collusion would emerge on its own.

Topkins's own later career is a small, almost wistful coda to the case that made him briefly famous in antitrust circles. He continued working in e-commerce and technology after his plea, his $20,000 fine and cooperation agreement long behind him, a minor footnote in an industry that has moved, in the decade since his prosecution, from primitive rule-based repricing bots that simply matched or undercut a reference price to reinforcement-learning systems capable of discovering pricing strategies no human programmer ever wrote down. The gap between what Topkins's code could do and what the software examined in the next chapter can do is not a difference of degree. It is closer to the gap between a thermostat and a chess engine — both technically "automated," both reacting to an environment, but only one of them capable of learning, on its own, that patience and a credible threat of punishment can produce a better outcome than an all-out price war, without anyone ever telling it that patience and punishment were the goal.
