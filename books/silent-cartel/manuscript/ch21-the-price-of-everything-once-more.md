# The Price of Everything, Once More

This book began with a promise, stated plainly in its first chapter's note on method, to treat every case as real, dated, and drawn from primary sources where they were reachable, and to distinguish carefully between an established fact, a contested allegation, and a laboratory finding rather than letting the three blur together into a single undifferentiated impression of menace. Returning to that promise now, in these closing pages, is itself a kind of accounting this book owes its reader: the RealPage settlement examined in chapter nine is real and binding; the Cendyn litigation's ultimate outcome, examined in chapter ten, remains genuinely unresolved as this book goes to print; Calvano's laboratory result, examined in chapter five, is a replicated scientific finding whose real-world magnitude, chapter eighteen showed at length, remains a live and legitimate subject of scholarly dispute. A reader who has followed this book through all twenty-one chapters should be able to sort any new claim about algorithmic pricing they encounter after finishing it into roughly the same three categories, resisting the flattening effect that turns "settled law," "active litigation," and "provocative research finding" into a single interchangeable claim that a given technology is simply dangerous, full stop — precisely the flattening this book has tried, throughout, to resist.

Return, one last time, to the telephone call that opened this book. On February 1, 1982, Robert Crandall told Howard Putnam to raise Braniff's fares twenty percent, promising to match the increase the following morning, and Putnam's tape recorder caught every word of it. The call worked as evidence precisely because it was a call: two human executives, speaking directly to each other, leaving behind an artifact — a cassette tape, a transcript filed in a government complaint — that a court could examine and a reader, four decades later, could still quote nearly verbatim. The entire architecture of twentieth-century antitrust law, this book has argued across twenty chapters, was built around the assumption that collusion would keep generating artifacts like that one: a document, a recording, a witness willing to testify about a conversation that actually happened between actual people who understood, at some level, that what they were discussing was wrong.

It is worth restating, one final time, the full roster of technologies and institutions this book has walked through, because the list itself is the argument in its most compressed form: a telephone call, an electrical-equipment cartel's coded meeting invitations, a fare-distribution database, NASDAQ quoting conventions, poster-pricing bots, a laboratory full of Q-learning agents, a German gas station's repricing software, four decades of legitimate yield management, an apartment-pricing algorithm, a casino-hotel's revenue-management platform, a ride-hailing app, a surveillance-pricing consultancy, an airline's individualized-fare ambitions, an insurance price-optimization model, a stalled Senate bill, a city ordinance, a European guideline, and, waiting somewhere just past this book's final page, whatever agentic AI systems turn out to become once they reach the commercial maturity this book's twentieth chapter could only sketch in outline. Every one of these, examined closely enough, turned out to be a variation on the same underlying question Robert Crandall's phone call posed in its crudest, most legible form in 1982: what happens to a price when the people setting it stop having to look each other in the eye.

RealPage's November 2025 settlement contains no equivalent artifact, and that absence is the entire story this book has tried to tell. There is no tape of a RealPage engineer agreeing with a Camden Property Trust executive to raise rents twenty percent. There is no email chain, no hotel-room meeting, no whispered warning against quoting an odd eighth of a percentage point too low. There is, instead, a technical settlement restricting how fresh a dataset can be and how finely a pricing model can be drawn across a map — a remedy built not around any single incriminating document, but around the theoretical understanding, developed across sixty years of economic research from George Stigler's 1964 monitoring theory through Emilio Calvano's 2020 reinforcement-learning experiment, that certain configurations of speed, granularity, and shared visibility let algorithms discover, on their own, the same reward-and-punishment strategies Crandall tried and failed to negotiate directly with a rival who happened to be recording him.

Consider what a reader of this book living through the events it describes could not have known at the time, and what that gap between lived experience and eventual understanding suggests about the years just ahead. A renter signing a lease in Atlanta in 2019, watching their rent climb steadily year after year, had no way of knowing that a Texas software company's pooled-data algorithm was part of the explanation, because the mechanism was, by design and by its very nature, invisible to the person paying its consequences — no notice on the lease, no disclosure in the listing, nothing distinguishing a RealPage-influenced price from an ordinary one except the pattern such a price left behind in aggregate, a pattern only a journalist, an economist, or a government subpoena could eventually surface. That same invisibility almost certainly characterizes some current pricing arrangement this book's twenty-one chapters never had occasion to examine, operating today in a market this book's author never thought to investigate, waiting for its own Heather Vogell, its own Assad-Clark-Ershov-Xu, its own tenant plaintiff patient enough to keep asking why the numbers on the page kept landing exactly where they did.

This is the paradigm shift this book's title promises, and it is worth stating in its most direct form now that the evidence for it has been laid out across twenty chapters of specific, dated, sourced cases: the century-old assumption that price-fixing requires a meeting of human minds, leaving behind the kind of evidence a meeting of minds inevitably generates, no longer reliably holds. It still holds in some of this book's cases — David Topkins's poster-pricing bots in chapter four were explicit, old-fashioned collusion wearing algorithmic clothing, and a jury would have understood the underlying conduct instantly if the case had ever gone to trial. It plainly does not hold in others — the Q-learning agents in Calvano's laboratory discovered supracompetitive pricing with no instruction to collude and no channel through which to communicate, and no forensic investigator, however skilled, could produce a transcript of a conversation that never happened between parties that were never, in any meaningful sense, capable of having one. Most of the real cases this book has documented sit somewhere between those two poles, in the genuinely contested territory the Ninth and Third Circuits split over in chapter ten, where reasonable judges examining identical software and comparable facts have reached opposite conclusions about whether an antitrust claim can even proceed to discovery.

That unresolved middle ground is not a failure of this book's research, and it is not a temporary gap waiting for the right test case to close it definitively. It is, this book has argued, the honest current state of a legal system built for one kind of evidence encountering a technology that increasingly does not generate it. Congress could close the gap through legislation like Klobuchar's stalled Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act, examined in chapter fifteen, which would make certain data-pooling practices illegal regardless of whether a plaintiff can prove an inferable agreement under the old doctrinal framework. Courts could close it gradually, case by case, the way Interstate Circuit's hub-and-spoke doctrine itself took decades to develop after 1939, with the Third Circuit's Cornish-Adebiyi decision representing one recent data point in what may eventually become a settled, if slowly built, body of precedent. Or the gap could simply persist, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, city ordinance by city ordinance, circuit by circuit, producing exactly the patchwork this book's fifteenth and sixteenth chapters documented — genuinely different legal exposure for genuinely identical software depending on which side of a state or circuit boundary a given transaction happens to fall.

What this book has tried to resist, across every chapter, is the temptation to resolve that genuine uncertainty into a tidier story than the evidence supports — to declare algorithmic pricing an unambiguous menace requiring sweeping prohibition, or to dismiss the entire phenomenon as regulatory overreach against ordinary, efficient business software. Both stories are available, both have serious advocates, and both, this book has argued, are wrong in the same way: they resolve a genuinely difficult empirical and legal question into a simpler moral one, because a simpler moral story is easier to tell and easier to act on than an honest account of conditional, market-specific, and still-developing evidence. The German gas-station study's critical-mass finding, examined in chapter six and revisited by the skeptics in chapter eighteen, is real evidence that algorithmic collusion is neither automatic nor universal. The RealPage and Cendyn litigation, examined in chapters eight through ten, is equally real evidence that in specific, identifiable market conditions — concentrated ownership, standardized products, near-universal software adoption, rapid and granular data pooling — something that functions exactly like a cartel can and does emerge, without anyone ever picking up a phone.

It is worth taking stock, in this book's final pages, of how much genuine progress the twenty chapters preceding this one have actually documented, because the cumulative weight of so many unresolved circuit splits, unenforced ordinances, and stalled federal bills could easily leave a reader with the impression that nothing has actually changed since Heather Vogell's original 2022 reporting first surfaced RealPage's software to public attention. That impression would be too pessimistic, and it is worth correcting before this book closes. RealPage operates today under a binding seven-year federal consent judgment it did not operate under in 2022. Twelve-month data-freshness restrictions and state-level geographic limits, whatever their imperfections, are real, judicially enforceable constraints that did not exist before the Justice Department's 2024 complaint. Nineteen American cities and three states have enacted, and in most cases continue to enforce, algorithmic-pricing prohibitions that simply did not exist before San Francisco's original 2024 ordinance. The Third Circuit's Cornish-Adebiyi decision has given plaintiffs in at least one major federal circuit a viable path to discovery that did not exist before the Ninth Circuit's Gibson ruling seemed, briefly, to have closed the door on hub-and-spoke theories entirely. None of this amounts to the phenomenon being solved. All of it amounts to a genuinely different, more contested, more legally uncertain landscape than existed five years before this book was written — which is itself a meaningful form of progress, even short of the clean, comprehensive resolution a tidier book might have promised.

The reader finishing this book in 2027 or later is likely to be renting an apartment, booking a hotel room, buying an airline ticket, or shopping online using pricing infrastructure that has continued evolving in the time since this manuscript was completed, and this book has tried, throughout, to supply durable analytical tools rather than a snapshot of facts destined to age quickly. The specific settlement terms in chapter nine will eventually expire, in 2032, seven years after they were signed; the specific circuit split in chapter ten may by then be resolved, by the Supreme Court or by further appellate rulings this book could not anticipate; the specific companies named throughout these chapters may be acquired, renamed, or replaced by successors using entirely different branding built on the same underlying mechanism. What should not go out of date, this book hopes, is the analytical apparatus itself: Stigler's monitoring-cost theory from chapter two, the folk theorem's patience-and-observation logic, the distinction this book has drawn repeatedly between a price that tracks genuine cost or scarcity and a price that tracks only what a pooled, algorithmic model has learned a captive buyer will tolerate. A reader equipped with that apparatus should be able to look at whatever pricing technology exists five, ten, or twenty years from now and ask the right diagnostic questions, even if this book's specific case studies have by then become historical curiosities in the same way Robert Crandall's 1982 phone call has become one.

A skeptic who read chapter eighteen carefully is owed a direct answer, in this book's final pages, to the fairest version of the objection that chapter raised: that this book, for all its stated caution, still spends far more of its twenty-one chapters describing harm than describing the genuine efficiency gains algorithmic pricing has produced. The answer is not that the efficiency case is wrong. Chapter seven's account of Robert Crandall's original yield-management system, which cut airfares and filled seats that would otherwise have flown empty, is real, and nothing in the chapters that followed retracted it. The answer is instead that efficiency and collusion are not opposites sitting at two ends of a single dial, where more of one automatically means less of the other; they are separate properties a given pricing system can possess in any combination, and the specific cases this book spent its middle chapters documenting were chosen because they combine both at once — real forecasting sophistication, genuinely reducing vacancy and waste, sitting alongside a genuinely collusive data-pooling mechanism layered on top of it. A book that only tallied efficiency gains would have missed the RealPage settlement entirely. A book that only tallied collusive harm would have missed why RealPage's product was commercially successful enough to reach 1.3 million rental units in the first place. Holding both facts in view at once, rather than collapsing them into a single verdict, is the most difficult and, this book hopes, the most valuable thing it has tried to do across the preceding twenty chapters.

Robert Crandall spent the last years of his long career, before his death in 2024, as one of the airline industry's most quoted elder statesmen, invited to speak at conferences and cited in business-school case studies as both the inventor of yield management and the cautionary tale behind his own taped phone call — the same career containing, within a few years of each other, the moment American antitrust enforcement nearly caught a human executive in the act of proposing a cartel, and the invention of a pricing discipline whose descendants, forty years later, a federal court would restrain not because anyone could prove what a human being had said, but because economists, journalists, and government lawyers had pieced together, patiently and indirectly, what a piece of software had done. Crandall never had to build a monitoring system to catch Braniff undercutting American's fares; he simply picked up a phone and asked. His successors, running landlords, casino-hotels, and airlines that never speak to each other at all, no longer have to ask. The software already knows what everyone else in the market is doing, because everyone else already told it, and it tells everyone back, continuously, automatically, without a single word passing between any two competitors who might otherwise be legally required to compete.

Consider, one final time, the full span of technology this book has walked through, because laying it out end to end in this closing chapter makes a pattern visible that no single chapter, read in isolation, could fully reveal. A shared database of airline fares, carrying no intelligence of its own, let American and its rivals float trial balloons at each other in 1994. A handful of Amazon sellers wrote primitive, rule-based repricing bots to enforce an agreement they had already reached the old-fashioned way, in 2013. A team of Italian economists built reinforcement-learning agents with no instruction to cooperate and watched them discover, unprompted, the same reward-and-punishment logic Robert Axelrod's tit-for-tat had won two prisoner's-dilemma tournaments with decades earlier, in 2020. A Texas software company pooled real-time, nonpublic lease data across landlords managing more than a million apartments, and a federal court restrained it not because any document proved an agreement, but because the pattern of behavior left a statistical shadow prosecutors and journalists could trace. Each step in that progression required less explicit human intention than the one before it, and each step, this book has shown, produced outcomes for renters, travelers, and shoppers that looked, felt, and functioned more like the product of a cartel than the step before it did. The technology did not become more collusive by design. It became more collusive by consequence, discovering on its own, faster with each new generation, strategies that used to require a Robert Crandall willing to risk his career on a phone call.

That progression is the real subject of this book, more than any single lawsuit, settlement, or circuit ruling examined along the way. Antitrust law spent a century building an elaborate, generally effective apparatus for catching human beings who say the illegal thing to each other, because for most of that century, saying the thing out loud was the only way to coordinate reliably enough to make coordination worth the risk. That apparatus is not obsolete — Topkins pleaded guilty, Aston went to prison, the NASDAQ market makers paid over a billion dollars, and every one of those outcomes depended on the same basic legal machinery Robert Crandall so narrowly escaped in 1983. What this book has tried to show, across the cases that make up its middle chapters, is that the same apparatus increasingly has to reach for evidence it was never designed to use — structural breaks in a pricing time series, compliance rates with a software recommendation, an economist's laboratory simulation of an algorithm nobody instructed to collude — because the artifact the old apparatus was built to find, the recording, the email, the witness who remembers the conversation, is simply no longer being generated in an enormous and growing share of the cases that matter most.

That is the quiet handshake this book's title describes, and it is very likely still happening, in some market this book never had space to examine, as this sentence is being read. The reader who finishes this book with a firmer grip on how to recognize it — the pooled data, the near-universal compliance, the vendor sitting at the center of a wheel whose spokes never speak to each other — has the one tool this book has tried hardest to supply: not a verdict on whether any specific price they are currently paying was set by a fair market or a silent cartel, because that verdict, this book has shown again and again, is genuinely difficult even for federal judges working with full discovery to reach. What this book offers instead is the standing question worth carrying into every lease renewal, every hotel booking, every fare search from here forward: not whether the price in front of you looks reasonable, but whether you have any way of knowing who, or what, actually decided it — and whether that decision was made independently at all, or merely dressed to look that way.
