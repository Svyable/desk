# United States v. RealPage

The United States Department of Justice, joined by eight state attorneys general, filed its civil antitrust complaint against RealPage, Inc. in the United States District Court for the Middle District of North Carolina on August 23, 2024 — a filing that arrived nearly two years after Heather Vogell's original ProPublica investigation, following what the government's own account of the case describes as an extended investigation that included subpoenaing internal RealPage documents, deposing company executives, and analyzing the software's actual pricing outputs across markets rather than relying solely on the company's public marketing claims. The complaint alleged violations of Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act, invoking both of the statute's central prohibitions rather than choosing between them: Section 1's ban on agreements in restraint of trade, and Section 2's separate ban on monopolization, reflecting a deliberate legal strategy of attacking RealPage's conduct from two directions simultaneously rather than betting the entire case on a single theory that a defense-friendly judge might reject.

The Section 1 theory, the one this book has spent its previous chapters building the conceptual apparatus to understand, alleged that RealPage's software functioned as the hub in a hub-and-spoke conspiracy of exactly the kind Interstate Circuit v. United States recognized as unlawful in 1939, coordinating pricing among landlords — the spokes — who competed with each other in the ordinary sense but who each fed the same hub nonpublic data about their own occupancy, lease terms, and pricing plans, receiving back price recommendations calibrated using the pooled intelligence of the entire group. The complaint's specific factual allegations mirrored and extended Vogell's original reporting: that RealPage's pricing algorithm adjusted unit-level prices based on nonpublic lease-expiration dates gathered across competing landlords, deliberately counteracting the downward pressure on price that would otherwise occur if too many nearby units became available for lease at the same time — a mechanism the government characterized as artificially suppressing exactly the kind of price competition that would occur in a genuinely competitive market when supply increases relative to demand. The Section 2 theory took a different angle, alleging that RealPage had built and maintained an unlawful monopoly specifically in the market for revenue-management software serving large multifamily landlords, with the complaint asserting that the company controlled roughly 80 percent of that specific software market — a concentration level, if accurate, that would make RealPage's product something closer to essential infrastructure than one vendor's offering among many genuine alternatives.

The Tunney Act review process itself deserves a brief explanation, because it shapes how much weight a settlement like this one can actually bear as precedent, and because it recurs as a procedural mechanism throughout the history of major American antitrust enforcement, including in the ATPCO matter examined earlier in this book. Congress passed the Tunney Act in 1974, in the aftermath of a series of controversial antitrust settlements that critics had argued were negotiated too cozily between the government and powerful corporate defendants, with too little independent judicial scrutiny of whether the proposed remedy actually served the public interest rather than merely the litigants' preference for a quick resolution. The statute requires the government to publish a proposed consent judgment, solicit public comment on its terms, and obtain a federal judge's independent finding that the settlement serves the public interest before it becomes final and enforceable — a process that gave outside groups, including the tenant advocates whose objections this chapter discusses below, a formal, if ultimately unsuccessful, opportunity to argue that the RealPage settlement's terms were too weak before the presiding judge signed off on it.

The scale the complaint documented was, by the standards of ordinary consumer antitrust litigation, immense. The government's amended complaint, filed in January 2025 after adding six of the country's largest landlords as named co-defendants — Greystar Real Estate Partners, Blackstone's LivCor, Camden Property Trust, Cushman & Wakefield, Willow Bridge Property Company, and Cortland Management — described those six companies as collectively managing more than 1.3 million rental units across 43 states, a footprint large enough that a meaningful share of American renters using RealPage-affiliated buildings would have had no practical way to know, from the outside, that their lease was governed by pricing software their landlord shared with competitors rather than by an independent, arm's-length assessment of their specific building's market position. The government's complaint pointed to fast-growing Sun Belt metropolitan areas — Atlanta, Austin, Charlotte, Dallas, Houston, Orlando, and Raleigh — as markets where RealPage's suggested prices governed rent-setting for between a third and two-thirds of an entire neighborhood's multifamily housing stock, a concentration echoing, with government subpoena power now behind it, the same Seattle-neighborhood figures Vogell's original reporting had already surfaced using nothing but interviews and public records.

Jonathan Kanter, the Assistant Attorney General heading the Antitrust Division at the time the original complaint was filed, delivered remarks the same day framing the case in terms that reach back explicitly to the conscious-parallelism doctrine this book examined in chapter two: "independent decision making by landlords — not RealPage — should determine rental prices," Kanter said, adding a line that antitrust reporters would quote repeatedly over the following two years of litigation: "RealPage has built a business out of frustrating the natural forces of vigorous competition." Both sentences are worth reading against Theatre Enterprises and Interstate Circuit, the twin pillars of mid-century conscious-parallelism doctrine introduced earlier in this book — Kanter was not arguing that RealPage's landlord clients had explicitly agreed with each other in the Crandall-and-Braniff sense; he was arguing, in substance, that the "independent decision making" the law is supposed to protect had been replaced by something that only wore independence as a costume, with a shared hub doing the deciding for a set of nominally independent spokes.

The complaint's Section 2 monopolization theory, distinct from the hub-and-spoke Section 1 claim this chapter has focused on primarily, deserves its own brief accounting, because it represents a genuinely separate legal vulnerability RealPage faced beyond the conspiracy theory this book's earlier chapters have emphasized. Monopolization claims under Section 2 do not require proving an agreement among multiple parties at all; they require proving that a single company holds monopoly power in a properly defined market and has acquired or maintained that power through exclusionary conduct, rather than through superior products, business acumen, or historical accident. The government's 80-percent market-share allegation, if proven, would satisfy the first element relatively comfortably by ordinary antitrust standards, which generally treat a share above 70 percent as strong evidence of monopoly power; the harder element to prove was the second, exclusionary conduct, which the complaint tied to allegations that RealPage had structured its contracts and data-sharing requirements specifically to entrench its dominant position and make it difficult for landlords to switch to a rival product without losing access to the pooled competitive intelligence that made RealPage's own recommendations valuable in the first place — a lock-in dynamic distinct from, though related to, the collusion mechanism this book's other chapters have emphasized.

RealPage's defense, consistent with the arguments previewed in the previous chapter, centered on the absence of any direct agreement among the landlord defendants and on the voluntary, non-binding nature of the software's price recommendations. The company's public statements characterized the lawsuit as an attempt to criminalize ordinary, lawful use of data analytics and software tools that had become standard practice across a wide range of industries, and pointed to research the company had commissioned or cited suggesting that revenue-management software of this kind, used properly, tends to make rental markets more efficient rather than less — filling vacancies faster and reducing the kind of prolonged, costly vacancy that ultimately raises costs for landlords and, RealPage argued, for renters as well, echoing the genuine efficiency case for yield management this book laid out at length in chapter seven. Whether that efficiency defense could survive the specific factual allegations about pooled, nonpublic data — rather than the more general, less legally fraught practice of dynamic pricing based on a landlord's own historical performance — was the central legal question the case was set up to answer, and it is a question the parties ultimately chose not to let a jury decide.

The resolution, when it came, arrived not as a verdict but as a negotiated settlement, filed by the Justice Department on November 24, 2025, roughly fifteen months after the original complaint. The proposed consent judgment, structured to run for seven years and subject to judicial review under the Tunney Act — the federal statute requiring courts to confirm that a government antitrust settlement genuinely serves the public interest before finalizing it — did not require RealPage to admit any wrongdoing and imposed no financial penalty on the company at all, a detail that struck several outside antitrust commentators as a strikingly light outcome for a case the government had spent well over a year litigating with the backing of eight state attorneys general. What the settlement did require, instead of money or an admission of guilt, was a set of specific, technical restrictions on how RealPage's software is permitted to operate going forward, restrictions that read, to anyone who has absorbed the argument of this book's earlier chapters, like an almost point-by-point response to the exact mechanisms Vogell's reporting and the government's own complaint had identified as the source of the harm.

The settlement barred RealPage from using rental pricing data less than twelve months old in generating its recommendations, directly targeting the "real-time" nonpublic lease-expiration data the original complaint had identified as the software's most collusion-facilitating input — data fresh enough to let the software counteract the downward pressure of new vacancies in close to real time. It separately prohibited RealPage from building or using pricing models calibrated at a geographic resolution finer than the state level, eliminating the granular, neighborhood-specific or building-specific pooling of competitor data that had allowed the software to tell a landlord, in effect, exactly what the ten other property managers on the same block were about to charge. Both restrictions target, with unusual technical specificity for a negotiated antitrust settlement, precisely the two variables — data freshness and data granularity — that the academic literature examined in chapters five and six identified as the load-bearing ingredients of algorithmic collusion: Calvano's Q-learning agents needed rapid, repeated interaction to discover and sustain their reward-and-punishment strategies, and the German gas-station study found collusive effects appearing only once every competitor in a tightly bounded local market had joined the same real-time monitoring system. A settlement that strips out real-time data and forces pricing models up to a state-wide resolution is, whether or not its drafters framed it this way explicitly, an attempt to break the specific monitoring mechanism this book's theoretical chapters identified as the thing that makes tacit algorithmic coordination possible in the first place.

The settlement did not resolve the case for everyone involved, and the litigation landscape remained genuinely unsettled as this book was being finished. Two of the six landlord co-defendants, Cortland Management and Greystar, reached their own separate settlements with the government and, in Greystar's case, with the private tenant plaintiffs as well, discussed in the previous chapter's account of the $141 million Greystar settlement and the broader $359.9 million class-action resolution that followed it. The remaining four landlord defendants — Camden Property Trust, Cushman & Wakefield, Pinnacle Property Management Services, and Willow Bridge Property Company — had not settled with the government as of early 2026, meaning the federal case against them continued to proceed even after RealPage itself had resolved its own portion of the litigation, a bifurcation that leaves open the possibility of contested trial testimony, and potentially a jury verdict, on the underlying Section 1 conspiracy theory even though the company whose software sat at the center of the alleged conspiracy had already settled its own liability. The Commonwealth of Kentucky, which had filed its own separate state-law lawsuit against RealPage and several major Kentucky landlords in July 2025, explicitly declined to join the federal settlement, meaning RealPage's legal exposure over its historical rent-pricing practices remained live in at least one additional venue even after the headline federal case had nominally concluded. Kentucky's decision to go its own way is itself a small but instructive data point about the federalist structure of American antitrust enforcement: unlike the criminal Sherman Act violations examined in earlier chapters, where the Justice Department holds essentially exclusive federal authority, individual states retain independent power to enforce both federal antitrust law and their own, often broader, state-level consumer-protection and competition statutes, meaning a single company's nationwide business practice can face materially different legal outcomes in different jurisdictions depending on which state attorneys general chose to litigate rather than settle, and on how each state's own antitrust and consumer-protection statutes differ from the federal Sherman Act framework this book has focused on almost exclusively up to this point.

The specific North Carolina venue where the government chose to file deserves a brief note, because forum selection in major antitrust litigation is rarely accidental and often signals something about the litigation strategy behind it. The Middle District of North Carolina was not RealPage's home turf — the company is headquartered in Texas — nor the venue of any single landlord defendant's principal place of business; the government's choice reflected instead a calculation about where the underlying conduct's effects were felt and where the case could proceed efficiently, a standard venue consideration in complex multi-defendant antitrust litigation that nonetheless meant the case would be decided by judges and, potentially, a jury pool with no particular regional connection to the housing markets — Atlanta, Austin, Seattle — where the conduct's most visible effects, examined in chapter eight, had actually been documented.

RealPage's corporate ownership adds one further layer of context worth noting briefly, because it bears on how the settlement's lack of a financial penalty should be read. RealPage has been owned since 2021 by the private-equity firm Thoma Bravo, which acquired the company in a leveraged buyout valued at roughly $10.2 billion — a scale of private capital investment that gives some sense of how commercially significant a business the pooled-data rent-pricing model had become well before any of the litigation examined in this chapter began, and a reminder that the company facing the Justice Department's lawsuit was not a scrappy startup experimenting with a novel technology but an established, well-capitalized, professionally managed enterprise whose product had become deeply embedded in how a meaningful share of the American multifamily housing industry set prices, long before regulators, reporters, or renters fully understood what that embedding implied.

The settlement's reception among housing advocates and tenant organizers was considerably less generous than its technical sophistication might suggest it deserved, and their objections are worth taking seriously rather than treating as background noise. K Agbebiyi, a senior housing campaign coordinator at the Private Equity Stakeholder Project, told the commercial real-estate outlet Bisnow that the settlement amounted to little more than a slap on the wrist, and argued that RealPage would remain free, in practice, to continue substantially the same rent-setting business it had operated for years. Andrea Beaty of the Revolving Door Project made a related, more explicitly political critique, writing that the settlement's leniency underscored what she characterized as a wide gap between the American public's appetite for holding price-gouging corporations accountable and the priorities of the administration overseeing the deal — a pointed observation given that Attorney General Pam Bondi had publicly described antitrust enforcement as a stated priority for her Justice Department around the same period the settlement's terms were finalized. The core of the advocates' complaint is straightforward and, this book thinks, largely correct as far as it goes: a settlement built entirely around data-freshness and geographic-resolution restrictions, with no monetary penalty attached and no admission of wrongdoing extracted, leaves the underlying business model — pooling competitor data to generate price recommendations landlords overwhelmingly follow — fundamentally intact, merely narrower in scope than before. Whether narrower scope is enough to actually break the collusive dynamic the academic literature predicts, or whether RealPage's software will simply recalibrate around twelve-month-old, state-level data and reproduce a diminished version of the same effect, is an empirical question no one, including the government that negotiated the restrictions, will be able to answer with confidence for years.

There is a structural echo here worth naming explicitly, because it connects this chapter directly back to the very first piece of shared pricing infrastructure this book examined. The RealPage consent judgment's seven-year term, its focus on restricting specific technical features of a shared system rather than banning the system outright, and its refusal to impose any monetary penalty on the central corporate defendant all closely resemble the shape of the 1994 ATPCO consent decree examined in chapter three, which ran for ten years, similarly left the underlying fare-distribution infrastructure standing, and similarly focused its remedy on stripping out the specific mechanical features — advance-dated filings, semantically hollow footnote codes — that had let the system function as a substitute for direct communication. Three decades separate the two settlements, and the underlying technology could not be more different, a shared airline fare database on one side, a machine-learning pricing model on the other. The legal instinct governing both remedies, however, is strikingly consistent: when regulators conclude that a piece of shared infrastructure has been functioning as an illegal channel of coordination, but cannot or choose not to prove the kind of clean, individually culpable conspiracy that would support criminal charges or heavy fines, the available remedy tends to be surgical rather than punitive — narrow the specific technical features that made coordination possible, and leave the underlying business intact to compete, in theory, on a fairer footing going forward.

It is worth asking, as this chapter closes, what the RealPage settlement actually proves about the broader legal and theoretical questions this book has been tracking since Robert Crandall's 1982 phone call. It does not establish, as a matter of binding legal precedent, that RealPage's software constituted an illegal hub-and-spoke conspiracy under Section 1 of the Sherman Act; a negotiated consent judgment, reached without a trial or a final judicial ruling on the merits, sets no such precedent and leaves the underlying legal question exactly as unresolved as it was before the government filed its complaint. What it does establish, more modestly but perhaps more durably, is a template — a specific, technically detailed account, blessed by both a sophisticated corporate defendant and the federal government, of which features of a pooled, algorithmic pricing system are dangerous enough to warrant restriction even without a court ever ruling definitively on whether they were illegal in the first place. That template, real-time data restricted, granular geographic pooling restricted, is likely to shape how every revenue-management vendor serving concentrated markets designs its next generation of software, whether or not any of them ever faces a lawsuit as prominent as the one RealPage spent three years fighting. The next chapter turns to a closely related piece of software, built by a different company for a different industry, that produced a very different set of legal outcomes — not a negotiated settlement, but two federal appellate courts, applying the identical statute to strikingly similar facts, reaching opposite conclusions within about a year of each other.
