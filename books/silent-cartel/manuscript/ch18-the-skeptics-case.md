# The Skeptics' Case

This chapter's placement near the end of the book rather than earlier, interspersed with the case studies themselves, reflects a deliberate structural choice worth explaining, since a reader could reasonably ask why the strongest counterarguments were held back rather than presented alongside each individual case as it arose. Scattering the skeptical case across chapters eight through fourteen would have let each rebuttal blunt its corresponding case study's force in the moment, weakening the cumulative, evidentiary weight this book has tried to build case by case, the same way a trial's closing arguments are reserved for the end rather than interrupting each witness's testimony as it is given. Gathering the skepticism here instead lets the evidence from RealPage, Cendyn, the German gas stations, and the rest stand fully on its own first, and then subjects that accumulated evidence to the most serious challenge available, in one place, where its cumulative strength and its genuine limitations can both be weighed together rather than piecemeal.

Every chapter in this book so far has built toward a single, cumulative impression: that algorithmic pricing, across apartments, casino hotels, gas stations, and airline seats, represents a genuine and rapidly growing threat to the kind of independent, competitive price-setting American and European law have spent more than a century trying to protect. That impression is not wrong, but it is incomplete, and a book that only accumulated evidence in one direction would be doing exactly what chapter one promised not to do: filtering out the cases that complicate its own thesis. This chapter gathers, in one place, the strongest arguments made by serious economists, antitrust scholars, and industry defenders that the "algorithmic collusion" framing running through this book's first seventeen chapters overstates a real but narrower phenomenon, and that a meaningful share of what gets labeled algorithmic collusion in the press is closer to ordinary, lawful, demand-responsive pricing than to the RealPage-style cartel this book's title invokes.

It is worth being honest about the institutional position from which most of the skeptical arguments in this chapter originate, not to dismiss them by association but because a reader deserves to weigh a source's incentives alongside its logic. Several of the most prominent skeptical voices this chapter draws on write for or are affiliated with organizations that receive funding from industries with a direct financial stake in a permissive legal environment for algorithmic pricing — a fact that does not make their arguments wrong, since a funded argument can still be a correct one, but that this book believes a careful reader should know rather than discover independently. The strongest response to that concern is not to discount industry-adjacent skepticism wholesale, which would be its own form of intellectual laziness, but to weigh it against the specific, falsifiable claims examined throughout this chapter rather than against the funding source alone — precisely the discipline this book has tried to apply to every other party in this story with a financial stake in the outcome, from RealPage's own defense filings to the tenant advocates criticizing the RealPage settlement's leniency in chapter nine.

The most fundamental skeptical argument returns to evidence this book has already presented, asking the reader to weigh it differently than the surrounding chapters have generally weighed it. The German gas-station study examined in chapter six, the single richest piece of real-world evidence in this entire book, found that algorithmic pricing raised margins only in markets where every competing station had adopted the software, and found no significant effect at all in markets where adoption remained partial. A skeptic reading that same study can reasonably draw a conclusion considerably more reassuring than the one this book's chapter six emphasized: rather than treating the finding as proof that algorithmic collusion is real and dangerous, a skeptic can treat it as proof that algorithmic collusion is fragile, requiring a specific, hard-to-achieve condition — near-universal simultaneous adoption within a small, geographically bounded market — that will rarely hold in the much larger, much more fragmented markets most consumers actually shop in. Apartment rental markets, airline routes, and most retail categories involve far more sellers, far more product differentiation, and far less complete software adoption than a three-station rural German gas market, all factors Stigler's own 1964 theory, examined in chapter two, predicts should make sustained tacit coordination considerably harder to achieve.

Ioana Marinescu and other economists who have studied concentration and pricing power in labor and product markets have cautioned, in work adjacent to though not always directly addressing algorithmic pricing specifically, that the leap from a laboratory result to a real-world antitrust harm requires considerably more evidence than a striking simulation alone can supply — a caution directly applicable to the Calvano et al. finding examined in chapter five. The Bologna researchers' own robustness checks, discussed at length in that chapter, already showed the collusive effect weakening as the number of simulated competitors increased; skeptics extend that finding to argue that most real-world markets, with far more than the two or three sellers Calvano's cleanest results depended on, would likely show effects too small to matter economically, even if the underlying mechanism is theoretically real. The paper "The Illusion of Collusion," mentioned briefly in chapter five, makes a related and more technical objection, arguing that some published findings of algorithmic collusion in academic simulations may partly reflect artifacts of how researchers measured convergence and profit gain, rather than a robust phenomenon that would reliably reproduce itself outside a carefully tuned experimental setup — a methodological critique this book takes seriously rather than dismissing as motivated reasoning, since it comes from within the same academic literature this book has otherwise relied on heavily.

A further empirical skeptical point, distinct from the doctrinal objections examined below, concerns the counterfactual baseline against which any "harm" from algorithmic pricing should actually be measured. Critics sympathetic to the industry have pointed out that comparing rents after RealPage adoption to rents before it, without carefully controlling for the broader housing-supply crunch and demand surge that characterized the same period across virtually every American rental market, risks attributing to software an effect that a more careful natural experiment might show was substantially, or even primarily, driven by ordinary macroeconomic conditions RealPage's software had nothing to do with. This is not a frivolous objection; chapter six's own account of the German gas-station study succeeded specifically because Assad, Clark, Ershov, and Xu were able to isolate adoption timing from the broader trend in fuel prices using careful statistical technique, precisely the kind of rigorous counterfactual analysis that a simpler, more impressionistic before-and-after comparison of national rent data cannot supply on its own. The RealPage litigation examined in chapter nine never proceeded to a trial that would have tested this specific empirical question rigorously, meaning the counterfactual objection remains, as of this book's writing, a genuinely open empirical question rather than one either side has definitively answered with the kind of clean, quasi-experimental evidence the German case provided.

A second, more legally grounded skeptical argument concerns the specific doctrinal expansion the RealPage and Cendyn cases represent, and it comes from a position considerably more sympathetic to strict, narrowly bounded antitrust enforcement than a casual reader might expect. Some antitrust scholars and defense-side practitioners have argued that stretching hub-and-spoke liability to cover software vendors offering voluntary, non-binding pricing recommendations risks chilling the adoption of genuinely beneficial forecasting and inventory-management technology across an enormous range of industries that have nothing to do with the specific collusive dynamics this book documents — hotels using demand forecasting to avoid empty rooms, retailers using inventory-optimization software to avoid stockouts, any business using aggregated, anonymized market data to make better decisions than it could make relying on its own historical performance alone. If courts adopt an overly expansive reading of what counts as a hub-and-spoke conspiracy, the argument runs, ordinary businesses face genuine legal uncertainty about routine software adoption decisions that have never previously carried meaningful antitrust risk, a version of the chilling-effect concern DLA Piper's memo on the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act raised, examined in chapter fifteen, applied now to judge-made common law rather than to proposed statutory language.

The Ninth Circuit's own reasoning in Gibson v. Cendyn, examined in chapter ten, supplies the clearest judicial articulation of this skeptical position available anywhere in this book's source material, and it deserves to be restated on its own terms rather than only as the losing side of a circuit split. The panel's core insight was that voluntary use of a common, publicly available commercial product does not, by itself, evidence an agreement among the product's various customers, any more than multiple competing bakeries using the same commercially available flour supplier evidences a conspiracy to fix bread prices. Businesses across every sector license shared software constantly — accounting platforms, inventory systems, customer-relationship-management tools — without any suggestion that shared vendor relationships alone constitute unlawful coordination; a rule that treated common software adoption as inherently suspicious would need some further, more specific limiting principle to avoid sweeping in an enormous amount of entirely ordinary commercial behavior that has nothing to do with the nonpublic-data-pooling mechanism this book's central chapters have identified as the actual source of concern.

Economists sympathetic to the efficiency case for revenue management, a case this book laid out at length in chapter seven, add a further empirical objection worth taking seriously on its own terms: the counterfactual matters enormously, and critics of algorithmic pricing rarely specify it with precision. If RealPage-style software genuinely helps landlords fill vacancies faster and price units closer to their true market-clearing level, the relevant comparison is not a simple "prices went up after adoption" finding but a comparison against what prices and vacancy rates would have looked like under the counterfactual of continued manual, imperfect, human pricing during the same period of tight housing supply and rising demand that characterized the American rental market through the early 2020s. Untangling how much of any observed rent increase reflects genuine, ordinary supply-and-demand pressure — construction costs, population growth, constrained new supply — from how much reflects the specific pooled-data mechanism this book's chapter eight and nine identified as legally actionable is a harder empirical problem than either side's advocates, plaintiffs' lawyers seeking damages or corporate defendants seeking dismissal, generally acknowledge in their public statements.

Some of the sharpest recent skeptical commentary has come from economists who specialize in the mechanics of cartel enforcement specifically, rather than from generalist industry defenders, and their objection deserves to be stated in its strongest form because it targets a genuine analytical gap in several of the court rulings this book has treated approvingly. The economist Jay Ezrielev has argued that at least two courts handling private antitrust suits over algorithmic pricing adopted economic reasoning he considers flawed, on a specific and technically precise ground: a successful price-fixing conspiracy, whether run by humans or software, requires some plausible mechanism for detecting and punishing a participant who deviates from the agreed-upon price, the exact enforcement problem this book traced back to Stigler's 1964 theory in chapter two. Ezrielev's critique is that some judicial opinions crediting hub-and-spoke theories against algorithmic pricing vendors have not carefully established that such an enforcement mechanism actually existed on the facts alleged, simply inferring one from the fact of shared software use and high compliance rates without independently verifying that a deviating landlord or hotel would have faced any genuine, functional punishment for ignoring the algorithm's recommendation. If no credible punishment mechanism operates, Ezrielev's argument runs, then Stigler's own theoretical framework — the framework this book's second chapter used to explain why algorithmic collusion should be theoretically possible in the first place — actually cuts against finding an actionable conspiracy, because a "cartel" nobody can be punished for leaving is not functioning as a cartel in the economically meaningful sense at all.

A related line of skeptical commentary, published by researchers affiliated with the free-market-oriented Mercatus Center and by contributors to the antitrust blog Truth on the Market, has pressed a broader consumer-welfare argument that this book's earlier chapters have touched on but not fully developed: that aggressive legal challenges to algorithmic pricing risk undermining genuine market-process improvements that benefit consumers on net, even accounting for the real harms this book has documented in its central chapters. These commentators point to computer-science researchers who have separately cautioned that much of the legal and economics literature on algorithmic collusion, including some of the academic work this book has relied on heavily, may be overestimating how readily recent advances in machine learning actually translate into the kind of sophisticated, sustained coordination Calvano's laboratory results demonstrated under carefully controlled conditions, when real algorithms operate instead amid the messy complexity, competitive entry, and product differentiation of actual markets. One survey of the broader literature concluded, in a formulation this book finds genuinely persuasive as a matter of intellectual honesty even where it complicates this book's own narrative, that while fully self-learning algorithmic collusion of the kind Calvano's team demonstrated remains too novel and too imperfectly understood in real-world settings for antitrust law to have caught up to it responsibly, the more mundane, better-evidenced problem of centralized, hub-and-spoke pricing algorithms — the RealPage and Cendyn pattern this book's central chapters document in detail — already provides a solid enough evidentiary foundation to justify the kind of targeted legislative and regulatory action chapters nine, fifteen, and sixteen have surveyed.

It is worth being precise, in response to these arguments, about what this book actually concedes and what it does not, because a fair accounting requires drawing that line carefully rather than either capitulating wholesale to the skeptics or waving their objections away. This book concedes that algorithmic collusion's real-world magnitude, outside the specific documented cases examined in its central chapters, remains genuinely uncertain, and that the German gas-station study's own critical-mass finding is real evidence the phenomenon is neither automatic nor universal. It concedes that shared software adoption alone, without evidence of nonpublic data pooling and unusually high, unusually uniform compliance with the resulting recommendations, should not by itself trigger antitrust liability, and that the Ninth Circuit's caution in Gibson reflects a legitimate, defensible reading of conscious-parallelism doctrine rather than judicial error. This book also concedes a related, more uncomfortable point that the skeptics examined in this chapter are right to press: the same evidentiary looseness that lets plaintiffs infer a hub-and-spoke conspiracy from high compliance rates and pooled data could, in a different court, in a different case, with a less careful judge, just as easily produce a false positive against a company genuinely engaged in nothing more than efficient, lawful demand forecasting. The pleading standard Twombly established in 2007, examined in chapter ten, was written specifically to guard against exactly this risk — to keep antitrust litigation from becoming a tool companies use to harass rivals or extract settlements based on nothing more than the fact that a shared, popular piece of software happened to produce parallel prices that a plaintiff's lawyer could dress up as evidence of a conspiracy. Every remedy this book has documented approvingly, from the RealPage consent judgment's data-freshness rule to San Francisco's algorithmic-device definition, works only if it can be applied with the same precision the skeptics are asking courts and regulators to bring to the underlying liability question: targeting the specific mechanism, pooled nonpublic data feeding a shared recommendation nearly everyone follows, rather than algorithmic pricing, dynamic pricing, or demand forecasting as broad, undifferentiated categories.

What this book does not concede, and what the evidence assembled across its previous seventeen chapters does not support conceding, is that the specific, well-documented cases at its center — RealPage's pooled, near-real-time lease data; Cendyn's alleged 90-percent compliance rate in Atlantic City; the Bologna researchers' repeatedly replicated finding that even simple, purely self-interested algorithms discover reward-and-punishment strategies on their own — amount to nothing more than ordinary, efficient market behavior mislabeled by overzealous plaintiffs and headline-hungry journalists. The skeptics are right that the phenomenon this book documents is narrower, more conditional, and more dependent on specific technical and market conditions than the most alarming popular coverage of "AI price-fixing" suggests. They have not shown, and this book does not believe the evidence allows anyone to show, that the phenomenon is not real at all.

The most useful way to hold both halves of this chapter's argument together, going forward, is to treat "algorithmic collusion" not as a single yes-or-no diagnosis to be applied to an entire industry or an entire technology category, but as a spectrum this book's own chapters have already implicitly mapped, running from the clearly criminal at one end to the clearly benign at the other. Topkins's explicit, emailed agreement, examined in chapter four, sits at the criminal end, no different in substance from a phone call. Ordinary yield management using a seller's own historical data, examined in chapter seven, sits at the benign end, indistinguishable from any other legitimate use of statistics to run a business well. The contested middle, where RealPage, Cendyn, and the still-hypothetical foundation-model scenarios of chapter twenty all reside, is defined not by the mere presence of an algorithm but by the specific combination this book has returned to in nearly every chapter: pooled nonpublic data, a small number of sellers, near-universal adoption, and an unusually high, unusually consistent rate of compliance with the resulting recommendation. A reader trying to apply this book's argument to some new piece of pricing technology not yet invented when this book was written should reach for that specific combination, rather than for the word "algorithm" itself, as the diagnostic test — a discipline the skeptics examined in this chapter are right to insist on, and one this book has tried, however imperfectly, to honor throughout.
