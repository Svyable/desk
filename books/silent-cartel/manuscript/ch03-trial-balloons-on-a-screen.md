# Trial Balloons on a Screen

The Airline Deregulation Act itself is worth a brief digression before this chapter turns to ATPCO specifically, because the statute's own history illustrates a pattern this book has traced in miniature through nearly every subsequent chapter: a genuine, well-intentioned reform creating unanticipated new vulnerabilities alongside its intended benefits. Congress passed the Act in October 1978 with broad, bipartisan support, motivated by a straightforward economic argument that federal price-setting through the Civil Aeronautics Board had suppressed competition and kept fares artificially high, insulating incumbent carriers from the kind of price competition that would otherwise have disciplined their costs and expanded consumer choice. Deregulation succeeded on its own terms by most conventional measures economists have applied to it since — average fares fell, new carriers entered previously protected routes, and the flying public gained access to price points that simply did not exist under CAB regulation. What the Act's drafters could not have anticipated, writing in the mid-1970s, was that stripping away federal price-setting authority would create exactly the kind of decentralized, multi-carrier pricing environment in which Stigler's monitoring problem, examined in chapter two, would become newly and urgently relevant to an industry that had never previously needed to solve it independently — airlines had not needed ATPCO's predecessor systems to coordinate anything before 1978, because the government had been setting their fares for them.

Airlines have always had an unusual pricing problem, one that makes them a natural place to look for early evidence of Stigler's theory in action. A single flight can be sold at dozens of different fares simultaneously — full fare, advance-purchase fare, senior fare, corporate-negotiated fare, each with its own restrictions on refundability, minimum stay, and blackout dates — and every one of those fares needs to be visible to travel agents and, later, computerized reservation systems, in order to be sold at all. Before deregulation in 1978, this complexity barely mattered, because the Civil Aeronautics Board set fares directly and airlines competed mainly on service and schedule. After deregulation stripped away that federal price-setting authority and let airlines compete on price for the first time in the industry's history, someone had to build the infrastructure that would let a fare set in Dallas be visible to a travel agent in Denver within hours rather than weeks. That infrastructure arrived in the form of a joint venture called the Airline Tariff Publishing Company, universally known by its acronym, ATPCO, founded by a group of major carriers to collect every airline's fare filings and distribute them, several times a day, to the reservation systems and travel agencies that needed to know what to charge.

ATPCO's stated purpose was entirely legitimate, and this chapter is not an argument that publishing fares is inherently corrupt — a travel agent cannot sell a ticket at a price they do not know, and a fare-distribution system of some kind is a practical necessity in a deregulated industry with thousands of daily flights and constantly shifting prices. What made ATPCO different from an ordinary price list, and what eventually drew the attention of the Justice Department's Antitrust Division, was a set of features embedded in how fares could be filed that went well beyond simple publication. Airlines could file a fare that would not take effect for days or weeks, giving every other airline advance notice of an intended price change before it applied to a single ticket. Airlines could attach short alphanumeric codes to a filing — footnote designators and fare-basis codes, in the industry's internal language — that were not required to convey anything about the fare's actual terms, and could instead be used as a kind of private signaling shorthand between carriers who understood what a given code combination meant even though the codes carried no substantive information for travel agents or the flying public. And airlines could withdraw a proposed fare change before it ever took effect, at essentially no cost, if the change failed to attract the response the filer had hoped for.

Put those three features together and ATPCO stopped looking like a publication system and started looking like something closer to a conversation. The Justice Department's 1992 civil complaint against ATPCO and eight major airlines, later amended and eventually settled by consent decree in 1994, laid out the mechanism in blunt terms: a carrier could file a proposed fare increase today, with an effective date weeks in the future, essentially announcing to every competitor on a route, "I am considering raising this fare — here is the number." If rival carriers responded by filing matching increases, the first filer would let its own increase take effect as scheduled, and the higher fare would become the new market price on that route. If rivals did not respond, the first filer could simply withdraw its filing before the effective date arrived, having lost nothing beyond the trouble of typing in a proposed number, and try again later, perhaps on a different route, perhaps with a different footnote code attached to see whether that combination produced a different response. The government's complaint used a specific, evocative phrase for this pattern that has stuck in the antitrust literature ever since: airlines using ATPCO to float "trial balloons," floating a possible price increase into the market the way a pollster floats a possible policy position, watching for the reaction, and adjusting or retreating based on what came back — all of it happening inside a database, at a fraction of the time and legal risk that a phone call between competitors would have carried, and all of it, crucially, executed through advance-dated filings and footnote codes that had no function except to let one airline signal an intention to a rival without directly and provably communicating it.

The Justice Department's complaint alleged that this pattern had produced more than fifty identifiable episodes of coordinated fare increases across the airlines named in the suit, covering hundreds of individual routes, and quantified the resulting harm to consumers as falling somewhere between $238 million and $1.9 billion in excess fares — a striking range for a government complaint to publish, and a useful reminder that even a well-documented antitrust case involving a mechanism this legible still left the government unable to pin the actual dollar cost of the scheme down more precisely than an order of magnitude — a gap of well over a billion and a half dollars between the low and high estimates, wide enough that reasonable economists on opposite sides of the case could have looked at the identical fare-filing data and reached conclusions that differed by a factor of eight. The complaint made a point that connects directly back to the theoretical apparatus built in the previous chapter: ATPCO's advance-filing and withdrawal features let airlines communicate "just as direct and detailed" a signal as conspirators meeting over the telephone or in a hotel room, in the government's own words, while leaving behind none of the paper trail that a spoken conspiracy generates. There was no Robert Crandall moment here, no single taped phone call that made the case self-evidently damning to a jury. There was, instead, a pattern, visible only in the aggregate, of proposed increases that correlated suspiciously well with the increases rivals ultimately adopted, and of proposed increases that vanished, without explanation, the moment it became clear no rival intended to match them.

American Airlines, the same carrier Robert Crandall had led through his 1982 phone call to Braniff, was among the airlines named as a defendant a decade later — a detail that makes the ATPCO case feel less like an isolated incident and more like the same underlying appetite for coordination finding a quieter, more defensible channel once the loud one had gotten an executive sued once already. American, along with the other major defendants, entered into a consent decree with the Justice Department that became final in August 1994. The decree did not ban ATPCO's fare-distribution function outright — the underlying need for airlines to publish prices travel agents could see remained as real as ever — but it surgically removed the features the government's complaint had identified as functioning like a private channel rather than a public one. Airlines were barred from using footnote designators or fare-basis codes to convey anything beyond the fare's actual, substantive terms; barred from linking different fares together with shared internal codes in ways that let one filing signal information about another; and barred, with narrow exceptions, from filing a fare that would not take effect for an extended period, closing off the "trial balloon" window that had let carriers propose an increase and gauge the market's reaction before committing to it. The decree ran for ten years, expiring in 2004, and the underlying ATPCO system continues to operate today as the industry's primary fare-distribution utility, now stripped of the specific mechanical features a federal court had concluded were functioning as a substitute for a phone call.

ATPCO was not the only piece of shared airline infrastructure drawing regulatory scrutiny in this period, and the parallel case is worth a detour because it shows regulators grappling with a closely related but distinct problem: not what a shared system lets competitors signal to each other, but what it lets one competitor do to the customers viewing it. American Airlines had built and operated one of the first computerized reservation systems, SABRE, which by the early 1980s had become the primary booking tool travel agents used across the industry — a position so commercially valuable that Crandall himself would later be quoted describing SABRE's profitability as potentially exceeding that of American's actual flying business. Federal investigators found that SABRE's flight-display algorithm was not neutral: it systematically ranked American's own flights above competitors' on the screens travel agents used to book tickets, a practice that became known in the industry as "screen science," and one that gave American enormous influence over which flights got booked regardless of price or convenience, simply through the ordering of a list. The Civil Aeronautics Board, and later the Department of Transportation after the CAB's dissolution, responded in 1984 with rules requiring reservation systems to display flights using neutral, disclosed criteria rather than criteria that favored the system's own owner. The SABRE display-bias episode and the ATPCO trial-balloon episode are, in a sense, mirror images of the same underlying lesson: whoever controls the shared infrastructure that an entire industry depends on to see and compare prices holds power that ordinary competition law, built around bilateral agreements between rivals, was not originally designed to reach — power to bias what customers see, in SABRE's case, or power to let rivals coordinate what they charge, in ATPCO's. The display-bias rules stayed in effect for two decades, loosened only in 2004, the same year the ATPCO consent decree itself expired, once the Department of Transportation concluded that the rise of rival global distribution systems and, increasingly, direct internet booking had given travelers enough alternative ways to compare fares that a single reservation system's ranking algorithm no longer carried the outsized influence it had in the early 1980s. American had by then spun SABRE off into an independent public company in 2000, a separation regulators and industry analysts alike understood as removing much of the original conflict of interest, even as the underlying lesson about who controls a shared comparison tool remained just as relevant to the pricing software this book turns to next.

Antitrust economists have a term for the analytical move the ATPCO case required, one that predates the case itself and that this book will return to repeatedly: a "facilitating practice." Courts have long recognized that certain business conduct, while not itself an agreement to fix prices, can make an unlawful agreement easier to reach and easier to sustain, and can therefore function as evidence — a "plus factor," in the case law's own phrase — that tips ordinary parallel pricing over into something a court will treat as circumstantial proof of conspiracy. Advance notice of price changes, industry-wide standardization of terms that serves no cost-based purpose, and exchanges of individualized, forward-looking pricing data among competitors have all been recognized, in various circuit courts over the decades, as classic facilitating practices — not illegal on their own, in most readings, but suspicious enough that combined with other evidence they can support an inference of the real agreement underneath. ATPCO's advance-dated filings and semantically hollow footnote codes fit the facilitating-practice framework almost perfectly, which is part of why the Justice Department's 1992 complaint was able to proceed without ever alleging that any single airline executive had picked up a phone: the plus factors were the shared architecture itself, not any one participant's individual behavior within it.

The ten-year consent decree expired in 2004, and ATPCO continues to operate, now under the tighter filing rules the 1994 settlement imposed, as the backbone of how nearly every airline in the world still publishes fares to the global distribution systems travel agents and booking sites rely on. No comparable, sweeping second prosecution followed the decree's expiration, which airline-industry observers have read in different ways — some as evidence that the 1994 remedy permanently fixed the specific vulnerabilities the government identified, others as evidence that the industry, having learned exactly which features drew a federal lawsuit once, simply became more careful about which parts of its coordination left a visible trace in a shared database. Both readings can be true at once, and this book does not attempt to adjudicate between them from the outside; what the ATPCO case leaves behind, regardless of which reading is more accurate, is a template — a fully litigated, judicially blessed account of exactly how a purely mechanical, pre-internet, non-intelligent piece of shared infrastructure can produce coordinated pricing outcomes without a single actionable phone call, three decades before software sophisticated enough to be called artificial intelligence would be accused of doing something structurally similar to apartment rents, hotel rooms, and casino suites.

The ATPCO case deserves a full chapter in this book, ahead of any of the software built decades later, because it is the clearest historical proof that Stigler's monitoring theory does not require artificial intelligence, machine learning, or even particularly sophisticated technology to operate at scale — it requires only a shared, fast, low-cost channel through which rivals can observe and respond to each other's pricing intentions, and a set of conventions, however informal, for using that channel to signal more than the channel was officially built to carry. ATPCO in the early 1990s was, functionally, a database and a fax machine. It had no learning algorithm, no artificial intelligence of any kind, no capacity to act on its own; every trial balloon in the government's complaint was floated by a human pricing analyst at a real airline, deciding to type a proposed fare into a shared system rather than call a rival directly. What made the scheme work was not the sophistication of the technology. It was the removal of two costs that Stigler had identified thirty years earlier as the central obstacle to sustained tacit collusion: the cost of observing what a rival was about to do, and the cost of testing a price change without fully committing to it. ATPCO reduced both of those costs to nearly zero for the eight airlines under investigation, and the fifty-plus coordinated fare increases the government eventually documented were the predictable, theory-consistent result.

It is worth pausing on what the ATPCO case does and does not establish, because both halves matter for the argument this book is building. It establishes, with unusual clarity for an antitrust matter this old, that a shared information infrastructure — one that no single competitor controls, that every competitor voluntarily feeds data into, and that turns private pricing intentions into something rivals can observe and react to in near-real time — can function as an instrument of coordination even when no individual participant ever violates the letter of the conscious-parallelism doctrine by picking up a phone. It does not establish that every shared pricing database, real-time data feed, or common software vendor produces the same result; ATPCO's specific combination of advance-dated filings, semantically empty signaling codes, and free withdrawal was a deliberately exploitable design, not an unavoidable feature of any system that lets sellers see prices. The government's 1994 remedy targeted those specific design features rather than banning fare transparency altogether, which is itself a tell: regulators understood, even then, that the problem was not information-sharing in the abstract but a particular architecture that let information-sharing do the work an illegal agreement would otherwise have to do.

That distinction — between an information system that merely makes a market transparent and one whose specific architecture reproduces the mechanics of collusion — is the analytical tool this book carries forward into every subsequent chapter, because it is precisely the distinction that modern pricing-software cases turn on, and precisely the distinction that is hardest to draw cleanly once the intermediary stops being a fax-machine-era clearinghouse and becomes a piece of proprietary software making pricing recommendations on its own. RealPage's defenders, examined at length in chapter nine, argue that its software is nothing more than a modern, automated version of the kind of market research a competent property manager could always have done by other means. The Justice Department's complaint against RealPage argues, in substance, exactly what its 1992 complaint against ATPCO argued: that the specific way the system pools nonpublic data across nominal competitors, and the specific way it turns that pooled data into a price recommendation those competitors overwhelmingly follow, reproduces the effect of an agreement without requiring one — trial balloons, floated not by a human pricing analyst typing into a shared database, but by a model trained on exactly the kind of information Stigler said a cartel needs and could never quite get cheaply enough, until someone finally built the tool to get it for free.

Every case examined so far in this book, from Crandall's phone call through the electrical-equipment conspirators' hotel rooms to ATPCO's advance-dated fare filings, shares one feature that makes the next chapter's case an important, clarifying contrast rather than simply the next entry on a growing list: a human being, in every one of these episodes, made a deliberate choice to coordinate, whether by speaking the words directly or by typing a signal into a system built to carry one. The airlines named in the ATPCO complaint did not stumble into trial balloons by accident; someone at each carrier understood what an advance-dated filing with a particular footnote code was for, and chose to use the tool that way. The next chapter turns to a case that looks, on the surface, like the logical next step in this progression — a price-fixing scheme carried out through software rather than a phone call — and shows why it is, in the end, a much simpler and more old-fashioned crime than it first appears: a handful of Amazon marketplace sellers who agreed, in plain written language, to fix a price, and then used code merely to enforce an agreement they had already made the ordinary way. The harder question this book is actually building toward — whether software can produce the same outcome without anyone ever agreeing to anything at all — does not arrive until the chapter after that.
