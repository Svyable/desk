# Ghosts in the Machine

Spencer Meyer filed a lawsuit against Travis Kalanick, then the chief executive of Uber Technologies, in December 2015, nearly nine years before the Justice Department would file its own complaint against RealPage, and the theory behind Meyer's suit anticipated almost every legal argument this book has traced through the RealPage and Cendyn litigation, applied to a business model that looked, on the surface, nothing like an apartment landlord or a casino-hotel. Meyer, an Uber rider, alleged that Kalanick had orchestrated an antitrust conspiracy through the very mechanism that made Uber's business possible: the pricing algorithm that set fares for every ride, uniformly, across a driver population that would otherwise have been free — and, absent Uber's platform, legally required — to compete with each other on price.

Uber's own founding and early growth, briefly worth recounting here, help explain why a pricing-conspiracy theory targeting the company specifically, rather than an established taxi fleet or a traditional livery service, struck contemporary observers as such a novel legal proposition when Meyer first filed it. Travis Kalanick and Garrett Camp founded the company in 2009, building its entire value proposition around the promise of instantly available, algorithmically priced rides summoned through a smartphone app, a model that grew explosively through the early 2010s precisely because it dispensed with the fixed-medallion, negotiated-fare structure that had governed taxi markets in most major cities for generations. That explosive growth had, by the time Meyer filed his complaint in December 2015, made Uber's pricing algorithm the default, unavoidable mechanism through which an enormous and rapidly growing share of urban ride-hailing transactions in the United States were priced, giving Meyer's theory a genuine, practical significance well beyond the specific dollar amount any individual rider might have overpaid on any single trip — a scale argument closely paralleling the one this book applied to RealPage's roughly 80-percent software-market share in chapter nine.

The theory, stripped to its essentials, treated Uber's drivers as horizontal competitors who had all agreed, through their shared participation in the platform, to accept a single pricing mechanism rather than undercut one another the way independent taxi drivers or livery operators traditionally would. No individual driver ever spoke to another driver about what to charge; that was precisely the point of Meyer's theory, and precisely why it reads, in retrospect, like an early and unusually prescient test of the hub-and-spoke reasoning this book has traced from Interstate Circuit's movie theaters through ATPCO's airlines to RealPage's landlords. Uber, in Meyer's telling, was the hub — setting a single algorithmic price and requiring every driver on the platform to charge it — and the thousands of individual drivers were the spokes, bound together into something resembling a price-fixing conspiracy not by any agreement among themselves, but by their shared, voluntary submission to the same centrally administered pricing algorithm.

It is worth situating Meyer's theory against the specific economic structure of Uber's business model, because the theory's plausibility depends on a feature of ride-hailing that distinguishes it from a conventional taxi market in a way worth spelling out. In a traditional, heavily regulated taxi market, individual drivers or fleet owners generally set or negotiate fares within a regulated band, retaining at least some independent pricing discretion even within that regulatory structure. Uber's platform, by contrast, was built from its earliest days around a single, centrally computed fare for every ride, with individual drivers having essentially no ability to negotiate or independently set the price a given rider would pay — a structural choice that gave the platform enormous operational efficiency and made the service dramatically easier to use than hailing a traditional cab, but that also meant, as Meyer's complaint argued, that drivers who would otherwise have been legally free to compete with each other on price had, by joining the platform, effectively surrendered that specific competitive dimension entirely to Uber's own centrally administered algorithm.

The case reached Judge Jed Rakoff in the Southern District of New York, and his handling of Uber's motion to compel arbitration produced one of the more quotable judicial passages in this entire book's research, precisely because it states the book's central thesis so directly, eight years before the RealPage complaint would make the same point using different software. Denying Uber's initial motion, Rakoff wrote that the case could proceed toward the merits, reasoning that "the advancement of technological means for the orchestration of large-scale price-fixing conspiracies need not leave antitrust law behind" — a line that could serve, almost without modification, as an epigraph for every chapter in this book's second half. Rakoff's opinion did not treat Uber's algorithm as somehow exempt from antitrust scrutiny merely because it was software rather than a hotel-room phone call; it treated the algorithm as a potential mechanism of coordination deserving exactly the same legal analysis a human-administered price-fixing scheme would receive, an analytical move that RealPage's opponents, the Cendyn plaintiffs, and the Justice Department's own lawyers would all, in effect, repeat years later against different defendants.

The case never reached a jury, and its actual resolution illustrates a different, quieter obstacle that recurs throughout the algorithmic-collusion litigation this book surveys: not necessarily whether the underlying antitrust theory is sound, but whether the plaintiff ever gets to test it in open court at all. Uber appealed Rakoff's initial ruling to the Second Circuit Court of Appeals, arguing that Meyer had agreed, when he registered for the app, to resolve any dispute with Uber through private arbitration rather than public litigation — a term buried in the clickwrap terms of service every Uber user accepts, usually without reading, before taking a first ride. The Second Circuit agreed with Uber in August 2017, holding that a hyperlink to the terms of service on the app's payment screen provided legally sufficient notice, and that Meyer's act of clicking "Register" constituted binding assent to arbitrate his claims privately rather than pursue them in federal court. The antitrust theory Rakoff had found substantial enough to survive an initial motion to dismiss was never tested on the merits in public; it was redirected into a private arbitration process whose outcome, by the nature of arbitration, was never made public in a way that could inform this book's account of it, or inform any subsequent plaintiff's litigation strategy, or build the kind of visible legal precedent that Gibson and Cornish-Adebiyi would go on to build in the casino-hotel context years later.

The arbitration clause that redirected Meyer's case away from public view is not a peculiarity of Uber's business alone; it is a structural feature of an enormous share of modern consumer-facing technology platforms, and its effect on this book's broader subject deserves to be named plainly. Every consumer who has ever clicked "I agree" on a mobile app's terms of service has very possibly signed away the right to bring exactly the kind of public antitrust claim this book documents, in favor of a private arbitration process that produces no published opinion, sets no binding precedent, and leaves no public record for a journalist, a regulator, or a future litigant to build on. The RealPage and Cendyn cases examined in the previous three chapters proceeded in open federal court in large part because their plaintiffs were landlords' actual tenants and government enforcers rather than consumers bound by a platform's own clickwrap arbitration terms — a distinction of legal posture, not of underlying economic substance, that has shaped which algorithmic-pricing disputes this book, and the public record more broadly, can actually examine in detail.

It is worth pausing on why Meyer's underlying antitrust theory against Uber never generated the kind of rich public case law this book's other chapters have been able to draw on, because the reason connects directly to a structural feature of the modern consumer economy that extends far beyond ride-hailing alone. Every major consumer technology platform examined across this book's central chapters — Amazon, examined for its 2000 dynamic-pricing controversy in chapter thirteen; Delta, examined for its 2025 individualized-pricing controversy in chapter twelve; Uber, examined in this chapter — relies on the identical clickwrap arbitration architecture that redirected Meyer's claims out of public view in 2017. A consumer who wants to challenge a platform's pricing practices in open court, building the kind of public record this book has relied on throughout, first has to survive a threshold argument that they agreed, by tapping "I accept" on a screen they likely never read closely, to resolve any such dispute privately instead. Landlords' tenants and government enforcers, the plaintiffs in the RealPage and Cendyn litigation this book's central chapters document in detail, generally do not sign comparable arbitration agreements with their landlord's software vendor, a party they have no direct contractual relationship with at all — which is a structural, almost accidental reason those two cases produced the rich public litigation record this book has been able to draw on, while a strikingly similar and considerably earlier theory against Uber disappeared into a private arbitration process this book, and every other public account of the underlying dispute, has no way to examine.

Uber's and Lyft's surge-pricing mechanisms raise a second, structurally different concern from the one Meyer's lawsuit targeted, and it is worth treating separately because it inverts the usual direction of the algorithmic-collusion story this book has told so far: instead of a shared software vendor helping nominally independent sellers coordinate against buyers, this second pattern involves drivers actively gaming the platform's own algorithm, coordinating with each other to manipulate the very pricing system Meyer's lawsuit had alleged was itself the instrument of an unlawful conspiracy. Drivers using both platforms have, for years, organized informally through forums with names like UberPeople and dedicated communities sometimes referred to as "Surge Club," along with more general platforms like Reddit and private group chats, to share real-time information about surge zones and, more consequentially, to coordinate deliberately logging off the app simultaneously in a given area — a maneuver intended to trick the platform's own algorithm into perceiving a shortage of available drivers relative to rider demand, triggering the automatic surge-pricing mechanism that raises fares for everyone in that zone, including the coordinating drivers, once they log back in a few minutes later. Researchers who have studied this behavior, drawing on interviews with engineers at ride-hailing companies including Uber, Lyft, and the Southeast Asian platform Grab, have documented the pattern as a genuine, recurring operational concern for the platforms rather than a rare or hypothetical curiosity, confirming that drivers really do organize this kind of coordinated, temporary supply withdrawal and that platform engineers have had to build detection systems specifically to identify and penalize accounts suspected of participating in it.

This driver-side gaming is, in the strict antitrust sense, a more conventional and more legally straightforward form of price-fixing than anything else examined in this book, because it involves exactly the ingredient every prior chapter has shown the law can reliably punish when it can be proven: an actual, coordinated agreement among competitors — in this case, drivers who are legally independent contractors competing for the same rides — to manipulate market conditions in their collective favor. A group of Uber drivers agreeing, over a private messaging app, to simultaneously log off in order to trigger artificial surge pricing bears a structural resemblance to the Topkins poster conspiracy examined in chapter four: human beings communicating directly, reaching an actual agreement, and then using technology — in this case, the ride-hailing platform's own supply-sensing algorithm — as the mechanism to execute a decision that was made the old-fashioned way. The platforms themselves have treated this behavior as a violation of their terms of service rather than a matter for public antitrust litigation, relying on account suspensions and algorithmic fraud detection rather than lawsuits, which means this particular strand of the book's subject has generated far less public legal record than the RealPage and Cendyn litigation, even though the underlying conduct maps more cleanly onto a century of settled Sherman Act doctrine than either of those harder, more novel cases does.

The academic literature studying platform-mediated gig work has documented the coordinated-logoff maneuver with enough specificity to move it well beyond driver folklore. Researchers examining ride-hailing markets have described drivers organizing through the same kinds of channels that would, in any other industry, read as a classic conspiracy's paper trail: dedicated online forums, Facebook groups, and private messaging threads where drivers compare notes on which specific city blocks or time windows are most likely to trigger surge pricing if enough drivers simultaneously go offline. Engineers at Uber, Lyft, and the Southeast Asian ride-hailing platform Grab have separately confirmed to researchers that this behavior is a real, recurring operational headache rather than a rare curiosity, forcing each company to build fraud-detection systems specifically calibrated to distinguish a genuine, organic supply shortage from an artificially manufactured one. The platforms' own incentive here runs directly counter to the drivers': a genuine surge, triggered by real demand exceeding real supply, serves the platform's interest by pulling more drivers onto the road exactly when riders need them; an artificial surge, triggered by drivers gaming the detection algorithm, serves only the coordinating drivers, at the direct expense of both the platform's margins and the riders paying the inflated fare.

Uber's and Lyft's own pricing algorithms have separately drawn direct antitrust scrutiny from a different angle entirely, distinct from both Meyer's original theory and the driver-coordination pattern this chapter has just described: state and federal regulators have periodically examined whether the two companies' historically overlapping investor base — venture-capital and private-equity firms holding significant stakes in both nominally competing platforms simultaneously — could itself function as a channel of coordination, under a legal theory economists and antitrust scholars call common ownership, where shared investors reduce each portfolio company's incentive to compete aggressively against a rival in which the same investor also holds a substantial stake. Common ownership theory, developed largely by economists studying airline and banking concentration rather than ride-hailing specifically, describes a mechanism structurally distinct from every other pattern examined in this book: not a shared vendor, not a shared algorithm, not even a shared data pool, but simply a shared financial stake creating a rational incentive for two portfolio companies' executives to compete less aggressively than fully independent ownership would produce. Whether that theory applies with meaningful force to Uber and Lyft specifically remains genuinely disputed among the economists who study it, and this book does not attempt to resolve the underlying empirical debate; the theory is worth flagging briefly here only because it illustrates, one final time, how many structurally distinct mechanisms — pooled data, shared algorithms, driver-side coordination, and now overlapping ownership — can all produce outcomes that look, from a rider's seat, like the identical phenomenon: a price that moved together with a competitor's price for reasons no rider shopping between two apps could ever directly observe.

This driver-side pattern also supplies a useful contrast with the German gas-station case examined in chapter six, because the underlying economic mechanism is, in a narrow technical sense, strikingly similar even though the moral and legal valence runs in opposite directions. Both cases involve market participants restricting the effective supply available to a shared, algorithmically mediated pricing system, in order to trigger a price increase that would not have occurred under conditions of genuine, undistorted competition. Assad, Clark, Ershov, and Xu's German gas stations achieved this through software quietly monitoring and matching competitors' prices once enough stations had adopted the same tool; Uber and Lyft's drivers achieve a structurally similar outcome by deliberately withholding their own labor supply from the platform's sensing mechanism for a few coordinated minutes. Neither scenario requires the kind of formal cartel meeting Robert Crandall attempted with Howard Putnam in 1982. Both nonetheless produce a price higher than an undistorted market would have generated, through nothing more than a shared understanding, however informally reached, that restraint by everyone produces a better outcome for each individual than competition by everyone would.

Taken together, the Uber and Lyft material in this chapter completes a kind of matrix this book has been implicitly building since its opening chapter, and it is worth making that matrix explicit before moving forward. Collusion facilitated by software can run in at least three distinct directions, and conflating them, as popular commentary on "algorithmic collusion" often does, obscures rather than clarifies the underlying legal and economic questions. It can run from a shared vendor down to nominally competing sellers, the RealPage and Cendyn pattern, where a hub pools data and issues recommendations to spokes who never speak to each other directly. It can run from a platform itself down to a large, atomized population of individual sellers bound to a single pricing mechanism by the platform's own terms of service, the theory Spencer Meyer's lawsuit tested against Uber and never got to prove in open court. And it can run laterally, from seller to seller, using a platform's own legitimate algorithm as an unwitting tool for old-fashioned, directly negotiated collusion among the sellers themselves, the pattern documented in driver forums coordinating simultaneous app shutdowns. Each of these three patterns implicates the same underlying statute and much of the same theoretical apparatus this book has built across its first ten chapters, but each raises a genuinely distinct legal question, with a genuinely distinct evidentiary path to proving it — a distinction the next several chapters, moving from pricing collusion toward the related but conceptually separate phenomenon of personalized price discrimination, will need to keep just as carefully in view.
