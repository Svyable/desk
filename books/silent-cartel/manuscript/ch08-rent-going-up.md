# Rent Going Up? One Company's Algorithm Could Be Why

Heather Vogell had covered education and nonprofit accountability for years before she turned her attention, in 2022, to a piece of apartment-industry software that most of her eventual readers had never heard of and that most of the landlords using it would never have to explain to a single tenant. ProPublica published her investigation on October 15, 2022, under a headline written as a direct question — "Rent Going Up? One Company's Algorithm Could Be Why" — and the piece did what the best investigative journalism does: it took a piece of infrastructure operating quietly in the background of an enormous, ordinary part of daily life, apartment rent, and made its mechanics visible to people who had spent years paying its consequences without knowing the mechanism existed.

Vogell's path to the story began, as is often the case with the strongest investigative journalism, with sources inside the industry willing to describe a practice they considered troubling even as their own employers profited from it, supplemented by public records that most reporters covering housing policy had never had reason to examine closely: property-management marketing materials, RealPage's own investor communications, and public court filings from smaller, earlier disputes that had drawn little attention on their own but that, read together and cross-referenced against interviews, began to reveal a pattern operating quietly across dozens of metropolitan markets simultaneously. The resulting piece exemplifies a form of journalism this book has relied on throughout its middle chapters: not a single dramatic leak or a single whistleblower's confession, but the patient accumulation of many small, individually unremarkable pieces of evidence into a picture no single source could have supplied on their own — the same kind of evidentiary patience this book's chapter six showed German academic economists applying to structural breaks in gas-station pricing data, translated into the different, faster-moving idiom of daily investigative journalism.

The software at the center of the story was built by RealPage, the Texas company whose YieldStar product this book's previous chapter traced back to its 2002 acquisition from Camden Property Trust, and which had by 2022 evolved into a broader suite the company marketed as AI Revenue Management. Vogell's reporting laid out, in plain and damning language, exactly what the software asked landlords to do and exactly what it promised in return. RealPage's own marketing, quoted directly in the piece, told prospective clients: "Find out how YieldStar can help you outperform the market 3% to 7%." The phrase is worth reading twice, because it concedes, in RealPage's own words, precisely the thing the software's defenders would later spend years in court denying — that the tool's purpose was not to help an individual landlord discover their unit's fair market value, but to help that landlord systematically exceed it, framing "the market" itself as an obstacle to beat rather than a competitive benchmark to respect.

Vogell did not rely on marketing copy alone. She reported that Greystar, the country's largest multifamily property manager and, not coincidentally, one of the six landlords the Justice Department would later name as a co-defendant, had found that even during an economic downturn, its buildings using YieldStar "outperformed their markets by 4.8 percent" — a specific, sourced figure that gave the phenomenon a concrete number rather than leaving readers to imagine an abstract, unquantified advantage. The mechanism behind that number, as Vogell reconstructed it through interviews with a former RealPage executive and other sources close to the industry, worked by pooling information no individual landlord could lawfully or practically gather on their own: competing property managers fed the software their occupancy rates, upcoming lease-expiration dates, and current asking rents, information that would ordinarily be closely guarded competitive intelligence, and the software combined that pooled, nonpublic data across every subscribing building in a given area to generate a daily price recommendation for every available unit. The former executive told Vogell the data gave landlords insight into what competitors within a half-mile to a mile were charging — a granularity of competitive intelligence that would have required, before software like this existed, either an illegal phone call to a rival or an army of employees physically canvassing nearby buildings, neither of which any individual landlord could realistically sustain across an entire metropolitan portfolio.

The most quietly devastating sentence in Vogell's reporting was not a statistic at all; it was a description of what the software's pooled visibility actually did to a landlord's psychology, attributed to the same former RealPage executive: the tool gave landlords "the courage to charge an inflated price by the implicit assurance that all of their competitors were doing the same." Read that sentence against the theoretical apparatus this book built across its first several chapters and it snaps into focus immediately. It is Stigler's monitoring problem, solved and handed back to the seller as a feeling rather than a fact — not merely information about what a rival was charging, but the reassurance that raising a price would not simply lose the unit to a rival undercutting it, because the software itself was the mechanism ensuring the rival would not undercut. Courage, in this account, was not the courage of an individual businessperson taking an entrepreneurial risk. It was the confidence that comes from knowing, with unusual precision for an ordinary competitive market, exactly how your rivals were about to behave, because the same software that was telling you what to charge was telling all of them the same thing.

Vogell's reporting used specific, geographically grounded examples to make the phenomenon legible rather than abstract, in exactly the way this book's earlier chapters have tried to do with the Crandall tape and the German gas pumps. In one Seattle neighborhood she examined, ten separate, formally competing property-management companies were all using RealPage's software to set rent on 70 percent of the units in that neighborhood — a concentration that meant a renter shopping among ten seemingly independent landlords in that pocket of the city was, in practical terms, often shopping among ten different storefronts for what was substantially the same underlying pricing engine, with the same nonpublic data feeding it. In fast-growing Sun Belt markets the Justice Department would later cite in its own complaint — Atlanta, Austin, Charlotte, Dallas, Houston, Orlando, and Raleigh among them — the government alleged that RealPage's recommendations governed rent-setting for between a third and two-thirds of entire multifamily building stocks in specific neighborhoods, a market presence broad enough that a renter attempting to comparison-shop across nominally independent buildings would frequently, without realizing it, be comparing outputs of the identical pricing model rather than comparing genuinely independent competitive offers.

The timing of Vogell's investigation mattered enormously to how quickly it caught fire, and it is worth situating the piece against the broader housing market of the period rather than treating it as a story that would have landed the same way at any other moment. American rents rose sharply and rapidly through 2021 and into 2022, as a wave of pandemic-era household formation, historically low apartment vacancy rates, and rising construction and financing costs combined to push asking rents in many metropolitan areas up by double-digit percentages year over year, a run-up severe enough that housing affordability had become a dominant national political concern well before Vogell's article named a specific technological mechanism that might be contributing to it. Renters who had spent two years watching lease-renewal notices arrive with startling increases, often with no obvious change in the unit, the building, or the local job market to explain them, had been searching, in a diffuse and largely unfocused way, for an explanation beyond the generic invocation of "supply and demand" that landlords and industry trade groups had been offering. Vogell's reporting gave that search a specific, concrete, actionable target — a named company, a named product, a quoted marketing promise to help landlords "outperform the market" — at precisely the moment a very large number of Americans were primed to receive it, which helps explain why a story about a piece of B2B software most readers had never encountered directly generated the kind of immediate congressional response usually reserved for far more visible controversies.

RealPage's public response to the growing controversy, consistent across its statements to ProPublica, its later court filings, and its public relations materials, rested on three linked arguments that the company has continued to make throughout the subsequent litigation, and that deserve to be stated in their strongest form rather than dismissed in passing. First, the company has argued that its software's price recommendations are advisory rather than binding, and that landlords retain full discretion to reject any given recommendation — a defense that echoes, deliberately or not, the same "no agreement, only independent choice" argument RealPage's lawyers would later deploy in federal court. Second, RealPage has pointed to constrained housing supply, rather than its own software, as the primary driver of the rent increases renters experienced during the period Vogell's reporting covered, arguing that the company's pricing tools help landlords respond efficiently to genuine scarcity rather than creating scarcity or artificial pricing power themselves. Third, and perhaps most pointed, the company has argued that better information generally makes markets function more efficiently rather than less, framing its own product as a technological upgrade to ordinary market research that any sophisticated landlord could, in principle, replicate through other lawful means — hiring analysts, subscribing to public rent-comparison data, or simply paying closer attention to publicly advertised competitor listings. Whether that framing survives contact with the specific, nonpublic, pooled nature of the data RealPage's software actually uses is the question this book's next chapter takes up directly, through the lens of the government's own formal legal complaint.

Reaction on Capitol Hill followed quickly, and it is worth documenting because it shows how directly Vogell's reporting shaped the government's subsequent legal action, a chain of cause and effect this book's later chapters take largely for granted but that deserves to be spelled out here. Three United States senators — reacting specifically to the ProPublica investigation — wrote that they were, in their own words, "concerned that the use of this rate setting software essentially amounts to a cartel to artificially inflate rental rates in multifamily residential buildings," using the word cartel deliberately, in a formal legislative letter, more than a decade before any court would rule on whether the word applied as a matter of law. Senator Elizabeth Warren and a growing group of Senate Democrats sent additional letters over the following months pressing the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission to investigate, and seventeen Democratic members of the House of Representatives sent a parallel letter of their own, an unusually broad and rapid bipartisan-adjacent congressional response for a piece of software business reporters and most ordinary renters had never heard of eight weeks earlier.

The Justice Department's own investigation into RealPage, which ProPublica would go on to report had already opened by the end of 2022, moved from inquiry to formal litigation within roughly two years — the August 2024 lawsuit examined in detail in the next chapter — a timeline that places Vogell's original reporting squarely at the origin of the entire legal saga this book's central chapters are built around. This is worth pausing on because it complicates a simple story in which government antitrust enforcement discovers wrongdoing entirely on its own initiative, through routine market surveillance or a whistleblower's tip reaching investigators directly. In the RealPage case, as in a meaningful share of major antitrust actions across American history, the chain ran instead from an individual reporter's sourced, patient, months-long investigative work, through public pressure from elected officials responding directly to that reporting, to a government agency that had access to subpoena power and prosecutorial resources ProPublica never had, but that needed the initial map Vogell's reporting drew before it knew precisely where to start digging.

Private litigation followed the same trajectory as the congressional letters, moving faster in some respects than the government's own case. Tenants across the country began filing individual and class-action lawsuits against RealPage within weeks of Vogell's original article, and by April 2023 the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation had consolidated more than twenty of these separate actions into a single coordinated proceeding in the Middle District of Tennessee, styled *In re: RealPage, Inc., Rental Software Antitrust Litigation* and assigned to Judge Waverly D. Crenshaw Jr. — a Nashville venue the panel selected for its central geography and accessibility rather than for any connection to the underlying dispute. The consolidated tenant plaintiffs alleged, using language that echoes the senators' letters almost exactly, that RealPage and the property managers using its software had formed "an illegal, horizontal price-fixing conspiracy" reaching back, in the plaintiffs' telling, as early as 2016 — years before Vogell's reporting brought the arrangement to public attention, suggesting the pattern had been operating quietly for the better part of a decade before anyone outside the industry understood what the software was doing. In December 2023, Judge Crenshaw denied RealPage's motion to dismiss the consolidated complaint, ruling that the tenants had alleged enough to proceed to the discovery phase rather than have their case thrown out before a single document changed hands — a significant early procedural victory for the plaintiffs, though not, importantly, a ruling on the ultimate merits of whether RealPage's conduct actually violated the Sherman Act.

That private litigation reached a landmark financial resolution years later and, as it happens, almost exactly as this book was being written: by August 2026, RealPage and a group of settling landlord defendants had agreed to pay just under $360 million — $359,925,000, according to the settlement documents — into a fund for eligible renters, covering tenants who paid rent at a property using RealPage's pricing software between October 2018 and November 2025. The settlement was structured to distribute the fund proportionally based on how much rent each eligible tenant had actually paid during that roughly seven-year window, after deductions for administrative costs and attorneys' fees, meaning the practical payout to any individual renter would likely amount to a modest sum rather than a life-changing one — but the aggregate figure, nearly $360 million, put a concrete number on harm that had spent years being described only in qualitative terms like "artificially inflate" and "cartel." A separate, smaller settlement earlier in the litigation had already resolved claims against Greystar specifically for $141 million, reached in October 2025, months before the larger, broader settlement covering RealPage itself and the remaining defendants. Taken together, the two settlements represent, by a wide margin, the largest financial acknowledgment — though, consistent with essentially every settlement examined in this book, not a formal admission of legal wrongdoing — that algorithmic rent-setting software has produced yet in the American legal system, private litigation succeeding, on the question of writing an actual check to affected consumers, where the government's own parallel case, examined in the next chapter, has so far produced regulatory restrictions on RealPage's business practices but no comparable direct payment to the renters who filed the original complaints.

RealPage disputed key elements of Vogell's reporting and the broader narrative it launched, both at the time and in subsequent litigation, arguing that its software merely provided pricing recommendations that individual landlords remained free to accept or reject, that no landlord was required to share data as a condition of using the product, and that competitive apartment markets with rents responsive to local supply and demand were the predictable, benign result of better information rather than evidence of a cartel. Those arguments are not frivolous, and this book takes them seriously rather than dismissing them, both here and at greater length in chapter seventeen's fuller treatment of the strongest skeptical case against the algorithmic-collusion framing generally. But the specific factual record Vogell assembled — the marketing language promising to help landlords "outperform the market," the former executive's own account of what the pooled data was for, the concrete, geographically specific concentration figures in Seattle and the Sun Belt — supplied the government with something considerably more substantial than a hunch to build a legal case around, and it is that record, more than any prosecutor's independent initiative, that set the RealPage litigation in motion.

ProPublica did not stop at the original October 2022 article, and the outlet's continued coverage over the following two years traces almost exactly the arc this chapter and the next one follow: a follow-up piece reporting that senators had formally asked the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission to open an investigation; another confirming, based on sourcing inside the department, that the Justice Department's Antitrust Division had in fact begun one; another documenting the first tenant lawsuits filed in the immediate aftermath of the original story; and, eventually, a piece reporting the Justice Department's own decision to formally join the private litigation and file its civil complaint, effectively putting the federal government's institutional weight behind allegations an independent newsroom had first surfaced using nothing but public records, interviews, and a former industry executive willing to talk. That sequence is a useful corrective to any assumption that antitrust enforcement in the algorithmic-pricing era operates primarily through routine regulatory monitoring or internal government initiative. In the RealPage case, as this chapter has tried to document in some detail, the sequence ran the other way: journalism found the pattern, public pressure amplified it, private litigation tested it in court first, and government enforcement arrived last, adopting a theory of the case that had already been substantially built by the people whose job was simply to report accurately on what a piece of pricing software was actually doing to the rent renters paid every month.
