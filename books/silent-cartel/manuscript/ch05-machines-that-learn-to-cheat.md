# Machines That Learn to Cheat

Q-learning's own technical history is worth a brief detour before turning to how Calvano's team applied it, because the algorithm's decades-long pedigree in fields entirely unrelated to pricing underscores just how ordinary and well-understood the underlying tool actually was by the time this book's central experiment used it. The technique traces back to Christopher Watkins's 1989 doctoral dissertation at Cambridge, which formalized a method for an agent to learn the long-run value of taking a given action in a given state through nothing but repeated trial and reward, without needing a pre-built model of how its environment worked. Q-learning went on to become a foundational building block across decades of subsequent reinforcement-learning research, powering, among other applications, DeepMind's landmark Atari-playing systems in the early 2010s and forming a conceptual ancestor of the more elaborate techniques that would later master Go and other complex games. By the time Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò, and Pastorello applied it to oligopoly pricing, Q-learning was, in the technical sense, old and well-worn machinery — not a cutting-edge or exotic method built specifically to explore collusion, but one of the most thoroughly studied, most widely taught reinforcement-learning algorithms available, chosen specifically because its behavior was already so well understood in other domains that any collusive result it produced in a pricing context could be attributed with unusual confidence to the underlying economic dynamics rather than to some idiosyncrasy of an unfamiliar or poorly tested technique.

In 2019, four Italian economists — Emilio Calvano and Vincenzo Denicolò at the University of Bologna, Giacomo Calzolari at the European University Institute, and Sergio Pastorello, also at Bologna — set out to answer the question the previous chapter left open, and they answered it in a way no courtroom, no regulatory investigation, and no historical case study ever could: they built the cartel themselves, from nothing, inside a computer, and watched to see whether it would form on its own. Their paper, "Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion," was published in 2020 in the *American Economic Review*, the most prestigious general-interest journal in the field, and it has since become the single most cited piece of research in the entire academic literature on this subject — cited, as of the mid-2020s, in several hundred subsequent papers, presented directly to regulators including a session at the U.S. Federal Trade Commission, and referenced in nearly every serious legal brief, legislative hearing, and competition-authority report that has grappled with what pricing software is actually capable of.

The experimental design was, in a sense, deliberately unglamorous, because the researchers wanted a result nobody could dismiss as an artifact of some exotic or unrealistic setup. They built a standard economic model of oligopoly — a small number of sellers competing on price for a differentiated product, the same basic Bertrand-style competition taught in any graduate industrial-organization course — and populated it not with the mathematically optimal, perfectly rational firms that economic theory traditionally assumes, but with algorithms using a well-established, decades-old machine-learning technique called Q-learning. Q-learning is not exotic or novel; it is one of the foundational methods of reinforcement learning, the same broad family of technique that had, by the time of the paper's publication, already taught computer programs to play Atari games, master the board game Go well enough to defeat the world's best human players, and control robotic systems, all without a programmer directly specifying the winning strategy. A Q-learning agent does not start out knowing anything about the game it is playing. It starts by trying actions essentially at random, observes the reward each action produces, and gradually updates its internal estimate of which actions tend to lead to good outcomes over time, favoring the actions that estimate rewards well while still occasionally trying something new in case its current beliefs are wrong. Applied to pricing, a Q-learning agent starts out setting essentially random prices, observes its own profit after each price it tries, and slowly learns, through nothing but repeated trial and error against its own experience, which prices tend to work well given how the other seller in the market is behaving.

Crucially, the two algorithms Calvano and his coauthors built were given no communication channel whatsoever. They could not send each other messages, could not see each other's internal code or strategy, and had no shared memory or coordination mechanism beyond the one thing any two competitors in an ordinary market can always observe: each other's publicly posted prices. They were instructed to do exactly one thing — maximize their own long-run profit — with no reward, bonus, or penalty tied to cooperating, colluding, matching, or in any way considering the other algorithm's welfare. If two firms behaving this way in the real world converged on high, stable, non-competitive prices, no antitrust lawyer alive could point to a single line of code and call it an unlawful instruction to collude, because no such instruction existed anywhere in the system. The algorithms were told to be selfish, not cooperative, and left alone to run for what amounted to hundreds of thousands or millions of simulated pricing rounds — a volume of repeated interaction no human oligopoly, however patient, could ever accumulate across an entire corporate lifetime, compressed by the researchers into a run of computation that took hours or days on ordinary hardware.

The result, replicated across many variations of the basic setup, was consistent and, for anyone who had absorbed the theoretical argument of this book's second chapter, unsurprising in direction if startling in magnitude: the algorithms did not converge to the competitive price that a textbook model of rational, profit-maximizing firms predicts. They converged instead to prices well above that competitive benchmark — supracompetitive prices, in the economics jargon — capturing a substantial share of the extra profit that a fully collusive cartel, coordinating explicitly and perfectly, would have been able to extract. When the algorithms were configured to be patient, weighting future profit nearly as heavily as present profit — the same "discount factor" concept that the folk theorem, discussed in chapter two, identifies as the key ingredient enabling cooperation to emerge from repeated interaction — the profit gain the algorithms captured relative to the fully competitive outcome exceeded 80 percent of the maximum a perfect cartel could have achieved. As the researchers made the algorithms less patient, weighting the present more heavily relative to the future, the collusive profit gain fell in a smooth, orderly way, consistent with the folk theorem's own prediction that patience is the fuel cooperation runs on — dropping to a still-meaningfully-positive 16 percent even at a substantially reduced discount factor, rather than collapsing to zero the moment patience was reduced at all.

What made the paper genuinely startling, rather than merely a numerical confirmation of theory economists already believed, was the specific shape of the strategy the algorithms discovered on their own. The researchers did not simply observe two prices settling at a high, stable level and stopping there; they examined what the algorithms actually did when one of them was forced, by the researchers, to deviate from the settled pattern and cut its price. What followed looked, to anyone familiar with the game-theory literature on repeated games, remarkably like a textbook trigger strategy of exactly the kind economists had theorized about since Stigler and formalized through the folk theorem decades before any algorithm existed to test it: the other algorithm responded to the price cut with a swift, sharp retaliation, dropping its own price close to the competitive level for a sustained period, inflicting a real, measurable profit loss on the algorithm that had deviated — a punishment phase, in the researchers' own terminology, that persisted for a calibrated interval before both algorithms gradually, and without being told to, worked their way back up toward the original high, cooperative price level. Roughly half of even the most tempting available price cuts turned out, once the researchers accounted for the retaliation that would follow, to be unprofitable for the deviating algorithm to attempt — meaning the punishment mechanism the algorithms had independently discovered was not merely present but strong enough to actually deter defection most of the time, doing the enforcement work a human cartel's hired muscle or reputational sanctions would otherwise have to do.

Nothing in the algorithms' training told them to build a reward-and-punishment scheme. Nothing told them that punishing a deviating rival was a legitimate strategic option, or that a temporary loss now could purchase a larger gain later, or that returning to cooperation after administering punishment was preferable to punishing forever. The algorithms discovered all of this purely through trial and error, purely by noticing, across an enormous number of simulated rounds, that certain patterns of behavior tended to produce higher long-run rewards than others — which is to say, they rediscovered, entirely on their own, essentially the same logic Robert Axelrod's tit-for-tat program had won two prisoner's-dilemma tournaments with in the late 1970s, and essentially the same logic Stigler had described in prose in 1964: watch your rival, tolerate no unpunished deviation, and let the shared understanding that deviation will be punished do most of the actual enforcement work, so that punishment itself rarely has to be deployed in practice once the pattern is established. The Bologna researchers had, in other words, built a machine that reinvented, from first principles and with no access to the relevant economic literature, an idea economists had spent sixty years working out on paper.

The paper's robustness checks matter as much as its headline finding, because a single striking result from one narrow experimental configuration would be easy, and reasonable, to dismiss as a laboratory curiosity. Calvano and his coauthors tested their basic setup against a long list of variations designed to probe whether the collusive result was a fragile artifact of one specific model or a genuinely durable feature of how these algorithms behave: they varied the number of competing algorithms in the market, tested markets with more than two sellers, introduced asymmetries in cost and demand so that the competing algorithms were not perfectly identical to begin with, and introduced various forms of uncertainty into what each algorithm could observe about the market it operated in. The supracompetitive outcome persisted, in some weakened form, across essentially all of these variations, though the size of the effect did shrink as the researchers added more competitors — a finding directly consistent with Stigler's original monitoring theory, which predicted exactly this pattern: more sellers means a noisier, harder-to-monitor market, which means weaker sustained cooperation, whether the sellers doing the (not) cooperating are twentieth-century oligopolists or twenty-first-century reinforcement-learning algorithms.

The paper's authors were notably careful, in ways this book tries to be equally careful about, not to overclaim what a laboratory simulation proves about real-world markets. A simulated economy with two or three algorithmic sellers, a single stylized product, and no entry, exit, regulation, or human oversight is a controlled experiment, useful precisely because it isolates the variable of interest — can pure profit-maximizing algorithms, with no instruction to cooperate, learn to sustain supracompetitive prices through nothing but repeated interaction? — from the enormous number of confounding forces present in any actual industry. The paper does not, by itself, prove that RealPage's software, or airline fare algorithms, or any specific real-world pricing tool is doing what the Bologna researchers' Q-learning agents did; it proves that the mechanism is real, that it does not require any instruction to collude, and that it is not merely a theoretical possibility dreamed up by economists worried about a hypothetical future. Chapter six turns to the harder and, in some ways, more consequential question the laboratory result raises but cannot answer on its own: does anything resembling this dynamic actually show up when real companies, selling a real product, in a real market, start using algorithmic pricing software built by real vendors rather than by economists running a controlled experiment?

The 2020 paper did not appear in a vacuum, and it is worth placing it inside the broader arc of regulatory attention this book traces throughout, because Calvano and his coauthors were not the first people inside a government building to worry about this problem — they were simply the first to demonstrate it this cleanly. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development had already circulated an internal working paper titled "Algorithms and Collusion" in 2017, three years before the Bologna paper's publication, flagging the theoretical risk that machine-learning pricing tools might independently arrive at collusive outcomes and urging competition authorities to begin thinking seriously about how existing law would apply if they did. Margrethe Vestager, the European Union's competition commissioner, had already begun warning publicly, as early as a March 2017 speech in Berlin, that companies could not escape antitrust liability for collusion "by hiding behind a computer program" — a formulation that anticipated, in the language of enforcement policy, exactly the empirical question Calvano's team would go on to answer with actual data three years later. What the 2020 paper supplied that the earlier warnings could not was proof: not a hypothetical risk flagged by regulators worried about the future, but a reproducible experimental result, published in economics' most prestigious journal, showing the mechanism actually working, exactly as the theorists had feared, inside a piece of ordinary, publicly available machine-learning code that any sufficiently motivated graduate student could run on a laptop.

The 2020 paper's prominence made it an obvious target for replication, extension, and criticism, and the literature that followed is worth summarizing here rather than left for the skeptics' chapter alone, because a serious reader deserves to know that the result did not simply stand unchallenged. Other researchers, using different reinforcement-learning architectures, different market structures, and different assumptions about what the algorithms could observe, generally reproduced some version of the same qualitative pattern — a 2022 paper presented at the Association for Computing Machinery's Conference on Economics and Computation, titled bluntly "Algorithmic Pricing Facilitates Tacit Collusion," found comparable supracompetitive outcomes using its own independent experimental setup, adding to a growing cluster of studies converging on the same basic conclusion from different technical directions. But the literature also produced genuine, substantive pushback, not merely from industry-funded skeptics but from academic economists working through the same mathematics. Some follow-up papers argued that the strength of the collusive effect depended heavily on specific, somewhat artificial features of the original experimental design — the size of the "state space" the algorithms could observe, the particular way experimentation and exploitation were balanced during learning, the assumption that both algorithms used the identical Q-learning method rather than competing, more realistic architectures against each other — and that more elaborate or more realistic setups sometimes produced weaker collusive tendencies, or none at all. A paper provocatively titled "The Illusion of Collusion" argued that some published findings of algorithmic collusion may reflect artifacts of how researchers measured and interpreted their simulations rather than a robust economic phenomenon that would survive contact with a real, messy market. This book takes that pushback seriously and returns to it at length in chapter seventeen; the honest summary, at this point in the argument, is that the core finding — patient, purely self-interested pricing algorithms with no communication channel can learn strategies that sustain higher-than-competitive prices — has held up across a meaningful range of replications, while the size, reliability, and real-world applicability of the effect remain genuinely, productively contested among the economists who study it most closely.

The finding also creates a specific, practical headache for the enforcement system built around the Sherman Act, one this book's later chapters return to repeatedly. A prosecutor who wants to bring a Topkins-style criminal case needs to find an agreement — an email, a phone call, a shared understanding among human beings that a court can point to as the moment the conspiracy began. Calvano's result describes a world in which that moment may never have existed at all, because no human being ever decided to collude; a training process, running on historical data and a profit-maximization objective, simply discovered on its own that certain pricing patterns outperformed others, the way a chess engine discovers an opening its programmers never taught it. Proving intent, in the traditional legal sense, against a piece of software that has no intent in any philosophically meaningful way, pushes antitrust enforcement toward a different kind of case entirely — one built on statistical evidence of outcomes rather than documentary evidence of agreement, closer to the circumstantial "plus factor" reasoning courts have used since Interstate Circuit than to the smoking-gun standard Topkins's prosecutors were fortunate enough to have. Whether American courts, built for over a century around finding a human agreement, can comfortably make that shift is one of the central open questions this book tracks through the litigation examined in chapters nine and ten, where real courts, applying real law to real pricing software, have reached genuinely different answers within the same eighteen-month window.

It is worth sitting, for a moment, with how strange this should feel to anyone used to thinking about crime as something that requires a criminal mind. Every case examined so far in this book — Crandall's phone call, the electrical-equipment conspirators' hotel-room meetings, the NASDAQ market makers' whispered warnings against odd-eighth quotes, ATPCO's trial balloons, David Topkins's carefully coded repricing bots — involved a human being who understood, at some level, that coordination was happening and chose to let it continue. The Q-learning agents in Calvano's experiment understood nothing. They had no concept of a rival, no concept of a market, no concept of fairness, law, or wrongdoing; they were, in the most literal sense available to a piece of software, simply following a mathematical procedure that rewarded certain sequences of numbers over others, and the sequence of numbers the procedure kept discovering, over and over, across every variation the researchers threw at it, happened to look exactly like a cartel. Calvano and his coauthors were careful, in interviews and follow-up commentary after the paper's publication, to note one further wrinkle that regulators found simultaneously reassuring and unsettling: because the algorithms' collusive strategies emerged from learning rather than instruction, they were also, in principle, disruptable by changing the environment the algorithms operated in — introducing more sellers, restricting how much historical pricing data an algorithm was allowed to see, or forcing algorithms to make pricing decisions less frequently, all tended to weaken or eliminate the supracompetitive outcome in follow-up simulations, echoing Stigler's original insight that collusion lives or dies on the cost of monitoring. That finding would go on to shape actual policy: the RealPage settlement's restriction on real-time lease data and its ban on granular geographic pricing models, examined in chapter nine, reads almost like a direct, if unacknowledged, application of the lesson learned in a Bologna computer lab five years earlier — regulators reaching, independently and through an entirely different process, for the same lever the economists had identified as the one most likely to matter.

If a century of American antitrust law rests on the idea that illegal price-fixing requires a meeting of minds, Calvano's paper is the clearest evidence available that a meeting of minds may no longer be necessary to produce the outcome the law was written to prevent — that two systems with no mind to meet, and no channel through which to meet it, can arrive at the same destination anyway, guided by nothing more than patience, observation, and the quiet, mechanical logic of reward and punishment that Stigler and Axelrod had already mapped out, decades before anyone built a machine capable of following the map on its own.
