# The Meaning Shortage

A blank page used to be an obstacle.

Now it can be a button.

Ask for ten ideas and ten appear. Ask for fifty names, three strategies, a wedding toast, a lesson plan, a business concept, a bedtime story, a logo, a workout, a speech, a song, or a list of possible lives, and the machine does not become tired of suggesting.

This is an astonishing expansion of possibility.

It also exposes a problem that production scarcity used to hide.

What is worth making?

The question is older than artificial intelligence. Artists have always abandoned sketches. Companies have always killed projects. People have always had more possible careers, friendships, books, journeys, and obligations than one life can hold.

But the cost of exploring an option used to perform some selection for us.

A draft took time. A prototype took money. A photograph required a camera and a moment. A recording required equipment. A presentation required somebody to build the slides. A business plan demanded enough effort that many passing ideas died before acquiring a spreadsheet.

Production cost was a crude filter.

Artificial abundance weakens it.

That is usually good. Many worthy ideas were never explored because the person who had them lacked money, training, language fluency, confidence, software skills, artistic technique, or enough uninterrupted hours. A system that lowers these barriers can increase participation. Someone who cannot draw can still explore a visual idea. Someone who struggles with formal prose can communicate a complicated thought. Someone learning a language can work beyond the limits of a dictionary.

The gate opens.

Then the hallway fills with doors.

The meaning shortage is not a claim that society has run out of meaning. Meaning is not oil, and there is no reliable gauge showing how many barrels remain. Nor is this a claim that AI causes depression, loneliness, or purposelessness. Those are clinical and social questions that require evidence much more specific than the argument here.

The title names a different scarcity.

When competent production becomes abundant, valuation does not become abundant with it.

A model can propose one hundred projects. It cannot settle which promise you should keep.

It can draft a memorial speech. It does not supply the relationship that makes the speech matter.

It can produce wedding vows in a dozen tones. It does not make the promise.

It can generate a curriculum for a child. It does not decide what kind of adult a family hopes that child becomes.

These examples do not prove that generated language is hollow. The opposite can be true. A person may use assistance to find words for something deeply felt. The generated draft can be edited until it becomes more faithful to an intention than the person could have expressed alone.

Meaning does not require mechanical difficulty.

This is important because the previous chapters have repeatedly encountered the temptation to turn effort into virtue. If AI makes a task easier, the task does not automatically become less meaningful. Parents use washing machines without loving their children less. Musicians use electronic tuners. Scientists use software. Architects use computer-aided design. People ask friends to edit important letters.

Human life has always been assisted.

The interesting change is the scale and intimacy of the assistance.

A tool that helps execute a chosen goal is one thing. A tool that continuously proposes goals, predicts preferences, ranks options, drafts decisions, and removes friction begins to participate in the upstream process by which goals become salient.

That can be helpful too.

A person stuck in a career may benefit from seeing possibilities she had not considered. A student can explore fields that nobody in his family knows. A disabled creator can bypass a motor or sensory barrier. A small organization can imagine projects previously beyond its staff capacity.

More options can be liberating.

They can also make commitment harder.

Commitment is the strange act of making the option set smaller on purpose.

You choose one city and lose the others. You marry one person and close other futures. You spend five years learning something that may not pay. You decide this problem deserves your Saturday. You finish one book instead of generating premises for twenty.

Abundance does not perform this narrowing for us.

In fact, a system optimized to remain useful may have an incentive to keep the option set open. There is always another alternative, another refinement, another rewrite, another scenario, another recommendation.

The user can remain in a state of productive-looking possibility indefinitely.

This resembles an older digital problem. Search made information easier to find, but knowing when to stop searching remained a judgment. Streaming services made entertainment catalogs enormous, and choosing what to watch became its own small task. Online shopping made comparison easier until comparison itself became exhausting.

Generative systems move the same problem into creation.

You can keep making alternatives after the old production budget would have forced a decision.

The cost of another draft approaches zero.

The cost of choosing one remains.

This is where meaning and scarcity meet.

A thing often matters because other things were given up for it. Not always, and not because suffering sanctifies the choice. The opportunity cost is what gives commitment shape.

A person who says yes to every possible project has not created infinite purpose. He has avoided deciding which projects deserve his finite life.

Time remains the unforgiving constraint.

Artificial intelligence may help us use time better. It cannot make an individual life contain every mutually exclusive experience. Even radical improvements in health and longevity would not remove the structure of choice. Attention is finite at a moment. Places conflict. Relationships require presence. Institutions have priorities. Budgets, even large ones, encounter alternatives.

The machine can expand the menu faster than it expands the meal.

This creates a new responsibility for organizations too.

A company with cheap generation can produce far more initiatives than it can execute. Every team can create proposals, analyses, product concepts, campaigns, experiments, and internal tools. The danger is not a shortage of ideas. It is organizational indigestion.

Someone still has to say which projects receive engineers, legal review, customer attention, capital, and executive patience.

The same applies to government. Generative systems can help draft policies, analyze comments, model scenarios, and prepare legislation. Political institutions remain constrained by legitimacy, coalition, implementation capacity, budgets, and the need to decide among conflicting values.

More policy text is not more government capacity.

A school can generate infinite assignments. A student cannot complete infinite assignments. A media company can generate infinite articles. Readers cannot attend to infinite articles. A game can generate infinite quests. A player still has one evening.

Abundant supply transfers pressure to selection.

This can produce a peculiar form of waste.

In the industrial economy, waste was often visible as excess inventory. Warehouses filled with goods nobody bought. Unsold newspapers were pulped. Overstock was discounted.

Generated cognitive waste can be almost invisible.

A company can create thousands of documents that nobody reads. An employee can produce reports because reports are easy to produce. A system can generate options that consume more human review time than they save. A school can drown teachers in individualized materials. A government can create explanations faster than citizens can determine which one is authoritative.

The marginal artifact is cheap enough that nobody feels the cost of making it.

The cost appears downstream as attention.

This is why the meaning shortage connects directly to the book's earlier chapters on judgment and attention. Meaning is partly the act of assigning weight. This matters more than that. This obligation outranks that opportunity. This person deserves my presence. This project is worth finishing even though another idea now looks shinier.

A model can assist these judgments. It can remind us of stated priorities. It can reveal inconsistencies. It can simulate consequences. It can ask questions we forgot to ask.

But assistance does not eliminate the normative part.

If you tell a model to maximize profit, it can help search for actions consistent with that objective. It does not establish that profit should be the objective. If you tell it to optimize health, it still needs a definition of health and tradeoffs among longevity, comfort, autonomy, cost, and risk. If you ask for the best school, it needs to know what best means for this child and family.

Objectives are compressed values.

The compression hides arguments.

This is one reason apparently technical systems can become political so quickly. A ranking system looks like computation after someone has decided what deserves to rank. An optimization system looks neutral after someone has chosen the objective. A recommendation system looks helpful after someone has decided which outcomes count as success.

Artificial abundance increases the supply of means.

It does not settle the ends.

That may sound philosophical, but it has ordinary consequences.

A writer who can generate a chapter in minutes still has to decide whether the chapter belongs in the book. A manager who can create a strategy deck before breakfast still has to decide whether the strategy deserves years of employees' work. A teenager who can receive detailed plans for twenty careers still has to live one sequence of Tuesdays.

The hard part migrates from possibility toward commitment.

There is counterevidence worth taking seriously. Assistance may not merely create more options; it can help people clarify what they care about. A conversational system can make reflection accessible to someone who would never hire a coach or counselor. A creative tool can let a person discover an interest through making rather than through abstract choice. A translator can let someone participate in a community that gives life more meaning, not less.

The technology can widen the space in which meaning is found.

If that becomes the dominant experience, then the shortage metaphor should shrink accordingly.

The durable claim is not that artificial intelligence drains meaning from work or art. It is that abundance removes one old excuse for avoiding the question of value.

When making the thing was difficult, finishing it could feel like sufficient justification.

When making becomes easy, we have to answer the less comfortable question.

Why this thing?
