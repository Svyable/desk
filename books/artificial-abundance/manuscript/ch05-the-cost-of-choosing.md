# The Cost of Choosing

A menu can become worse by getting longer.

This sounds strange because more options are usually described as a benefit. If a restaurant adds dishes without removing any, nobody has lost the old choices. If a streaming service adds another thousand films, the catalog is objectively larger. If a model can generate twenty plausible plans instead of one, the decision maker has received more information.

And yet the experience can deteriorate.

You open the menu and stop reading halfway down.

You scroll past fifty films and watch nothing.

You ask for twenty strategies, receive twenty polished strategies, and discover that the work you were trying to avoid has returned in a new form.

Now you have to choose.

Abundance reduces the cost of producing options. It does not necessarily reduce the cost of deciding among them.

Sometimes it raises it.

This is the next constraint after taste.

Taste helps distinguish among plausible alternatives. Choosing requires something more uncomfortable: commitment under uncertainty. You have to stop exploring the possibility space and accept the opportunity cost of the paths you did not take.

Machines are extremely good at postponing that moment.

Ask for another version and another version appears.

Ask whether there is a better option and the system can usually produce one that sounds better in some dimension.

Ask for objections and it will generate objections.

Ask for responses to the objections and it will generate those too.

The conversation can continue indefinitely because generation no longer supplies a natural stopping point.

Human production used to create many stopping points accidentally.

A designer had time for three concepts before the meeting.

A lawyer could examine four structures before the filing deadline.

A founder could interview ten customers before the money ran low.

A family chose among the houses that happened to be available during the month they needed to move.

The constraints were frustrating, but they forced decisions.

Some of those constraints are weakening.

A person with an intelligent system can continue searching after a human team would once have run out of hours. The model can generate another scenario, another comparison, another simulation, another draft, another forecast, another set of names, another counterargument.

This increases the chance of finding something better.

It also increases the chance of never becoming satisfied.

There is a difference between a search that has not yet found a good answer and a search that has lost the ability to stop.

The second problem is easier to create when the marginal cost of another attempt approaches zero.

Consider travel.

For most of history, a traveler did not compare every possible lodging option in a city. The search space was constrained by guidebooks, travel agents, personal recommendations, visible signs, geography, and simple ignorance. Online booking expanded the field dramatically. Now a traveler can filter hundreds of hotels by neighborhood, price, rating, room size, amenities, cancellation policy, photos, loyalty status, breakfast, view, parking, and review language.

The result is better matching.

It is also a small administrative job.

The search is useful up to the point where another thirty minutes of comparison produces less value than thirty minutes spent almost anywhere else.

The difficulty is that the interface rarely announces when that point has arrived.

Artificial intelligence can make this problem general.

A person can compare more products before buying.

A company can evaluate more vendors before signing.

A student can ask for more possible essay topics.

A scientist can generate more hypotheses.

An investor can request more scenarios.

A manager can examine more organizational designs.

A programmer can ask for more architectures.

A writer can see more possible openings before choosing one.

The old bottleneck was often access to alternatives.

The new bottleneck is the rule for ending the search.

Economics has a language for this even when ordinary life does not. Search has a cost. Information has value only when it changes a decision enough to justify obtaining and processing it. There is no reason to inspect one more option when the expected improvement is smaller than the cost of inspection.

George Stigler made this point explicit in 1961 in a paper about something much more ordinary than artificial intelligence: learning market prices. Standard economic models often behaved as if buyers simply knew the prices available to them. Stigler treated ignorance as an economic condition and search as an activity that consumes resources. Looking for another price can save money. Looking forever would cost more than the saving.

The paper did not solve the modern problem of a machine capable of producing alternatives on demand. It established the part that abundance does not repeal.

Information is not free merely because the next page is free to generate.

Someone still has to acquire, interpret, compare, and act on it.

A few years earlier, Herbert Simon had attacked the same idealized decision maker from another direction. Real people and organizations do not possess unlimited information or unlimited capacity to calculate. They simplify. They use aspiration levels, routines, rules, and partial representations of the world because exhaustive optimization is not available to them.

This is sometimes summarized as satisficing: finding an option that clears a threshold rather than proving that no better option exists anywhere.

The word can sound like an excuse for mediocrity.

It is often a description of finite life.

A hospital choosing supplies, a family choosing a home, a hiring manager choosing a candidate, and an executive deciding whether to launch a product are all making decisions under limits. The limits are not defects in intelligence. Some are the calendar. Some are the cost of delay. Some are the fact that information arrives after the decision as well as before it.

Artificial intelligence can relax one set of limits without eliminating the need for limits.

That is the important transition.

A system can search more vendors, summarize more reviews, model more scenarios, and compare more attributes than a person could manage unaided. It can improve the quality of a decision precisely by spending cheap computation where human search once stopped too early.

The rational response is not to distrust the search.

It is to notice that the stopping condition has moved.

Before, the meeting ended because nobody had another analysis.

Now the meeting may need to end because another analysis is unlikely to change the action.

Those are very different reasons to stop.

The first is a production constraint.

The second is a judgment.

That sentence is easier to write than to live.

The cost of inspection is not always visible as money.

It can be attention.

It can be delay.

It can be anxiety.

It can be the loss of momentum while an organization keeps discussing options that all clear the bar.

It can be the degradation of responsibility when everyone continues asking for more analysis because nobody wants to own the decision.

Cheap analysis can become an elegant form of procrastination.

A board requests another model.

A government requests another study.

A team asks for another round of scenarios.

An individual asks a system to compare the alternatives one more time.

The extra work looks responsible because information is being produced. The real question is whether the information has any realistic chance of changing the choice.

This is where abundance can make institutions slower even while making analysis faster.

If producing a memo once took two weeks, a request for another memo carried an obvious cost. People hesitated before asking. If a system can produce a plausible memo in two minutes, the request becomes easy.

But the receiver still has to read it.

The committee still has to interpret it.

The organization still has to reconcile it with the other memos.

The decision still has consequences.

Generation time can collapse while governance time expands.

The important cost may therefore move across the organizational chart rather than disappear. The person requesting another analysis experiences almost no marginal production cost. The people who must read, reconcile, approve, defend, and implement it pay the rest. Cheap generation can make a request privately rational while making the decision process collectively worse.

That is a cost-incidence problem. The machine lowers the price faced by the sender without necessarily lowering the price faced by the institution.

Once that happens, organizations need some way to make the downstream burden visible. Otherwise the easiest person to satisfy—the requester—can keep creating work for the scarcest people in the loop.

There is another cost that cheap analysis can hide: decay.

Some decisions become worse while they wait.

A supplier's capacity disappears. A candidate accepts another job. A market moves. A customer loses patience. A political coalition changes. The team that was excited about the project gets reassigned. An option that was available when the first analysis began may no longer be available when the twentieth analysis ends.

The search itself changes the decision environment.

This matters because generated analysis can create the illusion that the decision is stationary. The model can keep comparing the same alternatives while the world outside the document continues moving.

A stopping rule is therefore not only a defense against cognitive overload.

It is sometimes a way of protecting the value of acting while the opportunity exists.

This is not hypothetical in structure even if every institution will experience it differently. We already know what happens when communication becomes cheap. Email made it easy to send a message to a large group. It did not make the group's collective attention free. The ability to copy another person onto a thread created value and enormous ambient work at the same time.

Artificial intelligence can do something similar to analysis.

Every decision can acquire an entourage of machine-generated considerations.

The danger is not only overload.

It is false precision.

When a model can produce a table comparing twelve options across twenty dimensions, the format itself can create a feeling of rigor. A score appears. Cells fill. Tradeoffs become numbers. The document looks as if the choice has been transformed into calculation.

Sometimes that is useful.

Sometimes the decisive question is not in the table.

Will the team actually use the system?

Will the supplier behave well when something goes wrong?

Does this strategy fit the company's appetite for risk?

Would we still want this if the forecast were twenty percent worse?

Which downside are we willing to explain publicly?

What kind of organization are we trying to become?

The existence of more measurable dimensions can distract from the unmeasured judgment that controls the outcome.

Abundance therefore increases the value of decision rules.

Not universal rules. Local ones.

A company may decide that new internal software must save a minimum amount of recurring labor before it deserves integration work. A household may decide that once three options meet its needs, it will choose among them rather than continue searching for an ideal. A research team may define in advance what evidence would justify abandoning a hypothesis. An investor may establish what would make a promising idea uninvestable before falling in love with the story.

The point is not bureaucracy.

It is to move part of the choice upstream, before the option generator begins seducing you.

The strongest stopping rules are often tied to consequences rather than to a fixed number of options.

Stop when every surviving option meets the requirements and the differences no longer justify delay.

Stop when the next piece of information would not change the action.

Stop when the cost of reversibility is low enough that trying one option will teach more than another hour of comparison.

Stop when the irreversible downside has been investigated enough to fit inside the risk the institution already agreed to bear.

These are not mathematical laws. They are ways of reconnecting search to the purpose of the decision.

That reconnection matters because abundant systems are persuasive even when they are merely responsive.

A model asked to make the case for one option can make the case.

Asked to make the case against it, the model can do that too.

The user may experience this flexibility as intelligence. It is intelligence of a sort. But it also means the system often reflects the direction of inquiry. A person who keeps searching for reasons to prefer an option may eventually receive a very good reason.

The search process is not neutral.

Neither is the stopping rule.

A useful decision system therefore has to protect against the temptation to confuse more reasoning with better reasoning.

This is especially important when the alternatives themselves are generated.

Suppose a company asks a model for one hundred product ideas and then asks another model to rank them. It has automated generation and part of selection. That can be sensible. But the ranking still depends on criteria, data, assumptions, and a model of the future.

If the criteria reward addressable market, the list will favor scale.

If they reward strategic fit, it will favor proximity to the current business.

If they reward novelty, it may favor ideas the company is least equipped to execute.

If they reward predicted adoption, they may reproduce the patterns of historical customers and underweight changes that create a new customer.

The ranking system does not remove judgment.

It embeds judgment.

Once embedded, the judgment can become harder to see because it arrives as a sorted list.

There is a promising counterforce here.

The same systems that create more alternatives can reduce the cost of choosing among them. They can cluster duplicates, identify dominated options, expose assumptions, run tests, search for disconfirming evidence, and learn a user's stable preferences. A good system may shrink a thousand possibilities into three genuinely different choices before the person ever sees the list.

If selection technology improves as quickly as generation technology, the choice bottleneck may move again.

That possibility should remain alive.

The argument of this chapter is not that humans will be buried forever beneath machine options. It is that abundance removes an old physical and cognitive reason for stopping, so some other stopping mechanism has to take its place.

It can be automated.

It can be institutional.

It can be personal.

It can be a test.

But it has to exist somewhere.

This is why choosing cannot be reduced to generating enough options and applying a score.

A choice is a decision about what will matter after the spreadsheet ends.

The scarcer the production constraint becomes, the more important this becomes.

Imagine an architecture firm that can generate thousands of building variants before breakfast. The software can optimize for daylight, cost, energy use, structural efficiency, floor area, views, pedestrian flow, material quantity, and many other variables.

That is extraordinary capability.

It does not answer whether the building should make a street feel sheltered or monumental.

It does not answer how much inconvenience during construction is acceptable to the neighborhood.

It does not answer whether the cheapest maintenance regime will age beautifully or merely survive.

It does not answer which values deserve priority when every improvement creates another cost.

Optimization is not the same thing as choice because a choice includes the objective.

When option generation was expensive, this distinction could remain hidden. People spent so much time getting to a feasible answer that feasibility itself carried authority.

If only one design survived the budget and schedule, that design acquired momentum.

If only one candidate had the required expertise, hiring became easier.

If only one supplier could deliver, procurement became simple.

Abundance removes some of those accidental authorities.

That is liberating.

It is also destabilizing.

More situations become genuinely discretionary.

Someone has to say what they want.

This sounds trivial until you ask a group of people.

Organizations are often better at rejecting the impossible than choosing among several possible futures. Scarcity hides disagreements because the constraint decides for everyone. When the constraint loosens, values that were previously irrelevant become operational.

If software makes it cheap to personalize a service, should every customer receive a different experience?

If a school can generate a different lesson for every child, how much common curriculum should remain?

If a city can simulate hundreds of transport plans, what should it optimize: travel time, equity, emissions, tax cost, resilience, neighborhood continuity, economic growth?

If a company can build ten product variants, is serving ten micro-markets better than building one thing everyone understands?

Abundance creates choices that scarcity prevented us from having to make.

That may be one of its largest social effects.

A civilization built around shortage becomes skilled at allocation.

Who gets the hospital bed?

Who gets the broadcast slot?

Who gets the expert's hour?

Who gets the scholarship?

Who gets to publish?

Who gets the machine time?

A civilization built around artificial abundance faces another class of questions.

Which possibilities deserve to exist?

Which recommendations deserve to be followed?

Which personalization is helpful and which destroys common ground?

Which generated work deserves maintenance?

Which answer deserves responsibility?

Which option is good enough that we can stop searching and begin living with it?

The final question may become a professional skill.

There is a kind of competence in recognizing when the decision has crossed from information-poor to information-rich.

At the beginning, another question can reduce ignorance.

Later, another question merely delays ownership.

The transition is difficult to detect because the system remains productive. Words keep arriving. Charts keep improving. Alternatives keep appearing. Nothing announces that the useful part of thinking is over.

A mature user of abundant intelligence may therefore be distinguished less by how much the system can produce for them than by how quickly they can tell when production should stop.

The scarce resource is no longer the next option.

It is the confidence to close the menu.
