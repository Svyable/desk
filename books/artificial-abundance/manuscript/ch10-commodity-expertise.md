# Commodity Expertise

Expertise has always had two prices.

There is the price of knowing something, and there is the price of getting the benefit of someone who knows it.

The first can take years. The second might take an hour.

A junior programmer spends years encountering bad abstractions, race conditions, misleading error messages, awkward APIs, failed deployments, and code that looked clever until somebody had to maintain it. A customer-service representative learns which problem the customer is actually describing, which policy has an exception, and which apparently simple request is about to become expensive. A lawyer learns not only rules but where a plausible argument breaks when another lawyer attacks it.

Then a generative system arrives with an enormous inventory of patterns and can produce a competent-looking answer in seconds.

It is tempting to say that expertise has become a commodity.

That goes too far.

What becomes commodity-like first is not the expert. It is the repeatable manifestation of expertise.

The distinction is visible in software development. Three randomized field experiments involving 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture, and another large company found that access to an AI coding assistant increased completed tasks by a pooled 26.08 percent. The gains were larger among less experienced developers, who also adopted the tool more heavily.

This is exactly the kind of result that can be abused by a headline.

It does not mean a novice became a senior engineer. It does not mean one quarter of programmers became unnecessary. It does not establish the same effect in every language, company, codebase, or type of engineering work. Completed tasks are not the whole of software quality, architecture, security, maintenance, or product judgment.

But the result is economically important even after all those qualifications.

A portion of what experience used to buy became available through a tool.

The novice no longer had to remember the syntax, locate the example, reconstruct the boilerplate, or spend as long turning a familiar intention into working code. Patterns that once had to be carried in a person's memory could be summoned at the moment of use.

Customer support shows a related pattern. In a large study of a generative assistant used by support agents, less experienced and lower-skilled workers improved more. The most experienced workers gained much less, and on some measures their quality moved slightly in the wrong direction even as speed improved.

Again, the interesting story is not replacement. It is compression.

If the gap between a novice's routine output and an experienced worker's routine output narrows, the market value of that gap can narrow with it.

This has happened before.

Spreadsheets did not eliminate accountants, but they destroyed the scarcity of many calculations that once required clerical labor. Search engines did not eliminate researchers, but they reduced the value of remembering where a commonly available fact was stored. Digital cameras did not eliminate photographers, but they removed much of the technical scarcity involved in producing a correctly exposed image. Website builders did not eliminate designers or engineers, but they made a competent brochure site available to businesses that would never have commissioned one.

The pattern is not that a profession vanishes when one of its outputs becomes cheap.

The profession moves uphill.

The difficult question is what waits uphill.

For some experts, the answer is better work. If routine production consumes less time, the experienced programmer can spend more time on architecture, security, debugging unusual failures, understanding users, and making tradeoffs that cannot be resolved by generating another block of code. A lawyer can spend less time producing first drafts and more time on strategy, negotiation, verification, and the facts that make this client different. A doctor can spend less time composing routine documentation and more time examining the patient.

That is the complementarity story, and it is real enough to deserve more than ritual acknowledgment.

There is another story beside it.

Professions do not manufacture experts by hiring experts.

They manufacture experts by giving novices work.

A junior lawyer becomes useful partly by doing tasks that a senior lawyer could do faster. A new programmer learns a codebase by fixing small bugs, writing tests, reading old functions, and making mistakes under supervision. A young analyst builds intuition by constructing the model, not merely by reading the conclusion. An apprentice learns the material because the material resists.

The inefficiency is part of the education.

This creates a peculiar danger when AI improves novice output most.

The firm has an obvious incentive to give the novice the tool. The work gets done faster. The customer receives a better answer. The manager sees fewer errors. The novice may feel more capable and may in fact be more capable while the system is present.

But what exactly is being learned?

Research in education gives a warning without providing a direct answer for professional labor. In a randomized study involving high-school mathematics, students using a general-purpose AI tutor performed better while they had access to it, yet those using an unconstrained version performed worse later when the tool was removed. A more carefully designed tutoring system avoided much of that harm.

A classroom is not a law firm or an engineering team. Teenagers learning mathematics are not professionals learning a codebase. The experiment should not be stretched across that distance.

The mechanism, however, is worth taking seriously: assisted performance and acquired capability are not the same variable.

A person can produce a better answer because the environment has become smarter without becoming correspondingly smarter herself.

This was always true of tools. Nobody complains that a carpenter has failed to internalize the function of a power saw. We do not require an accountant to perform long division to prove moral seriousness. Civilization advances partly by allowing people to stand on accumulated technique instead of reenacting it.

The problem arises when the supposedly obsolete task was carrying information needed for the next level of judgment.

If you never debug the small failures, do you recognize the large one?

If you never draft the ordinary contract, do you notice the extraordinary clause?

If you never conduct the routine interview, do you know when a witness's answer is strange?

If you never build the model, do you know which assumption is doing all the work?

There is no universal answer because professions hide their tacit curriculum inside different tasks.

Some apprenticeship work is genuinely wasteful. It persists because senior people dislike doing it and junior labor is cheap. Automating it is not a cultural tragedy.

Some apprenticeship work is disguised training. Removing it may produce a generation that is excellent at supervising systems it does not understand until the day the system encounters something unusual.

This makes expertise more complicated, not less valuable.

The old expert often sold production bundled with judgment. The client paid for the memorandum and, invisibly, for the years that made the memorandum reliable. The software company paid for code and, invisibly, for the engineer's ability to know when the obvious implementation would fail. The hospital paid for a diagnosis and, invisibly, for pattern recognition formed across thousands of cases.

Artificial abundance begins unbundling those things.

Production gets cheaper first because production is what generative systems expose directly. The client can see the draft arrive. The manager can see the code compile. The customer can see the answer appear.

Judgment is harder to price because good judgment often looks like nothing happened.

The expert declined to file the weak claim. The engineer rejected the elegant architecture that would have been impossible to operate. The doctor ordered one more test because the ordinary explanation did not fit. The editor removed the paragraph that would have embarrassed the author six months later.

When generation is scarce, expertise can hide inside output.

When generation is abundant, expertise has to become more legible.

That may be uncomfortable for professions that have long used production difficulty as evidence of value. If a client can generate a plausible first draft alone, saying that the professional version took twelve hours will not restore the old price. The professional has to explain what was noticed, verified, rejected, insured, interpreted, or taken responsibility for.

Some experts will hate this because it feels like commodification.

Some of it is commodification.

A routine deliverable whose value came mainly from knowing the standard pattern is vulnerable when the pattern becomes cheap. The fact that producing it once required training does not preserve its scarcity after a machine can reproduce much of the result.

But expertise is larger than its most repeatable outputs.

The better way to think about the transition is that artificial abundance strips routine production away from expertise and asks what remains valuable when the draft is free.

Sometimes the answer will be surprisingly little.

Sometimes it will be the entire reason the expert mattered.

And the difference between those cases will not be settled by how impressive the generated artifact looks. It will be settled when somebody has to decide whether the artifact can be trusted, whether an exception matters, and who answers for the consequences.

That last question is where cheap expertise meets an expensive institution.

Responsibility.
