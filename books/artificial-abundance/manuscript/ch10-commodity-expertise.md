# Commodity Expertise

Expertise has always contained two different things.

Knowing something difficult.

And being one of the relatively few people who know it.

The first is intellectual.

The second is economic.

For a long time, the two traveled together.

If a body of knowledge took years to acquire, access to that knowledge was naturally scarce. A competent tax lawyer, radiologist, structural engineer, software architect, translator, actuary, chemist, accountant, or specialist technician represented a long investment in education and practice. The difficulty of learning the field limited supply.

That scarcity helped support professional pricing.

It also helped create status.

Artificial intelligence can separate knowledge from the person who spent years acquiring it.

Not perfectly. Not uniformly. Not with the reliability required for every decision. But enough to change the economics of many ordinary questions.

A person can ask a system to explain a contract clause, debug a program, compare financial statements, translate technical language, suggest a differential diagnosis, outline a compliance issue, interpret an unfamiliar standard, or walk through the logic of a statistical method.

The answer may be incomplete or wrong.

So can the answer from a person.

The important economic change is not that machine expertise becomes infallible.

It is that a plausible first pass becomes available to almost anyone at negligible marginal cost.

That makes a great deal of expertise feel less like a scarce appointment and more like an ambient utility.

You do not schedule electricity.

You do not retain a specialist to tell you what temperature your thermostat reports.

You reach for the capability when needed.

Some professional knowledge is moving in that direction.

This can sound insulting to experts because the word commodity suggests sameness and low value.

But commodities can be extraordinarily valuable.

Clean water is valuable. Electricity is valuable. Compute is valuable. Search is valuable. Basic arithmetic is valuable.

Commodity does not mean useless.

It means broadly available enough that possession of the capability by itself creates less differentiation.

That distinction matters.

When calculators became cheap, arithmetic remained useful. Being able to perform routine arithmetic manually became less valuable in many settings because the capability could be called from a device.

When search engines made information retrieval cheap, factual recall remained useful. But being the person in the room who knew where to find an ordinary fact became less special.

When spelling and grammar tools improved, correct mechanics did not stop mattering. The market simply became less willing to pay a premium for mechanics alone.

Artificial intelligence extends this pattern into more complex cognitive territory.

The first consequence is obvious.

Routine questions lose pricing power.

A consumer who once paid a professional for a basic explanation may begin with a machine. A junior employee who once interrupted a senior colleague may ask a model first. A small company that could not justify outside counsel for every low-stakes question may generate an initial analysis before deciding whether the issue deserves paid legal work. A programmer may ask an agent to inspect a bug before asking another engineer.

The expert enters later.

That changes the shape of professional demand.

The easy work can shrink while the difficult work becomes a larger share of what reaches humans.

This sounds like an upgrade.

Professionals get to spend less time on repetitive questions and more time on the matters that truly require judgment.

Sometimes that will be true.

It can also make the job harder.

A doctor whose routine cases are handled elsewhere sees a higher concentration of ambiguous cases.

A lawyer who loses straightforward drafting work receives more disputes, exceptions, and high-stakes judgment calls.

A support engineer may see only the failures the automated system could not solve.

A teacher may spend less time answering ordinary questions and more time with students who are confused in ways the tutoring system did not resolve.

The remaining human workload can become more cognitively intense even as total automation rises.

The profession is left with the tail.

The tail may be where the value is.

It may also be where the stress is.

This is one reason productivity gains do not automatically translate into easier jobs.

Automation often removes the predictable parts first.

The worker becomes an exception handler.

In an abundance economy, expert labor may increasingly mean dealing with what cannot be standardized, cheaply verified, or confidently delegated.

That changes training.

Today many professions teach by moving people through a ladder of increasingly difficult work. The beginner performs routine tasks under supervision. The routine tasks create repetitions. Repetition builds pattern recognition. Pattern recognition supports judgment.

If routine work becomes machine territory, the profession faces an awkward question.

How does the beginner become the expert who handles exceptions if the beginner no longer spends years handling the ordinary cases?

This problem is not unique to AI.

Pilots train extensively even though autopilot handles much of routine flight. Surgeons use simulation. Military organizations rehearse rare failures. Nuclear operators train for events they hope never occur. Apprentices practice controlled versions of work before being trusted with consequences.

Cognitive professions may need the same deliberate approach.

Instead of learning merely because the organization needed cheap junior labor, people may need designed practice environments.

Simulated cases.

Adversarial examples.

Supervised machine-assisted work.

Exercises where the model is intentionally wrong.

Rotations that preserve contact with ordinary cases even when automation could handle them.

A profession that automates its apprenticeship without replacing it will eventually automate its supply of experts.

The next consequence is less obvious.

Credentials change meaning.

A degree or license historically signals several things at once.

The person studied the field.

The person passed certain tests.

The person belongs to an institution with rules.

The person may have supervised experience.

The person can legally perform certain acts.

And, implicitly, the person has access to knowledge that outsiders generally do not.

Artificial abundance weakens the last signal first.

A non-lawyer can read an intelligent explanation of a legal concept. A non-programmer can generate working code. A patient can ask sophisticated questions about medical research. A small-business owner can receive accounting guidance in plain language. A student can have a conversation at a level once available only by finding the right teacher.

This does not make the outsider equivalent to the professional.

It changes what the credential must prove.

The scarce component moves away from mere access to information.

Toward responsibility.

Toward supervised experience.

Toward the ability to recognize when the apparent answer is wrong.

Toward authority to act.

Toward ethical obligations.

Toward judgment under uncertainty.

Toward the ability to explain and defend a decision after something goes badly.

The strongest professions already understand this.

A medical license does not mean the physician remembers every fact without assistance. It means the physician has passed through training, examination, supervision, and professional obligations that justify giving the person authority over consequential care.

A pilot is not valuable because no machine can hold altitude. The pilot is valuable partly because somebody has to understand the system well enough to manage conditions outside the normal envelope and carry responsibility for the flight.

A structural engineer is not valuable because software cannot calculate loads. Software has done calculations for decades. The engineer is valuable because a real structure has to be designed, reviewed, signed, built, inspected, and ultimately trusted with human weight.

Commodity expertise exposes the difference between knowing and standing behind what is known.

That creates a premium for professionals who can integrate machine capability without hiding behind it.

A weak professional uses AI to produce more impressive-looking work.

A strong professional uses AI to widen the search, test assumptions, expose edge cases, accelerate routine analysis, and still takes ownership of the final judgment.

The distinction becomes visible when the answer is challenged.

Why did you recommend this?

What evidence did you rely on?

What alternatives did you consider?

What would change your mind?

Which parts came from a machine?

What did you verify yourself?

What happens if this fails?

If the professional cannot answer, the machine did not make the professional more expert.

It made the weakness harder to see.

There is also a business-model consequence.

Professional services often bill for time partly because time was the scarce input.

A lawyer researches for ten hours.

A consultant builds a deck over two weeks.

A designer develops options across several rounds.

A programmer spends days implementing a feature.

When production time falls, billing time becomes a less stable proxy for value.

The customer may not care that the work took six minutes if the answer saves six million dollars.

The customer may also refuse to pay the old fee for a routine product that can now be generated cheaply.

These forces push in opposite directions.

Commodity outputs should become cheaper.

Consequential judgment may become more valuable.

The profession has to separate them.

This is difficult because firms often made excellent margins on routine work precisely because clients could not easily distinguish how hard the work really was.

Artificial intelligence makes effort less visible and alternatives more available.

That can destroy comfortable pricing.

It can also create better forms of value-based work.

A professional may charge for an outcome, a decision, a continuing relationship, a warranty, availability, or risk transferred rather than pages produced.

The most valuable service may become, “I will take responsibility for this category of problem,” rather than, “I will spend twelve hours making the document.”

That shift will be uneven and contested.

Regulation, ethics rules, liability, procurement systems, and customer habits all preserve old pricing structures. Some fields cannot simply move to outcome fees. Some outcomes are impossible to attribute cleanly.

Still, the direction is visible.

When the artifact becomes cheap, the contract around the artifact matters more.

There is another way experts can remain valuable.

They can own context the general system does not have.

A model may know medicine broadly. The physician knows this patient.

A model may know software broadly. The engineer knows why this particular codebase contains three ugly exceptions that cannot be removed without breaking a customer workflow nobody documented.

A model may know employment law broadly. The lawyer knows the history of the dispute, the personalities involved, the risk tolerance of the client, and which compromise is actually possible.

A model may know restaurants broadly. The chef knows tonight's staff, the supplier who failed to deliver, the oven that runs hot, and the regular at table twelve.

Context is not mystical.

It is accumulated local information.

Some of it can be digitized.

Some of it will be.

As organizations become better at capturing their own context, models will gain access to more of what used to live only in experienced employees' heads.

That will commoditize another layer.

Then the value moves again.

This is why “become more expert” is not enough advice for a world of abundant intelligence.

Expertise itself contains layers.

Some layers are facts.

Some are procedures.

Some are pattern recognition.

Some are relationships.

Some are authority.

Some are tacit knowledge.

Some are accountability.

Some are taste.

Some are the ability to act when the evidence is incomplete and every option has a cost.

The layers exposed to cheap generation will lose scarcity fastest.

The others may become more visible and more valuable.

This does not guarantee that experts keep their incomes or status.

A technology can make society richer while reducing what the market pays a particular profession. If a thousand people can do with assistance what once required a scarce specialist, the specialist may lose pricing power even if the specialist remains better.

Better is not the same as scarce.

That sentence is uncomfortable because many professional identities are built around merit.

I worked for years to become good at this.

Surely being good at it should remain valuable.

Markets do not compensate effort retroactively.

They compensate scarcity, demand, bargaining power, risk, and ownership among other things.

A capability can remain difficult to acquire personally while becoming easy to access externally.

The expert then competes not against another expert but against the customer's ability to rent expertise on demand.

The response cannot be to protect every old task from automation.

That would preserve scarcity by force and sacrifice the benefits of abundance.

The better response is to identify what the professional relationship is for after information and routine production become cheap.

For some fields, the answer will be verification.

For others, responsibility.

For others, empathy, negotiation, physical presence, or trust.

For others, the profession may genuinely shrink.

There is no law of nature requiring every existing occupation to survive the technology that changes its input costs.

The more durable principle is that people will still need help acting in a complicated world.

Cheap expertise increases the number of possible answers.

It does not remove consequences.

The person who can connect knowledge to a real decision, in a real context, under real responsibility, still has something scarce.

But the knowledge alone is becoming a weaker moat.

The library has left the building.

Now it answers back.
