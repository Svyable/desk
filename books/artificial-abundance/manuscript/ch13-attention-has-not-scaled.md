# Attention Has Not Scaled

The day still has twenty-four hours.

This is an embarrassingly simple fact to place near the center of a book about artificial intelligence.

It may also be the most important constraint in the book.

Production can scale.

Storage can scale.

Distribution can scale.

Computation can scale.

An intelligent system can run while you sleep, generate a thousand options, read a thousand pages, watch a thousand signals, and return with a summary before breakfast.

You still have one breakfast.

Human attention is not merely another resource waiting for an engineering breakthrough.

It is attached to a body moving through time.

A person can become more efficient at reading, listening, deciding, and switching tasks. Tools can compress information. Assistants can filter. Better interfaces can reduce friction. Drugs, sleep, training, and environment can change performance around the edges.

But the fundamental budget remains finite.

You cannot care deeply about a million things today.

You cannot form a close relationship with a million people.

You cannot make a thousand consequential decisions with equal seriousness.

You cannot experience every excellent book, film, game, restaurant, landscape, conversation, and idea that already exists.

Artificial abundance adds supply to a world that was already beyond human consumption.

That creates an unusual economic problem.

The output can become cheaper while the cost of receiving the output rises.

A five-page memo may be free to generate and expensive to read.

A hundred software prototypes may be cheap to build and expensive to evaluate.

A thousand personalized recommendations may be cheap to produce and psychologically exhausting to consider.

A company may save labor in creating reports while losing executive attention to an expanding mountain of reports.

The production system experiences abundance.

The receiver experiences a queue.

This is why one of the worst uses of cheap generation is making more things for other people to process.

The sender saves ten minutes.

The recipients collectively lose an hour.

An employee asks a system to produce a detailed update because producing the update is easy. Ten colleagues receive it. Each colleague has to decide whether the detail matters.

A salesperson generates highly customized outreach to thousands of people. The personalization improves the message. The recipients still have to spend attention recognizing whether the offer deserves a response.

A student submits a forty-page report because the system can produce forty polished pages. The teacher still has one pair of eyes.

A consultant creates twenty strategic options because twenty is now possible. The client did not gain nineteen extra boards of directors.

Artificial abundance can shift cost from producer to consumer.

This is not new.

Spam has always worked this way.

The sender's cost is low, so enormous volume becomes rational even when the response rate is tiny. Most of the cost is imposed on recipients, networks, filters, and institutions defending against the volume.

Generative systems improve the economics of spam because they reduce not only the cost of sending but the cost of making each message look individually relevant.

The old spam message announced itself through repetition.

The new one can know your name, job, company, recent work, interests, and vocabulary.

Personalization no longer proves that attention was spent on you.

This turns attention defense into infrastructure.

Email filters were an early version.

Recommendation systems are another.

Search engines rank abundance.

News feeds rank abundance.

App stores rank abundance.

Managers rank requests.

Assistants rank calendars.

Friends rank social invitations.

Every abundant system eventually creates a filtering layer because humans cannot consume the supply directly.

Artificial intelligence will make the filtering layer more intelligent too.

This is necessary.

It is also powerful.

The system that decides what deserves your attention gains influence over your life.

If an assistant reads your messages and decides which three matter, the assistant is not merely saving time. It is allocating awareness.

If a system condenses a hundred-page report into five bullets, it decides which distinctions survive compression.

If a news agent scans the world and gives you ten developments, it is exercising editorial power whether or not the product calls itself an editor.

If a work agent decides which exceptions require your review, it shapes your understanding of the process you nominally supervise.

Filtering is governance at the edge of consciousness.

This is why attention tools cannot be evaluated only by how much time they save.

A filter can save time by hiding the wrong thing.

A summary can be efficient because it removes ambiguity that mattered.

A recommendation system can feel helpful because it narrows the world around preferences you already have.

A calendar agent can optimize your schedule and accidentally eliminate the unstructured time in which you notice what the schedule is doing to you.

Efficiency is not a complete objective for attention.

The deeper question is what your attention is for.

This sounds philosophical because it is.

Attention is how a finite person turns possibility into life.

What you repeatedly notice becomes what you know.

What you repeatedly practice becomes what you can do.

Who you repeatedly attend to becomes part of your relationships.

Which problems you attend to becomes part of your work.

What you ignore does not disappear from the world, but it often disappears from your lived reality.

A technology that reallocates attention is therefore not neutral even when every recommendation is individually reasonable.

The allocation accumulates.

This helps explain why abundance can create a strange feeling of poverty.

A person can have access to more knowledge, entertainment, advice, communication, and opportunity than anyone in history and still feel behind.

The supply grows faster than the capacity to absorb it.

Unread books multiply.

Saved articles multiply.

Courses multiply.

Messages multiply.

Podcasts multiply.

Videos multiply.

Opportunities multiply.

The abundance becomes evidence of what you are not consuming.

Every choice implies an expanding set of foregone alternatives.

This is not only a psychological problem. It changes markets.

When production is scarce, producers compete to make enough supply.

When attention is scarce, producers compete to become the thing chosen from abundant supply.

That competition can reward quality.

It can also reward interruption.

The easiest way to earn attention is not always to deserve it.

Outrage works.

Fear works.

Novelty works.

Sex works.

Status works.

Urgency works.

Personal relevance works.

Algorithms can learn these regularities faster than people can become immune to them.

Generative systems can manufacture the attention hooks continuously.

The abundance economy therefore risks becoming an arms race between generation and filtering.

One system creates the message most likely to penetrate your defenses.

Another system screens it.

One agent negotiates for a meeting.

Another agent protects your calendar.

One platform generates the offer.

Another assistant evaluates whether the offer is worth seeing.

Machines transact around a human bottleneck.

The human receives the residue.

This could be liberating.

A good assistant could eliminate enormous amounts of cognitive clutter.

It could protect mornings from low-value requests, summarize routine updates, schedule necessary tasks, group related decisions, and notice when a commitment conflicts with a stated priority.

The person gets fewer interruptions and better information.

But delegation creates a dependence.

If your assistant becomes the gatekeeper to your attention, you need confidence that its incentives remain yours.

A free attention assistant funded by advertising has an obvious conflict.

An enterprise assistant trained to maximize organizational responsiveness may protect the company's priorities better than the employee's health.

A social assistant optimized for engagement may become a more intimate version of the feed.

A political assistant could filter information in ways that preserve ideological comfort.

The most valuable attention technology may therefore be one whose business model is boring.

You pay it to protect you.

No hidden sponsor.

No engagement objective.

No marketplace fee for letting certain messages through.

The scarcity of attention creates a market for loyalty.

This mirrors the trust premium.

When information becomes abundant, the person needs a trusted filter.

The filter's alignment with the user becomes part of the product.

Organizations face the same problem internally.

A company can generate infinite analysis and still have one chief executive.

It can instrument every process and still have managers who can meaningfully discuss only a finite number of issues.

It can create dashboards for every metric and still have a board with a few hours per meeting.

The limiting factor becomes organizational attention.

This is why priorities matter more in an abundance organization.

A priority is a rule for what will not receive attention.

If everything is priority one, the organization has not prioritized. It has merely described its desires.

Cheap intelligence can make this worse because every team can arrive with stronger analysis supporting its own importance.

The marketing team has data.

The security team has scenarios.

The product team has simulations.

The legal team has detailed risk analysis.

The finance team has forecasts.

Every case becomes more persuasive.

The executive still has to decide what loses.

Abundant analysis can raise the quality of every request without increasing the number of requests that can be granted.

That is a pure attention bottleneck.

Good organizations will learn to compress decisions before they reach scarce human layers.

Not by hiding complexity.

By deciding which complexity belongs where.

A system can handle routine approvals inside clear boundaries.

Teams can own decisions that do not require executive attention.

Dashboards can surface exceptions rather than displaying everything continuously.

Documents can state the decision required rather than presenting information without a purpose.

Meetings can end when no decision, coordination, or relationship requires synchronous attention.

These habits were useful before AI.

Artificial abundance makes them necessary because the cost of creating work can otherwise outrun the capacity to govern it.

The personal version is similar.

People will need stronger stopping rules around consumption.

Not because information is bad.

Because the marginal piece of information competes with sleep, relationships, thought, exercise, boredom, and every other use of finite time.

The old question was often, “Can I get access to this?”

The abundance question is, “Does this deserve to enter?”

That applies to media, advice, notifications, opportunities, requests, and even machine-generated possibilities.

An intelligent assistant can help answer the question.

It cannot decide what a life should be filled with unless the person has already supplied some answer.

This is the hidden difficulty of attention management.

You cannot protect attention without preferences about value.

A blank calendar is not a meaningful life.

A perfect filter is not useful if you do not know what should pass through it.

Attention is scarce because life is scarce.

The two are the same constraint viewed at different scales.

This is why the economics of attention eventually becomes moral philosophy whether technologists want it to or not.

A system can optimize what captures you.

A system can optimize what saves you time.

A system can optimize what predicts satisfaction.

None of those objectives fully answers what deserves your days.

The abundance economy will produce extraordinary tools for creating more.

The mature use of those tools may be refusing more.

Fewer messages.

Fewer projects.

Fewer options after the decision is good enough.

Fewer obligations accepted because an agent makes them seem manageable.

Fewer pieces of content consumed merely because they are excellent.

The world can contain more than you need.

That is what abundance means.

The difficult skill is learning to let most of it remain there.
