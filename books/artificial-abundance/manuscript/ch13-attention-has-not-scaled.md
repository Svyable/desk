# Attention Has Not Scaled

The machine can make another answer before you finish reading this sentence.

It can make ten.

It can summarize each one, rank them, argue against them, translate them, shorten them, lengthen them, put them in a table, turn them into a memo, generate objections, answer the objections, and produce a second memo explaining why the first memo was too cautious.

You still have Saturday afternoon.

That is the imbalance.

Artificial intelligence can expand the supply of plausible cognitive output much faster than it can expand the time available to receive that output. The cost of producing another explanation, image, recommendation, draft, plan, or argument can fall toward zero without adding one minute to the day.

For a while, this feels like pure abundance.

Then the queue forms.

The neglected side of every productivity story is the receiving side. Somebody has to notice the work. Somebody has to decide whether it matters. Somebody has to review it, believe it, route it, approve it, act on it, remember it, or ignore it.

Production can scale without reception scaling with it.

This is not a new problem. Long before generative systems, modern institutions had already become efficient at producing more information than their members could absorb. Email did not merely replace letters. It made sending a message so cheap that organizations could create vastly more messages. Slide software did not merely replace transparencies. It made producing another deck trivial. Digital publishing did not merely reduce printing costs. It created a world in which the number of things that could be read became effectively unbounded.

The abundance arrived first.

Attention became the price.

Researchers studying collective attention have found evidence of this pressure in cultural life. In a 2019 Nature Communications paper, Philipp Lorenz-Spreen and colleagues examined attention patterns across several domains and found that public attention cycles had become shorter over time. Topics rose and fell faster. Their modeling pointed to increasing rates of content production and consumption as an important driver.

The result does not mean human beings suddenly evolved shorter biological attention spans. It is not evidence that every individual has become incapable of concentration. The study concerns collective attention: the rate at which populations move from one object of attention to another.

That distinction matters.

A person may still be able to spend four hours reading a difficult book while living in a culture that cycles through public topics faster than before. A scientist may still concentrate deeply while receiving three times as many requests for comment. A manager may still be capable of careful judgment while operating inside an organization that can now produce one hundred analyses for every decision.

The individual capacity can remain intact while the competition for it intensifies.

This is what abundance does to attention.

It does not have to damage the resource to make the resource more expensive.

If a town has one theater, the audience's evening can support one show. Build a thousand theaters overnight and the town has not become more entertained by definition. It has created a competition for seats that now exist mostly as imagined possibilities. The limiting resource moved from stage time to audience time.

Artificial intelligence is doing something similar to cognitive production.

There will be more things worth reading than can be read.

More products worth trying than can be tried.

More advice worth considering than can be considered.

More music that is competent enough to deserve a listen than can receive one.

More software features that could plausibly help someone than can fit inside anyone's working habits.

More research questions that can be explored cheaply than institutions have people prepared to evaluate.

More personalized recommendations than a person has meaningful decisions to make.

This changes what competition looks like.

When production is expensive, creators compete partly by being able to produce at all. A person who can write a clear report, record a polished song, make an illustration, build a prototype, analyze a dataset, or write functioning code occupies a relatively scarce position.

When competent production becomes abundant, that gate weakens.

The next contest is distribution into a finite field of awareness.

That field is not fair.

Attention does not flow only toward what is best. It flows toward what arrives from trusted people, what fits existing habits, what provokes emotion, what appears at the right moment, what the platform recommends, what friends repeat, what an institution requires, what fear makes difficult to ignore, and what money can place in front of people often enough to become familiar.

The abundance of generation therefore does not dissolve power.

It can increase the power of whoever controls selection.

Consider the old fantasy of the open internet. If publishing costs fall, more people can speak. That part happened. But a world in which everyone can publish does not become a world in which everyone receives equal attention. It becomes a world in which ranking systems, search engines, social graphs, brands, reputations, platforms, and trusted curators determine which tiny fraction of the available material is seen.

The abundance of speech creates scarcity in listening.

AI pushes the same structure further.

The creator no longer has to choose between making one version and making ten because ten is affordable. Soon ten thousand may be affordable. The institution no longer has to choose which memo to commission because every department can generate one. The consumer no longer has to choose among the recommendations a human adviser had time to prepare because the system can make another list instantly.

The hidden problem is that every additional option has a review cost.

An option that costs nothing to generate can still cost something to reject.

This is where abundance becomes adversarial.

Spam has always exploited the asymmetry between sending and receiving. If sending one million messages costs almost nothing, the sender does not need most of them to work. The recipients pay the filtering cost. The sender's cheap action creates distributed expense for everyone else.

Generative systems can extend that logic far beyond email.

A low-cost legal filing can still require expensive review.

A synthetic job application can still consume recruiter attention.

A generated security report can still require an engineer to check whether the vulnerability is real.

A flood of plausible product proposals can still consume executive time.

A thousand customized political messages can still impose fact-checking costs on institutions that cannot answer each one individually.

The economics can become perverse: the cheaper it becomes to create a claim, the more expensive it can become to maintain a trustworthy environment around claims.

The receiving side becomes infrastructure.

This is why filters gain value.

Not because filters are glamorous, but because most abundance is useless until something is excluded.

A good search engine is valuable because the web is large.

A good editor is valuable because there are many sentences that could be printed.

A good physician is valuable partly because a patient can arrive with endless possible explanations for a symptom.

A good manager is valuable partly because the organization can imagine more projects than it can pursue.

A good friend is valuable partly because advice from someone who knows you saves you from having to consider advice from everyone else.

Selection is not the opposite of abundance.

It is what makes abundance inhabitable.

Artificial intelligence can of course help with the filtering problem it creates.

This is the obvious answer and an important one.

A system can summarize an inbox, rank research papers, triage customer requests, compare proposals, identify duplicated ideas, detect low-quality submissions, monitor alerts, and compress hours of material into a smaller decision surface.

The machine that generates the flood can also build the levee.

That does not make the scarcity disappear.

It changes the place where the scarcity sits.

If a model chooses which ten items out of ten thousand you should see, the important question is no longer whether you personally had enough time to inspect ten thousand items. You did not. The important questions become who set the ranking objective, what evidence the model used, which errors matter, what interests shaped the filter, what was suppressed, and how you would know if the filter became bad.

Attention can be delegated.

Control over attention cannot be treated as irrelevant merely because delegation works.

This is already visible in recommendation systems. People do not select most of the individual items placed before them. A system chooses the sequence. The user still chooses whether to continue, but the platform has enormous influence over the menu of continuation.

Generative systems make the menu more fluid.

A recommendation system once chose among existing songs, videos, articles, products, or posts. A generative system can increasingly manufacture the object itself around predicted interest. The distinction between deciding what to show and making what will be shown begins to blur.

That creates a new form of attention competition.

The object can adapt while pursuing your attention.

A headline can be rewritten.

A lesson can change examples.

A sales pitch can change tone.

A game can change difficulty.

A video can change pacing.

A synthetic character can remember what you responded to yesterday.

Personalization can improve relevance. It can also make competition for attention more intimate.

The system does not need to find the average person interesting.

It only needs to learn what keeps you there.

This raises a difficult point for the entire argument of artificial abundance.

We often talk as though abundance increases freedom because more options are available.

Sometimes it does.

A person with one source of information is vulnerable to that source. A person with one employer, one supplier, one newspaper, one teacher, one bank, or one cultural gatekeeper has limited alternatives. More available options can reduce dependence.

But option count and experienced freedom are not the same thing.

At some point, another option becomes another claim on attention.

A person who can choose among five good restaurants may feel free. A person asked to evaluate fifty thousand restaurants does not possess fifty thousand times as much useful freedom. The value comes from a manageable set and confidence that the important alternatives were not excluded.

Abundance therefore creates demand for trusted compression.

That phrase sounds technical, but the institutions built around it are human and old.

A newspaper compresses the events of a day.

A university compresses a field into a curriculum.

A professional credential compresses evidence about competence.

A brand compresses previous experience into a prediction about the next product.

A friend compresses years of mutual knowledge into a recommendation you are willing to act on.

A critic compresses a large cultural field into a small set of things worth noticing.

A public library compresses selection without pretending to contain every book.

These institutions do not merely transmit information. They reduce the number of decisions the recipient has to make.

That function becomes more valuable when generation becomes cheaper.

It also becomes easier to abuse.

The curator can become a censor.

The recommender can become a manipulator.

The ranking system can sell placement while pretending to measure relevance.

The credential can preserve insiders long after it stops measuring competence.

The brand can extract trust earned by yesterday's product while lowering the quality of today's.

Scarcity does not purify what becomes scarce.

It raises the stakes around it.

This is the pattern of the book so far. Cheap output increases the value of judgment, but judgment can be wrong. Cheap customization increases the value of invariants, but invariants can become rigid. Cheap expertise increases the value of responsibility, but responsibility can become a moat. Cheap generation increases the value of attention, but attention can be captured rather than earned.

The repricing is real even when the new scarce asset is morally ambiguous.

Companies will feel this first in places where production metrics have been easy to celebrate.

Suppose an AI system lets a marketing team produce ten times as many campaigns. The production dashboard looks excellent. Ten times as many drafts. Ten times as many variants. More copy, more images, more landing pages, more audience segments, more experiments.

But customers did not receive ten times as much attention to spend on advertising.

The organization may discover that its internal ability to generate campaigns has exceeded the external market's willingness to notice them.

At that point, the bottleneck is not creative output.

It is access to attention without destroying the relationship that makes attention available.

The same applies inside firms.

A worker can use AI to produce a longer report in less time. Her manager still has to read something. If every worker produces more, the manager may face an attention tax created by everyone else's productivity gain.

The local optimization becomes a global problem.

This is one reason the six-month experiment across 66 firms discussed earlier is so useful. Individual access to generative AI changed some worker behavior, including time spent on email among adopters, without producing broad detected changes in the composition of work. The tool could make parts of individual production cheaper. The organization still contained meetings, dependencies, approvals, expectations, and other structures that did not automatically reorganize.

Attention lives inside those structures.

The analyst can create the answer faster.

The committee still meets Thursday.

The engineer can produce five designs.

The customer can evaluate only one demonstration at a time.

The government agency can generate thousands of comments on a proposed rule.

The public servant still has to distinguish meaningful participation from generated volume.

The teacher can produce individualized feedback.

The student still has only so much capacity to absorb it.

More feedback can even become less useful if every sentence arrives annotated, explained, corrected, expanded, and linked to another explanation.

Help competes with itself.

That is the part abundance rhetoric misses.

The supply curve can move while the nervous system remains inconveniently physical.

We sleep.

We get bored.

We forget.

We need uninterrupted time for some kinds of thought.

We cannot care about every worthy cause at once.

We cannot maintain serious relationships with everyone who might benefit from one.

We cannot make every possible project part of our identity.

A life is selective by construction.

Artificial intelligence does not repeal that selectivity.

It makes the rejected possibilities more visible.

This may be one of its strangest psychological effects. The machine can always show the life not chosen. Another career path. Another itinerary. Another way to phrase the apology. Another business idea. Another book you could write. Another person you might date. Another neighborhood you might prefer. Another plan for spending the year.

Possibility becomes abundant enough to haunt commitment.

The scarcity of attention is therefore not only a market fact.

It is a condition of meaning.

To attend to something is to neglect something else.

A promise matters because attention is not infinite. A conversation matters because you cannot have every conversation simultaneously. Reading one book deeply means not reading hundreds of others during those hours. Raising a child, building a company, mastering an instrument, caring for a parent, maintaining a friendship, or learning a field all require sustained exclusion.

The value is partly in what the attention prevents you from doing.

That makes attention different from many goods made abundant by technology.

We might automate more of its low-level allocation. We might build better filters. We might use agents to read on our behalf and surface what matters. All of that is likely.

But the endpoint of filtering is still a life in which something receives priority.

There is no useful system that tells you everything matters equally.

The best filter eventually says: look here.

And once it does, another scarcity appears.

In a world full of generated messages, generated recommendations, generated concern, generated praise, generated sympathy, generated persuasion, and generated style, people will increasingly ask a question that has little to do with the surface quality of the words.

Who was actually here?
