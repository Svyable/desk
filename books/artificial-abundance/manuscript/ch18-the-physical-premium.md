# The Physical Premium

A ceramic mug is a terrible file.

It cannot be copied by pressing a key. It occupies shelf space. It breaks. It has to be boxed, shipped, stored, washed, and eventually thrown away or forgotten in the back of a cupboard.

A picture of the mug has none of these problems.

This difference sounds trivial until digital production becomes extremely cheap. Then the inconveniences of matter stop looking like background conditions and start looking like a price category.

Every physical object carries a tax that information does not pay in the same way.

Atoms need somewhere to be.

They have weight. They move at finite speed. They require materials and energy. They wear. They collide with other things. They can be stolen. They can be unsafe. They need maintenance. They are subject to weather, zoning, customs, fire codes, labor schedules, and the stubborn geometry of rooms.

Digital artifacts have physical infrastructure too, as the previous chapter made clear. But once that infrastructure exists, the marginal cost of one more copy can be extraordinarily low.

The gap between these cost structures creates what we might call a physical premium.

The phrase needs care. It does not mean physical things are inherently better. A traffic jam is physical. So is a leaking roof. A broken hip is more embodied than a spreadsheet and considerably less desirable.

The premium is economic before it is emotional.

When a digital substitute becomes abundant, the remaining embodied component can account for more of the price.

Consider a design consultation. Part of its historical cost came from cognition: understanding the request, producing alternatives, rendering them, explaining tradeoffs. If generative tools reduce those costs, the bill does not necessarily disappear. It may migrate toward site measurement, permitting, materials, installation, liability, and the person who arrives on Tuesday morning with tools.

The expensive part becomes the part that cannot be emailed.

We have seen earlier versions of this transition.

Software reduced the cost of distributing music, but a concert still requires a venue, performers, staff, security, transport, power, insurance, and a fixed number of seats. Online retail reduced the cost of browsing, comparison, and ordering, but the final object still has to be manufactured and delivered. Video conferencing reduced the need for some business travel, but when people decide physical presence matters, an airplane seat remains an airplane seat.

Artificial intelligence pushes the boundary because it can reduce not only distribution costs but some of the cognitive labor wrapped around physical production.

A contractor can produce an estimate faster. A manufacturer can explore designs faster. A warehouse can schedule work more intelligently. A restaurant can generate menus, marketing, training material, and forecasts. A hospital can automate portions of documentation and administration.

None of these examples proves that the physical service becomes more expensive in absolute terms. Better tools can lower its cost too.

But they can change what the customer is paying for.

If the menu costs almost nothing to write, the meal is more obviously about ingredients, kitchen capacity, location, labor, hospitality, and the hour of your evening that cannot be duplicated. If the architectural render costs almost nothing, the building is more obviously about land, materials, approvals, financing, and construction. If the lesson plan costs almost nothing, the school is more obviously about supervision, relationships, facilities, peer groups, safety, and the allocation of adult attention.

The informational wrapper thins.

The embodied core shows through.

This is one reason some businesses that look technologically unsophisticated may become more strategically interesting in an age of artificial abundance. A plumbing company has dispatch software and customer records, but its decisive asset is still the ability to get a competent person to a particular building when water is coming through the ceiling. A childcare center cannot respond to infinite demand by generating another classroom on a server. A dental practice cannot scale a chair by copying its appointment calendar.

These businesses can use AI extensively without becoming digital businesses in the economic sense that matters here.

Their constraints remain physical.

That creates both opportunity and danger.

The opportunity is that cognitive overhead can fall. Scheduling, quoting, documentation, translation, training, customer communication, purchasing, diagnostics, and administrative work can become easier. A small physical-service business may gain capabilities that once required a much larger office.

The danger is that investors and managers mistake cheaper coordination for unlimited capacity.

A better scheduling system can reduce idle time. It cannot schedule a technician into two houses at once. A hospital can optimize operating-room turnover. It cannot treat an unlimited number of patients with a finite staff and finite rooms. A port can improve routing. Containers still occupy space.

Optimization squeezes slack before it creates supply.

At some point the constraint has to be expanded, substituted, or accepted.

The same lesson appears inside AI infrastructure itself.

The amount of energy required for a particular inference can fall rapidly as chips, models, software, and serving systems improve. It is therefore tempting to assume that efficiency will reduce total physical demand.

It may. It may not.

The International Energy Agency's 2026 assessment described rapidly declining energy use per AI task while total data-centre electricity consumption nevertheless rose 17 percent in 2025. AI-focused facilities grew faster still.

There is no paradox once demand is allowed to move.

A cheaper task can be performed more often. New uses can appear. Larger models can be served. More users can gain access. Applications that were uneconomic can become ordinary. Efficiency lowers the resource cost of a unit while scale increases the number of units.

This resembles the family of effects associated with rebound and Jevons's nineteenth-century observation about coal efficiency, but invoking the name does not settle the outcome. Rebound varies. Prices matter. Substitution matters. Regulation matters. Demand can saturate. Efficiency improvements can still reduce total consumption in some settings.

The useful point is narrower: never infer aggregate physical relief from declining cost per digital action alone.

Artificial abundance can be physically hungry.

That hunger is not limited to electricity. Advanced computing depends on semiconductor fabrication, specialized equipment, cooling systems, construction, transmission, water in some facilities, and supply chains whose capacity cannot be summoned by generating another purchase order.

A digital service can scale at software speed until it hits something that scales at factory speed.

Then factory speed wins.

The pattern matters for policy because it complicates a familiar story about dematerialization. Richer economies can sometimes produce more value with less material per dollar of output. Digital services can substitute for travel, paper, inventory, and physical prototypes. These are genuine efficiencies.

At the same time, cheap computation can create demand for new physical infrastructure. The direction of total resource use is an empirical question, not a slogan.

The same caution applies to consumer life.

As generated media becomes abundant, some people may spend more on experiences precisely because screens are saturated. Others may prefer increasingly rich virtual experiences and travel less. Some physical objects may gain status because their provenance and manufacture are costly. Others may lose value because a digital substitute is good enough.

There will be no single physical premium across the economy.

There will be many premiums, each attached to a constraint.

Location is one.

A restaurant table overlooking a particular square cannot be duplicated without changing what it is. A home within walking distance of a particular school is tied to land. A seat in a stadium has coordinates.

Time is another.

A live performance happens at an hour. A caregiver can be fully present with only so many people. A skilled craftsperson has a finite working life.

Risk is another.

When a service can injure someone, damage property, violate a regulation, or create liability, the cost of physical execution includes assurance. A generated suggestion may be cheap; taking responsibility for acting on it is not.

Maintenance is another.

Digital demos end when the presentation ends. Physical systems begin accumulating obligations the moment they are installed. Roofs leak. Batteries degrade. Bearings fail. Paint peels. Roads crack. Buildings require cleaning long after the architect's rendering has been forgotten.

Ownership of matter is ownership of future work.

That future work is part of the premium too.

There is a cultural temptation to romanticize this. If synthetic goods become cheap, perhaps handmade objects, live performances, local food, analog hobbies, and face-to-face services will become badges of authenticity.

Some will.

But authenticity markets have always been capable of turning inconvenience into status. A deliberately inefficient process can become luxurious precisely because only wealthy people can afford the time and money it consumes.

We should be suspicious when scarcity is marketed as virtue by people who profit from the scarcity.

A hand-thrown bowl can carry the trace of a maker's choices and deserve a premium. That does not mean industrial ceramics were a mistake. A live teacher can provide attention no recording can match. That does not mean everyone should be denied inexpensive recorded instruction until a teacher is available.

Artificial abundance should make us more discriminating about the physical, not automatically reverent toward it.

The central economic shift is simpler.

As competent digital production gets cheaper, businesses and institutions will discover which parts of their value proposition were really information products and which parts were embodied capacity wearing an informational coat.

The coat may become almost free.

The body underneath still has to show up.
