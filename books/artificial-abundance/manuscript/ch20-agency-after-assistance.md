# Agency After Assistance

Turn-by-turn directions solved a problem and created a joke.

People began arriving at places they could not locate again without their phones.

The joke works because assistance and competence are easy to confuse. A navigation system can make a person extraordinarily capable at reaching an unfamiliar address while leaving that person with little internal map of the route.

This is not necessarily a failure.

The person wanted to reach the hospital, not pass an examination in urban geography.

We delegate constantly. We use calculators without reproducing long division, elevators without understanding control systems, translators without speaking the language, accountants without mastering tax law, and pilots without knowing how to fly the airplane ourselves. Civilization is partly the art of acting through capabilities distributed among other people and machines.

Agency cannot mean doing everything unaided.

If it did, modern life would be a story of near-total helplessness.

A better definition begins with consequential choice.

Can a person form or adopt a goal, understand enough about the available paths to choose among them, revise the goal when circumstances change, and contest or reverse important actions when the system is wrong?

Under this definition, assistance can increase agency dramatically.

A person who cannot speak Japanese can navigate Tokyo with translation software. A small-business owner can understand a contract well enough to know which questions to take to a lawyer. A student can receive explanations at midnight. A person with limited mobility can control devices through an interface that converts intention into action.

The tool expands the set of things the person can cause to happen.

That is agency gained.

The difficulty begins when the tool moves upstream.

A map that helps you reach the restaurant you chose is different from a system that chose the restaurant, decided you should go out, selected the people to invite, wrote the messages, booked the table, ordered the ride, and optimized the evening around a model of your predicted preferences.

You may still enjoy dinner.

You may also struggle to identify which part of the evening was your decision.

This is not a mystical concern about machine influence. Human beings have always shaped one another's options. Parents, friends, advertisers, employers, governments, architects, teachers, and markets structure the choices people see. No individual authors a life from a vacuum.

The new issue is scale, speed, and intimacy.

A system that observes thousands of small behaviors can infer preferences a person has never stated. It can rank options before they become visible. It can prefill the response. It can decide which information deserves interruption and which should remain silent. It can execute before the user has converted an intention into a deliberate action.

Convenience moves the decision boundary.

Sometimes this is exactly what we want.

Nobody wants to approve every packet sent by a computer network. Few people want to manually schedule every background software update. A diabetic using an automated insulin-delivery system may benefit precisely because the system acts continuously rather than waiting for conscious micromanagement.

Agency is not maximized by maximizing clicks.

This matters because badly designed oversight can imitate control without providing it. A user may be asked to approve hundreds of machine-generated actions. Technically, a human remains in the loop. Practically, the human learns to click yes.

Approval becomes ceremony.

The meaningful choices occurred earlier: which objective the system pursues, which actions it may take, what counts as an exception, how uncertainty is displayed, what happens when the user does nothing, and whether a mistake can be undone.

These are design choices about agency.

Consider an AI system that manages a household's finances. It could be built as an elaborate calculator: the family sets goals, specifies constraints, reviews recommendations, and explicitly approves consequential transfers. It could also be built as an autonomous optimizer that predicts the family's preferences and moves money continuously.

The second system may produce better financial outcomes.

It may also make the family's own goals less legible to them.

That does not automatically make it worse. If the family deliberately chooses the arrangement, understands the objective, can inspect the behavior, and can change course, delegation itself can be an exercise of agency.

Hiring an excellent manager is not surrender if you can fire the manager.

The problem is delegation without practical contestability.

A system can be nominally optional while becoming difficult to refuse because every surrounding institution assumes its use. A worker may technically be able to override an automated recommendation while knowing that doing so requires a long explanation to management. A customer may be able to appeal a decision through a process so burdensome that the right is mostly decorative. A user may be able to change a default only after navigating settings designed to discourage it.

Formal choice and practical agency can diverge.

This is familiar from bureaucracy. A person denied a benefit may possess a legal right to appeal but lack the time, language, money, records, or expertise needed to exercise it. Automation can reduce those barriers. It can also create new ones if the reasoning behind a decision becomes harder to inspect.

Artificial abundance therefore makes explanation valuable in a new way.

When a system can generate and execute many actions cheaply, people need to know which actions deserve scrutiny.

Not every decision requires a philosophical seminar. Most should disappear into reliable infrastructure. The challenge is identifying the decisions whose consequences, uncertainty, novelty, or irreversibility make human attention worthwhile.

This boundary will move.

Today's dangerous exception can become tomorrow's routine. Aviation automated tasks that once demanded constant manual control. Financial systems automate calculations that once required specialists. Medical devices make adjustments too frequent for a clinician to perform by hand.

There is no principled reason to assume that every form of verification, judgment, or exception handling must remain human forever.

The book's scarcity argument depends on resisting that comforting conclusion.

If judgment becomes automatable, value moves again.

Perhaps it moves toward specifying goals. Perhaps systems become better at eliciting those too. Perhaps it moves toward legitimacy: who has the right to set the objective for a school, company, family, or country? Perhaps it moves toward responsibility for rare failures. Perhaps even those arrangements change.

Scarcity is a moving frontier, not a sanctuary for human pride.

This makes agency more useful than a list of supposedly human tasks.

Agency is about the relationship between a person and the frontier.

Who set the objective?

Who selected the available options?

Who can notice that this case is different?

Who bears the consequence?

Can the affected person inspect what happened, refuse it, revise it, or reverse it?

These questions are not a framework to print on a consulting slide. They are ordinary questions that become more important when execution is cheap.

Imagine an employee whose AI assistant handles email. At first it drafts replies. The employee edits each one. Then the assistant learns the employee's style and begins sending routine messages automatically. It schedules meetings, declines invitations, summarizes documents, requests information, and negotiates minor deadlines.

The employee may gain hours every week.

That is not trivial. Time recovered from clerical communication can be spent on work, family, sleep, or nothing at all.

But the assistant is also shaping the employee's social world. Which invitations are routine enough to decline? Which colleague deserves a longer answer? Which weak signal in a message indicates that someone is upset? Which meeting looks useless in the calendar but matters because a relationship is fragile?

The system can learn many of these distinctions.

The question is what happens during the period when it has learned most of them.

A highly competent assistant is more likely to be trusted. Trust reduces monitoring. Reduced monitoring allows small errors of preference to accumulate unnoticed. The assistant may not make obvious mistakes. It may simply make the employee slightly more like the person the model predicts.

A declined invitation here. A softened disagreement there. A preference inferred from last year's behavior and carried forward after the person has changed.

Agency can erode without a dramatic failure.

It can erode through convenience that becomes identity by default.

There is a counterforce. The same system could make preference change easier. It could show the employee patterns: You have declined every invitation from this group for six months. Is that still what you want? You asked me to prioritize uninterrupted mornings, but your role has changed. Should that rule remain? This decision is unusual compared with your previous choices; would you like to review it?

Assistance can make habits visible instead of merely automating them.

That is agency gained again.

The design difference is not intelligence. Both systems may use the same model.

The difference is whether the system treats the person's current predicted behavior as the objective or treats the person as someone capable of revising the objective.

A prediction says what you are likely to choose.

Agency includes the ability to surprise the prediction.

This is one reason reversibility matters. Cheap generation encourages experimentation because drafts can be discarded. Cheap action is more dangerous when consequences cannot be discarded with them.

An assistant that rearranges a calendar can undo the change. An assistant that sends money, signs a contract, publishes an accusation, administers a drug, or terminates an employee crosses a different threshold.

The more irreversible the action, the more valuable a meaningful pause can become.

Not friction for its own sake. Not a checkbox that trains users to ignore it. A pause proportional to consequence.

We encountered this earlier when discussing friction as a feature. Agency gives the idea a sharper purpose. Good friction creates a moment in which authorship can return to the person who bears the result.

Artificial intelligence will remove enormous amounts of unnecessary friction. It should. People do not become more human by filling out forms, copying numbers between systems, remembering passwords, formatting documents, or waiting on hold.

The interesting task is to remove mechanical effort without accidentally removing the moments in which people decide what they are doing.

That distinction will not be stable.

Children raised with capable assistants may develop forms of agency that look strange to people who learned to operate software manually. They may be less skilled at producing intermediate artifacts and better at directing complex systems. Or they may become dependent on systems whose defaults they rarely question. Both outcomes are plausible. Different institutions will produce different mixtures.

We should be cautious about declaring one generation diminished because it delegates tasks an earlier generation had to perform itself. People once worried that writing would weaken memory, calculators would weaken arithmetic, and search engines would weaken recall. Sometimes skills do weaken. Sometimes cognitive effort moves elsewhere.

The important question is whether the person can still steer.

Artificial abundance gives us more capable vehicles.

Agency is what remains when we ask who chose the destination.
