# The Politics of Plenty

A technology can become cheap before it becomes equal.

This is one of the oldest mistakes in stories about abundance.

The price falls, the capability spreads, and the conclusion arrives too quickly: everyone now has it.

Sometimes that eventually becomes close enough to true that the earlier inequality looks temporary. Electricity, telephones, household appliances, computing, and internet access all moved through versions of this pattern in many countries. Costs fell. Infrastructure expanded. Products improved. What began as an elite capability became ordinary.

But the transition was not politically neutral while it was happening.

Someone received the new capability first.

Someone owned the infrastructure.

Someone lost bargaining power before finding another source of it.

Someone lived in the place where the new factory, power line, data center, or transmission corridor had to be built.

Someone paid for training.

Someone did not know that training was necessary until the old job changed.

Artificial abundance will be no different in this respect.

Its politics begins with an awkward fact: the thing becoming abundant is not a finished social good.

It is capability.

Capability enters a world where people own different assets, work in different occupations, live under different institutions, possess different levels of education, face different prices, and have different amounts of power over how technology is introduced into their lives.

The same fall in the price of cognition can therefore feel like liberation to one person and like competition to another.

A small business owner who cannot afford a marketing department may suddenly have access to competent copy, design drafts, translation, research, and software help.

A freelance writer may encounter the same tools as a buyer who now needs fewer billable hours.

A junior employee may become productive faster.

A senior employee may see part of the experience premium compressed.

A person who speaks a minority language may gain access to services that were never economically translated for them.

A worker whose job consists largely of producing routine digital material may discover that the market has become abundant in exactly the thing they sell.

There is no contradiction here.

A technology can create consumer surplus and labor anxiety at the same time.

It can widen access while reducing the price of some kinds of work.

It can improve productivity while making the distribution of productivity gains politically contested.

This is why the politics of artificial abundance cannot be reduced to a debate over whether AI is good or bad.

The distribution is the debate.

The International Labour Organization's 2025 global exposure index gives useful scale without supplying a prophecy. It estimates that roughly one in four jobs worldwide lies in an occupation with some potential exposure to generative AI. Only a much smaller share of global employment sits in the highest exposure category. The ILO's own framing emphasizes transformation more than wholesale replacement.

That word matters again.

If a quarter of jobs vanished, the political question would be obvious.

If a quarter of jobs change, the questions multiply.

Who pays for the transition?

Who captures the productivity gain?

Does the worker who becomes more productive receive higher pay, shorter hours, more demanding targets, or simply fewer colleagues?

Does a company use assistance to deepen expertise or to reduce the time available to acquire it?

Does cheaper service expand demand enough to create more work, or does the price fall faster than demand rises?

Does a profession use new tools to reach people who were excluded by cost, or use regulation to preserve old scarcity?

These are not questions a benchmark can answer.

They depend on markets, bargaining, policy, organizational design, and the particular technology being deployed.

Exposure is also uneven before any policy choice is made.

Occupations are not distributed randomly across society. The ILO's work finds different exposure patterns across income levels and between women and men because workers are concentrated in different kinds of jobs. Clerical and administrative work, for example, contains many tasks that current generative systems can address.

This does not mean every exposed worker loses a job.

It means the transition pressure arrives through existing social structure.

A technological shock can therefore inherit inequalities it did not create.

The same is true on the adoption side.

Across OECD countries, more than one-third of individuals used generative AI tools in 2025. That is astonishingly rapid diffusion for a capability that had barely entered mass public use a few years earlier.

It was not uniform diffusion.

Use differed sharply by age and meaningfully by education and income.

Again, none of these gaps should be treated as permanent. Young technologies often diffuse unevenly. Skills spread. Interfaces improve. Costs fall further. Public institutions adapt. Products become easier to use.

But temporary inequality still matters to people living through the temporary period.

A student who learns to use a powerful tool two years before another student may enter the labor market differently. A firm that reorganizes around automation before its competitor may compound an advantage. A worker who understands where a model fails may capture opportunities unavailable to someone who only knows how to accept its outputs.

Timing is an asset.

This makes access more complicated than the familiar question of who can open the website.

Nominal access can be broad while effective access remains scarce.

A free model does not help equally if one person has a fast connection, a modern device, proprietary business data, legal permission to use the data, a manager who supports experimentation, and enough domain knowledge to recognize a dangerous answer, while another person has none of those things.

The model may be the cheapest component in the system.

We encountered this pattern with physical infrastructure. Inference can become cheaper while electricity, chips, grids, land, and capital remain constrained. The social version is similar. Generated intelligence can become cheap while complementary assets remain expensive.

Education.

Compute.

Clean data.

Distribution.

Customer relationships.

Regulatory approval.

Reputation.

Capital.

Time to experiment.

Freedom to make mistakes without losing a livelihood.

The owner of complementary scarcity can capture value from abundant capability.

That capture is not predetermined either.

A productivity gain has to land somewhere.

It can become a lower price for customers. It can become a higher margin for the firm. It can become higher pay, fewer hours, more output, a better product, or enough slack to attempt work that was previously uneconomic. Often it becomes several of these at once, in proportions that change over time.

The technology alone does not tell us the proportions.

Competition matters. If many firms gain the same efficiency and customers can switch easily, some of the gain may be competed into lower prices. If one firm owns a difficult-to-copy complement, the gain may stay with the owner. If demand expands when price falls, cheaper production can create more work even while fewer labor hours are required per unit. If demand is already saturated, the same productivity increase can reduce labor demand instead.

Bargaining matters too.

A worker who uses an assistant to produce twice as much has not automatically doubled the value of the worker's claim on the resulting revenue. The firm may see the tool as capital it purchased. The worker may see the improved output as evidence of greater contribution. Customers may simply expect faster service at the old price. Competitors may force the price down before either side captures much of the surplus.

This is why arguments about whether AI will "benefit workers" or "benefit capital" can become too clean to be useful.

The answer can differ by occupation, firm, market, and year.

It can also change as adoption spreads.

Early users may earn rents from knowing how to use a scarce capability. Later the capability becomes ordinary and the premium moves toward whatever remains difficult: relationships, distribution, proprietary data, regulatory permission, physical assets, or judgment at the edge of the system.

The politics follows that movement.

Transition costs do not necessarily arrive on the same clock as benefits.

A consumer can enjoy a cheaper service immediately. A worker whose task loses value may need months or years to acquire a different source of bargaining power. A company can install software in a quarter. A school system cannot produce experienced mid-career workers on the same schedule. A community can lose an employer faster than it can attract another one.

Long-run gains can therefore coexist with short-run losses that are neither imaginary nor trivial.

This is where political arguments often talk past one another. One side points to the eventual expansion of capability. The other points to people who cannot live in an eventual average.

Both clocks are real.

A transition that looks efficient in a ten-year chart can still be brutal in the second year. A policy that preserves every current role can protect the second year by preventing the ten-year adjustment altogether.

The hard problem is separating protection of people from protection of a particular production method.

Those are not the same thing.

A society can decide that people should not bear every cost of technological transition alone without promising that every old task will remain economically scarce. Firms can invest in training without pretending they know exactly which future job will exist. Insurance, savings, portable benefits, education, labor contracts, community institutions, and public programs can all distribute risk in different ways without freezing the workflow itself.

None of these mechanisms is free of tradeoffs. Assistance can subsidize incumbents. Training can be mistimed or irrelevant. Benefits can weaken incentives or simply arrive too late. Firms can call a layoff "transformation" while shifting the cost onto workers. Workers and professions can invoke transition harm to defend rents that no longer serve customers.

There is no distribution mechanism that makes technological change painless by definition.

But there is also no reason to pretend that the pain is a natural property of the technology rather than partly a result of how risk is assigned.

This matters because abundance changes not only income but security.

The person deciding whether to experiment with a new tool behaves differently if one failure threatens the rent. The small firm behaves differently if a failed deployment can be rolled back cheaply. The worker behaves differently if learning a new role does not require gambling the household's entire financial stability.

Risk-bearing capacity is another complementary asset.

People with more of it can adopt earlier, wait longer, and survive more mistakes.

That advantage can compound even when the underlying tool is free.

This is where the politics becomes familiar economics.

When a new input gets cheaper, the returns do not automatically flow to the people who use the input most visibly. They can flow to whoever owns the bottleneck around it.

A company may save money on content and spend more on distribution because attention remains scarce. A software business may save on coding and spend more on customer acquisition, security, or infrastructure. A hospital may gain cheaper analysis while remaining constrained by beds, nurses, operating rooms, reimbursement, and clinical responsibility.

At national scale, the same principle applies.

OECD modelling of possible AI-driven productivity gains across G7 economies does not produce one universal number. Projected gains vary because countries differ in sector composition, adoption, exposure, and other conditions.

Technology does not arrive on a blank macroeconomic spreadsheet.

A country with many highly digitized, AI-exposed service industries may experience the transition differently from one where employment is concentrated in less exposed sectors. A country with reliable electricity, deep capital markets, strong universities, and large technology firms starts from a different position than one that imports the infrastructure and the models.

This creates a geopolitical version of value migration.

If model capability itself becomes more widely available, strategic value can move toward chips, energy, data centers, specialized talent, capital, standards, distribution platforms, and the institutions capable of absorbing the tools into productive systems.

The political temptation is to pick one of these bottlenecks and declare it the new permanent source of power.

That would repeat the mistake the book has been trying to avoid.

Scarcity moves.

A chip shortage can become overcapacity. A specialized skill can become a software feature. A dominant interface can be displaced. A regulatory advantage can invite reform. A country that leads in one generation of technology can miss the next.

Politics is partly the struggle to shape this movement before everyone knows where it will stop.

This uncertainty explains why reasonable people can look at the same technology and favor different policies without one side being ignorant of the facts.

One person emphasizes diffusion. If the capability is transformative, make it cheap and widely available. Remove barriers. Encourage competition. Let experimentation reveal valuable uses.

Another emphasizes transition costs. If gains are concentrated and adjustment is painful, workers and communities need support before the promised long-run benefits arrive.

Another emphasizes safety and rights. Cheap production can scale harm as well as benefit, and people need recourse when automated systems affect employment, credit, healthcare, education, or public services.

Another emphasizes innovation. Rules written too early can freeze current assumptions into law and protect incumbents who can afford compliance.

Another emphasizes sovereignty. Dependence on a small number of foreign providers can turn cheap intelligence into strategic vulnerability.

These concerns conflict because the underlying goods conflict.

Speed.

Safety.

Competition.

Continuity.

Privacy.

Access.

Local control.

Global scale.

There is no politics-free way to maximize all of them.

Artificial abundance can even intensify old political problems by lowering the cost of participation in forms that do not require much commitment.

Generating a letter to a regulator becomes easy.

Generating ten thousand variations becomes easy.

Producing policy proposals becomes easy.

Flooding public comment channels, customer-service systems, courts, media organizations, and political campaigns with plausible text becomes cheaper.

This does not make the speech illegitimate.

It changes the cost structure around speech.

Institutions that once treated volume as a rough signal of human effort may need new ways to distinguish mass generation from broad public commitment without inventing rules that silence people who use assistance legitimately.

The same problem appears in elections and public debate without requiring any claim about a particular party or country.

Persuasion can be personalized at scale. Translation can broaden participation. Summarization can help citizens understand complex proposals. Synthetic media can also make verification more costly. Campaigns can produce more messages than voters can inspect. Governments can communicate more effectively and surveil more effectively using related capabilities.

Abundance increases both expressive capacity and verification burden.

That asymmetry is political because the burden is not equally distributed.

A large institution can employ teams and systems to authenticate material. An individual may face a stream of claims with little time to investigate any of them.

Attention becomes a kind of civic infrastructure.

Trust does too.

This is why the politics of plenty eventually returns to institutions. Markets can distribute many benefits of falling production costs remarkably well. A cheaper tool can reach people through ordinary commerce without a national plan. Competition can drive prices down faster than committees ever would.

But markets do not decide every question artificial abundance raises.

They do not determine due process.

They do not decide which public records must remain accessible.

They do not by themselves guarantee that a worker has bargaining power during a transition.

They do not settle whether a school should optimize education around labor-market demand or around broader ideas of citizenship and development.

They do not establish what level of automated persuasion is acceptable in a political system.

These are collective choices because they involve rules that bind people who did not all choose the same product.

The opposite mistake is to assume collective choice requires centralized control of the technology itself.

Often it does not.

A society can set liability rules without choosing every product design. It can protect appeal rights without building the model. It can fund education without predicting which occupation every student will enter. It can preserve competition by limiting anticompetitive conduct rather than deciding which firm should win.

Good politics often governs the boundary rather than producing the answer.

That resembles the invariant problem from infinite versions.

What must remain true while everything else becomes easier to vary?

People will disagree about the answer.

That disagreement is not a bug in the abundance machine.

It is what happens when technology changes prices inside a society whose members value different things.

The phrase politics of plenty can sound optimistic, as though prosperity will make conflict disappear.

History gives little reason for that confidence.

People fight over surpluses as well as shortages.

They fight over who produced them, who deserves them, who controls them, and what obligations come with them.

Abundance can even sharpen positional competition. If competent output becomes common, people may spend more to distinguish themselves through scarce schools, neighborhoods, experiences, credentials, relationships, or symbols of human effort.

One scarcity falls.

Another becomes socially louder.

The political question is therefore not whether artificial abundance arrives.

Parts of it already have.

The question is what kind of society forms around the new price structure.

Does cheaper intelligence broaden practical capability or simply increase the output expected from every worker?

Does productivity become income, leisure, lower prices, higher profits, better public services, or some mixture?

Do people gain more room to choose, or do organizations use assistance to make every minute legible and optimizable?

Do communities preserve places where human participation matters even when automation would be cheaper?

None of these outcomes is contained in the model weights.

They are choices made through companies, unions, families, schools, markets, governments, professional bodies, and ordinary habits.

The politics of plenty is the politics of deciding what abundance is for.

That brings the argument uncomfortably close to the individual again.

Because after institutions and politics have distributed new capabilities, a person still has to decide what to value when cheap competence is everywhere.

The market will offer one answer immediately.

Whatever remains expensive must be special.

That answer will often be wrong.
