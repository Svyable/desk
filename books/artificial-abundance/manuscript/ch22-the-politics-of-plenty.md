# The Politics of Plenty

Scarcity produces politics.

So does abundance.

The first politics asks who gets the limited thing.

The second asks who owns the system making the thing plentiful, who captures the savings, who bears the transition costs, and which scarcities become more important after the old one weakens.

Artificial intelligence will not end distributive conflict by making cognition cheaper.

It will move the conflict.

Suppose a company can produce twice as much with half as many people.

The productivity gain is real.

Who receives it?

Customers through lower prices?

Workers through higher wages or shorter hours?

Owners through higher profits?

New entrants through lower barriers?

Governments through tax revenue?

Suppliers through increased demand?

The answer is not contained in the technology.

It depends on competition, bargaining power, ownership, regulation, labor markets, tax systems, and what people are willing to fight over.

This is the politics of plenty.

Technological abundance often arrives unevenly.

One input becomes cheap while complementary assets remain scarce.

The factory becomes more productive while land remains scarce.

Software becomes cheap while distribution remains concentrated.

Information becomes abundant while trusted institutions remain limited.

Cognitive labor becomes cheaper while energy, chips, data centers, housing, care, and physical infrastructure remain constrained.

People do not experience an average across these markets.

They experience their household budget.

A person can live in a world of astonishingly cheap intelligence and still feel economically squeezed if rent, health care, child care, transport, and energy absorb most income.

This is why the politics of artificial abundance will be shaped by the scarcities it does not solve.

A government celebrating cheaper cognitive services may receive little gratitude from citizens waiting for housing.

A worker given access to extraordinary tools may feel no richer if the tools increase performance expectations without raising pay.

A small business may gain capabilities once reserved for large firms while becoming more dependent on a few infrastructure providers.

A student may receive excellent tutoring while the credential required for employment remains expensive.

Abundance at one layer can coexist with insecurity at another.

This complicates the familiar argument that productivity growth eventually raises living standards.

Historically, productivity has been essential to higher material living standards. A society cannot sustainably consume far more unless it can produce far more or trade for the difference.

But the path from productivity to broadly shared welfare is institutional.

It can be fast or slow.

It can be equal or unequal.

It can produce new industries and new jobs.

It can also destroy local economies before replacements appear.

The aggregate can improve while particular people lose.

Those people vote, organize, protest, move, disengage, or demand protection.

Transition costs are politically real even when the long-run forecast is optimistic.

This is why “new jobs will appear” is not a complete response to displacement.

It may be true.

The new job may be in another city.

It may require another credential.

It may pay less.

It may appear ten years later.

It may be unsuitable for the person whose identity, mortgage, family, and social network were organized around the old work.

Economies reallocate labor through human lives.

Artificial abundance may accelerate this process across multiple cognitive professions at once.

That raises the value of institutions that make transition less destructive.

Training that actually connects to jobs.

Portable benefits.

Income support that does not trap people outside work.

Housing supply that allows mobility.

Credential systems that recognize demonstrated capability rather than requiring years of expensive signaling.

Labor markets that make it easier to combine machine assistance with existing domain experience.

Different political traditions will disagree about which mechanisms work best and who should provide them.

The disagreement is legitimate.

The shared problem is not.

People need a bridge between disappearing scarcity and emerging value.

There will also be arguments about working time.

If intelligent systems raise output per worker, one possibility is producing more with the same hours.

Another is producing the same amount with fewer hours.

History has contained both. Productivity gains helped support higher consumption and, over long periods, reductions in working time in many places. But shorter workweeks did not appear automatically each time technology improved.

The distribution of time is political.

Workers may bargain for shorter hours.

Employers may compete through flexibility.

Governments may regulate maximums or benefits.

High-income workers may choose to work just as much because the returns to leverage increase.

Some people may hold multiple projects because AI lowers coordination costs.

Abundance can create leisure or intensify work.

The technology does not choose.

There is a related argument about income floors.

If automation reduces demand for some forms of labor, proposals for universal basic income, negative income taxes, wage subsidies, social dividends, expanded public services, or other mechanisms will receive more attention.

Each approach carries different incentives and values.

A universal cash payment is simple and preserves individual choice, but it requires financing and may do little about scarce goods whose prices rise with demand.

Targeted benefits can direct resources toward need but create administrative complexity and eligibility cliffs.

Wage subsidies support attachment to work but may indirectly support low-paying employers.

Public provision can expand access to health, education, housing, or care but depends on state capacity and can ration through queues when physical supply remains limited.

Social dividends tied to public ownership of productive assets can spread returns but raise hard questions about governance, risk, and capital allocation.

The politics of plenty will not be settled by one clever transfer mechanism.

The deeper question is who owns the abundance-producing capital.

Artificial intelligence is capital-intensive at the frontier.

Models require chips, data centers, energy, networks, talent, and large investments. The resulting services can have low marginal cost while ownership of the infrastructure remains concentrated.

This creates a familiar pattern: abundant output supported by scarce capital assets.

If competition is strong, providers may pass much of the productivity gain to users through lower prices and better services.

If bottlenecks create durable market power, owners can capture more of the gain.

Antitrust, interoperability, open models, procurement, infrastructure policy, intellectual-property rules, and capital markets all influence this balance.

There is no single correct degree of concentration for every layer.

Large scale can be efficient where fixed costs are enormous.

Fragmentation can protect competition and resilience.

Open systems can accelerate diffusion.

Closed systems can sometimes support investment, safety controls, and coherent products.

Policy will involve tradeoffs rather than slogans.

The abundance lens clarifies the question.

Where is competition actually constrained?

If models become interchangeable, attention should move to chips, cloud contracts, distribution, proprietary data, workflow integration, identity, and customer switching costs.

If compute becomes abundant, another layer will matter.

Regulation that stares at yesterday's bottleneck can miss tomorrow's power.

This is the political version of value migration.

Tax systems face the same challenge.

Governments tax things that are visible and measurable.

Labor income.

Corporate profits.

Property.

Consumption.

Capital gains.

When value creation shifts toward intangible systems with high fixed costs and low marginal costs, political arguments about the tax base intensify.

Some will argue for taxing extraordinary rents where concentration produces them.

Others will warn that heavy taxes on frontier investment can slow capital formation and push activity elsewhere.

Some will propose taxes linked to automation or computational resources.

Others will argue that taxing the technology directly penalizes productivity and that ordinary income, consumption, or profit taxes are more neutral.

The right answer will depend on what exactly is being taxed and why.

A tax designed to raise revenue is different from a tax designed to slow adoption.

A tax on monopoly rent is different from a tax on a competitive input.

Politics becomes confused when these objectives are blended.

The same applies to regulation of employment.

Protecting workers from arbitrary automated decisions can be valuable.

Requiring a human to perform every automatable step can preserve cost without preserving dignity.

A good rule protects the human interest rather than the old procedure.

The worker may need a right to explanation, appeal, notice, compensation, privacy, or bargaining.

They may not need a rule requiring a person to manually type what a machine can type safely.

Institutions should defend people, not inefficiency.

This distinction will be difficult politically because jobs are bundles.

A rule preserving one task can preserve an occupation for a while.

A rule protecting income or transition without preserving the task may feel less secure even if it is economically healthier in the long run.

There will be real cases where slowing automation is justified because systems are unsafe, institutions are unprepared, or transition would impose unacceptable risk.

There will also be cases where “safety” becomes a language for protecting incumbents from competition.

The politics of plenty needs the maturity to tell them apart.

Public-sector use of AI will create another set of conflicts.

Government has enormous quantities of administrative work that could become cheaper.

Applications can be explained.

Documents can be classified.

Fraud signals can be detected.

Benefits can be routed.

Regulations can be searched.

Citizens can receive assistance in multiple languages.

Caseworkers can spend less time on paperwork.

The upside is large because administrative friction falls heavily on people with the least time and power to navigate it.

The risk is also large because the state possesses coercive authority.

A commercial recommendation can be ignored.

A government decision may affect liberty, benefits, taxes, immigration status, permits, or legal rights.

Efficiency cannot replace due process.

The public needs routes for appeal, explanations appropriate to the stakes, records of how decisions were made, and accountable officials who retain authority to correct the system.

A government should be able to use abundant intelligence without creating unaccountable abundant power.

This may require more institutional investment than private deployment.

Public systems serve people who cannot simply choose another provider.

That asymmetry raises the standard.

Geopolitics adds another layer.

If artificial intelligence becomes a general-purpose input into economic and military capacity, countries will care about access to chips, energy, models, data, talent, and infrastructure.

A technology can be abundant to consumers inside one country while remaining strategically scarce between countries.

Governments may subsidize domestic capacity, restrict exports, secure supply chains, and treat data centers as strategic infrastructure.

These policies can increase resilience and also increase cost.

Allies can cooperate and also compete.

Open diffusion can spread productivity and also spread capabilities governments consider dangerous.

The politics of plenty exists inside a world where access is never purely economic.

Power can deliberately recreate scarcity.

This is important.

Abundance is not a natural state that, once achieved, persists automatically.

Access can be restricted by law, ownership, geography, conflict, pricing, identity, standards, or technical control.

A capability can be cheap to produce and still expensive to obtain.

That gap creates political stakes around openness.

Who is allowed to use the system?

For what?

Under whose rules?

Can a small competitor access the same capabilities as an incumbent?

Can a researcher inspect the model?

Can a citizen run a local system privately?

Can a country obtain the necessary hardware?

Different societies will draw these boundaries differently.

Some will emphasize innovation and decentralized access.

Others will emphasize control, safety, sovereignty, or industrial policy.

Most will try to do several at once and discover the objectives conflict.

The politics of artificial abundance will therefore be less about whether abundance is good than about the terms on which it arrives.

Who owns the fixed capital?

Who controls the distribution layer?

Who receives the productivity gain?

Who pays for displaced routines and broken business models?

Which human rights remain invariant under personalization?

Which decisions stay accountable to people?

Which public goods become easier to provide?

Which physical scarcities become more painful by comparison?

Which capabilities remain open?

Which are restricted?

These are political questions because there is no technical answer everyone has reason to accept.

The abundance itself can make politics more contentious for one final reason.

People compare outcomes to what now seems possible.

Scarcity is tolerated differently when it appears unavoidable.

Once a system can provide high-quality individualized assistance for pennies, expensive administrative incompetence feels less natural.

Once a small team can build extraordinary software, bloated procurement looks stranger.

Once analysis is cheap, governments have less excuse for not examining tradeoffs carefully.

Once translation is cheap, language exclusion becomes harder to defend.

Once access to expertise expands, inherited professional barriers receive more scrutiny.

Abundance raises expectations of institutions.

That can improve politics.

It can also fuel anger when reality fails to match the technically feasible world.

The gap between possible and delivered becomes politically salient.

The abundance economy will create extraordinary wealth if its capabilities diffuse widely and improve real productivity.

Whether that wealth produces broad confidence or concentrated resentment is not a model capability question.

It is a political achievement.

Plenty does not remove the need to choose.

It gives society more to choose about.
