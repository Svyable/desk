# Reality Gets Expensive

A digital room can be copied.

A hotel room cannot.

The distinction sounds trivial until a large share of economic output becomes easier to generate digitally.

A model can create another explanation, another image, another design, another piece of software, another itinerary, another conversation, another simulation, another song-shaped object, another plan.

The physical world remains stubborn.

There is still one table by the window.

One apartment on this block.

One surgery slot at nine in the morning.

One seat in the front row.

One parcel of land overlooking the water.

One person who can be in one place at one time.

Artificial abundance does not abolish scarcity.

It changes where scarcity is easiest to feel.

The richer the digital world becomes, the stranger physical constraints can seem.

A person accustomed to instant answers becomes less patient with a six-month permit.

A company that can prototype a product overnight still waits for a factory tool.

A designer can render a perfect building in seconds while the actual building requires concrete, steel, crews, inspections, financing, weather, deliveries, and years.

A patient can receive a sophisticated explanation immediately and still wait months for a specialist appointment.

The mismatch grows.

Cognition moves quickly.

Atoms do not.

This is not because the physical world lacks technology.

Manufacturing, logistics, construction, agriculture, transportation, medicine, and energy have become dramatically more productive over time. Robotics and automation will continue to push these boundaries. Better planning systems can reduce delays. Better designs can cut material. Autonomous machines can extend labor. Digital twins can improve maintenance. AI can help route trucks, schedule crews, discover materials, design factories, and operate complex equipment.

Physical productivity can rise.

But physical output contains constraints that digital output does not.

Matter has to be moved.

Energy has to be supplied.

Machines wear.

Land has location.

Bodies need recovery.

Weather changes plans.

Safety imposes tolerances.

A factory has throughput.

A restaurant has tables.

A stadium has seats.

A day has daylight.

The marginal cost of one more physical experience rarely approaches zero in the same way as one more digital variation.

This creates a relative price effect.

If machine-generated cognitive goods become dramatically cheaper while physical goods improve more slowly, the physical share of what people value can become more expensive relative to the digital share.

The concert ticket rises while recorded music becomes nearly limitless.

The desirable neighborhood remains scarce while virtual environments multiply.

The good restaurant still has forty seats while recipes, reviews, and generated food imagery become abundant.

The skilled tradesperson's visit remains scheduled while diagnostic advice becomes instant.

The child-care hour remains one hour of another person's life while educational content becomes infinite.

Reality acquires a premium.

This does not mean all physical goods rise in price.

Manufactured goods can become cheaper through automation. Robots may reduce the cost of production dramatically in some sectors. New energy systems may lower input costs. Construction methods may improve. Autonomous vehicles may change transportation economics.

The premium attaches most strongly to physical things with location, time, human presence, biological limits, regulation, or irreproducibility.

A generic object can be mass-produced.

A specific place cannot.

A generic chair can be manufactured by the million.

The chair in a small restaurant at 8 p.m. on Friday cannot.

This is why location becomes more interesting in a digitally abundant world.

The internet weakened the importance of location for many information tasks. A person could work, learn, buy, publish, and communicate without being near the old institutional center.

Artificial intelligence extends this liberation.

A small-town founder can access sophisticated cognitive tools.

A student can receive high-level tutoring far from a university.

A rural doctor can use systems trained on knowledge from around the world.

A business can reach global customers without a large headquarters.

That should reduce the value of proximity for some work.

At the same time, it can increase the relative value of places people want for non-digital reasons.

Beauty.

Climate.

Safety.

Community.

Schools.

Walkability.

Culture.

Access to nature.

Friends.

A city may matter less because it contains the only office capable of employing you and more because you actually want to live there.

This is a different kind of geographic premium.

It is not access to information.

It is access to life in a particular place.

The same applies to events.

A digital performance can be replayed perfectly.

A live performance happens once.

Its imperfections help establish its scarcity.

The singer's voice is slightly different tonight.

The crowd reacts.

The actor misses a beat and recovers.

The game develops in a way no script guaranteed.

The meal arrives under the conditions of this evening.

The value includes the fact that the event cannot be cloned backward after it occurs.

You were either there or you were not.

Artificial abundance may make this feeling more valuable because so much else becomes revisable.

A generated image can be regenerated.

A document can be rewritten endlessly.

A virtual environment can restore from a checkpoint.

A conversation with a machine can begin again.

Physical events resist this flexibility.

Time leaves a mark.

This can create a market for irreversibility.

The wedding.

The game.

The dinner.

The performance.

The trip.

The conversation that happened in one room among people who will never again be exactly who they were that night.

These things were always scarce.

Digital abundance makes the scarcity more legible.

There is an economic irony here.

Technology can make people richer in available experiences while increasing willingness to pay for experiences that do not scale.

A person with unlimited recorded music may spend heavily to see one artist live.

A person with endless food content may pay for a tasting menu where the kitchen serves only a few people.

A person surrounded by synthetic imagery may travel to see a landscape directly.

A person with a perfect remote collaboration system may still fly across the country for one meeting because being physically present changes the relationship.

The scalable good expands demand for the unscalable complement.

This is not guaranteed.

Virtual experiences may substitute for physical ones more than romantics expect. A convincing simulation can satisfy many purposes at lower cost and lower environmental burden. Remote work can spare people commuting and relocation. Telemedicine can eliminate unnecessary trips. Digital companionship can help people who are isolated. Virtual travel can provide education to people who could never visit the place.

Substitution will be real.

So will complementarity.

People photograph more after cameras become abundant and still travel to photogenic places.

People stream sports and still fill stadiums.

People video-call and still value reunion.

A good digital substitute can reduce the need for physical presence in ordinary moments while making selected physical moments more intentional.

This is the pattern to watch.

Not “physical beats digital.”

Digital handles the routine.

Physical becomes concentrated where presence matters.

This has consequences for cities, businesses, and personal budgets.

A company may spend less on permanent office space and more on fewer, higher-value gatherings.

A university may deliver more content digitally and place greater emphasis on laboratories, seminars, mentorship, community, and other things that justify being on campus.

A retailer may sell more efficiently online and treat physical stores as places for discovery, service, events, and trust.

A medical system may move routine monitoring into homes while reserving scarce facilities for procedures and complex care.

A family may automate household administration and spend more on travel, sport, shared meals, and care.

The composition of value changes.

This is where wealth distribution becomes important.

Digital abundance can democratize access to many cognitive goods because replication is cheap.

Physical scarcity can remain brutally unequal.

Everyone may have access to excellent tutoring while good housing remains scarce.

Everyone may have sophisticated health information while specialist appointments remain scarce.

Everyone may have virtual access to beautiful environments while safe parks remain unevenly distributed.

Everyone may have machine assistance while child care remains expensive.

Abundance in one layer can make unresolved scarcity in another layer more politically visible.

It becomes harder to explain why a society can generate a personalized plan in seconds but cannot build enough homes in desirable places.

The technology does not solve zoning, infrastructure, land ownership, construction productivity, local politics, financing, or environmental constraints merely because it makes the problem easier to analyze.

This matters for public policy.

A country can become cognitively abundant and still feel materially constrained.

People live in houses, not forecasts.

They ride transportation systems, not simulations.

They need electricity, water, food, medicine, care, and physical safety.

The most consequential applications of artificial intelligence may therefore be the ones that escape the screen and improve these physical systems.

Better grid planning.

Faster materials discovery.

More efficient construction.

Improved logistics.

More productive agriculture.

Robotics that extends scarce labor.

Medical systems that increase actual treatment capacity rather than merely generating advice.

The digital abundance is most powerful when it pushes through a physical bottleneck.

That will take time.

A model update can deploy to millions of people overnight.

A new factory cannot.

A software patch can copy instantly.

A new transmission line cannot.

A new drug may be discovered computationally and still require physical trials, manufacturing, distribution, monitoring, and trust.

A new building technique still has to meet codes and survive weather.

This difference in speed will define much of the transition.

People will experience extraordinary abundance in some categories and infuriating scarcity in others.

The contrast may be psychologically harsher than ordinary scarcity because the fast layer teaches us to expect responsiveness.

Why does this take months?

Because reality has a queue.

The abundance economy will not abolish that sentence.

It will move it.

Away from many forms of information and generation.

Toward land, bodies, energy, materials, time, and presence.

Reality gets expensive not because technology stops progressing.

It gets expensive because everything that can be copied cheaply makes the things that cannot be copied stand out.
