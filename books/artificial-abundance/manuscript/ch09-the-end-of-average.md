# The End of Average

The average person is one of the great inventions of scale.

Not a real person. A planning device.

The average customer. The average student. The average patient. The average voter. The average employee. The average reader. The average commute. The average household.

Modern institutions use averages because they have to make decisions before meeting every individual case. A shoe factory needs sizes. A school district needs a curriculum. An insurer needs risk categories. A transit system needs routes. A publisher needs to imagine a reader. A software company needs defaults.

The average is not foolish. It is compression.

A complicated population becomes manageable when some of its variation can be summarized.

The problem begins when a compression method is mistaken for a person.

Nobody has 2.3 children. Almost nobody has the exact average body, income, reading level, schedule, risk profile, taste, knowledge, or set of constraints represented in a planning model. The average can describe a group while fitting no member of the group especially well.

Industrial systems learned to live with this because customization was expensive.

A textbook could not be rewritten for every student. A hospital could not assign a permanent team of specialists to every patient. A software company could not build a different interface for every customer. A bank could not have a senior analyst personally design the perfect explanation for every borrower. A retailer could not stock an infinite store.

So institutions created a middle.

The middle was often good enough.

Artificial abundance makes “good enough for the middle” less inevitable.

This does not mean averages disappear from statistics. It means fewer products and services have to be built around the fiction that the same version should fit everybody.

The distinction matters.

An average can remain useful for understanding a population while becoming less useful for serving one person.

A doctor may need population-level evidence to know how a treatment performs across thousands of cases. The patient in front of the doctor still has one particular body, history, set of medications, goals, fears, and tolerance for risk.

A teacher may need grade-level benchmarks to understand whether a class is progressing. The student asking why negative numbers multiply into a positive answer still needs an explanation that reaches this student.

A company may need aggregate customer data to decide whether a product works. The person contacting support has one exact configuration and one exact problem.

For most of modern history, the institution moved from the aggregate toward the individual by spending human attention.

A teacher personalized a lesson by staying after class.

A doctor personalized care during a visit.

A banker personalized a loan by assigning an officer.

A travel agent personalized a trip by spending time with the traveler.

A consultant personalized advice by billing hours.

Specificity was labor.

Now some specificity can be generated.

That changes the minimum economic scale at which individualized treatment becomes possible.

A small company can produce different onboarding material for different customers. A public agency can explain the same benefit in different languages and levels of detail. A school can generate practice problems around one student's errors. A piece of software can reorganize help around the task actually being attempted. A researcher can ask for a literature explanation at exactly the level needed to cross into an adjacent field.

The service can bend without employing another person for every bend.

This could be one of the broadest consumer benefits of artificial abundance because a large amount of frustration comes from being forced to translate yourself into somebody else's average.

The form asks a question that does not fit your situation.

The call-center script assumes the common case.

The tutorial explains the part you already understand and skips the part you do not.

The product has ten settings, none of which match the way you work.

The insurance category treats two very different risks as equivalent because the administrative system cannot afford finer distinctions.

The school lesson moves on because the class has moved on.

The frustration is not always incompetence. It is often the cost of serving many people with one system.

Cheap adaptive intelligence lowers that cost.

But there is a trap in celebrating the end of average.

Some averages protect people.

Standardization is sometimes a defense against arbitrary treatment.

A public benefit should not become larger or smaller because a persuasive system decides one applicant seems more sympathetic. A criminal sentence should not become a personalized product. A worker should not discover that every colleague has secretly been shown a different policy. A medical system should not use personalization as a pleasant word for giving expensive care to people predicted to be profitable and cheaper care to everyone else.

The individual fit can become individualized power.

When systems can adapt at low cost, institutions gain more capacity to differentiate.

Differentiation is not automatically fairness.

The same technology that can recognize a person's unusual needs can recognize a person's unusual willingness to pay.

The retailer can tailor the explanation.

It can also tailor the pressure.

The lender can tailor the guidance.

It can also discover exactly which presentation makes an unfavorable loan feel acceptable.

The political campaign can tailor the argument.

It can also make incompatible promises to different audiences.

The employer can tailor training.

It can also tailor the rules in ways that make unequal treatment difficult to see.

The end of average therefore creates a governance problem alongside a service opportunity.

Which parts of a system should be personalized?

Which parts should remain common?

A good answer usually separates interface from entitlement.

The explanation can vary. The underlying right should not.

The learning path can vary. The standard for the credential may need to remain comparable.

The software layout can vary. The security policy cannot depend on whether the interface believes you dislike confirmation steps.

The medical explanation can vary. The evidence threshold cannot quietly fall because a patient responds well to reassurance.

This returns us to the invariants from the previous chapter.

Personalization is safest when the stable core is explicit.

The more a system can vary, the more important it becomes to know what variation is not allowed to change.

There is another reason to resist a simplistic end-of-average story.

Human beings often want common things.

We buy the same shoe not because the factory forced us into statistical mediocrity but because the shoe is good. We learn a common language because communication requires overlap. We use standardized screw threads because machines need parts that fit. We drive on the same side of the road because individualized traffic rules would be fatal.

Some standardization is a triumph of coordination rather than a failure of imagination.

The abundance economy will have to distinguish between averages created by production constraints and standards created by shared purpose.

That distinction can be subtle.

Consider education.

A student may benefit enormously from individualized explanations, examples, pacing, and practice. But a school is not only a machine for maximizing each student's individual comprehension. It is also a social institution. Students learn to wait, argue, collaborate, compare interpretations, encounter people who do not resemble them, and participate in common rituals.

Perfectly personalized instruction can produce a strange educational world in which every student receives exactly the material predicted to suit them and fewer students encounter the same difficult object together.

The same question appears in culture.

A music service can learn your preferences and generate a song engineered around them. A news service can summarize every event in the style you find easiest to accept. A film can be altered to preserve the character you love. A game can tune itself to your frustration threshold.

The product becomes increasingly fitted to the consumer.

What happens to the consumer's ability to be unfitted?

A common work has the useful property of not caring what you wanted.

The book is the book.

The argument is the argument.

The symphony does not skip the difficult movement because your attention wandered.

A museum does not replace the painting because another image would test better for your demographic.

Other minds enter our lives partly through their refusal to become us.

This may create a premium for unpersonalized culture even as personalized culture becomes plentiful.

The fixed artifact becomes evidence of another point of view.

There is a labor-market version of the same shift.

Companies have always written jobs around bundles of tasks because managing thousands of unique arrangements was difficult. A role was a standardized package. The employee adapted to the job description.

If organizations gain cheap systems for decomposing, routing, supporting, and coordinating work, some roles may become less standardized.

One person may spend more time on customer judgment, another on technical review, another on relationship building, even if all three once carried the same title. A small firm may create a role around a person's unusual combination of abilities because artificial systems absorb the routine pieces needed to make the combination economically useful.

This could expand the range of viable careers.

The industrial job often asks, “Can you fit the slot?”

An abundance organization can ask, “What useful slot can be built around what you are unusually good at?”

That is a more humane possibility.

It is also less legible.

Standardized jobs make labor markets easier to navigate. A nurse, electrician, accountant, teacher, or machinist carries a category that communicates something about training and capability. Highly individualized roles may fit workers better while making credentials, compensation, promotion, and mobility harder to compare.

The average is a blunt instrument, but it is a shared instrument.

Remove it and institutions need new ways to create comparability.

This is why the end of average will not look like the disappearance of categories.

It will look like pressure on categories to justify themselves.

Which category exists because reality genuinely groups cases together?

Which exists because the old system could not afford to notice the difference?

A clothing size exists because bodies differ in predictable dimensions and manufacturing needs tolerances. A single customer-support script may exist mostly because writing individualized guidance used to cost too much.

A standard dosage may reflect tested evidence. A standard explanation of the dosage may reflect administrative convenience.

A shared exam may provide comparability. A shared worksheet may simply be cheap.

Artificial abundance turns these distinctions into economic questions rather than philosophical ones because the cost of individualized treatment falls.

Once variation is affordable, sameness needs another reason.

The most interesting companies and institutions will not eliminate the average.

They will use it at the right layer.

Population averages to understand groups.

Standards where coordination, safety, rights, and comparability require them.

Personalization where the individual experience can improve without corrupting the common rule.

This is harder than declaring that every person deserves a bespoke world.

A bespoke world can become lonely, manipulative, opaque, and impossible to audit.

A standardized world can become frustrating, exclusionary, and wasteful.

The abundance economy will live between those failures.

The deeper change is psychological.

People raised inside mass systems learn to accept mismatch as normal.

The form is confusing because forms are confusing.

The software makes you learn its terminology because software does that.

The textbook loses you because textbooks are written for classes.

The service does not understand your case because you are unusual.

As adaptive systems improve, tolerance for that mismatch will fall.

A customer who has experienced software that explains itself in context will become less patient with a product that sends them into a generic help center.

A student who has experienced useful individualized practice may find one-size-fits-all homework stranger.

A citizen who can receive a public explanation in clear language may ask why official communication was ever written as if obscurity were a legal requirement.

Abundance changes expectations before it changes every institution.

That matters because expectations are sticky in one direction.

Once people learn that a service can notice them, being treated as an average can feel less like efficiency and more like neglect.

The economic promise is not that every person becomes infinitely special.

It is that fewer people have to be poorly served merely because recognizing their difference was too expensive.

That is a real form of abundance.

It also raises the standard for what remains common.

If an institution still gives everyone the same thing after individualized alternatives become cheap, the sameness will need a reason.

Safety.

Fairness.

Coordination.

Shared culture.

Comparability.

A deliberate point of view.

Those are stronger reasons than “we could only afford one version.”

The average will survive.

But it will lose one of its oldest defenses.
