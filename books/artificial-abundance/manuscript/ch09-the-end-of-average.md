# The End of Average

The average customer has always been a suspicious person.

She has 2.3 children, a statistically ordinary income, a predictable tolerance for complexity, and just enough knowledge to understand the instructions written for everyone. No one has met her. Entire institutions have nevertheless been designed around her.

The reason was not stupidity. It was cost.

When one teacher had thirty students, one lesson had to do a great deal of work. When a bank printed a million copies of a disclosure, the language had to survive a million different readers. When software was expensive to build, the interface could not be reinvented for every person who opened it. When a consultant's hour was costly, advice had to be rationed toward clients who could afford enough hours to make customization worthwhile.

The average was often the cheapest place to stand.

Artificial intelligence does not abolish averages. It changes the price of departing from them.

That distinction matters because the slogan is much more dramatic than the economics. A world with cheap adaptive generation will still contain standards, medians, segments, protocols, common curricula, common laws, and common products. Some forms of sameness are valuable precisely because people need to coordinate. The width of a screw thread should not depend on the mood of the buyer. A tax rule should not become a different rule because its explanation has been personalized. A surgical checklist earns much of its value by refusing to improvise.

But a surprising amount of standardization was never sacred. It was an invoice.

Consider explanation. A good human tutor notices the moment a student has lost the thread. The tutor backs up, changes the example, asks a question, discovers that the problem is not fractions but vocabulary, and tries again. This is ordinary teaching and expensive teaching. It requires attention that cannot be copied across thirty conversations at once.

A generated explanation is cheap enough to be discarded.

That may be one of the most consequential facts about it. The student does not need to accept the first explanation because somebody spent two weeks writing the textbook. The customer does not need to decode the help article because the company cannot afford another support call. The employee does not need to sit through the same training module as everyone else because producing another route through the material is costly.

The version can fail and be replaced.

In the previous chapter, infinite versions created a governance problem: when variation becomes cheap, institutions have to decide what must remain invariant. The other side of that problem is liberation from the unnecessary average. Once the stable core has been identified, the surface around it can bend.

The interesting evidence is not that AI makes everyone excellent. It does not. It is that the effects can be large and uneven at the same time.

In a randomized experiment with 758 consultants at Boston Consulting Group, researchers gave some participants access to GPT-4 and compared their performance with colleagues working without it. On tasks that fell inside what the researchers called the system's capability frontier, AI users completed more tasks, worked faster, and produced higher-quality results. The gains were not confined to the people who had already been strongest. Participants who began below the performance threshold saw especially large improvements.

Then the experiment changed the task.

On a problem outside the model's reliable frontier, people using AI were more likely to produce an incorrect answer.

This is a useful antidote to two fantasies at once. The first fantasy says the tool simply lifts everyone by the same amount, preserving the old hierarchy at a higher level. The second says the tool erases differences and turns every worker into an expert. Neither describes a jagged frontier.

Cheap intelligence can compress some performance differences while creating new differences in knowing when not to use it.

The average therefore weakens in one place and reappears in another.

A mediocre first draft may become nearly free. A competent explanation may become nearly free. A customized spreadsheet formula, translation, sales email, lesson, code snippet, or summary may become available to people who would never have purchased the corresponding professional hour.

But access to generation does not make every surrounding institution adaptive.

A six-month randomized field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers is instructive here. Workers given access to Microsoft's generative AI tools who actually adopted them spent roughly two fewer hours per week on email. That is real time. Yet the researchers did not detect broad changes in the quantity or composition of work from individual access alone.

The worker changed faster than the organization.

This should be familiar. Offices contain many things that are not individual production problems. Meetings exist because people need agreement. Approval chains exist because somebody owns a risk. Budgets exist because resources remain scarce. Departments defend territory. Customers have contracts. Regulators require records. Managers want visibility. A person can write a memo in four minutes and still wait four days for the meeting in which the memo is discussed.

The average is sometimes embedded in coordination rather than production.

A company may be technically capable of giving every customer a unique experience and still choose three service tiers because accounting, support, pricing, compliance, and sales need a shared object to talk about. A school may be able to generate a different lesson for every child and still need a common curriculum because students move between classrooms and society has an interest in some shared knowledge. A hospital may personalize explanations while standardizing the protocol behind them.

This is why the end of average will arrive unevenly.

Digital surfaces will move first because their marginal variation is cheapest. Explanations, drafts, recommendations, interfaces, simulations, and configurations can change without moving a truck or retraining a factory. Physical products will change more slowly where variation creates inventory, tooling, certification, repair, or supply-chain costs. Institutions will change slowest where the average is doing political or organizational work that is easy to mistake for a production constraint.

The distinction creates an uncomfortable question for any standardized service: what is the standard protecting?

Sometimes the answer is fairness.

A common admissions rule can prevent a decision-maker from quietly inventing harsher standards for disfavored applicants. A standard medical protocol can protect patients from improvisation. A common price can reduce the opportunity to charge each buyer the maximum amount an algorithm believes that person will tolerate.

Sometimes the answer is legibility. People can compare two products because the products share recognizable features. Employees can transfer skills because the workflow does not mutate around every individual. Citizens can contest a government decision because there is a rule that was supposed to apply to everyone.

And sometimes the answer is simply that nobody could afford to make another version.

Those cases will be harder to defend.

If a person can receive a tax explanation in language she understands without changing the tax rule, why force her through prose written for a professional accountant? If software can expose complexity gradually, why make a first-time user confront every control needed by an expert? If training can spend more time on what an employee does not understand, why measure seriousness by hours spent inside a standardized module?

Artificial abundance makes these questions cheaper to test.

It also makes manipulation cheaper.

A service adapted to your needs can become a service adapted to your weaknesses. The company that knows you need a simpler explanation may also know which explanation makes a fee seem smaller. The political campaign that can answer your specific concern can also avoid saying the same thing to your neighbor. The insurer that can tailor guidance can potentially tailor discouragement.

Personalization is not automatically humane. It depends on whose objective is being optimized and which invariants survive the adaptation.

That is why the economic end of average should not become the moral end of common treatment.

We will need to become more precise about which averages are crude compromises and which are social commitments.

The crude compromises are vulnerable. A generic document written because custom writing was expensive. A generic lesson created because attention was scarce. A generic interface built because software teams could not anticipate every path. A generic analysis sold because professional time had to be rationed.

As the cost of variation falls, these products lose part of the scarcity that supported their price.

The social commitments are different. Equal treatment under a rule. Interoperable standards. Shared facts. Common records. Procedures designed so that power cannot quietly customize itself.

Those may become more valuable as everything around them becomes fluid.

The end of average, then, is not a destination where everyone receives a perfectly bespoke world. It is a repricing event. We are beginning to discover how much sameness we purchased because difference was expensive.

Some of that sameness will disappear quickly. Some will survive because institutions are slower than tools. Some will survive because it deserves to.

And as competent output becomes easier to tailor, another old source of scarcity comes under pressure.

For a long time, the person who knew how to produce the competent version was called an expert.
