# Stay Expensively Human

There is bad advice hiding inside every technological transition.

It tells people to become whatever the machine cannot do.

The machine can calculate, so become creative.

The machine can write, so become empathetic.

The machine can reason, so become physical.

The machine can generate, so become authentic.

This advice ages badly because the machine keeps moving.

A strategy built around permanent technical incapability is fragile.

Artificial intelligence will become better at creativity, conversation, persuasion, planning, design, coding, diagnosis, tutoring, and many other things people once described as uniquely human.

Robotics will become better too.

The safer strategy is not to search for a shrinking list of tasks machines cannot perform.

It is to understand which human scarcities remain valuable even when machines can imitate the surface of the task.

This is what it means to stay expensively human.

Not inefficient for the sake of nostalgia.

Not hostile to tools.

Not proud of doing manually what a machine can do better.

Use the abundance.

Then spend the saved human capacity where human scarcity creates value.

The first place is attention.

Do not compete with machines on volume.

You will lose.

A machine can read more, write more, monitor more, answer more, generate more, and remain available longer.

The human advantage is not producing one more unit.

It is deciding what deserves a unit of finite attention.

This means becoming more selective as production becomes easier.

Read fewer things more deeply when depth matters.

Run fewer projects after exploration has revealed the best ones.

Send fewer messages that impose attention on others.

Do not confuse the ability to create ten options with an obligation to show ten options.

Let the machine absorb the combinatorial explosion before the output reaches another person.

Compression becomes courtesy.

A good use of AI saves not only your time but other people's time.

The second place is judgment.

Use machines to widen the search before you narrow it.

Ask for alternatives you would not have considered.

Ask what evidence contradicts your view.

Ask which assumptions are carrying the conclusion.

Ask what failure would look like.

Ask what a person with different incentives would notice.

Then stop asking.

Judgment requires a moment when the generation ends and the decision begins.

Do not let one more analysis become a substitute for owning the choice.

Write down why you chose before the outcome is known.

Return later and compare belief with reality.

This is how judgment compounds.

The machine can give you more repetitions only if you preserve the feedback.

Without feedback, abundance becomes entertainment for the intellect.

The third place is responsibility.

Be careful what you sign.

This advice becomes more important as systems can produce more than you can inspect.

Do not become the person whose name sits under automated work nobody meaningfully owns.

If you are responsible, demand the authority, time, visibility, and controls required to make responsibility real.

If you delegate, define what remains yours.

If a system can act autonomously, decide where it must stop.

If the organization wants the leverage of automation, make sure it also accepts the exposure.

Responsibility is expensive because it creates consequences.

That expense is part of the value.

A professional willing to stand behind a decision has something different from a system willing to produce an answer.

The fourth place is relationship.

Let machines handle coordination around relationships without quietly replacing the relationships.

Use the reminder to make the call.

Use the scheduling agent to find the evening.

Use the translation tool to cross the language gap.

Use the assistant to remember the name of the restaurant your friend mentioned.

Then be there.

Do not automate every expression of care merely because care can be rendered convincingly.

A birthday message generated perfectly and sent automatically may satisfy etiquette while removing the small act of remembering another person.

Sometimes that is fine. Nobody can sustain intimate attention to everyone they know.

But know which relationships you intend to keep expensive.

Time with a child.

The difficult conversation with a partner.

The visit to an aging parent.

The friend whose crisis does not fit your calendar.

The employee whose problem requires listening rather than routing.

The machine can make these interactions more informed.

It cannot make your presence non-scarce.

The fifth place is taste.

Consume enough good work that your preferences are not trained entirely by recommendation systems.

Taste requires exposure beyond comfort.

Read old things.

Look at work that initially confuses you.

Learn why a boring design survives.

Notice the difference between novelty and quality.

Make something badly before asking the machine to make it well.

Compare versions and articulate why one belongs.

The purpose is not to become a critic of everything.

It is to build an internal standard strong enough that abundance does not collapse into acceptance of whatever looks finished.

A person with no taste receives infinite options as noise.

A person with taste receives a search space.

The sixth place is skill.

Do not preserve every obsolete manual capability.

That would be cosplay for a previous economy.

Preserve the capabilities that let you understand, verify, recover, or choose.

A programmer should know enough to recognize dangerous code even if an agent writes most of it.

A manager should know enough about the work to challenge an automated plan.

A student should learn enough arithmetic to reason about quantities even if calculation is automated.

A writer should be able to form a sentence and an argument even if a system can draft both.

A citizen should understand enough about institutions to notice when an explanation is hiding a choice.

The right baseline will change as tools improve.

The principle is stable.

Do not outsource the capability required to supervise the outsourcing.

The seventh place is the physical world.

Touch things.

This is not mystical.

Reality supplies feedback that representations omit.

Build something.

Repair something.

Cook.

Garden.

Exercise.

Learn the route through a place by walking it.

Sit with another person without converting the encounter into content.

Watch what customers actually do.

Visit the site described in the spreadsheet.

Use the product you manage.

Artificial intelligence is powerful partly because it compresses representations of enormous amounts of human experience.

Compression is not contact.

The world remains the final test.

People who stay in contact with it will notice when generated plausibility has drifted away from consequence.

The eighth place is difficulty.

Choose some things that remain hard.

Not because suffering is noble.

Because capability, health, craft, and confidence require resistance.

The machine can remove resistance faster than you can decide which resistance mattered.

Protect a few practices from automatic completion.

Write before asking for rewrite.

Attempt before asking for answer.

Navigate sometimes before asking for route.

Exercise without optimizing every minute.

Learn a skill with enough repetition that failure becomes informative rather than merely inconvenient.

The chosen difficulty should have a reason.

If you cannot name the reason, automate it.

The ninth place is commitment.

Abundance keeps options open.

A person can always generate another route, another career plan, another partner profile, another business idea, another design, another city, another version of the future.

Infinite optionality can become a way of never entering a life.

Some value appears only after alternatives are closed.

A craft becomes meaningful after years.

A relationship deepens after novelty.

A community becomes yours after repeated return.

A company develops a culture after choices accumulate.

A place becomes home after other places stop competing every morning.

Intelligent systems will become excellent at showing what else is possible.

A human life still requires saying: this one.

Commitment is expensive because it sacrifices optionality.

That sacrifice is part of why commitments mean something.

The tenth place is authorship.

Keep a layer of goals above the machinery.

An assistant can optimize your calendar.

You decide what deserves time.

An investment system can optimize a portfolio.

You decide what risks you are willing to carry and what the money is for.

A health system can optimize biomarkers.

You decide what kind of life the health is meant to support.

A business system can optimize profit.

You decide what the business will not do for profit.

A recommendation system can predict what you will like.

You decide whether preference is the right reason to choose.

This is not a one-time philosophical exercise.

Goals drift.

People drift.

Systems learn behavior faster than people articulate values.

The machinery can end up optimizing yesterday's self.

Agency requires periodic re-authoring.

What am I doing?

Why?

What has become easy enough that I should stop spending human time on it?

What has become easy enough that I now need to protect the human part more deliberately?

These questions are the personal economics of artificial abundance.

There is a business version.

Companies should stop building strategies around tasks that become cheap for everyone.

“We use AI” is not an advantage once everyone uses AI.

“We generate faster” is temporary if generation is commoditized.

The durable question is what the company owns next to abundance.

Trust.

Distribution.

Data created through real operations.

A physical network.

A community.

A regulatory position.

A brand with accumulated history.

A workflow customers depend on.

Responsibility for an outcome.

A feedback loop connecting generated possibilities to reality.

The same principle applies to careers.

Do not become merely the person who knows how to ask the current tool for output.

Tool fluency matters, but interfaces change.

Become the person who can define the problem, choose the standard, test the result, understand the context, negotiate the tradeoff, and carry the consequence.

Use AI deeply enough that you know what it is good at.

Know it well enough to stop performing tasks that the system can do reliably.

Then use the freed capacity to move upward or outward into the complement.

This does not mean everyone becomes a manager.

The complement may be physical work.

It may be care.

It may be sales.

It may be scientific judgment.

It may be craft.

It may be field operations.

It may be entrepreneurship.

It may be teaching.

It may be community leadership.

The important move is away from defending scarcity that technology is destroying and toward building value around scarcity that remains real.

There is one final way to stay expensively human.

Do things that make poor sense if measured only as output.

Travel slowly enough to know where you went.

Cook for people when food could be delivered.

Learn the instrument instead of generating the song.

Sit through the performance instead of consuming the highlights.

Read the argument before reading the summary when the argument matters.

Make the object you could have bought.

Teach somebody in person when the relationship is part of the teaching.

Keep promises that no optimization model would call efficient.

Return to the same place.

Help when nobody is tracking the contribution.

These acts do not defeat artificial intelligence.

They are not supposed to.

They use abundance to clarify what efficiency is for.

A civilization does not become rich so that every human act can be converted into a faster human act.

The point of abundance is not infinite throughput.

It is freedom from unnecessary scarcity.

Freedom is only useful if something is chosen with it.

Stay expensively human means spending your nonrenewable resources on purpose.

Attention.

Time.

Responsibility.

Commitment.

Presence.

A life becomes valuable partly because these cannot be regenerated after they are spent.

Use the machines lavishly.

Use yourself carefully.
