# Stay Expensively Human

A handmade chair can be worse than a factory chair.

The joint can wobble. The finish can be uneven. The maker can misunderstand the grain, rush the sanding, or simply have a bad week.

No law of craftsmanship guarantees quality.

And yet people will sometimes pay more for the handmade chair.

They are not necessarily confused.

They may be buying something other than the chair's measurable performance.

The maker touched it. The object carries a story. The buyer knows who made it. The irregularity proves that the production process was not optimized to remove every trace of the person. The purchase supports a particular craft, place, relationship, or idea about how things should be made.

Those values can be sentimental, sophisticated, status-seeking, generous, irrational, or all of them at once.

Artificial abundance is going to produce more markets like this.

When competent output becomes cheap, human production may become expensive in a newly visible way.

Not because people suddenly become less efficient.

Because efficiency stops being the only thing on sale.

A letter written by a person who spent an hour thinking about you is a different object from a letter generated in four seconds, even if the two contain identical sentences.

A portrait painted over three weeks is a different transaction from an image generated after a prompt, even if the resulting picture is less visually impressive.

A teacher who sits with one student through confusion is providing something that cannot be measured entirely by the information transmitted.

A musician performing in a room full of people is not merely a sound-generation system with bad reproducibility.

The human process can become part of the product.

This is dangerous territory because the argument can turn into nostalgia almost immediately.

Humans do not deserve a premium for making something badly when a machine can make it well.

Effort is not quality.

Pain is not virtue.

Slowness is not depth.

A person who spends six hours manually formatting a report has not created six hours of meaning. A physician does not become more caring by refusing useful diagnostic tools. A craftsperson using a power tool does not betray the object. A writer using spellcheck is not less human than a writer using a dictionary.

The point is not to preserve inefficient production so people can prove that they suffered.

The point is to notice when the human involvement itself is what someone values.

That distinction is easier to see in art because art has always made process legible.

A 2024 experiment published in Scientific Reports asked participants to evaluate human-made and AI-generated images. Participants could not consistently identify which was which. In the selected set, the AI-generated images were actually liked more on average.

Then source belief complicated the result.

Images that participants believed were AI-generated tended to be evaluated less favorably, regardless of their actual source.

This is not evidence that human art is better.

It is evidence that origin can enter the valuation function separately from the artifact's visible qualities.

The buyer is judging two things.

What is this?

And how did it come to exist?

Industrial economies have spent two centuries teaching us to ignore the second question for many goods.

The bottle does not need a biography.

The screw does not need a childhood.

The spreadsheet formula does not become more useful because someone remembers typing it.

Standardization works partly by making origin irrelevant. One unit can substitute for another.

That is the triumph of the commodity.

Artificial abundance extends this logic into domains that once resisted it.

A competent illustration can be generated without a commissioned illustrator. A competent first draft can exist without a hired writer. A competent jingle can exist without a studio session. A competent software prototype can exist without the same number of engineering hours.

This does not make the human version worthless.

It forces the human version to reveal what else it was selling.

Sometimes the answer will be nothing.

That is worth saying plainly.

Some professional work was expensive because producing it required expensive human time, not because buyers cared who performed the work. If a machine can perform that work more cheaply, reliably, and safely, there may be no hidden human premium waiting to rescue the old price.

Few people will insist that a human manually calculate every payroll deduction because human arithmetic has soul.

A traveler may prefer instant translation to waiting for a human translator for a routine interaction.

A small business may prefer a generated product description to paying hundreds of dollars for copy that no customer will read closely.

A programmer may prefer a correct generated test to a manually written one whose authorship nobody values.

Human origin is not a business model by itself.

Where the premium survives, it will survive because origin changes the good.

Research on the handmade effect has described one version of this for years. People can attach additional value to handmade products because they infer care, personal expression, producer involvement, or responsibility from the making process.

The inference can be wrong.

A handmade label can be marketing theater. A maker can dislike the work. A factory worker can care deeply about quality. A machine-assisted process can contain more thoughtful human judgment than a nominally handmade one.

Still, markets often price symbols as well as functions.

A wedding ring is metal with a social layer.

A signed book is printed paper with a trace of contact.

A meal cooked by a friend is calories plus relationship.

A child's drawing is an objectively poor rendering of the family and an object a parent may save for fifty years.

The economic value does not reside only in the artifact.

It resides in the relationship between artifact, maker, and recipient.

Artificial abundance may make this relational layer easier to see because the functional layer becomes cheaper.

Consider a restaurant.

A great deal of restaurant work can be optimized. Reservations, inventory, purchasing, forecasting, scheduling, menu descriptions, marketing, accounting, and portions of preparation can all benefit from software and automation.

If those systems improve, the restaurant does not necessarily become less human.

It may have more capacity to spend on hospitality.

A host remembers why the table matters tonight. A server notices that the guest wants privacy. A cook adjusts a dish for the person in front of them. The room contains other people who have chosen to be there at the same time.

These are expensive properties because synchronized human presence is expensive.

The restaurant cannot copy Tuesday night and sell it again on Wednesday.

It can sell another dinner.

Not that dinner.

Live performance has the same structure.

Recorded music made sound abundant. Streaming made recorded music more abundant still. Musicians did not respond by trying to make recordings artificially scarce enough to restore the nineteenth century.

Live performance remained a separate good.

The concert is inefficient by digital standards. Thousands of people travel to one place at one time to hear music that can be reproduced with extraordinary fidelity anywhere.

The inefficiency is part of the point.

Everyone is there.

This is a different kind of scarcity from content scarcity.

It is event scarcity.

Human beings can only be in so many places.

A particular performer can only play so many shows. A particular teacher can only have so many deep conversations. A particular therapist can only hold so many sessions. A particular friend can only attend so many dinners.

Artificial intelligence can extend these people.

A teacher can create adaptive material. A performer can produce synthetic variations. A therapist can use tools between sessions. A friend can let software remember the birthday.

Extension is not identity.

The difference matters most when the buyer values attention from the particular person rather than merely an output associated with them.

This suggests that human premiums will become increasingly specific.

"Human-made" is too broad.

Which human property matters?

Presence?

Responsibility?

Original physical action?

Creative selection?

Conversation?

Witness?

Risk?

Care?

The right to say that this particular person chose to spend time on you?

These are different goods.

A medical patient may not care whether a human produced the first draft of the visit summary. The patient may care very much that a physician takes responsibility for a difficult decision.

A reader may not care whether software helped copyedit a novel. The reader may care that the novelist chose the story, characters, and final sentences.

A customer may prefer an automated refund process to a warm human conversation because the desired good is the refund.

A grieving person may prefer a clumsy human message to a perfect generated condolence because the desired good is evidence that someone showed up.

Context decides.

Artificial abundance therefore creates a strange new etiquette problem.

When is assistance invisible and irrelevant, and when does hiding it change the meaning of the act?

If a colleague uses a model to summarize a forty-page report before a meeting, few people need an announcement.

If a friend asks a model to draft a difficult apology and then revises it until it is true, the assistance may not matter much.

If the friend presses one button, sends a synthetic apology without reading it, and allows the recipient to believe the words represent an hour of reflection, the transaction feels different.

The problem is not that a machine touched the sentence.

The problem is that the sentence may be signaling attention that was never spent.

This makes provenance a contractual question before it becomes a moral one.

If origin is irrelevant to the thing being purchased, demanding elaborate disclosure can become another form of ceremony. Nobody needs a provenance certificate for a routine spreadsheet formula. A customer who wants a refund does not benefit from learning whether the first three sentences of the support reply were drafted by a model.

But when origin is part of the bargain, hiding it can change the product.

Suppose a buyer pays for an original physical performance by a particular musician. A synthetic performance carrying the musician's name is not an equivalent substitute merely because the waveform is pleasing. Suppose a client retains a professional because the client wants that person's considered judgment. A memo produced elsewhere and signed unread is not the same service even if every sentence is correct. Suppose a family pays for a small class because they value sustained attention from a teacher. Replacing the teacher's contact with personalized automated messages may improve some learning outcomes and still alter what was sold.

The relevant disclosure is therefore not a binary stamp reading HUMAN or AI.

It is an account of which human property the transaction promises.

Was there a particular person's final judgment?

Was there physical presence?

Was the performance live?

Did a named professional accept responsibility?

Did the maker actually produce the object, or merely choose among generated variants?

Did someone spend scarce attention on this recipient?

The answers can be mixed.

Most valuable human work already is.

A surgeon relies on machines. A musician relies on amplification and software. A carpenter uses power tools. A writer works inside an editor, search engine, spellchecker, and increasingly a set of generative aids. Human involvement has never meant tool absence.

The interesting boundary is control and consequence.

Who chose what counted as finished?

Who could notice that the ordinary process did not fit this case?

Who was exposed to the outcome?

Who will remember what happened next?

That last question is easy to overlook.

Relationship goods have a future.

The teacher who watches a student struggle today can change tomorrow's lesson. The doctor who makes a difficult call may see the patient again and learn whether the call was right. The adviser who recommends a strategy has to live through the next meeting if it fails. The friend who says the wrong thing remains your friend on Thursday.

The interaction enters a continuing social ledger.

That continuity can be valuable because it changes incentives. A person who expects to encounter the consequences later may behave differently from a producer who disappears after delivering the artifact. Reputation is one way markets formalize this. Friendship, professional duty, repeated business, and community membership are other ways.

Artificial systems can participate in continuity. They can remember more than people do. They can maintain a record across years. An institution can make an automated service accountable through warranties, audits, escalation paths, and people who stand behind it.

So continuity is not proof of human superiority either.

The point is narrower: a human premium sometimes pays for mutual exposure rather than for production technique.

Two people are spending something that cannot be copied infinitely.

Attention goes one way.

Vulnerability often goes both ways.

The conversation can disappoint, surprise, obligate, embarrass, or change the future relationship. That is why a perfectly simulated exchange may not substitute for every real one even when it substitutes for many.

Reciprocity is a property of the relationship, not the sentence.

This is also where a human premium can become fraudulent in the other direction.

A luxury market can turn visible inefficiency into a status signal and call it authenticity. A company can charge more because a person nominally touched the process even when the person added no judgment, care, accountability, or relationship. A restaurant can boast that something was handmade while using the label mainly to justify a price. A professional can insist on personal involvement in work that the client would happily automate if the professional's guild did not control access.

Expensive is not the same as meaningful.

A useful test is to ask what changes if the human step disappears.

Does quality change?

Does responsibility change?

Does the relationship change?

Does the event cease to be the event that was promised?

Does a person lose a chance to exercise or receive attention that matters?

If the answer is no, the premium may be defending an old production cost rather than a human good.

If the answer is yes, then abundance has not made the human contribution obsolete. It has made the contribution easier to name.

We have already encountered proof of effort earlier in the book. Cheap generation weakens effort as an automatic signal because the visible artifact no longer reveals how much work occurred behind it.

The closing version of that argument is less about authentication than relationship.

People will need new ways to decide when they care about the hidden production process.

Most of the time, they will not.

That is good.

The economy should not force human labor into every transaction merely to reassure us that humans remain necessary.

If a process can be made cheaper, safer, faster, and more accessible without losing something people actually value, we should be suspicious of arguments for preserving the old cost.

The human premium is not a subsidy for obsolete inconvenience.

It is a price for human properties that cannot be obtained by making another unit of output.

This means some human experiences may become more expensive precisely because ordinary digital competence becomes cheaper.

A bespoke physical object.

A live event.

A small class.

A meal served in a room.

A guide who walks with you rather than generating an itinerary.

A professional who accepts responsibility in their own name.

A conversation in which the other person is not simultaneously available to a million people.

The premium will be unfairly distributed.

That is another boundary the romantic version tends to ignore.

If live human attention becomes a luxury, wealthy people may buy more of it while everyone else receives efficient synthetic service.

The rich get the human doctor; everyone else gets the assistant.

The rich get the tutor; everyone else gets the adaptive lesson.

The rich get the handmade object and the private performance; everyone else gets perfect replication.

This outcome is plausible enough to deserve scrutiny and simplistic enough to distrust.

Synthetic service may often be better than the scarce human service available before it. A person who had no tutor can gain an excellent one. A rural patient who waited months for basic guidance can receive immediate support. A small company that could not afford professional expertise can access competent help.

The relevant comparison is not always machine versus ideal human.

It is often machine versus no human at all.

Abundance can democratize quality while creating luxury markets around presence.

Both can be true.

The title of this chapter is therefore not an instruction to reject assistance.

Stay expensively human does not mean type every sentence yourself, refuse automation, and cultivate inefficiency as a personality.

It means spend human scarcity where human scarcity changes the good.

Do not waste a person's attention on work nobody values as attention.

Do not automate the part of a relationship whose value consists in someone having been there.

The distinction is personal as much as economic.

A parent can generate a bedtime story in seconds.

That may be delightful.

The scarce part is still the parent sitting on the edge of the bed reading it.

A system can plan the vacation, translate the signs, optimize the route, take photographs, and assemble the album.

The scarce part is still that these particular people went together.

A model can produce a thousand versions of what you might say to someone you love.

It cannot make the choice to spend your evening listening to them.

At least not for you.

This is where the human premium stops being a market category and becomes the final problem of abundance.

Once almost anything can be generated, what should receive the time that cannot be generated again?
