# The Last Scarce Thing

The machine can make another one: another answer, image, draft, plan, melody, another version written for you and then one for the person you might become if you click a different button. This is the promise running through artificial abundance. The cost of another cognitive artifact falls toward triviality. Not to zero, not everywhere, and not without electricity, chips, capital, data centers, networks, engineers, maintenance, and all the physical machinery we spent time recovering from the illusion of weightlessness. Cheap enough, though, to change behavior.

When another version is cheap, we ask for another version. Cheap simulations let us explore more possibilities; cheap explanations let us keep going until one finally works. That is real abundance, but it is not the end of scarcity. Across this book, the constraint kept moving from production toward selection, trust, responsibility, taste, the difficult edges of expertise, physical capacity, and agency. Every time the machine consumed one bottleneck, another became easier to see.

The temptation now is to choose a final task that machines can never perform: judgment, creativity, meaning, love, purpose, consciousness, something sacred enough to remain permanently outside the frontier. That would betray the argument. We do not know where the frontier stops. Models already perform tasks that sounded safely human a short time ago. They write usable code, translate nuanced language, generate images and music, summarize technical material, assist scientific work, conduct extended conversations, and perform portions of professional analysis. Some outputs are mediocre, some excellent, some fail in strange ways. The boundary moves anyway.

There is no intellectual dignity in protecting a shrinking list of tasks by definition. If machines become better at judgment, judgment becomes less scarce. The same goes for verification or eliciting goals. If they produce work that people experience as meaningful, human meaning will have to survive without a monopoly on meaningful-looking artifacts. Scarcity is a poor foundation for human worth.

What remains finite even when output becomes abundant is a life. Not life in the abstract. This life. A person's hours arrive once. Medicine may add years. Automation may return hours that once disappeared into forms, commutes, repetitive production, scheduling, search, and administrative work. Better tools may allow one person to accomplish in a week what once required a team for a month. None of that makes Tuesday repeatable. The person can ask for another draft. The person cannot ask for another age thirty-seven.

Time has always been scarce. The change is comparative: as producing things gets cheaper, deciding what deserves a finite life matters more. An economy of abundance can generate more books than anyone can read, films than anyone can watch, business ideas than anyone can pursue, explanations than anyone can evaluate, plausible careers than anyone can live, and potential relationships than anyone can maintain deeply. The machine keeps widening the menu. The diner does not grow another stomach.

Attention is time in usable form, the portion of a finite life currently available to be directed. Artificial abundance increases the number of things making claims on it, even as the same technology can defend attention by filtering, summarizing, scheduling, and suppressing noise. A capable assistant may save a person an hour of message triage only for everyone else's assistants to generate ten times as many requests. Productivity can become an arms race against other people's productivity.

The saved hour can also be claimed by the institution that helped save it. A worker who once wrote two reports a week may use AI to write ten and discover that ten is now the minimum. A consultant who once answered clients within a day may be expected to answer within an hour. A manager who can review summaries more quickly may attend more meetings because preparation got cheaper and meetings did not. The machine gives back time; institutions decide whether the person gets to keep it.

That is partly why the politics of plenty matters. A productivity gain can become lower prices, higher wages, higher profits, shorter hours, greater output, or some unstable mixture of them. The technology does not choose the mixture, and neither does the individual alone. Even in the best case, if artificial abundance returns enormous quantities of unwanted labor to human beings, another problem appears: what should people do with the time?

"Whatever they want" is correct and incomplete. Wanting is work too. A person can be liberated from a task and discover that the task had been providing structure, social contact, competence, identity, and a reason to get dressed before noon. A job can be tedious and still answer the question of what Tuesday is for. Remove the job and the question remains.

The evidence does not justify claiming that AI therefore causes a psychological crisis, and work has never been a universal source of meaning. Plenty of jobs are alienating; plenty of people find meaning outside employment. Research on discretionary time complicates the simpler fantasy that well-being rises with every additional empty hour. Studies using large U.S. data sets and experiments found that too little discretionary time was associated with lower well-being, but the relationship did not rise forever. Very large amounts could also be associated with lower well-being when people lacked a sense of productive purpose. Other research finds that people who prioritize time over money tend to report greater well-being even after accounting for income and available time. Neither result turns into a universal prescription. Money buys safety, health, housing, education, mobility, and time itself. The narrower point is that time is not valuable merely because it is empty. Its value depends on what becomes possible inside it.

A person could own access to astonishing capability and still live badly: every message answered instantly, every route optimized, every meal selected by predicted preference, every idle minute filled with personalized entertainment, every task assisted and every choice improved according to a model trained on previous choices. The life could become frictionless and strangely unchosen. Agency is not the refusal of assistance. It is the ability to steer through assistance.

The scarce resource, then, is not just chronological time but committed time: time given a direction that cannot simultaneously serve every other possibility. A promise consumes alternatives. So does a marriage, raising a child, learning an instrument badly for years, building a company, caring for a parent, joining a community, writing a book, training for a marathon, tending a garden, mastering a field, or showing up for the same friends long enough that they stop being a network and become part of a life.

Commitment is expensive because it closes doors. Economists have a useful language for what sits on the other side: option value. An option is valuable partly because it preserves the right to decide later. Under uncertainty, waiting can be sensible; more information may arrive, the price may change, a better opportunity may appear. Artificial abundance can increase option value everywhere. Before choosing a design, generate twenty more. Before accepting the plan, simulate five alternatives. Before hiring, search a larger pool. Before committing to a strategy, ask for another analysis.

Cheap exploration is one of the great gifts of generative systems. A small business can model several approaches before risking cash. A student can try several explanations before deciding that a subject is beyond them. A designer can explore a space before committing materials. A patient can prepare better questions before making a consequential choice with a professional. The trouble begins when the cost of one more option falls faster than the cost of choosing among options. Stopping starts to feel irrational. Why accept this draft when another costs a second? Why commit to this plan when the system can produce a stronger one?

The opportunity cost was always there. Choosing one job meant not choosing another; reading one book meant not reading thousands. Artificial abundance gives the foregone possibilities excellent copy. The road not taken can now arrive with a business plan, a mood board, a financial model, a simulated conversation, and a personalized argument for why it was the road you should have taken. A person can spend a great deal of finite life evaluating lives that will never be lived.

Commitment starts to look less like a restriction when you notice what duration produces. A reputation requires repeated choices that become observable. Friendship requires enough returns that both people begin to predict the next one. Skill requires practice after the first burst of information has stopped being interesting. A company accumulates culture and operating knowledge because people remain long enough to experience consequences. A place becomes familiar because novelty is allowed to expire. These are assets built by closing some options. The return arrives through duration.

Artificial abundance can help build them by reducing the administrative tax around practice, making a small team more capable, helping friends coordinate, removing language barriers, preserving memories, and shortening the distance between intention and action. It can also keep interrupting the compounding process with better-looking alternatives. A system optimized to keep offering useful options does not need malicious intent to create this effect. It may simply be doing exactly what was requested: improving the menu. The user still needs a way to finish ordering.

Stopping rules appeared earlier in the book as a response to the cost of choosing among abundant outputs. At the scale of a life, the same problem becomes less procedural. A deadline passes, an application is submitted, a design ships, a family moves, a book ends. Closure is not always optimal, but many things cannot exist outside simulation without it. A person who refuses every irreversible decision can preserve extraordinary flexibility and still find that flexibility has consumed the years it was supposed to protect.

This is not an argument for staying by default. People should leave harmful relationships, abandon bad strategies, stop useless projects, change careers, revise beliefs, and use better tools when they become available. The point is that many human goods require duration. Trust, skill, friendship, and knowledge accumulate. A body becomes capable because training continues after novelty disappears. A craft becomes expressive because someone survives the period when imitation is easier than judgment. A model can simulate the visible result, imitate intimacy, generate the artifact associated with practice, or reconstruct the language of a long relationship. Sometimes that simulation will be useful. It still does not retroactively create the shared history. History is expensive because it already happened.

This is one reason real-world experience can acquire a premium under artificial abundance. Not because reality is morally superior to simulation in every case, but because events impose irreversible sequence. You were there or you were not. You made the call or you did not. You apologized or waited too long. You accepted the job or turned it down. Generation produces alternatives. Living collapses them, and that collapse is how a life acquires shape.

Time also has a distribution. A wealthy person can buy back hours by hiring help, living close to work, paying for faster travel, outsourcing administration, or purchasing services that turn money into convenience. A person with less money may spend more of the day waiting, commuting, filling forms, comparing prices, repairing old equipment, arranging care, or navigating institutions that charge for their own complexity in hours instead of dollars.

Artificial intelligence can reduce some of these taxes dramatically. A form understood in five minutes instead of fifty returns forty-five minutes. A small business that resolves a routine legal or technical question without a day of search gets a piece of the owner's life back. Translation, scheduling, comparison, summarization, and preparation can all return practical capacity. But if the same systems raise expected output, intensify monitoring, or move more administrative work onto individuals because assistance supposedly makes it easy, the dividend disappears.

The task saved is therefore an incomplete unit of welfare. What matters is what happens to the hour. An hour of unwanted paperwork and an hour with a dying parent occupy the same length on a clock and almost nothing else in common.

A capable system may eventually perform not only the paperwork but much of the choosing around it. It can triage, schedule, negotiate, filter, and recommend. That can be liberating. It also creates one last delegation problem. If the assistant decides what deserves the recovered time, who received the dividend?

A person can outsource selection and still remain the owner of the consequences. The assistant can clear the calendar, but it cannot establish that the newly empty afternoon should belong to work, family, recovery, curiosity, exercise, another optimization project, or nothing at all unless the person has supplied some objective by which to choose. Even that objective may be learned from behavior, and what held attention before is not necessarily what deserves attention next.

There is room here for deliberate non-optimization. Not every dinner needs a better dinner. Not every walk needs a route improvement. Not every conversation needs a summary. Not every hour has to justify itself against every possible alternative. Optimization needs a boundary because a life is not an infinite-horizon computation with a reset button.

Artificial abundance may be most generous when it reduces the amount of finite life spent satisfying constraints nobody values: recovering lost information, formatting documents whose format nobody cares about, waiting for administrative systems to reconcile facts that machines can reconcile safely, performing repetitive production after the skill has already been learned, navigating complexity that exists mostly because institutions were never redesigned. Giving those hours back is more than a productivity statistic. The value of the recovery depends on who receives it.

If saved time is captured entirely as higher output expectations, the individual may experience abundance as acceleration. If the savings become concentrated wealth while others absorb the transition costs, abundance may feel like dispossession. If assistance removes the formative work through which people learn to judge, it can save hours now and create dependence later. Systems designed around agency can instead make people more capable of spending time deliberately.

Artificial abundance is not a destination where economics disappears because everything important is free. It is a repricing. As some cognitive production gets cheaper, trust, attention, physical presence, responsibility, infrastructure, legitimacy, and human time can rise in relative importance. The precise list will keep changing. What matters is noticing the human and physical systems that cannot scale at the same rate, the old prices that existed mainly because production was expensive, and the new bottlenecks people have begun competing to own. Then we have to decide whether that is where we want value to collect.

The machine can make another one. This one still costs part of a life.
