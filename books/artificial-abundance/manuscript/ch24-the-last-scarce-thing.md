# The Last Scarce Thing

The machine can make another one: another answer, image, draft, plan, melody, another version written for you and then one for the person you might become if you click a different button. This is the promise running through artificial abundance. The cost of another cognitive artifact falls toward triviality. Not to zero, not everywhere, and not without electricity, chips, capital, data centers, networks, engineers, maintenance, and all the physical machinery we spent time recovering from the illusion of weightlessness. Cheap enough, though, to change behavior.

When another version is cheap, we ask for another version. When another answer is cheap, we stop protecting the first answer because of the labor invested in producing it. Cheap simulations let us explore more possibilities; cheap explanations let us keep going until one finally works. This is real abundance, but the book has also been an argument against mistaking abundance for the end of scarcity. Scarcity moved from production toward selection, trust, responsibility, taste, the difficult edges of expertise, physical constraint, and agency. Every time the abundant machine consumed one bottleneck, another became visible behind it.

The temptation at the end of a book like this is to declare victory for humanity by choosing a final task the machines can never perform: judgment, creativity, meaning, love, purpose, consciousness, something sacred enough to remain permanently outside the frontier. That would betray the argument. We do not know where the frontier will stop. Models already perform tasks that sounded safely human a short time ago. They write usable code, translate nuanced language, generate images, compose music, summarize technical material, assist scientific work, conduct extended conversations, and perform portions of professional analysis. Some outputs are mediocre, some excellent, some fail in strange ways. The boundary moves anyway.

There is no intellectual dignity in protecting a shrinking list of tasks by definition. If machines become better at judgment, judgment becomes less scarce. The same goes for verification or eliciting goals. If they produce work that people experience as meaningful, human meaning will have to survive without a monopoly on meaningful-looking artifacts. Scarcity is a poor foundation for human worth.

The more useful question is what remains finite even when output becomes abundant.

A life. Not life in the abstract. This life. A person's own hours arrive once. Medicine may add years. Automation may return hours that once disappeared into forms, commutes, repetitive production, scheduling, search, and administrative work. Better tools may allow one person to accomplish in a week what once required a team for a month. None of that makes Tuesday repeatable. The person can ask for another draft. The person cannot ask for another age thirty-seven.

Time has always been scarce. Artificial intelligence did not invent mortality or calendars. The change is comparative: when the cost of producing things falls, the cost of deciding what deserves a finite life rises in importance. An economy of abundance can generate more books than anyone can read, films than anyone can watch, business ideas than anyone can pursue, explanations than anyone can evaluate, plausible careers than anyone can live, and potential relationships than anyone can maintain deeply. The machine keeps widening the menu. The diner does not grow another stomach.

This is why attention became central earlier in the book. Attention is time in a usable form, the portion of a finite life currently available to be directed. Artificial abundance increases the number of things capable of making a claim on it, while the same technology can defend attention by filtering, summarizing, scheduling, and suppressing noise. A person with a capable assistant may spend less time triaging messages and more time on work that matters, then receive ten times as many requests because everyone else has an assistant generating them. Productivity can become an arms race against other people's productivity.

The saved hour can also become a newly available hour for more tasks. A worker who once wrote two reports a week may use AI to write ten and discover that ten is now the minimum. A consultant who once answered clients within a day may be expected to answer within an hour. A student who can generate study materials instantly may be assigned more material. A manager who can review summaries more quickly may attend more meetings because preparation got cheaper and meetings did not. The machine gives back time; institutions decide whether the person gets to keep it.

That is partly why the politics of plenty matters. A productivity gain can become lower prices, higher wages, higher profits, shorter hours, greater output, or some unstable mixture of all five. The technology does not choose the mixture, and neither does the individual alone. Even in the best case, if artificial abundance returns enormous quantities of unwanted labor to human beings, another problem appears: what should people do with the time?

Whatever they want is a correct and incomplete answer. Wanting is work too. A person can be liberated from a task and discover that the task had been providing structure, social contact, competence, identity, and a reason to get dressed before noon. A job can be tedious and still answer the question of what Tuesday is for. Remove the job and the question remains.

This is why the meaning shortage chapter avoided claiming that AI causes a psychological crisis. The evidence does not justify such a sweeping story, and work has never been a universal source of meaning. Plenty of jobs are alienating; plenty of people find meaning outside employment. Still, abundance exposes the allocation problem more clearly. If fewer hours are required to produce the same material standard of living, what fills them?

Research on discretionary time complicates the fantasy that the answer is simply more free time. Studies using large U.S. data sets and experiments found that too little discretionary time was associated with lower well-being, but the relationship did not rise forever. Very large amounts of free time could also be associated with lower well-being when people lacked a sense of productive purpose. This is not a defense of overwork. Time is not valuable merely because it is empty. Its value depends on what becomes possible inside it.

Other research finds that people who prioritize time over money tend to report greater well-being even after accounting for income and available time. That does not mean everyone should take a pay cut or that money stops mattering. Money buys safety, health, housing, education, mobility, and time itself. Once basic constraints are met, though, the allocation of time becomes a central form of wealth. Artificial abundance may make that wealth more visible.

A person could own access to astonishing capability and still live badly: every message answered instantly, every route optimized, every meal selected by predicted preference, every idle minute filled with personalized entertainment, every task assisted and every choice improved according to a model trained on previous choices. The life could become frictionless and strangely unchosen. This is the agency problem at full scale. Agency is not the refusal of assistance. It is the ability to steer through assistance.

The final scarce resource is therefore not just chronological time. It is committed time, time given a direction that cannot simultaneously serve every other possibility. A promise consumes alternatives. So does a marriage, raising a child, learning an instrument badly for years, building a company, caring for a parent, joining a community, writing a book, training for a marathon, tending a garden, mastering a field, or showing up for the same friends long enough that they stop being a network and become part of a life.

Commitment is expensive because it closes doors. Economists have a useful language for the thing on the other side: option value. An option is valuable partly because it preserves the right to decide later. When uncertainty is high, waiting can be sensible. More information may arrive, the price may change, a better opportunity may appear. Artificial abundance can increase option value everywhere. Before choosing a design, generate twenty more. Before accepting the plan, simulate five alternatives. Before hiring, search a larger pool. Before committing to a strategy, ask for another analysis.

Much of this is beneficial. Cheap exploration is one of the great gifts of generative systems. A small business can model several approaches before risking cash. A student can try several explanations before deciding that a subject is beyond them. A designer can explore a space before committing materials. A patient can prepare better questions before making a consequential choice with a professional.

The danger begins when the cost of one more option falls faster than the cost of choosing among options. Then stopping starts to feel irrational. Why accept this draft when another costs a second? Why commit to this plan when the system can produce a stronger one? The problem is not that another option exists. Abundance makes the counterfactual permanently visible. Every commitment acquires a ghost version that might have been better.

The opportunity cost was always there. Choosing one job meant not choosing another. Building one company meant not building several others. Reading one book meant not reading thousands. Artificial abundance does not create those foregone possibilities. It gives them excellent copy. The road not taken can now arrive with a business plan, a mood board, a financial model, a simulated conversation, and a personalized argument for why it was the road you should have taken. A person can spend a great deal of finite life evaluating lives that will never be lived.

This is where commitment stops looking merely like a restriction and starts looking like production. A reputation requires repeated choices that become observable. Friendship requires enough returns that both people begin to predict the next one. Skill requires practice after the first burst of information has stopped being interesting. A company accumulates culture and operating knowledge because people remain long enough to experience consequences. A place becomes familiar because novelty is allowed to expire. These are assets built by closing some options. The return arrives through duration.

Artificial abundance can help build them. It can reduce the administrative tax around practice, make a small team more capable, help friends coordinate, remove language barriers, preserve memories, and shorten the distance between intention and action. It can also keep interrupting the compounding process with better-looking alternatives. A system optimized to keep offering useful options does not need malicious intent to create this effect. It may simply be doing exactly what was requested: improving the menu. The user still needs a way to finish ordering.

Earlier in the book, stopping rules appeared as a response to the cost of choosing among abundant outputs. Here the stakes are larger. A deadline passes, an application is submitted, a design ships, a family moves, a book ends. Closure is not always optimal. It is necessary for many things to exist outside simulation. A person who refuses every irreversible decision can preserve extraordinary flexibility and still find that flexibility has consumed the years it was supposed to protect.

Some commitments should be reconsidered. Staying is not virtue by default any more than effort is quality by default. People should leave harmful relationships, abandon bad strategies, stop useless projects, change careers, revise beliefs, and use better tools when they become available. The point is not to worship permanence. It is to notice that a life made only of preserved optionality never compounds into much of anything.

Many human goods require duration. Trust, skill, friendship, and knowledge accumulate. A place becomes home because a person returns to it. A body becomes capable because training continues after novelty disappears. A craft becomes expressive because someone survives the period when imitation is easier than judgment. These goods cannot be generated instantly because their substance includes the time that passed.

A model can simulate the visible result. It can write in the style of expertise, imitate intimacy, generate the artifact associated with practice, or reconstruct the language of a long relationship. Sometimes that simulation will be useful. It still does not retroactively create the shared history. History is expensive because it already happened.

This is one reason real-world experience acquires a premium under artificial abundance. Not because reality is morally superior to simulation in every case, but because events impose irreversible sequence. You were there or you were not. You made the call or you did not. You apologized or waited too long. You accepted the job or turned it down. You made a different choice and became a slightly different person. Generation produces alternatives. Living collapses them, and that collapse is how a life acquires shape.

There is another implication in the word finite: time has a distribution. A wealthy person can buy back hours by hiring help, living close to work, paying for faster travel, outsourcing administration, or purchasing services that turn money into convenience. A person with less money may spend more of the day waiting, commuting, filling forms, comparing prices, repairing old equipment, arranging care, or navigating institutions that charge for their own complexity in hours instead of dollars.

Artificial intelligence can reduce some of these taxes dramatically. A form understood in five minutes instead of fifty returns forty-five minutes. A small business that resolves a routine legal or technical question without a day of search gets a piece of the owner's life back. A person who can translate, schedule, compare, summarize, and prepare without hiring specialists gains practical capacity. But if the same systems raise expected output, intensify monitoring, or move more administrative work onto individuals because assistance supposedly makes it easy, the time dividend can disappear.

The unit of welfare is not the task saved. It is what happens to the hour. Hours are not interchangeable from the inside. An hour of unwanted paperwork and an hour with a dying parent occupy the same length on a clock and almost nothing else in common.

A capable system may eventually perform not only the paperwork but much of the choosing around it. It can triage, schedule, negotiate, filter, and recommend. That can be liberating. It also creates one last delegation problem. If the assistant decides what deserves the recovered time, who received the dividend?

A person can outsource selection and still remain the owner of the consequences. The assistant can clear the calendar, but it cannot establish that the newly empty afternoon should belong to work, family, recovery, curiosity, exercise, another optimization project, or nothing at all unless the person has supplied some objective by which to choose. Even that objective may be learned from behavior, and what held attention before is not necessarily what deserves attention next.

Abundance therefore increases the value of deliberate non-optimization in a narrow sense: moments when a person refuses to ask which available use has the highest predicted return and simply inhabits a commitment already made. Not every dinner needs a better dinner. Not every walk needs a route improvement. Not every conversation needs a summary. Not every hour has to justify itself against every possible alternative. Optimization needs a boundary because a life is not an infinite-horizon computation with a reset button.

Artificial abundance may be most generous when it reduces the amount of finite life spent satisfying constraints nobody values: recovering lost information, formatting documents whose format nobody cares about, waiting for administrative systems to reconcile facts that machines can reconcile safely, performing repetitive production after the skill has already been learned, navigating complexity that exists mostly because institutions were never redesigned. Giving those hours back is not trivial productivity. It is the recovery of scarce life.

The value of that recovery depends on who receives it. If saved time is captured entirely as higher output expectations, the individual may experience abundance as acceleration. If the savings become concentrated wealth while others absorb the transition costs, abundance may feel like dispossession. If assistance removes the formative work through which people learn to judge, it can save hours now and create dependence later. Systems designed around agency can instead make people more capable of spending time deliberately.

There is no automatic ending. Artificial abundance is not a destination where economics disappears because everything important is free. It is a repricing. As the price of some cognitive production falls, trust, attention, physical presence, responsibility, infrastructure, legitimacy, and human time can rise in relative importance. The precise list will keep changing. The method matters more: when a technology makes something abundant, look for the human and physical systems that cannot scale at the same rate, the old price that existed only because production was expensive, the social value that survives after the production cost disappears, and the new bottleneck people have begun competing to own. Then ask whether that is where we want value to collect.

The machine can make another one.

You still have to decide whether this one deserves part of your life.
