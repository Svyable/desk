# The Taste Economy

A designer once had to protect an idea from the cost of execution.

Every serious variation consumed time. A different layout meant another round of work. A new illustration meant another commission or another hour. A different type treatment meant another decision carried through the file. Even bad ideas were expensive enough that most of them died in the imagination.

Generation changes that filter.

Ask for fifty logos and you can have fifty logos.

Ask for another fifty in a different direction and the machine does not complain that the brief changed. Ask for a hundred packaging concepts, three hundred names, twenty versions of a landing page, ten arrangements of a song, thirty interior schemes, or enough rough product interfaces to fill a wall. The cost of exploring the possibility space collapses.

This sounds like pure creative freedom.

It is also a new burden.

Someone still has to look at the wall.

The industrial age trained us to think of creativity as a production problem. Can the artist make the image? Can the architect produce the drawing? Can the writer find the sentence? Can the composer hear the arrangement? Can the product team turn a concept into a working prototype?

Those questions do not disappear. The best work still contains choices no model can rescue from a weak brief. But when competent variations become cheap, another ability moves toward the center of the work.

Rejection.

The creator of abundance has to become a ruthless editor of abundance.

This is what I mean by the taste economy.

Taste is an awkward word because it can sound aristocratic, decorative, or purely subjective. It brings to mind wine lists, galleries, clothes, furniture, and people arguing about things with no correct answer.

That is too narrow.

Taste is the ability to discriminate among plausible alternatives when the rules do not settle the choice.

It appears in software architecture, hiring, research, investing, strategy, medicine, writing, engineering, product design, teaching, and management. A technically valid option can still be a bad option. A correct answer can still be the wrong answer to emphasize. A feature can work and still make the product worse. A sentence can be grammatical and still sound dead. A candidate can satisfy the job description and still be wrong for the team. A scientific hypothesis can be testable and still not be worth the experiment.

Taste begins where compliance with a specification stops being enough.

Artificial intelligence increases the number of specification-compliant objects.

That is precisely why taste becomes more visible.

Imagine two organizations using equally capable models.

The first asks for a strategy, receives one, polishes the presentation, and implements it.

The second asks for several competing strategies, identifies the assumptions that differ among them, designs ways to test those assumptions, discards the most seductive output because the evidence is weak, and combines parts of two less glamorous proposals into a plan that fits the actual organization.

The intelligence available to both firms may be identical.

The outcomes will not be.

The difference is not prompting technique in the narrow sense. It is judgment about what deserves belief.

That judgment is built from contact with reality.

A restaurant owner develops taste partly by eating, cooking, watching what customers leave on the plate, dealing with suppliers, noticing what travels badly, learning which dishes collapse during a rush, and discovering that the menu item everybody praises may still be a terrible business.

A software engineer develops taste by maintaining systems after the original excitement has passed. Elegant abstractions reveal costs. Clever dependencies break. A shortcut that saved a day creates six months of friction. A boring design survives because everyone can understand it.

An editor develops taste by reading thousands of sentences and seeing which ones readers misunderstand. A physician develops judgment through cases that resemble one another until one important detail makes them different. An investor learns that a beautiful story and a good asset are not the same thing. A teacher learns that an explanation can be perfectly clear to the person giving it and useless to the person receiving it.

Taste is compressed experience.

The compression matters because the expert rarely retrieves every lesson explicitly.

A senior engineer can dislike a design before being able to articulate the whole argument against it. A physician can notice that a case does not fit the usual pattern. An editor can feel a paragraph sag before identifying the repeated abstraction causing it. This does not make intuition mystical. Much of what looks like intuition is experience compressed below the level of a checklist.

That compression is useful and dangerous.

Experts can overlearn the past. A veteran can reject an unfamiliar solution because it violates conventions that no longer matter. An investor can mistake pattern recognition for a law. A manager can call prejudice judgment. Taste deserves deference only when it remains corrigible by evidence.

The scarce good is therefore not confidence.

It is discrimination that can survive contact with results.

Taste is also social.

What counts as good depends partly on the people who will use, hear, buy, trust, interpret, regulate, repair, or live with the result. A chair is not merely an arrangement of shapes. Someone has to sit in it. A policy is not merely a consistent argument. People have to respond to it. A product name is not merely novel. Customers have to say it aloud, remember it, search for it, and perhaps defend the purchase to somebody else.

This is one reason a model can generate impressive objects without eliminating the need for human judgment.

The model sees patterns in representations of the world.

The decision eventually returns to the world.

The chair gets sat on.

The medicine gets taken.

The software meets a user who refuses to behave as the product manager expected.

The advertisement reaches a culture that interprets it differently from the training examples.

The contract lands in a dispute.

Reality performs the final review.

A person with taste is not somebody who is always right before that review. Nobody is. The useful person is somebody whose internal filters have been trained by enough contact with consequences that the search can be narrowed intelligently before reality charges the full price of testing everything.

This becomes more valuable when generation explodes.

Suppose a model can produce ten thousand potential drug molecules, product concepts, research hypotheses, headlines, marketing messages, or pieces of code. The abundance is useless unless something ranks the candidates.

Ranking can also be automated, of course.

One model can judge another. A reward model can score outputs. Tests can eliminate code that fails. Simulation can reject designs. Customer data can rank messages. Search algorithms can optimize toward measurable outcomes. In many domains, machines will become better selectors as well as better generators.

That does not end the problem.

It moves it again.

Who chose the metric?

Who decided that click-through rate matters more than brand trust?

Who decided which test suite represents correctness?

Who decided that predicted engagement is a good proxy for satisfaction?

Who decided that the simulated environment contains the constraints that matter?

Who decided which risks should be tolerated?

Who decided what the product is for?

Selection can be automated inside a frame.

Taste often appears in the choice of frame.

This is why the most powerful human role in an abundant creative system may resemble direction more than production.

A film director does not personally perform every craft required to make a film. The director coordinates specialists, protects a point of view, makes decisions under incomplete information, and rejects enormous amounts of competent work that do not belong in this particular movie.

The analogy should not be stretched too far, but it captures something important. When production capacity increases, coherent intention becomes harder to maintain, not easier.

A model can make each local piece better while the whole thing becomes worse.

The brochure has excellent copy, beautiful images, and a confused identity.

The software contains elegant components and no clear product.

The book has polished chapters repeating the same argument.

The company launches dozens of experiments and loses track of what it believes.

The abundance of local competence can hide the absence of global judgment.

This is one of the dangers of machine-assisted creation because models are unusually good at producing material that looks finished.

A rough human sketch announces its incompleteness. A fluent generated artifact can arrive wearing the visual and linguistic signals of completion before the underlying decision has been made.

The polish encourages premature acceptance.

Taste resists that pressure.

It asks whether the thing should exist in this form at all.

That makes taste economically useful, but it also makes taste difficult to credential.

We know how to test many forms of technical knowledge. We can administer exams, review code, inspect a portfolio, check a license, or verify a degree. Taste often reveals itself only across a sequence of choices.

What did the person choose when several options were defensible?

What did they refuse?

What did they notice early?

Which compromise did they make when every path had a cost?

Did their work improve after contact with evidence?

Did they preserve coherence as the project changed?

A portfolio becomes more important than a claim because judgment leaves a trail.

This may create a strange labor-market effect.

AI can make it easier for inexperienced people to produce work that resembles the surface of expert output. At the same time, employers may become more interested in evidence of repeated judgment over time.

The polished artifact becomes easier to fake.

The history of choices becomes more valuable.

This is already visible in coding. A generated program can look sophisticated while containing subtle errors or maintenance problems. The question shifts from "can you produce code?" toward "can you define the problem, inspect the result, understand the failure, choose the architecture, and be responsible for what ships?" Anthropic's 2026 research on agentic coding found a related division of labor in its own usage data: people tended to make more of the planning decisions while the system made more of the execution decisions. Expertise still improved outcomes and allowed users to delegate more effectively.

That pattern will not generalize cleanly to every profession. Anthropic observes one product ecosystem, and coding is unusually rich in tests, repositories, and executable feedback. But it offers a useful picture of the transition.

The valuable expert does not necessarily disappear when execution becomes easier.

The expert becomes a better allocator of execution.

There is a danger in celebrating this too quickly.

Taste itself requires practice, and abundant generation can short-circuit practice.

A writer who never struggles through a first draft may have fewer opportunities to learn why one sentence works better than another. A designer who jumps directly from prompt to polished variants may not build the same sensitivity to proportion, hierarchy, and constraint. A programmer who accepts generated code without tracing it may accumulate output faster than understanding. A student who receives excellent explanations may learn less if the system removes the productive confusion that forces a concept to be reconstructed internally.

A society can become rich in finished objects and poor in people who know how the objects became good.

That claim needs more than an appealing apprenticeship story.

An experiment in high-school mathematics provides a bounded example of the mechanism. Hamsa Bastani and colleagues studied nearly one thousand students in Turkey using GPT-4-based assistance. Students given a general-purpose AI interface performed substantially better while the tool was available. But when the tool was removed for a later unassisted exam, that group performed worse than students who had never had AI access.

The striking part of the study is what happened in the second treatment.

Another group used a tutor designed with teacher-informed guardrails. Instead of simply supplying answers, the system was structured to provide hints and preserve more of the student's own problem solving. Those students improved even more during assisted practice, while the later learning penalty was largely eliminated.

The lesson is not that AI makes people stupid.

The same experiment points in the opposite direction when the assistance is designed differently.

The useful distinction is between performance borrowed from the tool and skill being formed in the person.

Those can rise together.

They can also diverge.

The experiment does not prove that junior lawyers, programmers, designers, or physicians will be deskilled by AI. It took place in one high school, in one subject, with a 2023 deployment of GPT-4, and measured short-term learning rather than a professional career. A workplace is not a mathematics classroom. Experienced workers bring context, incentives, collaboration, and responsibility that students in an experiment do not.

But the mechanism deserves attention because it clarifies what apprenticeship was doing.

Practice is not valuable merely because it consumes hours.

It is valuable when the learner has to attempt, predict, notice error, diagnose why the prediction failed, and revise an internal model.

A tool can remove the clerical burden while preserving those acts.

Or it can remove the acts themselves.

The difference may not be visible in the immediate output.

Two students can submit the same correct answer. One may have reconstructed the concept. The other may have received a polished completion. Two junior programmers can ship the same patch. One may understand why the system failed and which alternative was rejected. The other may know only that the tests passed.

The artifact does not tell you how much judgment was formed while producing it.

This creates a new design problem for education and work.

If abundant assistance makes novice output more valuable immediately, organizations have a strong incentive to use it. Customers benefit from fewer rookie mistakes. Managers get more throughput. Juniors can contribute to harder work sooner.

The cost, if there is one, may arrive years later when the organization needs people capable of handling cases the tool cannot resolve.

That delay makes apprenticeship easy to underinvest in.

A firm captures today's productivity gain directly. The future shortage of independent judgment is diffuse, uncertain, and may occur after the employee has left.

This is a familiar economic structure. Organizations underinvest in things whose benefits are delayed, hard to measure, or portable to another employer.

Artificial abundance can intensify the problem because the short-term output looks so good.

The obvious response is not to ban the tool from junior work.

That would preserve effort rather than preserve learning.

A better response is to redesign the work around the acts that form judgment.

Ask the junior to predict before seeing the generated answer.

Require an explanation of why one option was rejected.

Expose the model to adversarial cases and have the learner diagnose the failure.

Let the system generate many examples while making the person state the principle that separates them.

Use assistance to increase the number of feedback cycles rather than to eliminate the cycle.

These are not universal prescriptions. Different fields form expertise differently. Some routine work is genuinely wasteful and teaches very little. A generation of professionals should not be forced to reproduce obsolete drudgery because their mentors suffered through it.

The goal is not to preserve the old task.

It is to preserve whatever learning function the old task accidentally served.

This reframes the apprenticeship question.

When AI removes a junior task, the institution should ask what information that task used to feed back into the learner.

Did it teach the vocabulary of the field?

Did it expose edge cases?

Did it force the learner to notice conflicting constraints?

Did it create contact with customers?

Did it reveal how long implementation actually takes?

Did it make mistakes cheap enough to learn from under supervision?

If the answer is nothing, automate it without nostalgia.

If the answer is something important, build another route to the same experience.

This is not inevitable. Tools can also accelerate learning by increasing feedback, exposing alternatives, and letting beginners attempt work previously beyond their reach. The same abundance that enables laziness can enable deliberate practice at extraordinary scale.

The difference lies in how the tool is used.

Do you ask it to finish the work before you have formed a view?

Or do you use it to generate contrast, expose assumptions, critique choices, and increase the number of cycles through which judgment can improve?

The second path is harder because it preserves a role for discomfort.

That discomfort may be part of the scarce good.

Not because difficulty is sacred.

Because judgment requires a place where your prediction can be wrong before somebody else's answer rescues you from finding out.

When making becomes easy, caring enough to choose becomes work.

When options become infinite, saying no becomes productive.

And when machines can generate an answer to almost any ordinary brief, the person who can write the brief that should exist—and recognize the answer that deserves to survive—moves closer to the center of value.
