# Responsibility Does Not Scale

A machine can produce a thousand drafts before lunch.

It cannot be embarrassed by any of them.

This sounds like a philosophical complaint until a generated sentence enters a courtroom, a medical record, a financial filing, a safety review, or a contract. Then the asymmetry becomes practical. Production can multiply almost without friction. Consequences still have addresses.

Someone signs.

Someone approves.

Someone holds the license.

Someone carries the insurance.

Someone has a duty to the client, patient, customer, shareholder, regulator, or public.

Artificial intelligence makes it possible to separate these functions more sharply than many institutions are used to. The person who produced the words and the person responsible for the words have often been the same person, or at least members of the same accountable organization. When a system produces the first draft, the production chain becomes stranger. The output may contain no human sentence at all and still arrive under a human name.

Law has been forced to confront this early because courts make responsibility unusually visible.

The American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512 does not prohibit lawyers from using generative AI. It does something more revealing. It treats the new tool as entering a profession whose old duties remain in place. Competence remains. Confidentiality remains. Communication with clients remains. Supervision remains. Candor to tribunals remains. The obligation to charge reasonable fees remains.

The machine changes the method. It does not inherit the license.

That arrangement is not proof that every profession will organize AI the same way. Lawyers have distinctive duties, and professional rules often move more slowly than technology. Liability can migrate through statutes, contracts, insurance, product law, and new institutional forms.

But the example exposes a basic economic fact.

Lowering the cost of producing an answer does not lower the social cost of a wrong answer by the same amount.

In some settings, it can increase it.

Suppose a junior lawyer once spent six hours researching a point. The expense naturally limited how many authorities entered the draft. A generative system can produce twenty candidate arguments and dozens of citations in seconds. That abundance is useful if verification scales with it. If verification does not, the organization has created a new bottleneck and disguised it as productivity.

In September 2026, the District of Columbia Court of Appeals criticized lawyers after nonexistent AI-generated authorities appeared in litigation involving a Deutsche Bank subsidiary. The durable lesson is not that language models invent citations. By now, that is not surprising. The lesson is that the invented citation became consequential only because a human professional allowed it to cross an institutional boundary.

The court could sanction a lawyer. It could not meaningfully discipline a probability distribution.

This is where the fantasy of autonomous production collides with the architecture of responsibility.

Organizations are built from permissions. A person can authorize a payment up to a certain amount. A doctor can prescribe a drug. A corporate officer can bind a company. A licensed engineer can stamp a plan. A manager can terminate an employee. A regulator can approve an application. These acts are not valuable merely because they require cognition. They are valuable because institutions recognize the actor's authority and attach consequences to its use.

Generation scales more easily than authority.

A model can recommend ten thousand loans. A bank still has to decide what process counts as its decision and how that process satisfies law, policy, risk limits, and fairness obligations. A model can draft ten thousand medical notes. A health system still has to decide who attests that the record is accurate. A model can propose ten thousand code changes. Someone still owns the production system that may fail after deployment.

The scarcity therefore migrates from making the candidate action toward accepting it.

This is why many AI workflows acquire a familiar shape. The system drafts; a person reviews. The system proposes; a person approves. The system classifies; exceptions go to a person. The system handles the ordinary case; a person owns the escalation.

It is easy to dismiss this as a temporary stage before better automation.

Some of it certainly is.

Human review can become theater. A worker faced with hundreds of machine-generated outputs may click approve because the organization has created more review than a human can perform. A radiologist who receives too many alerts learns to ignore alerts. A manager who must sign every automated decision may provide accountability on paper without supplying meaningful scrutiny.

Putting a human in the loop does not guarantee that a human is thinking.

That phrase can become an institutional sedative: a box checked after the real design work was avoided.

The serious question is not whether a human appears somewhere in the diagram. It is whether responsibility and control remain connected.

Can the responsible person understand what the system did well enough to intervene?

Does that person have time to verify the output?

Can she reject it without being punished for slowing the process?

Does the organization preserve evidence needed to reconstruct what happened?

Are unusual cases routed toward greater attention rather than hidden inside average confidence?

Is authority given at the same scale as the information required to exercise it?

These are organizational questions, not model questions.

They become more important as the model improves.

A terrible system is easy to distrust. Nobody builds a serious workflow around a tool that fails constantly and obviously. A mostly competent system creates the harder problem. Its reliability invites dependence. Dependence increases volume. Volume makes verification expensive. The organization then feels pressure to reduce review precisely because the system has become useful enough to generate more consequential work.

This is the responsibility paradox of artificial abundance.

The better production gets, the easier it becomes to create more things that someone must stand behind.

Consider a consulting firm that can now produce ten analyses in the time it once produced one. Does it have ten times the partner attention to decide which recommendation should go to the client? A newsroom can generate hundreds of local summaries. Does it have hundreds of editors able to catch the one that falsely identifies a person? A small software company can ship code much faster. Has its capacity for security review, incident response, and operational ownership grown at the same rate?

The answer need not be no. Verification can also be automated. Tests can run automatically. Models can critique other models. Formal methods can prove some properties. Statistical monitoring can identify unusual behavior. Rules can block actions outside defined limits. Responsibility does not require that every check be performed manually.

What cannot be automated merely by generating more text is the institutional decision about acceptable failure.

How many false positives are tolerable?

Which errors require a person before action?

Who may change the threshold?

Who compensates the harmed party?

When the system behaves exactly as designed and the outcome is still bad, who owns that design?

Those questions can eventually receive technical components, but they are questions about authority and consequence.

This is why responsibility may become a premium service.

When anyone can generate a plausible answer, the valuable sentence may become: I have checked this, and I will stand behind it.

That sentence is expensive.

It carries reputation. It may carry malpractice exposure. It may require insurance. It may require capital reserves. It may require a license, an audit trail, a warranty, or a contractual promise. Most of all, it requires an institution capable of surviving when the promise turns out to be wrong.

Artificial abundance could therefore produce a strange split in markets.

At the bottom, enormous quantities of unguaranteed cognition: drafts, suggestions, analyses, plans, diagnoses, code, and advice available at negligible cost.

Above it, a thinner layer of warranted output: work somebody has verified, insured, certified, approved, or otherwise agreed to own.

The generated answer may be nearly free.

The signature may not be.

This does not guarantee permanent human supremacy. Responsibility itself can be redistributed. A manufacturer can warrant an automated system. An insurer can price its risk. A legislature can assign liability to a provider. A corporation can delegate authority to software within specified limits. Institutions are inventions, and they can invent new accountable actors and new forms of assurance.

But that change requires more than a better model.

It requires deciding where losses go.

That is why responsibility does not scale at the same rate as production. Production is a capability. Responsibility is a relationship among authority, evidence, incentives, and consequences.

A machine can make another answer almost instantly.

Society cannot make another accountable party quite so cheaply.

And once generation outruns responsibility, the scarce work becomes deciding which outputs deserve to cross the boundary from possible to accepted.

That work has an old name.

Judgment.
