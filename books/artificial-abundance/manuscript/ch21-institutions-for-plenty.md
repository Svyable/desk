# Institutions for Plenty

Institutions are often machines for allocating scarcity.

A school has a limited number of seats.

A hospital has limited appointments.

A court has limited hearing time.

A company has limited headcount and capital.

A government has limited staff.

A newspaper has limited pages.

A broadcast network has limited airtime.

A university library once had limited copies of a book.

Rules emerge around the shortage.

Applications.

Queues.

Prices.

Credentials.

Priorities.

Gatekeepers.

Budgets.

Calendars.

Committees.

The rule is often treated as if it were the institution itself.

Then technology changes the scarcity.

The institution keeps the rule.

This lag is one of the recurring stories of progress.

A document that once required a trip to an office becomes downloadable, but the office still requires an appointment.

A form that once needed a specialist to interpret becomes machine-readable, but the process still assumes the applicant cannot understand it.

A lecture that once could reach only the people in a room becomes globally accessible, but the credential remains tied to admission into the room.

A filing that once required physical handling becomes digital, but the organization preserves the old sequence because the sequence became policy.

Artificial intelligence will create many more of these mismatches.

An institution designed around scarce cognition can continue rationing cognition after cognition becomes cheap.

The result looks like absurdity.

A citizen waits weeks for an answer to a question a system could explain immediately.

A student receives one-size-fits-all feedback because the school cannot reorganize assessment even though individualized feedback is cheap.

A small business pays for routine professional interpretation because the licensing and service model still bundle interpretation with higher-value responsibility.

A manager waits for a monthly report because the reporting rhythm was designed around human production time.

A patient navigates a maze of administrative calls because every department still behaves as if explaining the process consumes scarce staff hours.

The abundance exists technically.

The institution has not metabolized it.

This is why the impact of artificial intelligence will depend as much on institutional redesign as on model capability.

A powerful model inside a bad process can produce faster bad process.

The workflow becomes cheaper without becoming better.

The queue moves faster into another queue.

Institutions for plenty begin by asking what scarcity the existing rule was built to manage.

If that scarcity changes, the rule must justify itself again.

Consider expert advice.

An institution may have created layers of triage because expert attention was expensive. A customer talks to a generalist before a specialist. A patient receives administrative screening before a clinician. An employee asks a junior analyst before escalating to leadership.

If basic interpretation becomes abundant, the triage system can change.

People can arrive at the scarce expert with better information, clearer questions, and routine issues already resolved.

This can increase the value of the expert's time.

But only if the institution lets the cheap layer connect to the scarce layer intelligently.

Otherwise the system adds another chatbot in front of the same old bottleneck.

The customer becomes more frustrated because the institution now has abundant language for explaining why nothing can happen.

This is a useful test.

Artificial intelligence should not merely make the institution more articulate.

It should change what the institution is capable of doing.

A public agency that can answer questions twenty-four hours a day is useful.

An agency that can also prefill forms, identify missing evidence, explain eligibility, route unusual cases, translate decisions, and reduce avoidable appeals is more useful.

A hospital that generates visit notes faster is useful.

A hospital that turns the saved time into more patient attention, better follow-up, or increased care capacity changes the service.

A school that generates worksheets is using abundance at the surface.

A school that gives every student timely practice while preserving teacher attention for diagnosis, mentorship, and social learning is redesigning around abundance.

The institution should move the scarce human layer toward the work where scarcity is meaningful.

This requires clarity about what must remain human or constrained.

Not because human activity is automatically virtuous.

Because authority, legitimacy, responsibility, relationship, and physical presence often sit at different layers from information production.

A court can use abundant intelligence to search precedent, translate filings, summarize arguments, and improve access.

The judge's authority remains scarce because society does not want a million contradictory final judgments generated for the same dispute.

A regulator can analyze more evidence.

The rule remains common because regulation has to coordinate behavior across people.

A school can personalize instruction.

The credential remains scarce if it represents a threshold society expects others to trust.

A company can generate endless strategies.

Capital and organizational commitment still require selection.

Institutions for plenty therefore combine abundant services with scarce decisions.

They do not pretend scarcity has disappeared.

They relocate it deliberately.

This sounds administrative.

It has deep distributional consequences.

When a capability becomes cheap, institutions decide whether the abundance reaches everyone or is captured behind a new gate.

The internet made publishing cheap, but distribution platforms became powerful.

Digital music made copying cheap, but attention and platform access remained scarce.

Open-source software made sophisticated capability widely available, while complementary services and infrastructure created new businesses.

Artificial intelligence can democratize expertise and still concentrate power in compute providers, data holders, distribution channels, identity systems, and organizations with permission to act in regulated domains.

Abundance at one layer can support monopoly at another.

Institutions determine which layer becomes the toll road.

This is why access questions should be asked early.

If a public school uses powerful tutoring systems, who owns the student data?

If a government uses private models to explain benefits, can citizens access the same capability without surrendering sensitive information?

If a hospital relies on a small number of AI vendors, what happens to bargaining power and continuity when prices change?

If a profession allows machines to perform routine work but keeps legal authority tightly licensed, does the public receive lower prices or simply higher margins for the licensed layer?

If a company replaces entry-level jobs with systems, what institution trains the people needed for senior judgment later?

Abundance creates second-order institutional questions because the old scarcity did more than set price.

It financed training.

It justified organizational layers.

It created cross-subsidies.

It supported professional obligations.

It funded public goods.

A law firm may have used routine work to train junior lawyers and subsidize deeper expertise.

A newspaper may have used classified advertising to finance reporting.

A university may use large introductory courses to support laboratories, libraries, and specialized faculty.

A hospital may use profitable services to support unprofitable ones.

When technology collapses one revenue stream, the valuable complement does not automatically fund itself.

Institutions for plenty need new financial structures, not just cheaper production.

This is where technological optimism often becomes thin.

The capability is free.

Therefore the social problem is solved.

No.

Free capability can destroy the business model that supported the institution performing a broader function.

Sometimes that destruction is healthy. The institution may have been extracting rents.

Sometimes society discovers that the expensive bundle contained something worth preserving.

The difficult work is unbundling honestly.

Which part should become cheap?

Which part needs a new funding mechanism?

Which part was never valuable?

Which part becomes more important because abundance weakens an old signal?

Journalism illustrates the pattern.

If generated summaries become abundant, summarization itself captures less value.

Original reporting remains costly because somebody has to cultivate sources, obtain records, travel, verify claims, withstand legal pressure, and risk reputation.

An institution that treats all text as equivalent may flood the world with summaries while starving the processes that generate facts worth summarizing.

The abundance layer consumes the scarce layer.

A sustainable institution has to reconnect them.

Education faces a similar problem.

If explanation becomes abundant, tuition cannot be justified merely by access to explanations.

The institution must show what the credential, community, laboratory, mentorship, selection process, network, research environment, and structured development provide.

Some of those things may also change.

The abundance does not merely lower cost.

It forces institutional self-examination.

Why do you exist after your most visible output becomes cheap?

This is an uncomfortable question for every organization.

A consultancy after analysis becomes abundant.

A software company after code generation becomes abundant.

A media company after content becomes abundant.

A school after tutoring becomes abundant.

A government office after explanation becomes abundant.

A professional association after information becomes abundant.

A manager after coordination becomes abundant.

The answer cannot be “because we have always been the place where this work happened.”

Abundance removes historical excuses.

Institutions that survive will identify their irreducible function.

Trust.

Certification.

Coordination.

Physical capacity.

Capital allocation.

Risk bearing.

Collective decision.

Research.

Community.

Legitimacy.

Long-term memory.

Responsibility.

These are not automatically safe from automation. They are simply harder to replace by generating another piece of text.

There is also a problem of public abundance.

Private markets can supply cheap intelligence where customers can pay or where providers can monetize attention and data.

Some of the largest benefits may occur where the customer has little purchasing power.

A person navigating disability benefits.

A student in an under-resourced school.

A small municipality with limited staff.

A public defender with too many cases.

A community clinic.

A small nonprofit.

A family trying to understand a complex government process.

Because marginal cognitive cost can be low, these settings become more economically addressable than before.

But “low marginal cost” is not the same as “free public service.”

Infrastructure, integration, oversight, privacy, security, procurement, and maintenance still cost money.

A society that wants shared abundance has to build the institutional layer that makes cheap capability dependable.

This may resemble public infrastructure more than ordinary software purchasing.

Standards matter.

Portability matters.

Auditability matters.

Interoperability matters.

Public records matter.

The ability to switch vendors matters.

The ability to explain a decision to a citizen matters.

The ability to function when one provider fails matters.

Abundance without institutional resilience can become dependence.

A city that replaces staff knowledge with one opaque service may become efficient until the service disappears.

A school that outsources pedagogy to a vendor may gain personalized instruction and lose the ability to understand how instruction is being shaped.

A company that lets agents accumulate procedural knowledge may discover that nobody can operate the process when the system is unavailable.

Institutions need retained competence at the layers they cannot afford to lose.

This is another form of redundancy.

Abundance encourages consolidation because the same system can serve many people.

Resilience may require some plurality.

Multiple providers.

Open standards.

Human fallback.

Local capability.

Archives that remain accessible independent of one tool.

A society should not confuse cheap centralization with permanent robustness.

Institutions for plenty also need better ways to handle excess.

Scarce systems know how to ration.

Abundant systems need to filter.

A regulator receiving ten thousand machine-generated comments must distinguish genuine public participation from automated volume.

A journal receiving a flood of plausible papers must preserve review quality.

A company receiving thousands of highly polished job applications needs better evidence of capability.

A court receiving cheap filings needs rules against procedural flooding.

A university receiving perfect-looking essays needs different assessment methods.

Abundance can overwhelm institutions built on the assumption that producing the submission was itself costly.

The response cannot be to restore scarcity arbitrarily.

It has to create better signals.

Reputation.

Identity.

Random audits.

Process evidence.

Deposits returned for legitimate use.

Rate limits proportionate to rights and context.

Sampling.

Machine screening supervised by accountable people.

The design should protect access while making abuse costly.

This is hard because the old friction often performed the anti-abuse function accidentally.

An application was limited because writing it took time.

A letter to an official was limited because printing and mailing took effort.

A research paper was limited because producing one required months of work.

When production cost collapses, institutions need explicit defenses instead of relying on effort as a filter.

That is a recurring theme of the book.

Artificial abundance removes accidental signals.

Good institutions replace them with deliberate ones.

They distinguish information from trust.

Output from responsibility.

Personalization from rights.

Efficiency from legitimacy.

Assistance from agency.

Difficulty from learning.

Volume from value.

The institutions that fail will often fail by preserving the old surface while losing the old function.

The school keeps the essay but no longer knows what the essay proves.

The company keeps human approval but gives humans no time to review.

The government keeps the form but automates the language around it.

The profession keeps the credential but cannot explain what scarce capability the credential now protects.

The media company keeps publishing more while original reporting disappears.

These are institutional zombies.

The shell remains.

The scarcity that justified the shell has moved.

Institutions for plenty do the opposite.

They let cheap things become cheap.

They protect scarce things because they can name why the scarcity matters.

They move human attention toward responsibility, relationship, judgment, and exceptions.

They redesign revenue around the value that remains rather than defending the cost that disappeared.

They make abundance accessible without pretending abundance removes risk.

They preserve common standards where infinite versions would destroy coordination.

They reintroduce friction only where friction performs a real function.

Most importantly, they understand that abundance is not an endpoint.

It is a new input condition.

The institution still has to decide what it is for.
