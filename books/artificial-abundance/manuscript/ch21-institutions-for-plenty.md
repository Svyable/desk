# Institutions for Plenty

A town with one well has an obvious problem.

Who gets the water?

A town with ten thousand wells has a different one.

Which water is safe, who maintains the pipes, what counts as contamination, who pays when a system fails, and how does anyone know which tap to trust?

Abundance does not eliminate institutions.

It changes their job.

For most of this book, artificial abundance has appeared as a falling production cost. Drafts become cheap. Explanations multiply. Expertise can be packaged. Versions proliferate. Attention, trust, responsibility, physical infrastructure, and judgment become relatively more valuable because output no longer consumes the same amount of scarce professional time.

It would be easy to carry that logic one step too far and imagine that institutions themselves become unnecessary.

If everyone can generate an answer, why maintain a bureaucracy that used to ration answers?

If every small business can summon a capable analyst, why maintain layers of professional intermediation?

If a citizen can ask a model to explain a regulation, why employ people to translate government into ordinary language?

If software can coordinate schedules, draft contracts, screen applications, reconcile records, and route decisions, why keep the organization that once employed people to do those things?

Some organizations will shrink for exactly these reasons.

Some departments exist because information was expensive to move. Some roles exist because documents were expensive to produce. Some approval chains are fossils of a time when only a few people possessed the information required to decide. Cheap generation and better automation will expose a great deal of institutional scar tissue.

But institutions do more than produce paperwork.

They decide what the paperwork means.

A bank does not merely generate loan documents. It defines who may commit the bank's capital, under what rules, with which records, subject to which appeal rights, and with what responsibility when the decision is wrong. A university does not merely produce lectures. It establishes admission, assessment, credentials, research norms, disciplinary procedures, and a shared account of what qualifies as demonstrated knowledge. A court does not merely produce legal prose. It has jurisdiction.

The prose can become cheap while the jurisdiction remains scarce.

That distinction will become increasingly important because generative systems are very good at imitating the visible surface of an institution.

They can produce the memo, the lesson, the legal argument, the policy option, the medical explanation, the employee evaluation, and the customer response. The artifact appears first because artifacts are what institutions leave behind.

But an institution is also a set of permissions.

Who is allowed to do what?

Whose decision binds whom?

Which record becomes authoritative?

How can the decision be challenged?

Who is responsible for maintaining continuity after the person who made the decision leaves?

Those questions do not disappear when the memo becomes easier to write.

They become harder to hide behind the memo.

This is already visible in ordinary business adoption.

A 2025 OECD survey of more than five thousand small and medium-sized enterprises across seven countries found generative AI in use at roughly thirty-one percent of surveyed firms. The interesting part is not simply that adoption had become substantial. It is what happened around the tool.

Firms reported benefits. They also reported barriers involving legal and regulatory concerns, information handling, suitability for the work, and employee skills. Twice as many surveyed SMEs said generative AI increased their need for highly skilled workers as said it decreased that need.

That is not the clean substitution story.

The tool enters the organization and creates a second-order problem: how should the organization use it?

The answer is not another prompt.

A company that can produce a thousand analyses needs a way to decide which analysis can move money. A hospital that can generate individualized explanations needs a way to preserve the clinical facts that cannot vary. A government office that can make every form conversational needs a way to make sure the conversation still reaches the same lawful decision.

This is what institutions for plenty have to do.

They turn abundant possibility into legitimate action.

Legitimacy sounds grand until something goes wrong.

Imagine two employees receive different answers from an internal AI system about the same travel policy. One answer is generous. The other says the expense is prohibited. Both are fluent. Both sound official.

The immediate problem is not model intelligence.

It is authority.

Which answer counts?

If the organization has maintained an authoritative policy and designed the system to derive from it, the error is repairable. If the conversational layer has quietly become the policy, the institution has allowed convenience to dissolve its own source of truth.

The same problem appears in more consequential forms everywhere.

A benefits system can explain eligibility without possessing the authority to change eligibility. A clinical assistant can summarize evidence without acquiring a medical license. A procurement agent can compare bids without being allowed to obligate public money. An automated hiring system can rank applicants while remaining embedded in an employer that bears legal and organizational responsibility for the process.

Abundant intelligence creates pressure to collapse these distinctions because the generated answer feels complete.

It is often not complete.

An answer becomes an institutional decision only after it crosses a boundary of authority.

That boundary is expensive.

It requires rules, identity, records, permissions, audit, appeal, continuity, and some mechanism for absorbing disagreement.

None of these things are glamorous.

They are exactly the things that look wasteful during periods when every institution is being compared with the speed of a chat interface.

Why does the bank need three days when the model answered in three seconds?

Sometimes it doesn't.

Sometimes the three days are simply old process. Someone waits for a spreadsheet. Someone copies a number. A manager signs because managers have always signed. The latency contains no meaningful governance at all.

That friction should disappear.

The useful distinction is between institutional delay and institutional function.

An appeal is a function.

Re-entering the same information into four forms is delay.

Separation of duties around a large transfer is a function.

Waiting for someone to return from vacation because only one person knows the password is delay.

Maintaining an authoritative record is a function.

Printing the record so another department can type it back into a computer is delay.

Artificial abundance should make institutions more ambitious about this distinction.

For decades, bureaucratic friction survived partly because removing it was expensive. Systems did not talk to one another. Rules were encoded in paper. People served as translators among software, policy, and reality. A clerk who knew how to navigate a broken process could become indispensable precisely because the process remained broken.

Better automation can remove that scarcity.

The mistake would be to remove the function with it.

NIST's generative-AI risk guidance offers an unromantic picture of what replaces the old process. Organizations need to identify risks, define responsibilities, measure behavior, monitor systems, and govern their use across a lifecycle. The language is procedural because the problem is procedural.

No one is coming to admire the governance diagram.

That is fine.

A functioning institution is often most valuable where it prevents an impressive system from doing something impressively wrong.

There is a danger here too.

Every new technology invites a governance industry.

Committees multiply. Policies metastasize. Organizations discover that writing rules about a new capability is safer than learning to use it. A system capable of saving ten hours can be surrounded by eleven hours of mandatory review.

Institutions can defend scarcity after scarcity has ended.

A profession may preserve an expensive credential for work a competent system can now perform cheaply. A company may require human drafting where human drafting adds no value. A government may force people through obsolete intermediaries because the intermediary has become part of the political structure.

Not every institution that controls access is protecting the public.

Some are protecting themselves.

Artificial abundance will intensify this conflict because many old forms of gatekeeping were justified by genuine production scarcity.

If expert analysis requires years of training and expensive professional time, access naturally concentrates. Once part of that analysis can be generated nearly free, the old gate looks different.

The institution then has to answer a harder question.

Which part of the gate protected quality, and which part merely rationed scarcity?

Medicine provides an intuitive example even without predicting any particular regulatory future. A patient may be able to receive a sophisticated explanation of a laboratory result without paying for a physician's time. That can be good. Information that was effectively scarce becomes available.

But the physician's role never consisted only of reciting the explanation.

Diagnosis involves evidence, uncertainty, history, examination, responsibility, and a plan that can change when the patient changes. Some of those functions may also become more automatable. If they do, the boundary moves again.

The institution should move with it.

The wrong response is to protect every historical task because a professional once performed it.

The other wrong response is to assume that once the task is automated, the surrounding social function has vanished.

Institutions for plenty will have to become unusually good at decomposing themselves.

What are we actually here to do?

Which part of that work is production?

Which part is coordination?

Which part is protection?

Which part is legitimacy?

Which part exists only because changing it used to cost too much?

These questions are difficult because institutions accumulate purposes the way old houses accumulate wiring.

A school educates, supervises children, certifies achievement, distributes meals, organizes social life, employs adults, transmits norms, and gives communities a calendar. A company produces goods, allocates capital, creates identity, distributes income, trains workers, concentrates risk, and negotiates with other institutions. A government agency administers law but also becomes the practical interface through which a citizen experiences the state.

A generative system may transform one of these functions and barely touch the others.

That is why the phrase artificial intelligence is often too large for institutional analysis.

Institutions do not adopt intelligence.

They change tasks, decisions, interfaces, staffing, authority, and risk.

The unit matters.

The International Labour Organization's global exposure work reaches a similar conclusion from another direction. Its 2025 index estimated that one in four jobs worldwide sits in an occupation with some potential exposure to generative AI, while only a small fraction of global employment occupied the highest exposure category. The ILO's emphasis was transformation more than wholesale replacement.

That word is institutionally inconvenient.

Replacement is clean. A role exists, then it does not.

Transformation requires redesign.

A job loses some tasks and gains others. A team that once produced drafts begins reviewing them. A manager who once allocated labor begins allocating machine permissions and human attention. A profession that once charged for information charges for judgment, responsibility, access, or relationship.

The organization chart survives, but its economic logic changes underneath it.

This may be the central institutional problem of artificial abundance.

The first-order gains are easy to see and easy to measure. Faster document. Faster code. Faster analysis. Faster service.

The second-order work is slower.

Who gets the gain?

Which process should change because the gain exists?

Which standard becomes obsolete?

Which new failure mode requires a control?

What happens to the people whose old work trained them for the new work?

How much variation can the institution tolerate before it loses a common operating picture?

The answers will differ by institution because institutions solve different coordination problems.

There will not be one AI-native organization.

A small design studio can tolerate variation that an air-traffic system cannot. A family can delegate decisions through informal trust that a pension fund must document. A game company can let a model improvise where a pharmaceutical manufacturer needs validation and records.

Plenty does not erase context.

It makes context part of the remaining scarcity.

The best institutions may therefore become less visible at the surface.

They will let abundant systems handle the cheap variation while maintaining a smaller number of clear invariants underneath. They will automate approval where approval is ceremony and preserve it where another person genuinely needs to own the risk. They will make authoritative records easier to reach, not harder. They will give people practical ways to challenge automated decisions without forcing every ordinary action through a tribunal.

This is not a prediction that institutions become wise.

Many will not.

Some will use abundance to increase surveillance, intensify work, flood employees with generated demands, or create procedural complexity faster than they remove it. Some will adopt automation because competitors did, without changing the objective the automation is supposed to serve. Some will discover that making output cheaper merely encourages everyone to demand more output.

Plenty can produce congestion.

That is another reason institutions remain.

When everyone can generate a proposal, someone still has to decide which proposal gets a budget.

When every department can produce a plan, someone still has to reconcile the plans.

When every citizen can generate a persuasive argument, a public body still needs procedures for hearing arguments without treating rhetorical volume as votes.

Abundance increases the supply of claims.

It does not increase the number of Tuesdays in the calendar.

The scarce resources underneath collective life remain stubbornly physical and social: land, energy, money, authority, legal rights, human attention, organizational capacity, and time.

Institutions allocate these things.

That is why the future of institutions is inseparable from politics.

Once production becomes cheaper, the argument does not end.

The argument moves to who receives the benefit, who absorbs the disruption, which new scarcities become privately owned, and which forms of abundance become public expectations.

The well was never only about water.

It was about who controlled the bucket.
