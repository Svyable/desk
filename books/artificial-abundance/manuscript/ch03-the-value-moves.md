# The Value Moves

The easiest way to misunderstand a new abundance is to stare at the thing becoming cheap.

When the price falls, attention follows the falling line. Newspapers become cheaper to print. Music becomes cheaper to copy. Photographs become cheaper to take. Storage becomes cheaper. Communication becomes cheaper. Computation becomes cheaper.

The first story is always about the collapsing cost.

The second story is about where the money, status, leverage, and attention go next.

That second story is usually more important.

Consider photography.

For much of the twentieth century, taking a photograph required film, processing, physical prints, and some willingness to spend a limited exposure on a particular moment. The economic act included both capture and reproduction. Digital cameras weakened those constraints. Phones weakened them further. Today the marginal cost of taking one more ordinary photograph is so close to zero that people routinely produce images they never expect to revisit.

Photography did not disappear.

It exploded.

The world got more photographs and, in many contexts, less value per ordinary photograph.

That sounds obvious until you notice what happened around the photograph.

Attention became harder to earn because images multiplied. Curation became more important because libraries became unmanageable. Search and metadata became more useful because memory alone could not navigate the archive. Social distribution became powerful because the bottleneck was no longer making an image but getting an image seen. Professional photographers still mattered where timing, trust, access, technical control, direction, editing, and responsibility mattered. A wedding photographer was not being paid merely because cameras were scarce.

The abundance changed what the customer was buying.

Recorded music offers another version.

For a consumer who remembers buying individual albums, the modern music catalog can feel absurdly abundant. A monthly subscription can provide access to more recorded music than a person could hear in several lifetimes. The scarcity is not the next song.

It is discovery.

It is attention.

It is cultural relevance.

It is the ability to become the artist someone chooses when millions of alternatives are one tap away.

Live performance also acquires a different relationship to the recorded object. A concert is inconvenient compared with streaming. You have to travel. You may stand in line. The sound may be worse than in headphones. The ticket is expensive. The artist cannot rewind the evening if something goes wrong.

Those defects are part of the product.

The event is scarce because it happens once, in one place, among a finite group of people. The recording can be reproduced. The night cannot.

This does not prove that every digital abundance creates a premium for the physical world. It shows a recurring economic move: when one layer becomes easy to reproduce, value can migrate toward a layer that remains difficult to reproduce.

That layer may sit upstream.

It may sit downstream.

It may be adjacent.

It may be social rather than technical.

It may not have looked important until the old bottleneck disappeared.

The internet supplied a particularly large demonstration.

Before the web, publishing to a broad audience required access to printing presses, broadcast licenses, distributors, bookstores, newsstands, editors, or other institutions controlling scarce channels. Those institutions excluded a great deal of bad work and a great deal of good work. They also made publication itself a signal. If something appeared in a major newspaper, magazine, or book catalog, somebody had spent money and institutional reputation putting it there.

The web made publication radically cheaper.

The result was not a world in which writing ceased to matter. It was a world with more writing than any reader could process.

Publication lost some scarcity.

Distribution platforms gained power.

Search engines gained power.

Feeds gained power.

Recommendation systems gained power.

Brands that could reliably attract an audience gained power.

Individuals who could hold attention gained power.

The scarce asset moved from the ability to place information into the world toward the ability to route attention through an overfull world.

Artificial intelligence pushes the same dynamic deeper into production.

The internet made it easy to publish the thing you had made.

Generative AI makes it easier to make the thing.

That distinction is enormous.

A person who wanted to flood a forum with messages once had to write them, copy them, or use crude automation. A person who wanted one hundred product descriptions needed a writer, templates, or simple rules. A company wanting localization had to commission translations. A developer wanting a prototype needed code. A student wanting a bespoke practice problem needed someone to create one.

Generation adds supply before distribution begins.

That means the attention problem intensifies, but it also means value can migrate within the production process itself.

Suppose a marketing department used to spend most of its time making campaign assets.

Copy had to be drafted. Images had to be commissioned. Variations had to be produced. Regional versions had to be adapted. The organization had a production bottleneck, so many ideas died before they were ever tested.

Now imagine that generation becomes cheap enough that the team can create hundreds of plausible variants.

The department has not become infinitely productive.

It has changed jobs.

Someone has to decide what the brand should sound like. Someone has to define what is unacceptable. Someone has to choose which hypotheses deserve testing. Someone has to design the experiment. Someone has to interpret the result. Someone has to decide whether a short-term conversion gain is worth a long-term change in how customers perceive the company.

The work shifts from making the options toward governing the options.

That shift can feel like a loss if the old craft was where the worker found identity.

A copywriter may not experience "more strategic responsibility" as compensation for doing less writing. A junior designer may need the repetitive production work that once trained visual judgment. A new lawyer may have learned by drafting the very documents a system can now draft instantly. A programmer may have built intuition by writing code that an agent can now produce.

Value migration is not automatically humane.

The market does not promise to preserve the route by which people used to become valuable.

This creates a training problem hidden inside abundance.

If entry-level production becomes cheap, where do experts come from?

A profession can decide that its senior members should spend more time exercising judgment only to discover that judgment was partly built through years of lower-level work. The apprentice tasks were not merely cheap labor for the institution. They were practice for the apprentice.

The same tension appeared in earlier technologies. Automation often removes drudgery and, with it, a portion of the environment in which tacit skill was formed. Sometimes we invent better training. Sometimes we discover too late that the boring task contained information.

Artificial abundance will force this question across cognitive professions because the tasks easiest to automate are often the tasks given to beginners precisely because they are bounded, reviewable, and lower risk.

The market may then place a higher premium on judgment while simultaneously weakening the traditional process for producing it.

That is a classic abundance problem.

The scarce complement gets more valuable just as its supply chain becomes less obvious.

There is another complication.

Value does not always migrate to people.

It can migrate to infrastructure owners.

If everyone can generate competent material, the platform controlling distribution may capture more value. If every company can use similar models, proprietary customer data may become more valuable. If models become interchangeable, integration into workflows may matter more than model identity. If generation is cheap but verification is costly, the certifier may gain pricing power. If creation is plentiful but a few channels control discovery, those channels can become toll roads.

Abundance can therefore increase concentration at a complementary layer even while democratizing production.

This is why a simple story about AI lowering barriers can be true and incomplete.

A teenager may gain access to capabilities once reserved for a company.

At the same time, the teenager may depend on infrastructure owned by a handful of companies, distribution governed by a few platforms, identity systems controlled elsewhere, and payment rails that can decide whether the resulting work reaches a customer.

The ability to produce is only one layer of an economy.

Once production gets easier, the other layers become easier to see.

This has strategic consequences for companies.

A firm should be suspicious of any advantage described primarily as "we can generate this now" when competitors can buy the same capability.

The question is what sits next to the generation.

Unique data?

A trusted relationship?

Distribution?

A regulatory license?

A physical network?

A brand customers believe?

A feedback loop competitors cannot easily reproduce?

A workflow deeply integrated into the customer's operations?

The authority to make a consequential decision?

The ability to verify the result cheaply?

The important asset may be whatever the model cannot commoditize because it exists outside the model.

This can sound like a comforting defense of incumbents: do not worry, something will remain scarce. That is not the point.

Sometimes the migrating value is smaller than the value that disappeared.

A newspaper can lose an enormously profitable classified-ad business and gain a modest events business. A musician can gain direct audience access without replacing the economics of recorded sales. A professional can become more productive while the market price of the profession's output still falls. A company can create more total value while capturing less of it.

Migration is not conservation.

The old profit pool does not have to reappear somewhere else at the same size.

What does persist is the need to ask where the next constraint lies.

This question is more useful than asking whether a technology is good or bad for a category in the abstract.

If legal drafting becomes abundant, what remains scarce in legal work?

If personalized tutoring becomes abundant, what remains scarce in education?

If software prototypes become abundant, what remains scarce in entrepreneurship?

If generated entertainment becomes abundant, what remains scarce in culture?

If advice becomes abundant, what remains scarce in management?

The answers will differ.

But the method is the same.

Find the thing that just became cheap.

Then stop looking at it.

Look for the line forming somewhere else.
