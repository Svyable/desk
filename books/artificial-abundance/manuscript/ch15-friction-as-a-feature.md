# Friction as a Feature

Modern product design has spent decades removing clicks.

This was usually sensible.

A form that can be completed in two minutes is better than one that takes twenty. A payment system that works is better than a line at a counter. A search box is better than walking through stacks hoping to find the right shelf. A map that reroutes around traffic is better than discovering the closure at the barricade.

Friction wastes time.

It creates errors.

It excludes people who do not have the patience, money, knowledge, mobility, or institutional confidence to fight through it.

Much of progress is the removal of unnecessary friction.

Artificial intelligence can remove more of it than almost any previous software layer.

The system can fill the form.

Write the reply.

Schedule the appointment.

Translate the document.

Compare the options.

Find the clause.

Generate the code.

Prepare the application.

Remember the deadline.

Negotiate the routine terms.

The interface shrinks toward intention.

I want this done.

Done.

This is a powerful vision.

It also reveals that not all friction was accidental.

Some friction was carrying information.

Some was creating commitment.

Some was protecting reflection.

Some was building skill.

Some was making abuse expensive.

Some was creating a boundary around something society wanted people to approach carefully.

Remove the friction and the hidden function disappears with it.

Consider a cooling-off period.

The delay is deliberately inconvenient.

Its purpose is to create time between impulse and consequence.

A consumer may have to wait before a transaction becomes final. A financial institution may impose extra steps before moving a large sum. A system may require a second person to approve a dangerous change. A doctor may pause before an irreversible treatment. A court may require formal process before the state can deprive someone of liberty.

In each case, efficiency is not the only objective.

The friction is a feature.

It says: this action should not occur merely because it is easy to initiate.

Artificial intelligence increases the importance of these designed pauses because it can make initiation almost effortless.

A person can instruct an agent to close accounts, move money, contact hundreds of people, change production systems, buy inventory, terminate subscriptions, edit records, or publish material.

The command may take one sentence.

The consequences may take years to unwind.

This creates a growing mismatch between the effort required to act and the cost of the action.

Historically, effort often served as a crude speed limit.

To send a thousand letters, somebody had to print, address, and mail them.

To make a thousand sales calls, somebody had to dial.

To alter thousands of records, somebody had to write the code and run the process.

To start dozens of businesses, apply for hundreds of jobs, submit thousands of complaints, or contact every legislator, the human cost eventually became a constraint.

Agents weaken that constraint.

This can democratize access.

It can also industrialize nuisance.

A complaint that costs five minutes to generate may cost an institution an hour to investigate.

An application that costs seconds to submit may consume meaningful review time.

A personalized legal threat can be produced cheaply while imposing real fear and expense on the recipient.

A negotiation agent can open thousands of low-probability bargaining attempts because the cost of trying approaches zero.

The abundance of action creates a need for anti-abundance mechanisms.

Rate limits.

Deposits.

Identity checks.

Proof of stake.

Reputation.

Waiting periods.

Human confirmation.

Fees.

Quotas.

These mechanisms all reintroduce friction.

The obvious danger is that incumbents use this argument to defend pointless barriers.

They will.

Every bureaucracy has described its inconvenience as necessary.

Every monopoly has reasons why switching should be difficult.

Every gatekeeper can invent a safety story.

The fact that some friction is valuable does not rehabilitate bad friction.

The question is what function the friction serves and whether that function can be achieved more cleanly.

A signature may create evidence of consent.

If a secure digital confirmation creates better evidence, keep the confirmation and lose the paper.

A waiting period may protect reconsideration.

If the delay matters, preserve the delay without forcing somebody to make three unnecessary phone calls.

A professional review may protect safety.

If automated checks can handle routine cases, reserve scarce expert attention for the cases that actually require it.

Good friction is intentional.

Bad friction is inherited.

Artificial intelligence gives institutions an opportunity to tell the difference.

There is another kind of friction that belongs to learning.

A student struggles with a problem.

The struggle is inefficient.

An intelligent tutor can supply the answer immediately.

If the objective is finishing the worksheet, this is an improvement.

If the objective is building the student's ability to solve future problems, immediate rescue can destroy the task.

Learning often requires a gap between question and answer.

The learner has to attempt retrieval.

Form a hypothesis.

Notice confusion.

Make an error.

Try again.

The difficulty forces the mind to construct something.

A tool that removes every moment of confusion can produce smooth performance and shallow capability.

This is one reason educational AI cannot be evaluated only by whether students complete work faster or report greater satisfaction.

The right amount of friction depends on the purpose.

A person learning multiplication should not need to rediscover mathematics from first principles.

A person preparing for an exam should not have the system answer every question before they attempt it.

The tutor should know when help is assistance and when help is theft.

That is a design problem.

The same is true at work.

A junior programmer who never traces a bug because an agent fixes every bug may deliver more code and build less understanding.

A young lawyer who never drafts an argument may have fewer opportunities to learn how arguments fail.

A manager who delegates every difficult conversation to an assistant may become more efficient at avoiding the very practice management requires.

A writer who asks for finished prose before forming a view may accumulate pages faster than ideas.

Friction can be training load.

Remove all the load and the capability does not grow.

This does not mean people should perform unnecessary manual work forever.

Athletes do not become stronger by carrying groceries inefficiently. They train deliberately.

Knowledge workers may need the same distinction between production and practice.

Use machines aggressively in production where the objective is the result.

Preserve or create difficulty in practice where the objective is the person.

That distinction could become a defining habit of people who remain capable in an abundance economy.

There is also social friction.

Human relationships are full of inconvenient coordination.

You wait for someone to answer.

You negotiate where to eat.

You misunderstand each other.

You repeat a story because the other person forgot.

You attend an event you would not have chosen because it matters to someone else.

You sit through a conversation that is not optimized to your interests.

An intelligent system can remove parts of this friction.

It can coordinate calendars, remember preferences, mediate disputes, suggest replies, summarize history, and warn you that a message will probably be interpreted badly.

Useful.

But a relationship with no friction is suspicious.

Another person is not a personalized service.

They have preferences that conflict with yours.

They become tired.

They misread things.

They change.

They make demands.

The difficulty is part of the evidence that the relationship exists between two separate people rather than one person and a responsive mirror.

This is why machine companionship, however sophisticated it becomes, may sharpen appreciation for certain forms of human inconvenience.

A machine can always be available.

A friend who makes time was not always available.

A machine can adapt its tone.

A partner can ask you to adapt yours.

A machine can avoid topics that upset you.

A real community sometimes forces difficult encounters with people you did not choose.

The friction can be painful.

It can also be where mutual obligation forms.

Political systems use friction for similar reasons.

Democracy is slow.

Bills move through committees.

Courts hear arguments.

Agencies take comments.

Elections occur on schedules rather than whenever a poll changes.

Checks and balances are inefficient by design.

The slowness frustrates anyone convinced their preferred action is obviously correct.

The purpose is partly to make power harder to use quickly.

Artificial intelligence will make it easier for governments to analyze, draft, communicate, administer, and perhaps enforce policy.

The temptation will be to treat every procedural delay as legacy inefficiency.

Some will be.

Others are constitutional friction.

A society should be cautious about letting abundant administrative intelligence turn bounded authority into frictionless authority.

The faster the machinery can act, the more important deliberate checks may become.

This principle applies at smaller scales too.

A company that gives agents broad permissions may need stronger approval boundaries for irreversible actions.

A family using autonomous purchasing tools may want spending thresholds.

A person using communication agents may want drafts held before certain emotionally charged messages are sent.

An investor may want a delay before a strategy can liquidate a long-term position based on one anomalous signal.

A medical system may require human contact before a life-changing decision even if the information transfer could occur automatically.

The ideal interface is not always the one with the fewest steps.

It is the one that places steps where thought, consent, safety, learning, or commitment actually require them.

This will become harder because frictionless products feel good in demos.

Instant is persuasive.

One click is persuasive.

No forms is persuasive.

Just ask is persuasive.

The benefit is immediate and visible.

The value of friction often appears only in the bad event that did not happen, the skill that remained intact, or the impulse that faded before becoming a consequence.

That makes good friction politically and commercially difficult to defend.

A competitor can remove the step and advertise speed.

The cautious product has to explain why the step remains.

Users will not always believe it.

Sometimes they should not.

The burden is on the institution to make valuable friction legible.

Tell people why the delay exists.

Let them see what risk it controls.

Remove every surrounding inconvenience that does not serve the same purpose.

A two-day cooling-off period can be clear and simple.

It does not need a fax machine.

A high-risk approval can be deliberate without becoming bureaucratic theater.

A learning system can require an attempt before revealing the answer without humiliating the learner.

A relationship can preserve human conversation without forcing people through bad scheduling software.

The point is not to celebrate difficulty.

It is to stop treating difficulty as a single category.

Some difficulty is waste.

Some is protection.

Some is practice.

Some is commitment.

Some is the cost of dealing with another free person.

Artificial abundance can remove an enormous amount of useless friction.

That will make life better.

It will also make the remaining useful friction more important to identify because the default direction of technology is toward removal.

When a machine can make almost any action easier, choosing which actions should remain hard becomes a form of design.

Easy is not always humane.

Sometimes the step you want to remove is the place where judgment enters.
