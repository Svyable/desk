# The Price of a Thought

In November 2022, a system performing at roughly the level of GPT-3.5 on one widely used language benchmark cost about twenty dollars per million tokens to query. By October 2024, Stanford's AI Index estimated that a system reaching the same benchmark level could be queried for about seven cents per million tokens.

The comparison is imperfect in all the usual ways. Benchmarks compress many abilities into one score. Tokens are not thoughts. A cheap model that is wrong at the wrong moment can be more expensive than an expert. The physical infrastructure behind an apparently weightless answer is not free: chips have to be fabricated, data centers financed, electricity generated, cooling built, networks operated, and engineering teams paid.

Still, a price moving from dollars to cents changes behavior before it changes philosophy.

At twenty dollars, you think about whether a million tokens are worth spending. At seven cents, the question begins to sound silly.

The same transition has happened many times before. A resource does not need to become literally free to become abundant in practice. It only has to become cheap enough that people stop rationing it in the old way.

A household that once counted long-distance minutes behaves differently after voice calls become effectively unlimited. A photographer with thirty-six exposures makes different choices from someone carrying a phone with room for another ten thousand pictures. A programmer thinks differently about storage when a megabyte is expensive than when a terabyte disappears into the price of a laptop. The threshold that matters is behavioral: the point at which conservation stops organizing ordinary decisions.

Artificial intelligence is crossing that threshold unevenly across cognitive tasks.

The first change is obvious. People ask for more.

They request another draft, then another. They ask for five versions instead of one. They translate material they would previously have left untranslated. They summarize documents they would not have had time to read. They generate explanations at different levels of difficulty. They ask software systems to inspect code that no colleague had time to review. They hand over repetitive correspondence, first-pass research, notes, formatting, classification, and increasingly long chains of work.

The second change is less obvious.

People begin attempting things they would not have purchased from a person at all.

This is where the economics becomes interesting.

When a technology merely substitutes for an existing expensive service, we can imagine the old market becoming cheaper. A company spent ten thousand dollars on a task; now it spends one thousand. The gain is visible.

But many uses of abundant intelligence have no previous invoice.

The owner of a small plumbing company was not hiring a strategy consultant every Tuesday night. A high-school student was not retaining a private tutor for every question. A hobbyist was not paying a software engineer to build a small personal tool. A middle manager was not commissioning a professional editor to rewrite every uncomfortable email. A traveler was not employing a translator to interpret a restaurant menu. A researcher was not hiring a junior analyst to pursue every speculative branch of an argument.

The relevant comparison is often not expensive human work versus cheap machine work.

It is some work versus no work.

That distinction matters because abundance expands demand.

Economists have known for a long time that efficiency can produce effects that look paradoxical if you assume demand stays fixed. When a resource becomes cheaper to use, people often find more uses for it. Better lighting did not lead humanity to sit in the dark after buying fewer candles. Cheaper computing did not make the world spend less on computation. It put computation inside cars, watches, cameras, toys, thermostats, warehouses, farms, hospitals, phones, and objects that previously had no plausible reason to contain a computer.

Something similar can happen to cognition.

A thought that cost one hour of a professional's time had to justify the hour. A thought that costs fractions of a cent can be generated speculatively.

What would this contract look like if rewritten for a twelve-year-old?

What are ten failure modes I have not considered?

How would a skeptical regulator read this proposal?

Can you make a version for Brazil, Japan, and Germany?

What if the pricing model changes completely?

Can you inspect every file in this repository for the same bug?

Can you keep watching this process and tell me when something changes?

Can you generate one hundred candidates and rank them?

The questions multiply because asking them becomes inexpensive.

This does not mean the answers become equally valuable. In fact, it produces the opposite problem. Once it is cheap to generate candidates, the cost moves into evaluation.

A company with one proposed strategy can argue about the strategy. A company with one thousand machine-generated strategies has a search problem.

Which assumptions matter? Which outputs are duplicates wearing different language? Which proposal is clever but impossible to implement? Which one creates legal exposure? Which one looks attractive because it mirrors the evaluator's preferences? Which recommendation survives contact with customers? Which candidate is merely plausible prose with no causal mechanism underneath it?

The abundance of answers creates demand for better questions and better filters.

This is the first migration of value in the book.

Production becomes cheaper. Selection becomes more important.

You can see an early version of this in software.

For decades, software development was constrained by the number of people who could translate intent into working code. The constraint still exists, but tools increasingly let programmers generate, inspect, refactor, test, and explain code with machine assistance. More recent agentic systems can remain on tasks long enough that the unit of work starts to shift from a suggestion in an editor toward a delegated objective.

The measurement is still young and narrow, but it is moving. METR's evaluations track the length of software tasks that frontier systems can complete with a given probability of success. The important point is not a single headline number, which will age quickly. It is that the evaluated horizon has been extending. Systems are being asked to hold more context, make more intermediate decisions, recover from errors, and finish work that would have occupied a skilled person for longer periods.

That does not turn software engineering into a free good. It changes what is worth attempting.

A team that once chose three experiments because it only had time for three may now run thirty. A developer may build an internal tool that would never have survived a normal prioritization meeting. A founder may test several product directions before hiring a specialist. An engineer may ask a model to produce a disposable implementation whose only purpose is to reveal whether an idea is worth taking seriously.

Disposable cognition is a strange category.

We are familiar with disposable objects because manufacturing got cheap enough to produce them. We are less familiar with disposable analysis: an argument generated to test another argument, a prototype made to be thrown away, a simulation built only to expose an assumption, a translation created for one conversation, a personalized explanation that exists for five minutes and is never read again.

Abundance makes this rational.

It also complicates conventional measures of value.

A large share of the benefit from cheap digital goods can be hard to see in revenue because the price approaches zero while consumer surplus rises. Stanford's 2026 AI Index estimated rapidly growing consumer surplus from generative AI tools at the same time many of those tools remained free or close to it. Whatever one thinks of the exact estimate, the measurement problem is real. A service can become more economically useful while generating less revenue per unit of use.

This is one reason arguments about whether artificial intelligence is "worth" its investment can become confused.

There are at least three different questions hiding inside the word worth.

Is the capability useful to the person receiving it?

Can the provider capture enough of that usefulness as revenue?

Will the capital invested in the infrastructure earn an attractive return?

Those answers can diverge dramatically.

Railroads can transform a country and bankrupt investors. The internet can create enormous consumer surplus while destroying some of the businesses it displaces. Open-source software can become indispensable infrastructure without charging a license fee for every use. A technology can be socially productive, commercially disruptive, and a disappointing investment for particular owners at the same time.

Artificial abundance makes that divergence easier to imagine because the marginal price of cognitive output can fall faster than the fixed costs required to produce the systems behind it.

The data center is expensive.

The next paragraph is cheap.

The model may require billions in infrastructure. The next customer question may cost almost nothing to answer relative to the value of the transaction it helps complete.

This split between high fixed cost and low marginal cost has shaped digital economics for decades. What changes with AI is the category of output sitting on the low-marginal-cost side.

Not just copies of information.

Variations.

Interpretations.

Plans.

Code.

Decisions proposed for someone else's approval.

Language adapted to one person.

Work that looks, at least superficially, like the product of time.

That last feature matters.

Human beings have long used visible effort as a rough proxy for value because effort was difficult to fake at scale. A detailed report implied that someone had spent time researching and writing it. A large catalog suggested years of accumulated work. A polished proposal signaled preparation. A custom illustration implied attention from an illustrator. A thoughtful letter represented a portion of another person's finite afternoon.

Once output can be generated faster than the receiver can consume it, the signal weakens.

This does not make the output bad. It makes effort less legible.

A hundred-page report may be excellent, useless, or unread machine exhaust. Its length tells you less than it used to. A personalized message may have required thirty minutes of care or three seconds of automation. A beautifully rendered concept may represent months of design work or one prompt among thousands. A job applicant may submit technically polished work that demonstrates either competence or access to a competent system.

The price of production falls before institutions learn how to interpret the new signals.

That lag creates opportunity and confusion.

People who learn to use cheap cognition well can reach into work that previously sat beyond their budgets. Organizations can examine more options. Consumers can demand more personalization. Scientific and technical teams can test more possibilities. Public agencies can potentially provide forms of guidance that were impossible to staff at human scale.

At the same time, every receiver faces a growing problem of surplus.

More proposals than can be funded.

More media than can be watched.

More advice than can be followed.

More code than can be maintained.

More analysis than can be verified.

More messages than can be answered.

A civilization can solve one shortage by creating another.

The first shortage was capable production.

The next one is deciding what deserves a finite human life.
