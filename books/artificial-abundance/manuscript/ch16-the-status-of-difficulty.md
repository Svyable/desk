# The Status of Difficulty

A marathon is a terrible way to travel twenty-six miles.

That is not the point.

Nobody enters a marathon because roads have failed to provide more efficient transportation. The difficulty is chosen. The time, pain, training, and uncertainty are part of the value.

This seems obvious in sport.

It is less obvious in knowledge work because difficulty there has often been confused with economic necessity.

The accountant works through the spreadsheet because somebody has to.

The programmer writes the code because the software does not exist yet.

The student solves the problem because that is how the answer is produced.

The lawyer reads the cases because the argument depends on them.

The designer creates the variants because there is no other way to see the alternatives.

When artificial intelligence makes more of these outputs easy to produce, difficulty changes status.

Some of it disappears.

Some of it becomes optional.

And optional difficulty becomes meaningful in a different way.

A skill practiced after the market no longer requires it can become a signal of identity, discipline, taste, autonomy, or belonging.

People still bake bread in homes surrounded by industrial bakeries.

They garden while supermarkets exist.

They knit, carve wood, restore cars, develop film, play acoustic instruments, run long distances, learn dead languages, solve puzzles, climb mountains, and cook meals that take longer than delivery.

These activities persist because efficiency is not the only source of value.

The work can be the product.

Artificial abundance may expand this category into intellectual life.

A person may write without machine completion because they want to know whether they can form the thought.

A programmer may build something manually because understanding the system matters more than shipping it quickly.

A student may solve a proof without assistance because the capability being trained is the point.

A designer may sketch before generating variants because the hand slows perception enough to notice form.

A reader may choose the full book rather than the summary because the time spent inside the argument is part of what reading means.

These choices can look inefficient from outside.

They are only inefficient if the objective is the artifact.

If the objective is the person, the calculation changes.

This creates a distinction between productive difficulty and ceremonial difficulty.

Productive difficulty changes capability, perception, commitment, or relationship.

Ceremonial difficulty exists mainly to preserve a status hierarchy after the original reason for the difficulty disappears.

Societies often confuse the two.

A profession may insist that newcomers perform a tedious task because veterans had to perform it.

A school may preserve an exercise because difficulty looks rigorous even when the exercise no longer teaches the intended concept.

A company may celebrate long hours after tools have made the hours unnecessary.

A bureaucracy may require suffering as proof of seriousness.

This is not valuable friction.

It is inherited pain.

Artificial abundance will provoke fights over which difficulties deserve to survive.

Those fights will often be moralized.

Using assistance is cheating.

Refusing assistance is elitist.

Manual work is authentic.

Manual work is nostalgia.

The tool makes people lazy.

The tool liberates people from drudgery.

All of these claims can be true in particular cases.

The useful question is what the difficulty was doing.

If the difficulty existed only because production was expensive, removing it is progress.

If the difficulty built the capability we still care about, removing it requires a replacement.

If the difficulty served mainly as a gate protecting status, the gate deserves suspicion.

This can be seen clearly in education.

Suppose a student is assigned an essay.

There are at least three possible objectives.

The teacher may want to know whether the student understands the subject.

The teacher may want the student to practice constructing an argument in writing.

The teacher may want a polished essay as the final product.

Before generative AI, one assignment could serve all three objectives because producing the essay required much of the understanding and writing process.

Now the objectives can separate.

A student can produce a polished essay without developing the same writing capability.

That does not mean the tool should be banned from education.

It means the assignment has to decide what it is testing.

If the objective is subject understanding, an oral defense, live discussion, annotated process, or adaptive questioning may provide stronger evidence.

If the objective is writing skill, the student may need protected periods of unaided composition.

If the objective is producing a useful final document, assistance may be entirely appropriate.

The old difficulty was doing several jobs at once.

Artificial abundance forces the jobs apart.

The same is true in professional training.

A surgeon practices procedures in simulation not because the hospital values inefficient surgery but because performance under pressure requires embodied skill.

A pilot rehearses failures that automation will usually handle because rare moments still require understanding.

A lawyer may need to construct arguments personally even if a system can draft them because the lawyer has to recognize when the generated argument is weak.

A programmer may need to learn data structures even if an agent can implement them because architecture and debugging depend on understanding what the code is doing.

Some manual capability becomes insurance.

It may not be used often.

That does not make it worthless.

A fire extinguisher is rarely productive.

The value appears when the normal system fails.

As artificial systems absorb routine work, human difficulty may increasingly function this way.

People train for the edge cases.

The skill survives because the consequences of total dependence are unacceptable.

This will not apply to every skill.

Few people need to retain the ability to perform long division on paper as an emergency backup for civilization. Societies let capabilities disappear when tools replace them safely enough.

The challenge is knowing which capabilities are structural.

Can the person recognize nonsense in the machine's answer?

Can they operate when the network is unavailable?

Can they reason when the objective changes?

Can they explain the system to someone who does not trust it?

Can they notice the edge case the training examples did not contain?

Can they take over when the automated process enters an unfamiliar state?

Difficulty that builds these abilities may remain worth paying for.

There is another reason chosen difficulty will gain status.

Abundance weakens traditional effort signals.

If anyone can generate polished work, the fact that polished work exists says less about the person behind it.

People will look for harder-to-fake signals.

Completion of a demanding course.

A live performance.

A long apprenticeship.

A difficult physical accomplishment.

A portfolio showing years of decisions.

A public body of work created under constraints.

Service in an institution that imposes real obligations.

These signals can become valuable precisely because they resist cheap generation.

This is not automatically good.

Status competition can turn chosen difficulty into waste.

People may pursue increasingly elaborate tests simply because easier achievements no longer distinguish them.

If a degree becomes common, the market demands another degree.

If a marathon becomes ordinary in a status group, ultramarathons become interesting.

If machine-assisted coding makes competent software common, employers may invent increasingly artificial interview rituals to identify candidates.

Scarcity can migrate into ordeal.

The economy has seen this before through credential inflation.

Artificial abundance could accelerate it.

A society rich in capability can still become poor in access if every desirable position requires costly proof that the applicant could have done the work without the abundant tools everyone uses on the job.

The response should not be to abolish difficult tests.

It should be to ask whether the difficulty predicts the thing we care about.

A firefighter should meet difficult physical standards because the job can require difficult physical acts.

A surgeon should pass demanding clinical assessments because errors have serious consequences.

A software company should be cautious about rejecting an excellent engineer because the engineer cannot perform a puzzle under interview conditions that never occur in the actual job.

The status of difficulty should follow function.

This sounds obvious.

Status rarely follows function cleanly.

People admire costly signals because cost itself suggests commitment.

The expensive watch.

The rare degree.

The difficult climb.

The hand-made object.

The long book read in full.

The skill learned after the skill stopped being economically necessary.

Artificial abundance will create more opportunities for these signals because cheap substitutes make chosen cost conspicuous.

Handwritten code may become like handwritten calligraphy in some contexts.

Manual research may become a form of craft.

A live, unedited performance may command a premium precisely because it cannot be endlessly regenerated.

A restaurant may advertise that a dish takes three days to prepare even though faster methods exist.

The cost becomes part of the story.

This can look absurd to an economist focused only on output.

Human beings do not consume only outputs.

They consume meanings attached to processes.

The process tells us who made something, how much they cared, what tradition it belongs to, what discipline it required, and what kind of person we imagine ourselves becoming by participating in it.

A machine can help produce those meanings.

It can also make them easier to counterfeit.

That is why the strongest status signals will migrate toward constraints that are difficult to simulate.

Time is one.

Embodiment is another.

Public accountability is another.

Repeated performance is another.

A single polished artifact becomes cheap.

A decade of coherent choices remains harder.

This changes creativity.

The artist of abundance may need constraints more than tools.

When every color, style, arrangement, voice, and variation is available instantly, the problem becomes choosing a small space in which to work.

A poet chooses a form.

A filmmaker chooses a lens and a runtime.

A musician chooses an instrument.

A designer limits the palette.

Constraint gives decisions weight because not every possibility remains open.

Generative systems can remove technical constraints.

Artists may reintroduce conceptual ones.

One camera.

One take.

No synthetic correction.

Only found materials.

Only sounds recorded in one place.

A novel written under a formal rule.

These restrictions are not technological necessities.

They are ways of creating a shape that abundance would otherwise dissolve.

Difficulty becomes composition.

The same can happen in personal life.

A person may choose to walk when transport is available.

Cook when prepared food is available.

Memorize when retrieval is available.

Navigate without assistance occasionally.

Sit with a problem before asking the machine.

Have a conversation without a device mediating the pauses.

These are small acts of retained capability.

They are not a rejection of technology.

They are decisions about which parts of the self should not be entirely outsourced.

The danger is turning this into purity culture.

People with time and money can afford elaborate manual practices that others cannot. A parent working two jobs does not need a lecture about the spiritual value of hand-cooking every meal. A disabled person using automation to remove friction is not less authentic. A small business using AI to compete with a larger company is not failing a moral test because it did not perform every task manually.

Chosen difficulty is valuable only when it is genuinely chosen.

Forced difficulty is scarcity.

That distinction should remain sharp.

Artificial abundance is good when it removes forced difficulty.

The opportunity is then to decide which difficulties to invite back for reasons other than necessity.

Training.

Craft.

Community.

Health.

Autonomy.

Meaning.

A person may become able to produce far more while deliberately choosing a few things to do the hard way.

That is not contradiction.

It is what abundance makes possible.

When difficulty stops being mandatory, difficulty can become expressive.

The question changes from “Why is this so hard?”

To “Why am I choosing for this to remain hard?”

The answer tells you what the activity is really for.
