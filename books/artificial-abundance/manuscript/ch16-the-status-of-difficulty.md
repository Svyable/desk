# The Status of Difficulty

For a long time, difficulty was evidence.

A thing that took years to learn was probably scarce.

A thing that took weeks to make was probably not disposable.

A person who could do something difficult had probably paid some cost that other people had not.

The cost might have been time, practice, failure, money, discomfort, access, patience, or repeated embarrassment. Whatever its form, difficulty helped separate the person who had done the work from the person who had not.

Then a machine learns to produce the visible result without paying the visible cost.

What happens to the status attached to difficulty?

The first answer is obvious and wrong.

It is tempting to say that difficulty loses value once difficulty is no longer necessary.

Sometimes it does.

Nobody is impressed that a clerk can add a column of numbers by hand when a spreadsheet can do it instantly. We no longer treat the ability to set movable type as a prerequisite for publishing an argument. A traveler does not earn status by refusing GPS and getting lost with dignity. When technology turns an old difficulty into routine infrastructure, the social premium can disappear quickly.

But difficulty has never been valued only for productive necessity.

People run marathons even though cars exist.

They climb mountains even though helicopters exist.

They bake bread at home while supermarkets sell bread.

They learn instruments after recordings have made professional performances available everywhere.

They make furniture, knit sweaters, restore old cars, develop film, write by hand, memorize poems, play chess, grow vegetables, and choose long trails to places reachable by road.

The existence of an easier route does not always kill the value of the harder one.

Sometimes it creates the value.

This is where artificial abundance becomes culturally strange.

If competent output becomes cheap, difficulty can separate from utility and become a signal in its own right.

That signal is unreliable.

It is also powerful.

Psychologists have studied several mechanisms that help explain why.

One is often called the IKEA effect. In experiments by Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely, participants who successfully assembled objects valued their own creations more highly than people who had not invested the same labor. The effect had boundaries. When people failed to complete the assembly or built something only to destroy it, the increase in valuation disappeared.

The work mattered partly because it became theirs.

This does not prove that labor makes an object objectively better. Anyone who has seen a badly assembled bookcase understands the distinction.

It shows that effort can become part of the value experienced by the maker.

That is different from a second phenomenon, the effort heuristic.

In a set of experiments published in 2004, researchers found that observers sometimes rated the same kind of work more highly when they believed more effort had gone into producing it. If people were told a poem, painting, or object had taken longer to make, perceived quality and value could rise.

The intuitive story is familiar.

That took a long time.

It must be good.

Or at least serious.

Or at least worth looking at.

But the finding should not be turned into a law of human nature.

A 2023 preregistered replication project produced mixed results. Some of the original patterns held in some conditions; others did not. Effects on monetary valuation were inconsistent. The broader picture is more useful precisely because it is less neat.

People sometimes use effort as information about value, especially when quality is hard to judge directly.

They do not do so mechanically.

Context matters.

That caveat opens the interesting question for artificial abundance.

What happens when effort becomes easier to separate from outcome?

Suppose two illustrations are equally attractive to a buyer.

One was generated in twenty seconds.

The other took an artist three weeks to paint.

If the buyer only needs an image for a disposable banner, the production history may be irrelevant. Paying for three weeks of labor would make no more sense than paying extra for arithmetic performed with an abacus.

If the buyer wants an original painting for a home, the three weeks may be part of what is being purchased.

The same visual quality can enter two different markets because the process changes the meaning of the object.

This distinction already exists everywhere.

A machine-made rug and a hand-knotted rug both cover a floor.

A factory watch and a hand-finished mechanical watch both tell time.

A restaurant meal and a meal cooked by a friend both provide calories.

A recorded concert and a live concert can contain the same composition.

A photograph of a mountain and climbing the mountain can produce the same view.

Utility does not exhaust value.

When utility becomes cheap, non-utility can become easier to price.

This is the economic opening for difficult things.

The dangerous mistake is to assume that all difficulty becomes prestigious.

People do not generally admire a tax website because it is hard to use.

Nobody boasts that a hospital made scheduling surgery unnecessarily confusing.

A long checkout flow does not become luxury retail merely because it wastes time.

Difficulty earns status only when it can plausibly signal something the audience values.

Skill.

Commitment.

Taste.

Discipline.

Endurance.

Tradition.

Access.

Identity.

Or membership in a group willing to bear the same cost.

This last category matters more than it first appears.

Many difficult activities are social sorting devices.

A degree is not valuable only because the coursework teaches useful material. It can also signal that the holder passed through a selection process, persisted for years, learned institutional codes, and belonged to a network. A marathon medal is not useful because metal is scarce. The value lies in the training and the shared meaning attached to finishing. A difficult professional credential can signal competence partly because the candidate had to satisfy an expensive screening process.

Artificial intelligence challenges these systems where the visible output used to serve as evidence of the hidden work.

The essay was evidence of reading.

The code was evidence of programming ability.

The design was evidence of craft.

The analysis was evidence that the analyst could perform the analysis.

The fluent foreign-language email was evidence of language skill.

Once a system can produce the artifact cheaply, the artifact stops carrying the same amount of information about the producer.

Institutions can respond in at least three ways.

They can stop caring about the hidden work and judge the output.

They can find a new way to verify the hidden capability.

Or they can make the process deliberately difficult again so that completion once more becomes evidence.

The third response is the most fascinating and the most dangerous.

Artificial abundance can create an arms race in costly signaling.

If anyone can submit a polished application, perhaps the serious applicants will be asked to complete a live exercise.

If anyone can generate a beautiful portfolio, perhaps the respected portfolio will include process evidence.

If anyone can produce a plausible essay, perhaps selective schools will move more evaluation into supervised environments.

If anyone can build an attractive website, perhaps design status will move toward custom physical installations, live experiences, or work whose production is difficult to automate.

Each move can restore information.

Each can also create waste.

A signal is socially useful when the cost is connected to the underlying quality we care about.

A difficult medical board examination can be defensible if it measures knowledge needed to practice medicine.

A difficult initiation ritual tells us much less about whether someone will be a good engineer.

A ten-hour unpaid hiring exercise may reveal persistence. It may also select for applicants with enough free time to tolerate a bad process.

An insistence on “handmade” work may preserve meaningful craft. It may also force people to avoid efficient tools merely to satisfy a romantic story about production.

The signal can outlive the reason.

This is where status gets expensive.

Economists and sociologists have long observed that some goods derive value from their cost or scarcity rather than despite it. The point here is narrower. Artificial abundance can manufacture new conditions in which unnecessary difficulty becomes attractive because necessary difficulty has disappeared.

The easier ordinary competence becomes, the more some people will seek contexts in which competence still has to be demonstrated through costly action.

This is already visible in fitness.

Industrial economies made physical exertion less necessary for many workers. The result was not the end of physical effort. It helped create markets in chosen effort: gyms, endurance races, climbing, boutique training, long-distance cycling, obstacle competitions.

People pay for the privilege of performing labor their ancestors might have performed unwillingly.

That sounds absurd only if effort is understood purely as production input.

For the modern athlete, effort is the product.

Artificial intelligence may create analogous markets for cognition.

People may choose to write without assistance, calculate without tools, navigate without recommendations, learn languages without instant translation, make art without generative systems, or study from primary material rather than summaries.

Some of this will be nostalgia.

Some will be training.

Some will be pleasure.

Some will be status.

Some will be an attempt to preserve capabilities that deteriorate when they are never used.

We should not assume which motive dominates.

The person writing by hand may be performing identity rather than improving prose.

The student solving a problem unaided may be doing the exact work necessary to learn.

The musician refusing a generative composition tool may be protecting a creative process that produces unexpected ideas.

The executive refusing AI summaries may simply be wasting time.

Difficulty has to be evaluated by function, not aesthetics.

This links the chapter back to friction.

Protective friction adds difficulty because the difficulty prevents a consequential mistake.

Status difficulty adds difficulty because the difficulty communicates something.

Training difficulty adds difficulty because the difficulty changes the person doing the work.

These can look identical from the outside.

A student closes the laptop and works through the problem on paper.

Is that inefficient nostalgia?

A test of competence?

A learning exercise?

A moral performance about technology?

The answer depends on what the activity is for.

Artificial abundance makes purpose harder to avoid because the shortcut is always visible.

Before the shortcut, effort did not need justification. There was no alternative.

After the shortcut, effort becomes a choice.

Chosen difficulty carries meaning that compulsory difficulty did not.

That is why luxury often migrates toward labor-intensive processes after mass production improves.

A handmade shoe becomes more notable once factory shoes are cheap and good.

A letterpress invitation becomes special after ordinary printing becomes trivial.

A hand-drawn animation can become aesthetically meaningful in a world of digital production.

A restaurant may advertise fermentation that takes days, dough that rests for hours, broth that cooks overnight, or ingredients grown in a difficult place.

Time itself becomes an ingredient.

The consumer is not merely buying delay.

The consumer is buying a story in which delay is evidence that the producer refused a cheaper path.

This is easy to parody because status markets invite parody.

A company can invent difficulty purely for the marketing benefit of claiming difficulty.

A “human-crafted” object may involve little more than a final manual touch.

A maker may emphasize the least efficient part of production because inefficiency has become proof of care.

An organization may forbid useful tools so that employees can display effort to managers who still confuse effort with contribution.

A worker may spend six hours producing what could have been produced in one because visible struggle remains easier to reward than invisible judgment.

This is where artificial abundance can collide with workplace culture.

Many organizations have never been good at measuring value directly.

They measure proxies.

Hours at the desk.

Speed of response.

Number of documents.

Complexity of presentation.

Volume of visible activity.

Generative AI can destroy some of these proxies almost overnight.

If a ten-page report can be produced in minutes, page count stops proving diligence.

If a polished email can be generated instantly, polish stops proving care.

If code volume rises cheaply, lines of code become even worse evidence of engineering contribution.

If a junior employee can produce an executive-looking deck on the first day, deck polish stops proving seniority.

The organization then faces a choice.

It can improve its measurement of actual outcomes.

Or it can search for new forms of visible difficulty.

The second path is easier.

That is how rituals survive technological change.

The old ritual loses information, so a new ritual appears.

More meetings.

More live presentations.

More credentials.

More supervised exercises.

More demands to prove that the work was done “personally.”

Some will be sensible.

Some will be theater designed to restore the manager's ability to see effort.

This is not merely annoying. It can distort who succeeds.

Costly signals favor people who can afford the cost.

If professional status requires unpaid time, expensive degrees, travel, low-paid apprenticeships, or performative overwork, the signal can select for wealth and tolerance for exploitation alongside whatever capability it was intended to measure.

Artificial abundance could make this better by weakening the need for some old credentials.

It could also make it worse if institutions respond by inventing harder ways to prove human competence.

The politics of difficulty therefore matters.

Who gets to choose difficulty and who has it imposed on them?

A wealthy person can choose an expensive retreat with no phone and call the inconvenience restorative.

A poor person without reliable connectivity experiences the same absence as exclusion.

A hobbyist can choose to build furniture by hand.

A worker denied effective tools may experience manual production as drudgery.

A traveler can choose a multi-day hike to reach a remote village.

A resident may wish the road were better.

Chosen scarcity and imposed scarcity are not the same product.

This distinction should discipline any celebration of the “expensively human.”

There is a seductive cultural story in which machines take the cheap, easy, synthetic work while human beings return to craft, presence, slowness, difficulty, and meaning.

Some people will be able to live that story.

Others may be asked to perform low-paid human labor precisely because wealthy customers value visible human service.

A handcrafted economy can contain artisans.

It can also contain servants.

A live-attention economy can contain teachers and therapists doing meaningful work.

It can also contain people paid to provide emotional presence at industrial scale.

A premium on human effort does not automatically create dignity.

It creates a price signal.

Institutions decide how that price is distributed.

There is also a deeper mistake hiding in the phrase “easy is cheap.”

Ease can be the result of enormous invisible difficulty.

A simple interface may sit on top of years of engineering.

A clear paragraph may be the product of deep knowledge.

A musician can make a difficult piece sound effortless because of decades of practice.

A good surgeon can make a procedure appear calm because preparation has absorbed the chaos.

A mature technology often converts prior difficulty into present ease.

To reward visible struggle too strongly is to punish mastery.

This is why effort cannot become our replacement metric for value.

The person who takes ten hours is not necessarily more committed than the person who takes one.

The handmade object is not necessarily better than the manufactured one.

The human-written analysis is not necessarily more accurate than the AI-assisted analysis.

The difficult route is not necessarily more meaningful.

Difficulty is evidence only when we know what it is evidence of.

That is the rule artificial abundance will force us to learn.

When output was expensive, effort, competence, and value often traveled together closely enough that we could be lazy about distinguishing them.

When output becomes cheap, the connections loosen.

We will see extraordinary work made quickly.

We will see terrible work made slowly.

We will see people deliberately choose difficult processes because the process is the point.

We will see institutions preserve difficult processes because they have forgotten what the process was meant to prove.

We will see markets place premiums on handwork, live presence, and visible commitment.

We will see counterfeit effort designed to capture those premiums.

And we will have to become more precise about why difficulty deserves respect in the first place.

The best answer will rarely be because it was hard.

Sometimes difficulty builds skill.

Sometimes it protects against error.

Sometimes it proves commitment.

Sometimes it creates a valued experience.

Sometimes it is simply what happens when a scarce human being chooses to spend finite time on one thing rather than another.

Those are real sources of value.

The difficulty itself is not sacred.

If artificial intelligence makes useful things easier, we should let easy things become easy.

The interesting question is what people do with the difficulty they choose to keep.

The next part of the book moves toward the place where that choice becomes unavoidable.

As digital competence gets cheaper, the physical world does not become less physical.

A chair still has to exist somewhere.

A body still has to be somewhere.

A building still has to stand.

A meal still has to be grown, moved, cooked, and eaten.

Reality has not discovered infinite versions.
