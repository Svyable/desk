# The Human Signal

A handwritten note can be worse than a generated one.

The handwriting may be hard to read. The joke may be weak. The apology may be clumsy. The sentence may repeat itself. The person may choose the wrong word and cross it out.

Still, the note can mean more.

Not because imperfection is automatically beautiful. Not because human beings possess a mystical substance that leaks through ink. The difference can be simpler.

Someone spent part of a finite life making this particular thing for you.

That fact has always carried value. Artificial abundance makes it easier to see.

For most of modern history, effort, origin, and quality were tangled together because production costs forced them to overlap. A professional-looking photograph probably involved a photographer who knew how to expose film. A polished illustration probably involved someone who could draw. A fluent translation usually implied access to a person with the relevant languages. A letter in a familiar voice probably came from the person whose name appeared at the bottom because producing convincing imitations at scale was difficult.

Those connections are weakening.

A polished surface no longer tells you as much about the process behind it.

That creates a strange market for evidence of presence.

The first mistake is to call this authenticity and move on.

Authenticity is one of those words that becomes less useful the more valuable it becomes. A restaurant is authentic. A musician is authentic. A travel experience is authentic. A founder is authentic. A brand is authentic. A social-media post is authentic. A handmade chair is authentic. The word often means little more than “I want to believe the thing came from the kind of process I admire.”

Artificial intelligence forces a more precise question.

What exactly do you value about human origin?

Sometimes the answer is quality.

A person may believe a human artist will make a better image, a human lawyer will make a better argument, or a human teacher will give a better explanation. That belief can be right or wrong depending on the task, the person, and the system.

Sometimes the answer is responsibility.

A customer wants to know that a human professional stands behind the diagnosis, the contract, the safety decision, or the promise. The words themselves may have been drafted with assistance. The valuable signal is that someone with a name, role, duty, and reputation accepted them.

Sometimes the answer is relationship.

A birthday message from a friend matters because the friend chose to send it. If the same words were generated automatically by a service that noticed the date, the semantic content might be identical while the social meaning changes.

Sometimes the answer is process.

The buyer of a hand-thrown bowl may care that a person shaped clay on a wheel. The point is not that industrial ceramics cannot be better made. The process is part of the product being purchased.

Sometimes the answer is sacrifice.

A live performance can matter because the performer cannot restart the evening after every mistake. A difficult meal cooked for friends can matter because time was spent. A visit can matter because travel was inconvenient. The cost itself is not the whole value, but it proves something about priority.

These are different human signals.

They should not be collapsed into a single claim that human-made things are better.

The research on AI-generated art is helpful because it refuses that simplification.

In one experimental study published in Scientific Reports, participants were not consistently able to distinguish human-created from AI-generated images. That alone is a useful correction to the confidence with which people often claim they can simply feel the difference.

They often cannot.

The same study found something more interesting. Participants' beliefs about origin affected evaluation. Images believed to be AI-generated tended to be evaluated less favorably, even though the actual AI-generated works could perform well on liking measures.

The source belief mattered independently of perfect source detection.

That is a remarkable economic fact.

A signal can have value even when the underlying object does not reliably reveal the signal.

Wine already works this way. So do luxury goods, professional credentials, antiques, signatures, geographic indications, and branded products. The physical object does not always let an ordinary buyer infer the process or origin unaided. Markets build labels, certificates, reputations, provenance records, experts, and enforcement around attributes people care about but cannot easily observe.

Human origin may increasingly become one of those attributes.

That does not mean every market will care.

Nobody needs a human being to personally calculate a square root to make the answer meaningful. Most people do not value a handmade airline reservation. A manually typed password reset is not more authentic than an automated one. If a system can translate a train schedule accurately at midnight, the exhausted traveler is unlikely to demand evidence that a bilingual employee suffered through the sentence.

Utility can overwhelm process.

This is why the human signal will not command one universal premium.

Its value depends on what is being bought.

A useful distinction is whether the process is merely a means to the result or part of the result itself.

When I buy a weather forecast, I mainly care whether it predicts the weather.

When I buy a painting, the history of the object may matter to me alongside the image.

When I receive medical advice, accuracy matters enormously, but I may also care that an accountable clinician has reviewed the recommendation.

When my daughter writes me a letter, I am not purchasing informational efficiency at all.

The same sentence can therefore have different value depending on the relationship around it.

“I am proud of you” is cheap language.

Its price can be close to infinite or close to zero depending on who says it and why.

Generative systems do not destroy this distinction. They make it impossible to ignore.

For years, digital communication trained people to infer intention from text because producing the text still required enough effort to be evidence of attention. An email from a colleague probably meant the colleague had at least thought about the message. A long response implied time. A polished recommendation implied somebody had written it.

Those inferences are becoming less safe.

The cost of producing the signal has fallen.

This matters because many social systems depend on costly signals without admitting it.

A job application used to cost enough effort that sending one thousand tailored applications was difficult. A thoughtful outreach email used to imply some preparation. A detailed school essay used to contain at least partial evidence that the student had engaged with the subject. A long customer complaint suggested someone had taken time to describe the problem. A letter of recommendation implied that another person had put words behind a candidate.

Cheap generation weakens the link between visible polish and underlying commitment.

The rational response is not nostalgia for worse writing.

It is to move the signal.

If a polished letter no longer proves attention, institutions will seek other evidence.

A hiring process may place more weight on work performed in a shared environment, references that carry accountable reputational cost, trial projects, verified portfolios, conversation, or records of previous collaboration.

A school may care less about the mere existence of a fluent take-home essay and more about oral defense, process notes, drafts, in-class work, or the student's ability to use the ideas elsewhere.

A customer may value access to a named professional who stands behind an AI-assisted answer rather than insisting that every sentence be composed without assistance.

A fan may pay for a live performance even when recorded or generated music is abundant.

The human signal migrates toward places where presence is harder to fake cheaply.

That movement can look like a return to the physical world, but physicality is only one mechanism.

A live video call is digital and still contains evidence of synchronous presence. A cryptographically signed statement can be entirely digital and still establish who accepted responsibility. A long-term reputation can exist online. A collaborative history in a version-control system can show years of accountable work even if many individual lines were generated with tools.

The scarce property is not meatspace.

It is credible linkage between an output and a person who was actually involved in the relevant way.

That final phrase matters because “human involved” is too weak.

A company can put a human in a loop so perfunctory that the phrase becomes a legal decoration. A reviewer can click approve without reading. A creator can type a prompt and claim authorship over thousands of outputs while exercising almost no selection. A professional can attach a name to a generated document without understanding it.

Presence has degrees.

So does responsibility.

So does authorship.

The interesting future market will not be divided cleanly between AI-made and human-made. Most valuable work will probably sit somewhere in between.

A novelist may use a model to test a chronology but write the sentences herself.

A programmer may generate routine code but make the architecture decisions.

A physician may use an AI system to summarize a chart and still perform the examination, interpret the context, and sign the plan.

A musician may use synthetic instruments and still compose, perform, and select the work.

A designer may generate hundreds of variations and spend days choosing, combining, revising, and rejecting them.

Calling all of these simply AI-generated destroys useful information.

Calling them all human-made can be equally misleading.

The signal needs to describe the thing people actually care about.

Did the person choose the objective?

Did the person make the consequential decisions?

Did the person accept responsibility?

Did the person perform the difficult part?

Did the person witness the event?

Did the person spend meaningful time?

Did the person have something at stake?

Different markets will care about different answers.

This is where provenance becomes economically interesting.

Provenance sounds bureaucratic because it often is. Museums use it to establish the history of objects. Supply chains use records to trace where goods came from. Food labels identify protected origins. Software packages carry signatures and dependency information. Scientific papers list authors, methods, affiliations, and conflicts because readers care about more than the abstract text.

Artificial abundance expands the number of ordinary objects for which provenance may matter.

A photograph can become evidence only if its origin can be trusted.

A recommendation can become more valuable if the recipient knows which human accepted it.

A piece of journalism can carry value because reporters were physically present, called sources, reviewed documents, and risk reputational damage if the account is false.

A piece of art may carry value because the buyer cares that a particular person made particular choices rather than merely owning an attractive image.

The object and the trail around the object begin to separate.

The trail can become the scarce part.

This invites fraud.

Whenever origin commands a premium, somebody will sell counterfeit origin.

The luxury market has fake labels. The art market has forged signatures. Academic publishing has fraudulent credentials. Organic food can be mislabeled. Social media has fake followers and fake testimonials.

A human premium will produce fake humanity.

That may take banal forms.

“Written by our team” might mean a model wrote almost everything and an employee changed two words.

“Handcrafted” may become a fuzzy marketing category applied to processes dominated by automation.

A creator may emphasize a dramatic manual step that contributes little to the final object because the manual step sells better than the actual workflow.

A service may insert deliberate typos, pauses, or stylistic roughness to simulate human production.

Once imperfection becomes evidence of humanity, imperfection becomes generatable too.

This is why style cannot serve as reliable proof of origin for long.

The model can learn the style.

It can add hesitation.

It can vary sentence length.

It can misspell a word.

It can imitate roughness as easily as polish.

The human signal has to move from aesthetics toward evidence.

Even then, caution is necessary.

A second body of experimental work on AI and human expression shows that labeling effects are not uniform. In experiments involving jokes and other expressive work, people sometimes associated successful output with human creators, but explicit framing did not simply produce a reliable penalty for anything labeled AI in every condition.

That should make us suspicious of an easy story in which consumers permanently reject machine-made culture once they know what it is.

People adapt.

Genres adapt.

Norms adapt.

The novelty of AI itself will fade.

A teenager who grows up making music with generative tools may not experience the boundary the same way a person does who learned recording before those tools existed. A future reader may find the question “Was AI used?” about as informative as “Was software used?” The answer will be yes so often that the useful question shifts to how.

This is already how we treat many older technologies.

A photographer can use autofocus without surrendering authorship.

An architect can use computer-aided design.

A writer can use spellcheck.

A filmmaker can use digital effects.

A mathematician can use a calculator.

The presence of a tool does not settle the human contribution.

What changes with generative systems is the range of contributions the tool can make. It can propose content, not merely execute a narrow operation. That makes the boundary worth examining, but it does not make a binary label sufficient.

The most durable human premium may therefore attach less to purity and more to commitment.

Purity asks whether the work was untouched by AI.

Commitment asks whether a person stood behind the relevant choices.

Purity will become difficult to prove and often economically pointless. Commitment is already how consequential institutions work.

A judge may rely on clerks and databases and still issue the ruling in her name.

A chief executive may rely on analysts and advisers and still approve the acquisition.

A surgeon may rely on imaging software, instruments, nurses, and other physicians and still carry specific professional responsibility for decisions made during the operation.

A scientist may use statistical software while remaining accountable for the claims in the paper.

Tools distribute production.

Institutions concentrate responsibility.

That pattern may become a template for abundant intelligence.

The valuable human signal is not “I personally performed every operation that produced this artifact.”

It is “I was present at the points where presence mattered, and my name means something if this is wrong.”

That is a stronger signal because it is costly in a way generation is not.

Reputation is finite.

Legal liability is finite.

Professional standing is finite.

Trust accumulated over years is finite.

A person cannot credibly stand behind infinite outputs if every output can damage the same name.

This creates a natural throttle.

A model can generate one million recommendations.

A respected professional cannot thoughtfully sign one million recommendations without making the signature meaningless.

The signature becomes scarce because accountability does not scale at the same rate as production.

This is the same structure we saw in the previous chapters, appearing through another door.

When generation becomes abundant, a finite human commitment can become the valuable layer wrapped around generated output.

That layer can be expensive.

A human-reviewed service may cost more than an automated one.

A live instructor may cost more than an adaptive tutor.

A handmade object may cost more than a functionally superior mass-produced one.

A reporter on location may cost more than a system summarizing online material.

A professional willing to sign the work may cost more than the system that drafted it.

The premium will sometimes be rational.

Sometimes it will be sentimental.

Sometimes it will be status consumption.

Sometimes it will be regulatory requirement.

Sometimes it will be a fraud.

That variation does not make the premium unreal.

Markets routinely price attributes that are partly symbolic. A wedding ring is not valued only by its material properties. A ticket stub can become worth more after the event. An original painting can be worth vastly more than a visually perfect reproduction. A meal cooked by someone you love can matter more than a technically better meal delivered by a restaurant.

Economic value is not embarrassed by meaning.

Artificial abundance will expose how much value was always hiding there.

This could be good for human work.

If machine production makes competent output cheap, human beings may be relieved from having to prove their worth by producing ordinary units faster. Work may migrate toward relationship, accountability, live presence, interpretation, care, taste, craft, and experiences where people actually want another person involved.

That is the hopeful version.

There is a less attractive version.

Human involvement becomes a luxury good.

The wealthy receive a named doctor while everyone else receives automated triage.

The wealthy receive a live teacher while everyone else receives software.

The wealthy receive a human advocate while everyone else navigates automated bureaucracy.

The wealthy receive handmade objects, staffed service, live culture, and private attention while cheaper machine-mediated versions become the default elsewhere.

The human premium can become another way scarcity maps onto class.

That possibility should prevent us from celebrating the signal too quickly.

If a human interaction remains valuable because systems are unsafe or institutions are unaccountable, making the human layer expensive is not a cultural renaissance. It is rationing.

The moral question depends on why the person is needed.

If the person is needed for warmth, craft, relationship, or a form of presence people freely value, a premium can make sense.

If the person is needed to make sure the automated system does not ruin your life, access to that person begins to look more like infrastructure.

The human signal therefore has two futures.

One is elective: a premium for presence in a world where machines can handle the functional core.

The other is protective: a requirement that someone accountable remain reachable when consequences exceed what the system should bear alone.

Those futures will overlap.

They will also be confused for one another by companies eager to charge for either.

The only useful defense is specificity.

Do not ask whether something is human.

Ask what human contribution you are paying for.

The answer may be judgment.

It may be responsibility.

It may be relationship.

It may be provenance.

It may be performance.

It may be sacrifice.

It may be nothing worth paying for at all.

Artificial intelligence makes fluent surfaces easy. It will also make simulated humanity easy.

The scarce thing will be credible evidence that a real person was present where presence mattered.

And once that evidence becomes valuable, we will start designing systems that deliberately slow down the places where a person is supposed to matter.
