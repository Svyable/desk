# Chapters 1–3 opening depth controls

Purpose: support the next coherent opening-band manuscript pass without widening the factual surface casually. These controls deepen the mechanism that connects cheap cognitive generation to repricing elsewhere in the system. They supplement the research brief; they do not replace its source trail or falsification notes.

## Chapter 1 — The Price of a Thought

### Revision target

Make the economic object more precise. The chapter should distinguish at least five prices that can move differently: provider inference cost, user-facing price, user time, verification cost, and expected error cost. The abundance claim concerns the effective price of attempting another cognitive task, not a claim that every component of the transaction approaches zero.

A useful mechanism is the **attempt threshold**. Many cognitive tasks were historically too small, uncertain, or awkward to justify procurement: a five-minute explanation, a disposable translation, one more scenario, a personalized practice problem, a rough first pass over a small dataset. When the cost of attempting such work falls far enough, demand can appear where there was no previous invoice. That is an extensive-margin effect, not merely a cheaper substitute for an existing professional engagement.

Keep transaction costs visible. Human expertise can be economical at the hourly level and still be uneconomical for tiny transactions because finding, scheduling, briefing, contracting, and coordinating with a person impose minimum engagement costs. Cheap generation changes the minimum viable transaction size.

Do not equate cheap attempts with cheap completed decisions. A generated answer may cost little while verification, integration, legal responsibility, or correction remains expensive. In consequential domains, the effective price of a trustworthy outcome can therefore fall much more slowly than the price of generation.

### Counterpressure

The attempt threshold can move back upward. Usage limits, energy or compute constraints, latency, weak reliability, security requirements, or expensive review can cause users to ration tasks again. If verification dominates the transaction, lower inference cost may have little effect on the final economics.

### Evidence boundary

The Stanford AI Index fixed-performance inference-cost series can establish a historical price-performance decline at a named benchmark threshold. It cannot establish that intelligence is literally free, that future declines continue at the same rate, or that benchmark-equivalent performance implies trustworthy completion of a professional task.

Consumer-surplus estimates belong in a separate conceptual box from provider revenue and expenditure. They can support the proposition that access creates user value not captured by sticker price; they should not be narrated as cash savings, GDP, or realized investor return.

## Chapter 2 — The Abundance Machine

### Revision target

Strengthen the apparent paradox: expensive industrial infrastructure can produce cheap marginal cognitive output. The chapter should make clear that downstream abundance can be manufactured by upstream scarcity when high fixed costs are spread across enormous volumes of repeatable inference.

Separate **capacity scarcity** from **marginal-output economics**. Data centers, chips, electricity, cooling, networking, land, capital, and engineering remain rival inputs. Once capacity exists, however, the incremental user-side transaction can be cheap enough to support uses that would never justify dedicated human coordination.

The important mechanism is not only scale but utilization. High fixed-cost systems have strong incentives to keep expensive capacity productive. Pricing, batching, model routing, smaller models, caching, hardware improvements, and software optimization can all change the cost of serving the next request without making the physical plant disappear.

Make the distinction between Chapter 2 and Chapters 17–18 explicit in the prose logic. Chapter 2 explains how a scarce industrial base can create abundant downstream output. Chapters 17–18 ask what happens when the demand unleashed by that output runs back into power, facilities, embodied services, geography, maintenance, and other physical constraints.

### Counterpressure

Do not imply that marginal cost must monotonically approach zero. Jevons-like demand expansion, higher-capability models, longer contexts, agentic workloads, reliability techniques, redundancy, or new infrastructure constraints can raise total resource use even while price-performance improves. Cheap units and enormous aggregate expenditure can coexist.

Do not infer durable abundance from temporary subsidy. If user prices are held below sustainable economics by capital competition, the book's claim should be framed around observed user-side behavior during cheap access, with durability left conditional.

### Evidence boundary

Use infrastructure and compute evidence to establish physical dependence and changing price-performance, not to predict a fixed endpoint. Avoid translating company capex announcements directly into inference capacity or future unit cost unless the source supports that bridge.

## Chapter 3 — The Value Moves

### Revision target

Deepen the distinction between **value creation**, **value capture**, and **cross-subsidy**. These are already present in the chapter; the next pass should make their separation operational rather than merely rhetorical.

When a layer becomes cheap, ask four different questions:

1. What new activity becomes possible or more frequent?
2. Which complementary constraint becomes relatively more important?
3. Who owns or controls that constraint?
4. Does the new owner capture enough value to replace what disappeared elsewhere?

The fourth question prevents the chapter from drifting into a conservation story. A market can create more consumer value while destroying an incumbent profit pool. A complement can become more valuable without compensating the workers or institutions displaced in the original layer. A new bottleneck can accrue to a platform, regulator, distributor, physical asset owner, or trusted intermediary rather than to the original producer.

Add the idea of **incidence**. Saying that verification becomes more valuable is incomplete until the chapter asks who pays for it. The cost can fall on the producer, platform, buyer, insurer, regulator, or an unpaid reviewer. A new scarcity may therefore show up as price, delay, uncompensated labor, exclusion, or risk rather than as a clean new revenue category.

The newspaper-classified case is especially useful because it demonstrates a lost cross-subsidy. The economic loss to journalism was not simply that digital publishing became cheap. A profitable local matching function separated from the reporting bundle. Consumer matching could improve while the institution financing local reporting became weaker. That is a distributional and organizational consequence of unbundling, not evidence that social value fell one-for-one with newspaper revenue.

The recorded-music case should remain asymmetric. Mortimer, Nosko, and Sorensen support a complement effect for smaller artists in the studied period, not a universal transfer from recordings to live performance. The difference by artist size is useful because it shows that a complementary scarcity can matter most to producers for whom discovery was previously a binding constraint.

### New mechanism: bottleneck ownership

A scarcity becomes strategically valuable only if somebody can control access to it or credibly supply it. Attention can be scarce without any one publisher capturing it. Trust can be scarce while consumers rely on informal networks rather than paying a certifier. Physical presence can be scarce while competition among providers keeps margins low.

So the manuscript should distinguish **scarcity** from **appropriable scarcity**. The former shapes behavior; the latter can support pricing power. This keeps the argument from assuming that every scarce complement becomes a profitable business.

A second distinction is between a bottleneck and a moat. A bottleneck is the current constraint on throughput. A moat is an advantage that competitors cannot readily erode. Abundance can expose a bottleneck that attracts so much investment that it stops being scarce. The strategic question is therefore not only “what is scarce now?” but “what prevents supply, substitution, or institutional redesign from attacking that scarcity?”

### Counterpressure

Some abundance transitions primarily destroy rents. The next constraint may be easy to expand, competitively supplied, or impossible for any one actor to monetize. Consumer surplus can rise while both old and new producer margins remain thin.

Complementary scarcity can also be temporary. Verification tools improve; distribution fragments; standards reduce switching costs; new infrastructure arrives; regulation changes; users learn. Do not present today's bottleneck as a permanent refuge from commoditization.

### Evidence boundary

Keep the FCC newspaper figures bounded to the reported period and causes. The recession, broader advertising decline, Craigslist, and specialized online marketplaces all belong in the causal picture; do not assign the full classified decline to a single platform.

Keep the music result bounded to the paper's empirical setting and heterogeneous effect. Do not imply that total live revenue, total artist income, or employment mechanically replaced lost recorded sales.

## Cross-chapter continuity

The opening sequence should read as one causal chain:

- Chapter 1: the effective cost of attempting another cognitive task falls far enough to change behavior.
- Chapter 2: that downstream cheapness is produced by a physically scarce, capital-intensive industrial system rather than by the disappearance of material constraints.
- Chapter 3: once generation is less binding, relative advantage migrates toward complements, but the gains, costs, and bargaining power do not move together or accrue to the same people.

The handoff to Chapter 4 should then be narrow: among the complements exposed by cheap generation, discrimination among plausible alternatives becomes more important. Chapter 4 can develop taste and apprenticeship without Chapter 3 trying to become a full theory of judgment.

## Prose guardrails for the next pass

- Preserve the existing calm, example-first voice. Do not add a named framework to the manuscript merely because these notes use analytic labels.
- Prefer one deeper consequence over a rapid list of new examples.
- Do not add invented scenes, quotations, or statistics.
- Avoid repeated “not X but Y” pivots and aphoristic paragraph endings.
- Keep uncertainty alive: abundance can expose a complement without making that complement durable, profitable, human-controlled, or socially beneficial.
- Preserve the central falsification condition: if generation remains materially rationed by price, reliability, or verification in most meaningful domains, narrow the abundance claim rather than defending it rhetorically.
