# Chapter 14 fact-check: the human signal

This note tightens the evidence boundaries for the Chapter 14 revision pass. It is a source-control document, not manuscript prose.

## 1. Grassini & Koivisto (2024): art source attribution and evaluation

Primary source: Simone Grassini and Mika Koivisto, “Understanding how personality traits, experiences, and attitudes shape negative bias toward AI-generated artworks,” *Scientific Reports* 14, 4113 (published February 19, 2024), https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54294-4

The chapter’s core use is supportable with important limits. In a Prolific sample of 201 participants, using 40 images (20 Midjourney v4 images and 20 human-created images across five selected styles), participants did not reliably discriminate the true source of the images. In this stimulus set, the AI-generated images were actually liked more and evoked more positive emotion than the selected human works. Separately, images participants *believed* were more likely AI-generated were liked less and evoked less positive emotion. That supports the chapter’s narrower claim that believed origin can affect evaluation even when true origin is not reliably detected.

### Keep explicit

- This was a particular stimulus set, not evidence that AI art is universally preferred or universally indistinguishable from human art.
- The sample was relatively highly educated and recruited online through Prolific; the authors identify generalizability limits.
- The negative-bias result concerns subjective source belief. It should not be rewritten as a simple experimental finding that an “AI” label always causes a penalty.
- The study does not establish a durable market-wide “human premium,” a willingness-to-pay premium, or a universal preference for human origin. Those remain analysis/hypotheses in the book.

### Falsification boundary

Evidence that source labels cease to affect evaluation across mature AI-mediated creative markets, or that provenance has no independent value once quality is held constant, would weaken the chapter’s broader prediction. This study alone cannot settle either question.

## 2. Shank et al. (2021): human expression and jokes

Primary source: Daniel B. Shank et al., “Perceptions of AI engaging in human expression,” *Scientific Reports* 11 (2021), https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-00426-z

This is useful counterevidence against a universal labeling penalty. In Experiment 1, when joke sources were not disclosed, participants tended to attribute funnier jokes to humans and poorer jokes to AI; actual human jokes were also funnier in that stimulus set. But Experiment 2 explicitly framed the same human-created jokes as either human- or AI-created and found no statistically reliable difference in funniness ratings between those frames (reported p = 0.083, with the authors also reporting Bayesian evidence for essentially equivalent ratings).

### Revision implication

Chapter 14 is right to resist the easy story that consumers simply discount anything labeled AI. Keep the distinction sharp:

- inferred source can covary with perceived quality;
- explicit source framing does not impose a reliable penalty in every expressive domain or experimental condition;
- “human origin” therefore cannot be treated as a single universal preference with a stable price.

The chapter’s stronger argument should remain about *which human contribution is being valued*—accountability, relationship, provenance, performance, sacrifice, or consequential judgment—not about categorical human superiority.

## 3. Later evidence that strengthens the caution rather than the premium

A 2024 *Scientific Reports* study on poetry found that non-expert participants often misidentified AI-generated poems as human-authored and rated the AI poems more favorably on several dimensions. This is useful corroboration for the narrow point that surface quality and source detection can come apart; it is not necessary to make Chapter 14’s central case and should not be added merely to accumulate examples.

Source: Brian Porter and Edouard Machery, “AI-generated poetry is indistinguishable from human-written poetry and is rated more favorably,” *Scientific Reports* 14 (2024), https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-76900-1

A 2023 *Scientific Reports* experiment on presumed deepfake faces found that source belief affected responses to smiling faces but not uniformly across emotional expressions. Again, the useful lesson is heterogeneity: origin beliefs can matter, but effects depend on task and context.

Source: “Deepfake smiles matter less—the psychological and neural impact of presumed AI-generated faces,” *Scientific Reports* 13 (2023), https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-42802-x

## 4. Prose controls for the revision

1. Preserve the opening distinction between human origin and mystical “authenticity”; the evidence supports precision, not romanticism.
2. When discussing the 2024 art study, state that the true AI images in that selected set were liked more, while subjective belief that an image was AI-generated predicted lower evaluation. This is more interesting and more accurate than implying a simple human-made preference.
3. Keep the jokes study as genuine counterevidence. Do not bury the null explicit-framing result.
4. Do not infer willingness to pay from liking ratings. The economic “premium” remains an argument that requires provenance, responsibility, relationship, or process to carry independent value.
5. Preserve the chapter’s class objection: if human involvement is needed for safety, appeal, or accountable recourse, pricing it as a luxury is rationing rather than evidence of cultural renaissance.
6. Prefer “credible linkage to accountable human involvement” over binary “human-made” language. The research does not justify treating tool purity as the scarce attribute.

## Revision target

The manuscript pass should make the art evidence less slogan-like and more paradoxical: participants could not reliably identify true source in this stimulus set, the AI images themselves scored well, and yet subjective belief about AI origin tracked lower evaluation. That pattern supports the chapter’s move from aesthetics toward provenance without pretending provenance always commands a premium.
