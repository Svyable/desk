# Chapters 9–12 — research controls

This note prepares the next four chapters as one coherent argument: cheap generation weakens the economic value of average production, but the resulting gains are uneven; expertise changes shape rather than simply disappearing; responsibility remains attached to accountable people and institutions; and judgment becomes more valuable precisely because model capability is jagged.

## Chapter 9 — The End of Average

The chapter should not claim that AI abolishes average performance or makes every worker exceptional. A better claim is that cheap adaptive production makes it less necessary to ration many cognitive services around one standardized middle case.

Useful evidence comes from the randomized BCG knowledge-work experiment published in *Organization Science*. Across 758 consultants, AI assistance improved speed, completion, and quality on tasks that lay within the model's capability frontier, while performance worsened on a task outside that frontier. The important mechanism for this chapter is heterogeneity: the same general-purpose system can make the average less economically binding while leaving a jagged distribution of task-level reliability.

The cross-firm Microsoft/NBER experiment provides a second boundary. In a six-month randomized field experiment spanning 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers, treated users who actually adopted the tool spent about two fewer hours per week on email, but researchers did not detect broad changes in the quantity or composition of work from individual-level AI access. Cheap customization therefore does not automatically reorganize institutions. Coordination costs and work design can preserve old averages after production technology changes.

Falsification / counterevidence: if adaptive AI remains too unreliable to support meaningful tailoring, or if coordination and compliance force most organizations back toward standardized outputs, then the "end of average" should be narrowed to specific digital surfaces rather than treated as a general organizational transition.

## Chapter 10 — Commodity Expertise

The phrase should describe repricing, not extinction. Three field experiments with 4,867 software developers at Microsoft, Accenture, and another Fortune 100 company found a pooled 26.08% increase in completed tasks from access to an AI coding assistant. Less experienced developers showed higher adoption and larger productivity gains. In customer support, the peer-reviewed Brynjolfsson-Li-Raymond study similarly found larger gains among less experienced and lower-skilled agents, while the most experienced workers saw small speed gains and small quality declines.

These results support a bounded argument: when a tool cheaply packages patterns previously available mainly through accumulated experience, some routine manifestations of expertise become less scarce. They do not show that expertise as a whole becomes a commodity. Domain experts still choose objectives, recognize exceptional cases, integrate tacit context, and bear consequences. The evidence also comes from particular tools and workflows rather than the whole economy.

This chapter should make the apprenticeship problem concrete without assuming one answer. If entry-level workers gain more from assistance, firms may be able to raise novice output quickly. But if the tasks through which novices formerly learned are automated away, output convergence can coexist with weaker formation of future experts. Chapter 4's Bastani evidence is relevant as a mechanism: assistance can improve performance during use while reducing later unassisted performance when guardrails are absent. Do not generalize a high-school mathematics experiment directly to professional labor.

Falsification / counterevidence: if experienced workers consistently capture larger gains as systems improve, or if AI primarily complements tacit expertise rather than compressing performance differences, the commodity-expertise claim should shift from people to particular deliverables.

## Chapter 11 — Responsibility Does Not Scale

This chapter needs a clean distinction between production and accountability. Generative systems can multiply drafts, analyses, filings, recommendations, and decisions under consideration. They do not thereby acquire professional duties, legal standing, insurance, organizational authority, or moral responsibility.

The American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512 is a useful institutional example. It treats generative AI as compatible with legal practice while leaving lawyers' existing duties of competence, confidentiality, communication, supervision, candor, and reasonable fees intact. The point is not that law is uniquely conservative. It is that a profession can accept cheaper production while refusing to let responsibility migrate to the tool.

Recent court practice makes the mechanism concrete. On September 3, 2026, the District of Columbia Court of Appeals criticized lawyers after nonexistent AI-generated authorities appeared in litigation involving a Deutsche Bank subsidiary. The durable lesson is narrower than "AI hallucinates": when an output crosses into a consequential institutional record, someone remains responsible for verification.

California's SB 574, passed by the legislature on September 1, 2026 and awaiting the governor's action at the time of this note, is current evidence of the same direction rather than settled law. It would prohibit delegating the practice of law to AI and require reasonable verification of AI-generated content. Because its legal status can change, the manuscript should either date the description precisely or rely primarily on the already-issued ABA opinion and decided cases.

Falsification / counterevidence: responsibility can be redistributed by contract, statute, insurance, product-liability doctrine, and organizational design. The chapter should not claim that today's allocation is permanent. Its stronger point is that lowering generation cost does not itself lower the social cost of being wrong.

## Chapter 12 — The Judgment Bottleneck

Judgment should be defined as choosing what to trust, what matters, when a model is outside its competence, and what consequence is acceptable—not as a mystical human residue.

The BCG experiment is again useful because it demonstrates a jagged frontier rather than a smooth ranking of human and machine capability. AI users did better on many tasks inside the frontier but were more likely to produce incorrect answers on a task outside it. That creates a verification problem: a tool can be valuable often enough that blanket distrust is irrational while remaining unreliable enough that blanket trust is dangerous.

The 2026 Stanford AI Index's responsible-AI chapter supplies broader evaluation context. It reports wide hallucination-rate variation across 26 leading models on a new accuracy benchmark and emphasizes that benchmark behavior depends strongly on framing. Use this only to establish continuing calibration and reliability problems; do not translate benchmark hallucination percentages into ordinary-user error rates.

The cross-firm work-pattern experiment adds an organizational boundary: individual access changed email time more readily than coordinated work. Judgment bottlenecks can therefore live above the individual. An organization may generate more options without becoming faster at approvals, risk acceptance, prioritization, or collective commitment.

Falsification / counterevidence: selection and verification themselves are increasingly automatable. The chapter should not assume every judgment task remains human. The scarcity claim is strongest at the moving boundary where objectives, acceptable error, exception handling, and responsibility have not yet been reduced to reliable rules.

## Revision boundaries for the four-chapter run

- Do not equate productivity gains with job loss.
- Do not treat a pooled field-experiment estimate as universal across occupations.
- Do not use current hallucination benchmarks as ordinary-use error rates.
- Do not imply that legal ethics proves all professions will retain the same human accountability structure.
- Keep "commodity expertise" attached to outputs and repeatable patterns unless evidence supports a stronger labor-market claim.
- Preserve the possibility that better AI automates parts of judgment and verification; the book's thesis is value migration, not permanent human exceptionalism.
