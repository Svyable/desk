# Artificial Abundance Chapters 16–18 — difficulty, reality, and physical-premium revision controls

Date: 2026-09-07

This note prepares one coherent revision pass across Chapters 16–18. The sequence should move from difficulty as a signal, to physical constraints as allocation problems, to the price of embodied capacity. The pass should not romanticize hardship or imply that digital abundance mechanically makes every physical good more expensive.

## Chapter 16 — The Status of Difficulty

### Revision objective

Keep the chapter’s existing distinction among training difficulty, protective difficulty, and status difficulty, but make the falsification test sharper. Difficulty deserves preservation only when the process itself changes capability, reduces consequential risk, or supplies information that cannot be reproduced more cheaply. Difficulty that survives only because incumbents benefit from the barrier is rent, not evidence.

### Additions worth making

1. Separate **costly signal** from **useful formation**. A hard exercise can both signal persistence and build skill. If a machine makes the signal cheap while the formation still matters, institutions should test capability directly rather than preserve hardship for nostalgia.
2. Ask who can afford the signal. Expensive credentials, unpaid apprenticeships, travel, endurance rituals, and handmade production can signal commitment partly because access to time and money is unequal. Artificial abundance can expose that status difficulty was sometimes measuring resources as much as merit.
3. Preserve the countercase that easier tools can broaden participation without erasing mastery. Removing mechanical difficulty may let more people reach the point where taste, judgment, or deeper technique becomes visible.
4. Give status difficulty an expiry condition: when observers learn that the hard process no longer predicts the quality or trait they care about, the premium should weaken.

### Counterevidence to preserve

- Deliberate practice and difficult training can remain valuable when the learner, rather than the artifact, is the product.
- Constraints can improve attention or craft without proving moral superiority.
- Easy tools can increase the population capable of producing excellent work.
- A difficult path can be chosen for meaning, play, identity, or community even when it has no productivity advantage.

## Chapter 17 — Reality Gets Expensive

### Revision objective

Tighten the energy example around **global share versus local constraint** and **projection versus observation**. The chapter already handles this boundary well; the revision should make it impossible to read the 2030 figure as a forecast with false precision.

### Primary evidence checked

**International Energy Agency, _Energy and AI_ (published 10 April 2025).** The IEA estimates data centres consumed about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity consumption. Its Base Case projects around 945 TWh in 2030, just under 3% of global electricity consumption. The report explicitly uses sensitivity cases because hardware/software efficiency, AI uptake, and energy bottlenecks are uncertain. It also notes that a data centre can sometimes become operational in roughly two to three years while wider energy infrastructure often requires longer planning and construction lead times.

The IEA also reports that nearly half of US data-centre capacity is concentrated in five regional clusters. This supports the chapter’s argument that a modest global share can create a large local planning problem; it does not establish that every proposed data centre creates a grid shortage.

### Additions worth making

1. Label 415 TWh as a **2024 estimate** and 945 TWh as the IEA **2030 Base Case**, not as equivalent observations.
2. Treat locality as the mechanism. Electricity is constrained by connection point, network topology, equipment, timing, and regional supply; global totals can conceal local scarcity.
3. Preserve substitution. Better efficiency, flexible siting, workload shifting, new generation, storage, transmission, and slower deployment can all weaken a local scarcity story.
4. Distinguish **demand revelation** from **demand creation**. Better matching can expose queues that already existed; it can also stimulate new demand. The two mechanisms have different policy implications.
5. Keep allocation visible. Once information and matching costs fall, scarce physical capacity still has to be allocated by price, queue, rule, lottery, relationship, priority, or expansion.

### Falsification pressure

The chapter’s physical-bottleneck argument weakens where digital substitution materially reduces demand for the embodied service, where capacity can expand on the same clock as demand, or where flexible loads can move cheaply across time and place. A queue is evidence of scarcity only after checking whether the queue is caused by real capacity limits rather than bad process design.

## Chapter 18 — The Physical Premium

### Revision objective

Make the chapter’s “physical premium” explicitly relative rather than inflationary. The claim is about where value and cost remain after cognitive and informational inputs become cheaper. It is not a prediction that atoms must rise in nominal price.

### Fresh primary evidence checked

**International Energy Agency, _Key Questions on Energy and AI_ (2026).** The IEA reports that energy use per AI task has fallen by at least an order of magnitude annually in recent years while global data-centre electricity demand still grew 17% in 2025; electricity use at AI-focused data centres grew faster. The report attributes the aggregate outcome to the interaction of efficiency, rapid uptake, and more energy-intensive uses such as video, reasoning, and agentic workloads.

This is useful as a rebound-style counterexample, but the chapter should not turn it into a universal Jevons-law claim. Falling unit energy and rising total demand can coexist; they do not prove that efficiency always increases total consumption.

The IEA’s 2026 materials also identify tightening near-term bottlenecks in grid connections, transformers, gas turbines, advanced chips, and planning/approval systems. Use this only to support the narrower point that embodied expansion has supply chains and lead times that software demand does not automatically erase.

### Additions worth making

1. Define the premium as a **share of residual value/cost**. If AI makes design, scheduling, quoting, or documentation cheaper, land, installation, liability, materials, skilled presence, and capacity can account for more of the remaining bill even when the total bill falls.
2. Separate utilization gains from capacity expansion. Better scheduling can create real output from idle physical assets, but utilization approaches a ceiling and can reduce resilience if every buffer is treated as waste.
3. Treat slack as conditional option value. Spare rooms, inventory, grid headroom, appointment capacity, and staffing margin are expensive until a disruption or surge makes them useful. Not all slack deserves preservation; the relevant question is what failure or demand spike it buys the system the ability to absorb.
4. Preserve the strongest countercase: AI can lower physical costs through design optimization, predictive maintenance, logistics, energy management, robotics, substitution, and faster permitting/engineering work. A physical premium is therefore not an anti-productivity thesis.
5. Distinguish a **real bottleneck** from a **market-design bottleneck**. A full restaurant at 7 p.m. is physically constrained; an appointment queue created by poor scheduling may be largely administrative. Cheap intelligence should attack the second before the book calls it scarcity.

### Falsification pressure

The physical-premium thesis weakens in domains where cognitive cost was the dominant cost, digital substitution is close, physical capacity is elastic, or automation expands embodied supply as quickly as it expands demand. The chapter should welcome those cases because they clarify where abundance actually reaches the physical layer.

## Coherent manuscript pass

Keep the three chapters distinct:

- **Chapter 16:** What does difficulty still tell us when visible output becomes easy?
- **Chapter 17:** Which constraints remain real when representations and matching become cheap?
- **Chapter 18:** When cognitive overhead falls, which embodied inputs become the residual source of cost and value?

The connective tissue is repricing, not nostalgia. Do not add a new branded framework. Prefer concrete tests: what trait does the difficulty predict, where is the physical constraint located, how quickly can capacity expand, what does the queue actually measure, and which portion of the remaining price is truly embodied.

## Scope discipline

This research pass does not justify claims that:

- difficult work is inherently more valuable or virtuous;
- AI makes physical goods universally more expensive;
- data centres are currently a dominant share of global electricity demand;
- the IEA Base Case is a certainty rather than a scenario;
- efficiency gains necessarily increase total resource consumption;
- every queue is evidence of physical scarcity;
- physical presence is always preferable to digital substitution.

The stronger argument is conditional: as competent cognitive production gets cheaper, some signals lose information, some physical constraints become more visible, and some embodied inputs account for a larger share of residual value. Whether that happens in a particular market depends on substitution, capacity elasticity, allocation rules, and the speed at which physical supply can respond.
