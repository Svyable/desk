# Chapter 15 friction depth controls

This note prepares a focused depth pass for `ch15-friction-as-a-feature.md`, currently the shortest chapter in the manuscript by repository byte size. It supplements, rather than replaces, the landed Chapters 13–16 research controls.

## Revision target

The chapter already distinguishes wasteful friction from protective friction and already uses reversibility as an important discriminator. The next pass should deepen the economic mechanism rather than add another catalogue of confirmation dialogs.

The missing middle is **where the cost of a pause should sit**. A checkpoint is not free merely because it prevents some errors. It consumes attention, delays legitimate action, and can move risk from the institution onto the person least able to bear delay. The useful question is therefore not whether friction catches mistakes in the abstract, but whether the expected reduction in consequential loss exceeds the cost imposed by the checkpoint, and whether the party bearing that cost is also the party creating or controlling the risk.

That gives the chapter a stronger treatment of externalities. A nearly irreversible action whose downside falls mostly on other people can rationally deserve more resistance than an equally expensive but privately borne and easily reversed mistake. Conversely, a reversible private action should not inherit a heavy approval chain merely because the institution is uncomfortable with variance.

## Queueing and selective friction

Selective review creates a queue. The chapter should make the operational consequence explicit: once only high-risk cases are routed to people, the human queue becomes enriched for ambiguity, novelty, and disagreement. Average handling time can rise even while total case volume falls. Staffing the review function from the old average case time can therefore make the supposedly safer process brittle precisely when it encounters the cases for which human judgment was retained.

This should connect cleanly to Chapter 12 without repeating it. Chapter 12 is about judgment becoming the bottleneck after routine work is automated. Chapter 15 should use that result to ask what a well-designed bottleneck looks like: enough capacity to investigate rather than rubber-stamp, authority to stop the process, and a path for cases that do not fit the risk classifier.

A useful design principle is that **friction needs capacity behind it**. A second signature with no time to inspect is ceremony. An escalation channel nobody staffs is decoration. A pause that expires automatically unless a reviewer objects can quietly convert review into approval-by-timeout. The chapter can make these institutional failure modes concrete without claiming that every review system behaves this way.

## Reversibility is more than an undo button

Deepen reversibility into three separate questions:

1. Can the action technically be undone?
2. Can the affected parties actually be restored to their prior position?
3. Will the error be detected soon enough for reversal to matter?

A deleted draft restored from history is highly reversible. A mistaken public accusation may be technically retractable while remaining socially difficult to reverse. A bad automated decision may have a formal appeal path but still be practically sticky if the affected person cannot discover why the decision happened or cannot reach someone with authority to change it.

This distinction prevents the chapter from treating rollback as a universal substitute for ex ante review. Cheap reversal is valuable only when detection, restoration, and recourse are real.

## Friction can allocate power

The existing chapter correctly notes that adaptive friction can burden some people more than others. The revision should connect that observation to institutional incentives.

When the organization chooses the risk threshold but the customer bears the delay, the organization can rationally choose more friction than it would if it paid the full cost. When the organization bears fraud or liability losses but a user bears the inconvenience of false positives, the threshold embeds a distributional choice. That does not make the control illegitimate; it means the control cannot be evaluated only by aggregate error reduction.

Preserve the chapter's distinction between chosen and imposed delay. A premium product that offers a deliberate cooling-off period is different from a public-benefit process that makes an applicant wait because administrative capacity is scarce. Both contain time. Only one is plausibly a feature for the person experiencing it.

## Commitment devices without romanticizing inconvenience

The chapter can also distinguish safety friction from **commitment friction**. Some pauses are valuable because they separate intention from execution long enough for a person to reconsider an impulse, compare alternatives, or make a decision legible to a future self. This is different from an institution inserting delay for its own protection.

Keep this bounded. A commitment device is useful when the person has meaningfully chosen the constraint or when the constraint protects a clearly defined interest. Do not turn every obstacle into paternalism with a friendly label.

## Continuity into Chapter 16

The ending should preserve the handoff already present: once protective difficulty is separated from waste, difficulty can be examined as a signal and a chosen practice. Chapter 15 should not pre-argue Chapter 16's status thesis. Its job is to establish that some deliberately retained difficulty has a functional reason even in a world where the underlying production step could be automated away.

The clean bridge is: **first ask whether difficulty changes risk, learning, commitment, or reversibility; only then ask what difficulty communicates socially.**

## Counterevidence and falsification checks

Preserve these constraints in the prose pass:

- Automation can make controls cheaper as well as actions cheaper; do not imply every increase in machine capability creates a proportional human-review burden.
- Human review can be worse than automated review when people are overloaded, inconsistent, poorly informed, or structurally unable to disagree.
- A high false-positive rate can make a safety control net harmful even when the errors it targets are serious.
- Reversibility can justify less ex ante friction, but only when detection and restoration are credible.
- Friction that shifts cost onto less powerful parties should be treated as a distributional choice, not automatically as evidence of prudence.
- The chapter should not imply that slowness itself produces care. Capacity, authority, information, and consequence determine whether the pause does useful work.

No new numerical claims are required for this pass. The strongest revision can be made by developing mechanisms already supported by the book's landed research trail and by keeping the countercases inside the argument rather than adding decorative caveats at the end.
