# Chapters 17–20 — research controls

This run prepares the next coherent argument: digital abundance does not abolish physical constraint; it can increase the premium on scarce physical experiences and infrastructure, while assistance creates a separate question about meaning and agency. The chapters should move from material bottlenecks into human choice without treating either scarcity or human exceptionalism as permanent laws.

## Chapter 17 — Reality Gets Expensive

The chapter should distinguish cheap digital representation from physical access. A generated image of a coastline can be nearly free while a safe, uncrowded visit to that coastline remains constrained by land, transport, time, weather, maintenance, regulation, and ecological carrying capacity. Cheap simulation can increase demand for scarce originals rather than replace them.

Use AI infrastructure itself as counterevidence to any claim that the digital world has escaped matter. The IEA estimated data centres consumed about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity consumption, and projected roughly 945 TWh by 2030 in its 2025 base case. The important point is not that AI uniquely dominates world electricity use—it does not—but that apparently weightless cognition still arrives through servers, grids, cooling, buildings, chips, finance, and permits.

The IEA also emphasizes geographic concentration. Large AI-focused facilities can create severe local grid constraints even when data centres remain a modest share of global electricity. Its 2025 analysis says nearly half of U.S. data-centre capacity is concentrated in five regional clusters. This is useful for the book's scarcity thesis: global abundance can coexist with acute local scarcity.

A second useful physical boundary is timing. The IEA notes that a data centre can sometimes become operational in two to three years while broader energy infrastructure often requires longer planning, investment, equipment procurement and construction cycles. Use this to support the manuscript's "two clocks" distinction between rapidly scaling digital demand and slower physical capacity. Do not convert this into a universal lead-time claim for every data centre or grid project.

Falsification / counterevidence: virtual goods can substitute for some physical travel, meetings, prototypes, entertainment, and training. The chapter should not assume every digital substitute increases demand for the physical original. The stronger claim is that physical constraint becomes more visible where substitution is incomplete.

## Chapter 18 — The Physical Premium

Treat the physical premium as conditional, not universal. Physical objects and services carry inventory, transport, maintenance, safety, property, labor, and time costs that generated digital artifacts can avoid. When digital substitutes become abundant, those costs can make physical presence relatively more expensive.

But expensive is not synonymous with valuable. A broken appliance is physical. So is a traffic jam. Scarcity can produce rents, exclusion, congestion, and bad service. The chapter should separate the premium created by embodied presence from the burden created by material constraint.

AI infrastructure offers a useful inversion. Inference can become cheaper per task while aggregate physical demand rises because usage expands and applications become more intensive. The IEA's updated 2026 assessment says energy use per AI task has fallen rapidly, yet data-centre electricity consumption rose 17% in 2025 and electricity use at AI-focused data centres rose faster. Efficiency therefore does not guarantee lower aggregate resource demand.

The same 2026 IEA report supplies a second concrete mechanism: power density. It reports that AI-server power density rose elevenfold between 2020 and 2025 and could rise sharply again by 2027; the executive summary compares the projected peak demand of one advanced rack with the electricity demand of dozens of households. Use the comparison to make facility-level constraints legible, not to imply every rack has the same load. The report also points to pressure on transformers, power electronics, chips, grid connections and approvals.

This is a rebound mechanism, not proof of a universal Jevons paradox. Demand depends on prices, adoption, application mix, substitution, regulation, and efficiency gains.

Falsification / counterevidence: manufacturing automation, robotics, better logistics, modular construction, and energy innovation can make physical goods and services cheaper too. The chapter should describe a relative premium where digital marginal costs fall faster, not a permanent law that matter only gets more expensive.

## Chapter 19 — The Meaning Shortage

Meaning should not be treated as a measurable commodity with a known aggregate shortage. The title names a tension: when systems can cheaply generate competent answers, plans, images, and options, people may face more difficulty deciding which goals deserve pursuit and which outputs matter.

Keep the argument behavioral and institutional rather than clinical. Do not claim AI causes loneliness, depression, purposelessness, or a mental-health epidemic without specific evidence. A person can find genuine meaning in AI-assisted work, art, learning, caregiving, play, or collaboration.

The chapter should distinguish production from valuation. A model can propose a hundred projects without deciding which obligation a person should accept. It can draft wedding vows without making the promise. It can generate a memorial speech without supplying the relationship that gives the words weight. These are conceptual examples, not evidence that generated language is necessarily hollow.

Choice-overload research provides a bounded empirical bridge from option abundance to selection difficulty. Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman's 2015 meta-analysis synthesized 99 observations involving 7,202 participants and found that larger assortments were more likely to create overload under conditions such as high choice-set complexity, difficult decisions, preference uncertainty, and effort-minimizing goals. Use this against a universal "more choice is bad" story: the burden is conditional and depends partly on whether the chooser has stable criteria.

Counterevidence matters: tools can widen participation by reducing barriers of skill, disability, language, money, or confidence. Assistance can also help people articulate criteria, experiment cheaply, and discover preferences through action. Meaning can migrate with capability rather than disappear.

Falsification / boundary: if people consistently experience assisted creation as equally or more meaningful, the chapter's claim should narrow to the need for goal selection under abundance, not a general loss of meaning. If abundant options plus cheap experimentation reliably improve commitment, the strongest version of the option-overload mechanism should be reduced further.

## Chapter 20 — Agency After Assistance

Define agency operationally: the capacity to form or adopt goals, make consequential choices, revise them in light of evidence, and remain able to understand or contest important decisions. Do not define it as doing every intermediate step unaided.

Assistance and agency are not opposites. Calculators, maps, search engines, translators, colleagues, editors, and institutions all let people act through capabilities they do not personally reproduce. AI can similarly expand agency when it makes previously inaccessible actions feasible.

The risk appears when assistance quietly becomes default-setting. If a system chooses the options a person sees, frames the trade-offs, drafts the response, predicts the likely preference, and executes with little opportunity for inspection, the user may retain formal approval while losing practical authorship of the decision.

The older automation-bias literature gives a mechanism without pretending older systems are identical to generative AI. Parasuraman and Manzey's 2010 review found automation complacency under divided-attention and multitasking conditions and reported effects in both novice and expert users; simple practice did not remove the problem. Use this to support the claim that a nominal human approver can become a weak control when reliable automation pulls attention elsewhere. Explicitly keep the transfer bounded because modern language-model agents have different interfaces, error profiles, incentives and scopes of action.

The chapter should avoid a romantic test in which manual effort proves agency. A person can exercise strong agency by deliberately delegating. Conversely, clicking an approval button can represent very little agency when the surrounding system made the meaningful choices upstream.

Useful questions for the prose: Who set the objective? Who selected the available options? Who can notice an exception? Who bears the consequence? Can the person inspect, refuse, revise, or reverse the action? These questions should remain prose, not become a branded framework.

Falsification / counterevidence: better interfaces, provenance, reversible actions, preference controls, and agent oversight can make delegation more legible and controllable. Future systems may also automate portions of verification and exception handling. The scarcity claim is therefore about accountable goal-setting and contestability at the moving frontier, not an assertion that every consequential choice must remain manually human.

## Revision boundaries for the four-chapter run

- Do not describe data-centre electricity as if it were synonymous with AI electricity; AI is one important and growing workload.
- Keep IEA projections labeled as projections and retain their uncertainty ranges and efficiency caveats where relevant.
- Do not turn IEA facility-level power-density comparisons into universal per-rack consumption claims.
- Do not equate physical scarcity with moral worth.
- Do not claim generated work is intrinsically meaningless or human work intrinsically meaningful.
- Do not medicalize the meaning argument without clinical evidence.
- Do not present choice overload as a universal result of larger option sets; preserve the moderators and counterevidence.
- Do not define agency by manual labor or refusal of assistance.
- Do not treat older automation-bias findings as direct performance estimates for current generative-AI agents.
- Preserve the possibility that well-designed delegation increases rather than decreases human agency.
