# Artificial Abundance Chapters 10–13 — expertise, responsibility, judgment, and attention revision controls

Date: 2026-09-07

This note prepares one coherent revision pass across Chapters 10–13. The sequence should move from cheaper access to competent expertise, through the persistence of responsibility, into judgment as a selection bottleneck and attention as a finite receiving capacity. The pass should distinguish measured adoption and occupational exposure from forecasts about substitution, and should preserve the countercase that AI can expand capability without making human expertise, accountability, or attention universally scarcer.

## Chapter 10 — Commodity Expertise

### Revision objective

Make the chapter distinguish **cheap access to expert-like output** from **the disappearance of expertise as a production capability**. A model can lower the price of drafting, explanation, classification, comparison, and first-pass analysis while leaving verification, domain context, unusual cases, implementation, and responsibility costly.

### Primary evidence checked

**OECD, _Generative AI and the SME Workforce_ (5 November 2025).** The report draws on a representative 2024 survey of more than 5,000 SMEs in Austria, Canada, Germany, Ireland, Japan, Korea, and the United Kingdom. It reports GenAI use in about 31% of surveyed SMEs and says users commonly report improved performance and help with skill gaps. It also reports increased need for highly skilled workers rather than a simple collapse in skill demand. Treat these as survey findings from the covered countries, not a universal productivity estimate.

**OECD, _Compendium of Productivity Indicators 2025_.** OECD notes that AI is expected to affect future productivity but that its impact was not yet evident in aggregate productivity statistics through the period summarized. This is useful counterpressure against treating striking task-level capability as already-measured economy-wide abundance.

### Additions worth making

1. Separate the **price of an expert-shaped artifact** from the **price of being right in context**. A memo, diagnosis candidate, contract draft, code patch, or market analysis can become cheaper before the cost of validating and acting on it falls by the same amount.
2. Treat expertise as partly an error-distribution problem. Routine cases may commoditize first while rare, adversarial, ambiguous, or high-consequence cases retain a premium.
3. Preserve the possibility that cheaper tools increase demand for experts by expanding the number of projects worth attempting and the number of people able to reach the frontier where expert judgment matters.
4. Do not turn SME survey evidence into a claim that AI has already raised aggregate productivity. Keep firm-reported performance and macroeconomic measurement distinct.

### Falsification pressure

The chapter's residual-expertise thesis weakens where systems can reliably validate their own outputs, handle tail cases, implement decisions, and bear or eliminate the consequence of error at lower cost than human specialists across real deployments.

## Chapter 11 — Responsibility Does Not Scale

### Revision objective

Sharpen the distinction between **production scale** and **accountability scale**. AI can multiply recommendations, decisions, drafts, and actions. Responsibility does not necessarily have to remain one-human-per-action, but consequential systems still need identifiable ownership of policy, escalation, remedy, and risk.

### Primary evidence checked

**NIST, _Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile_ (NIST AI 600-1, published 26 July 2024; NIST page updated 8 April 2026).** The profile treats risk management as a lifecycle and organizational problem involving governance, measurement, management, and multiple AI actors. Use it as evidence that credible governance does not reduce responsibility to a human manually approving every model output.

**NIST AI RMF 1.0.** The framework is voluntary, rights-preserving, non-sector-specific, and designed for organizations developing, deploying, or using AI systems. As of September 2026 NIST states that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. Keep the dated status explicit rather than presenting the framework as frozen.

### Additions worth making

1. Separate **approval** from **responsibility**. A person can be responsible for an automated policy without reviewing every execution, just as organizations already govern payment, security, and industrial systems through rules and controls.
2. Make remedy concrete. Responsibility matters when somebody can investigate, compensate, reverse where possible, change policy, suspend authority, and answer to an external institution.
3. Preserve the countercase that automated controls can improve consistency and reduce arbitrary human error. The chapter should oppose responsibility theater, not automation itself.
4. Ask whether responsibility is matched to authority and information. Naming an executive is weak accountability if that person cannot inspect evidence, alter the system, or obtain an independent view of failures.

### Falsification pressure

The chapter's bottleneck claim weakens where institutions can scale automated action while maintaining low-cost, legible, effective remedy and governance without concentrating review or liability into a scarce human queue.

## Chapter 12 — The Judgment Bottleneck

### Revision objective

Define judgment less mystically. Judgment is the allocation of scarce commitment under uncertainty: deciding which evidence matters, which tradeoff is acceptable, which exception deserves escalation, and which option should consume real resources. AI can improve judgment inputs and sometimes the decision itself; the bottleneck appears when option generation grows faster than the institution's capacity to commit, test, absorb consequences, and revise.

### Evidence boundary

**ILO–NASK, _Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure_ (20 May 2025).** The index estimates that one in four workers globally are in occupations with some potential GenAI exposure and 3.3% of global employment falls in the highest exposure category. The study measures potential task exposure, not observed displacement, and the ILO emphasizes transformation rather than wholesale replacement as the more likely general outcome.

**OECD, _Macroeconomic productivity gains from Artificial Intelligence in G7 economies_ (30 June 2025).** This is scenario-based modeling, not an observed productivity result. Its estimated AI contribution to annual aggregate labour-productivity growth varies materially by country, exposure, sector mix, adoption, and assumptions. Use it to show why cheap cognition can matter greatly without pretending the magnitude is settled.

### Additions worth making

1. Distinguish **ranking** from **commitment**. A model can rank a thousand options; an institution still has finite capital, staff, political tolerance, customer attention, and implementation capacity.
2. Treat judgment as partially automatable. The argument is stronger if it allows that machine systems can make excellent bounded decisions where objectives, state, and feedback are sufficiently clear.
3. Locate the residual bottleneck where objectives conflict, evidence is incomplete, consequences are difficult to reverse, or the cost of being wrong falls on people who did not choose the system.
4. Preserve learning loops. Judgment improves when decisions generate observable feedback and can be revised. A supposedly scarce human intuition that cannot be tested should not receive a premium merely because it is human.

### Falsification pressure

The judgment-bottleneck thesis weakens where objectives can be specified cheaply, consequences are reversible, feedback is rapid, and machine decisions outperform human alternatives without creating a new review or legitimacy bottleneck.

## Chapter 13 — Attention Has Not Scaled

### Revision objective

Make attention a **receiving-side constraint**, not a claim that human attention is fixed in every meaningful sense. AI can summarize, filter, prioritize, personalize, schedule, and protect attention. It can therefore increase effective attention even though the number of human waking hours does not expand.

### Current evidence checked

**OECD, Generative AI topic indicators (2025 usage).** OECD reports that more than one-third of individuals across OECD countries used generative-AI tools in 2025, with substantial differences by age, education, income, and labour-market status. This supports rapid diffusion and uneven adoption, not a claim about attention outcomes.

**NIST AI RMF / Generative AI Profile.** These materials support lifecycle risk management and evaluation rather than a single-output view of system quality. For this chapter, they are useful mainly as counterpressure: filtering and prioritization can be governed as system functions rather than treated as inherently manipulative.

### Additions worth making

1. Separate **information abundance** from **attention demand**. More generated material does not automatically consume more attention if filtering improves faster than production.
2. Define the scarce object as trusted consideration at a consequential moment: a reader actually reading, a voter noticing, a manager examining an exception, a customer deciding, a friend responding.
3. Preserve machine filtering as the strongest countercase. AI may make attention more effective by suppressing low-value material and routing only exceptions. The risk is then control over the filter, not simply volume.
4. Treat attention markets as distributional. People with better filters, assistants, institutional support, or bargaining power may protect their attention while others absorb more automated persuasion, requests, and administrative load.
5. Avoid assuming that every synthetic message competes equally. Recipients adapt, channels develop norms, and low-cost generation can cause entire categories of communication to lose signaling value.

### Falsification pressure

The attention-scarcity thesis weakens if filtering, delegation, and machine-to-machine handling reliably absorb the growth in generated communication while preserving human awareness of the few decisions that genuinely require it.

## Coherent manuscript pass

Keep the four chapters connected but distinct:

- **Chapter 10:** Which parts of expertise actually become commodity-like when competent artifacts get cheap?
- **Chapter 11:** Which forms of accountability remain necessary when production and action scale beyond individual review?
- **Chapter 12:** Where does scarce commitment move when option generation accelerates?
- **Chapter 13:** Who or what gets access to finite human consideration after generation becomes abundant?

The connective tissue is the migration of scarcity. Do not add another branded framework. Prefer concrete boundaries: artifact versus validated outcome, approval versus responsibility, ranking versus commitment, and information supply versus receiving capacity.

## Scope discipline

This research pass does not justify claims that:

- expertise has already become a commodity across professions;
- current GenAI adoption has already produced a clearly measured macroeconomic productivity boom;
- responsibility requires a human to approve every automated action;
- judgment is uniquely or permanently human;
- occupational exposure is observed job displacement;
- human attention cannot be amplified by filtering, delegation, or better tools;
- more generated content necessarily produces proportionally more human attention demand.

The stronger argument is conditional: artificial abundance lowers the cost of many expert-shaped outputs and expands option supply, but value migrates toward validation, remedy, commitment, and trusted consideration when those receiving-side functions do not scale at the same rate.
