# Chapters 13–16 — research controls

This note prepares a coherent argument about scarcity after judgment: attention remains bounded while production expands; signals of human origin can acquire value without becoming reliable proxies for quality; deliberate friction can protect consequential decisions; and visible difficulty can carry status without deserving to become a universal measure of worth.

## Chapter 13 — Attention Has Not Scaled

The chapter should distinguish information production from attention capacity. More content does not create more hours in a day or remove cognitive limits. A useful empirical anchor is Lorenz-Spreen et al. (2019), who analyzed attention dynamics across multiple cultural domains and found that periods of collective attention had become shorter over time. Their model and empirical results point to increased rates of content production and consumption as an important driver. This supports an abundance-pressure mechanism, not a claim that any one platform or AI system has caused a universal decline in individual attention span.

The chapter should also distinguish collective attention from individual concentration. A faster turnover of public topics does not prove that a given person's biological capacity to focus has deteriorated. The stronger claim is economic: when the supply of plausible things to notice rises faster than the time available to notice them, selection, distribution, recommendation, reputation, and trusted filtering become more valuable.

The earlier work-pattern evidence remains relevant as an organizational boundary. Individual AI access changed some time use more readily than it changed the composition of coordinated work. That helps separate producing more from securing the scarce attention required for review, approval, adoption, and action.

Falsification / counterevidence: if AI systems increasingly perform selection and filtering well enough that human attention is needed only at rare exception points, the scarcity may move from ordinary attention toward control of objectives, escalation, and final commitment. The chapter should not treat every minute of human attention as permanently valuable.

## Chapter 14 — The Human Signal

The chapter should not claim that people can reliably detect human-made work or that human origin guarantees quality. Experimental work on AI-generated art is useful precisely because it complicates that story. Bellaiche et al. (2023/2024, Scientific Reports) found that participants were not consistently able to distinguish human from AI-generated images; at the same time, artworks believed to be AI-generated were evaluated less favorably, even though the AI-generated images in the study were often liked on their own merits. The source belief affected valuation.

A separate Scientific Reports study on human expression found that source framing can matter differently by task. In experiments involving jokes and poetry, participants often associated more successful expressive output with humans, but explicit human-versus-AI labels did not mechanically lower ratings in every condition. Use this as a counterweight to any simple 'human label creates a premium' claim.

The chapter's durable distinction is between origin, evidence of origin, and value. In an abundant environment, a verified human signal can become economically meaningful because it communicates process, responsibility, relationship, or intentional presence. That signal is not proof of excellence. A handmade object can be bad; an AI-assisted object can be excellent; a false 'human-made' label can exploit the premium.

Falsification / counterevidence: as audiences become accustomed to AI-assisted work, source labels may matter less in many utilitarian domains. The human premium should therefore be tied to contexts where process, relationship, authenticity, responsibility, or identity are part of what the buyer or audience values—not to all outputs.

## Chapter 15 — Friction as a Feature

The chapter should separate wasteful friction from protective friction. The goal is not to romanticize bureaucracy, latency, passwords, or needless difficulty. The useful category is a deliberate pause, checkpoint, confirmation, or second-person review that prevents a low-cost action from becoming a high-cost mistake.

The World Health Organization surgical-safety checklist is a strong non-AI example of structured friction. In the original multicenter study reported in the New England Journal of Medicine, introduction of a 19-item checklist across eight hospitals was associated with lower observed complication and inpatient death rates. Later population-level implementation research in Ontario found that checklist mandates did not automatically reproduce the original magnitude of benefit. This is an important boundary: the value lies in functioning safety practice and team behavior, not in the ceremonial existence of a checkbox.

NIST's AI assurance work supplies the technical analogy. Trustworthy deployment requires testing and evidence across data quality, algorithm performance, security, explainability, and other dimensions. Those assurance steps cost time precisely because 'generate and ship instantly' is not an adequate control policy for consequential systems.

Falsification / counterevidence: friction can become exclusion, delay, rent-seeking, dark-pattern obstruction, or compliance theater. A useful checkpoint must be proportionate to consequence and should disappear when it no longer catches meaningful errors.

## Chapter 16 — The Status of Difficulty

The chapter should distinguish three ideas that are often collapsed: effort can improve a product, effort can change the maker's valuation of a product, and observers can use perceived effort as a cue for quality. None implies that harder work is inherently better.

Norton, Mochon, and Ariely's IKEA-effect experiments found that people valued successfully self-assembled products more highly, with the effect disappearing when assembly failed or the product was destroyed. This supports a bounded ownership/effort mechanism rather than a general law that suffering creates value.

Kruger et al.'s 2004 'effort heuristic' experiments reported that people sometimes rated identical work more highly when they believed more effort had gone into producing it. A 2023 preregistered replication effort found mixed results: some original effects replicated, others did not, and effects on monetary value were inconsistent. The mixed replication is essential to the chapter. Difficulty can be a social signal, but the signal is contextual and unreliable.

Labroo and Kim's work on metacognitive difficulty adds another boundary: in goal-pursuit settings, effortful means can sometimes be perceived as more instrumental. Again, that does not establish that friction or difficulty is objectively useful; it shows that people can infer value from the experience of effort under particular conditions.

The chapter should connect these mechanisms to artificial abundance carefully. If competent output becomes easier to produce, visible difficulty may become a prestige signal in selected markets—hand craft, live performance, endurance, bespoke work, difficult travel, or forms of creation where process is part of the product. But status markets are historically unstable. Once difficulty becomes performative rather than productive, people can waste resources merely to prove they were not taking the cheap route.

Falsification / counterevidence: audiences may increasingly value outcomes and transparency over difficulty, especially in utilitarian work. The chapter should not imply that an AI-assisted result is worth less because it was easier to make.

## Revision boundaries for the four-chapter run

- Do not equate collective attention turnover with a measured fall in individual biological attention span.
- Do not claim people can reliably identify AI-generated work.
- Do not treat 'human-made' as a quality guarantee.
- Do not romanticize delay or bureaucracy; friction needs a concrete protective function.
- Preserve the weaker Ontario checklist result as evidence that ritualized friction can fail.
- Do not turn the IKEA effect or effort heuristic into a universal law of value.
- Keep the 2023 mixed replication next to the original effort-heuristic finding.
- Treat difficulty as a possible status signal, not a moral virtue.

## Deepening map for the next manuscript pass

The four chapters already carry the right empirical controls. The next revision should therefore deepen mechanisms and consequences rather than add more named studies for decoration.

### Chapter 13

Strengthen the distinction between **attention as time** and **attention as decision authority**. A model can compress ten thousand items into ten, but the compression does not abolish scarcity; it moves value toward whoever specifies ranking objectives, controls escalation, and decides what deserves interruption. The chapter should make this migration explicit without drifting into the separate Search Frontier manuscript. The strongest added material would follow one organizational chain from cheap generation to expensive review: more analyses create more claims on a manager, regulator, customer, or other finite receiver. Avoid a montage of examples.

The existing collective-attention evidence is sufficient. Do not add popular claims about shrinking human attention spans unless they can be tied to a rigorous individual-level measure. The chapter's defensible claim is about competition for finite reception, not neurological deterioration.

### Chapter 14

Deepen the difference between **human origin**, **human presence**, and **human commitment**. The chapter is strongest when it argues that the premium can attach to a finite person putting reputation, liability, time, or relationship behind an output. It is weaker when it approaches a general provenance-infrastructure argument, which belongs to *Proof of Human*.

The revision should also sharpen the distributional countercase already present: human review is attractive as an elective premium in art, craft, performance, and relationship, but access to an accountable human is not merely a luxury attribute when the automated system can deny benefits, misdiagnose a patient, or impose another consequential decision. That boundary keeps the chapter from romanticizing expensive humanity.

### Chapter 15

Make **reversibility** the chapter's most concrete design test. A pause is valuable when it gives someone informed authority to change a consequential path before harm becomes difficult to undo. This is more precise than treating delay itself as protective. The surgical-checklist evidence should remain paired with the Ontario result so the manuscript shows that a ritual can preserve the appearance of review after its function has disappeared.

The AI-specific extension should emphasize that falling action cost can increase the number of actions requiring control. Cheap drafting is not itself the risk; cheap execution, repeated execution, and delegated execution change the expected cost of weak permissions. Keep the chapter centered on repricing: as action gets cheaper, permission boundaries, auditability, exception handling, and meaningful refusal become more valuable complements.

### Chapter 16

Separate **training difficulty**, **protective difficulty**, and **status difficulty** earlier and more firmly. The chapter currently reaches this distinction, but it should govern the argument rather than appear as a late clarification. Training difficulty changes the person doing the work; protective difficulty changes the probability or reversibility of error; status difficulty communicates something to an audience. Only the third category depends primarily on signaling.

Preserve the mixed replication of the effort heuristic close to the original result. The point is not that observers reliably reward effort. It is that effort can become one cue among several when quality or commitment is hard to observe. The strongest economic consequence is therefore not a universal handmade premium but the creation of markets in **chosen costly process** after the functional output becomes cheap.

The class boundary should remain explicit. Chosen difficulty can be leisure, craft, training, identity, or status; imposed difficulty can be exclusion, underinvestment, or drudgery. Artificial abundance should not turn avoidable hardship into a moral test for workers or consumers.

## Coherence test

Read as a sequence, Chapters 13–16 should move through four different scarce complements without collapsing them into one vague human premium:

1. Generation expands faster than finite reception, repricing attention and control over selection.
2. Cheap fluent surfaces weaken old inferences about effort and origin, repricing credible human commitment in selected contexts.
3. Cheap action makes consequential mistakes easier to execute at scale, repricing meaningful checkpoints and reversibility.
4. Once functional difficulty is optional, chosen difficulty can acquire training, experiential, or status value—but only when the cost serves a legible purpose.

If a revision paragraph does not advance one of those four mechanisms, it is probably repetition or belongs in an adjacent Desk book.
