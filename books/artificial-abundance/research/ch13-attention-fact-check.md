# Chapter 13 fact-check — attention and workplace evidence

Checked 2026-09-05 for the next prose revision of `ch13-attention-has-not-scaled.md`.

## Collective attention

Primary source: Philipp Lorenz-Spreen, Bjarke Mørch Mønsted, Philipp Hövel, and Sune Lehmann, “Accelerating dynamics of collective attention,” *Nature Communications* 10, 1759 (2019), DOI 10.1038/s41467-019-09311-w.

Safe claim: the paper reports steeper rises/falls and shorter periods of collective attention across several longitudinal cultural datasets and models increasing content production/consumption as a driver of faster turnover.

Required boundary: this is population-level collective attention, not evidence that individual human cognitive attention spans biologically shortened. The paper explicitly distinguishes its construct from individual-level attention in cognitive psychology.

Counterevidence to preserve: the acceleration was not uniform across every dataset. Scientific citations and Wikipedia traffic showed negligible growth in relative gains/losses under the study’s measures. Chapter prose should not imply a universal law across all information environments.

Interpretive boundary: the model is explanatory evidence, not proof that information abundance is the sole cause. Phrase causal language as the authors’ modeling result or as a supported mechanism rather than settled causation.

## Six-month workplace experiment

Primary source: Eleanor W. Dillon, Sonia Jaffe, Nicole Immorlica, and Christopher T. Stanton, “Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI,” NBER Working Paper 33795, issued May 2025 and revised November 2025, DOI 10.3386/w33795.

Safe claim: the field experiment covered 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers. Workers were randomly selected for access to a generative-AI tool integrated with work applications. In the second half of the six-month experiment, the 80% of treated workers who used the tool spent about two fewer hours per week on email and reduced work outside regular hours.

Required boundary: beyond those individual time savings, the authors report that they did not detect shifts in the quantity or composition of workers’ tasks from individual-level AI provision. “No detected shift” should not be rewritten as proof that no organizational change occurred or can occur.

Disclosure to preserve in research reasoning: some authors were Microsoft employees at the time of the study; Microsoft made M365 Copilot and reviewed the paper for privacy concerns, while the authors state that they retained discretion over results and estimates.

## Revision instructions for Chapter 13

Keep the chapter’s distinction between finite individual time and population-level collective-attention dynamics. When using Lorenz-Spreen et al., add the non-uniform-dataset limitation close enough that readers do not leave with a universalized “attention spans are shrinking” claim. When using Dillon et al., retain the randomized field-experiment scale and the two-hours-per-week email result, but keep the null organizational result framed as non-detection rather than impossibility.

The strongest argumentative move is not “AI destroys attention.” It is narrower: cheap generation can increase competition for finite receiving and review capacity even when individual concentration remains intact, and delegation of filtering moves scarcity toward control over selection rather than eliminating it.
