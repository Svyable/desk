# Chapter 4 — learning under abundant assistance

Chapter 4 argues that abundant generation can either short-circuit practice or accelerate learning, depending on how the tool is used. Bastani et al. provide unusually useful causal evidence for that tension rather than only an analogy.

In a randomized field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school students studying mathematics in Turkey, students using a standard GPT-4 interface performed 48% better than the control group on assisted practice problems. But when the tool was removed for an unassisted exam, that group scored 17% worse than students who had never received AI access. A second treatment, designed with teacher-informed guardrails that emphasized hints rather than simply supplying answers, improved assisted practice performance by 127% and largely eliminated the later learning penalty.

This is strong evidence for the chapter's distinction between producing a good answer and building the judgment needed to produce or evaluate one later. It also sharpens the chapter's existing counterargument: assistance is not inherently corrosive to skill. Tool design mattered substantially in the same experiment. The more defensible claim is that systems which collapse the learner's need to attempt, diagnose, and reconstruct the problem can trade short-term performance for weaker independent skill, while systems that preserve those cognitive acts may avoid much of that cost.

The boundary matters. This was one high school, one subject, and a 2023 deployment of GPT-4. The study measured short-term learning, not professional apprenticeship or long-run expertise, and the authors explicitly caution against broad generalization. It should therefore support the chapter's apprenticeship warning as a concrete mechanism, not prove that AI use generally deskills workers.

Primary source:

- Hamsa Bastani, Osbert Bastani, Alp Sungu, Haosen Ge, Özge Kabakcı, and Rei Mariman, “Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics,” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 122, no. 26 (2025), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
