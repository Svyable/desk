# Chapters 13–16 revision controls

This note bounds the next prose pass across the adjacent attention, human-signal, friction, and difficulty chapters. It is an editorial control sheet, not a substitute for chapter-level sourcing.

## Chapter 13 — Attention Has Not Scaled

The chapter’s strongest claim is an economic one: cheap generation can increase the value of scarce reception, filtering, and selection. Keep the distinction between **collective attention dynamics** and an individual biological “attention span.” Lorenz-Spreen et al. studied accelerating collective-attention cycles across several cultural domains; their result should not be rewritten as proof that individuals have biologically lost the capacity for sustained concentration.

The harder revision target is delegated selection. If software successfully summarizes or ranks a flood of output, the scarcity has not simply vanished. Some of it moves into setting the ranking objective, auditing errors, deciding which omissions matter, and retaining the ability to override the filter. The prose should make that migration concrete rather than assuming human beings must personally inspect everything.

Counterevidence to preserve: better search, recommendation, summarization, and agentic triage can reduce the amount of human attention required per unit of useful output. Abundance need not produce overload when filtering improves faster than generation. The chapter therefore argues that attention and control over selection can become relatively valuable complements, not that every additional generated object imposes a net attention cost.

Primary research anchor:
- Philipp Lorenz-Spreen et al., “Accelerating dynamics of collective attention,” *Nature Communications* 10, 1759 (2019): https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-09311-w

## Chapter 14 — The Human Signal

Do not let “human-made” become a universal quality claim. Separate at least four reasons origin can matter: expected quality, accountable responsibility, relationship, and process/provenance. The chapter is strongest when it shows that these can command different premiums in different markets.

The revision should move away from purity as the durable signal. Generative tools can participate heavily in production while a person still makes consequential choices and accepts responsibility. Conversely, nominal human review can be ceremonial. The useful test is whether a person was involved **where involvement changes the meaning, risk, or accountability of the output**.

Counterevidence to preserve: experimental findings about AI labels and aesthetic evaluation are mixed across tasks and framings, and people are not reliably able to infer origin from surface style. Do not forecast a permanent, universal consumer penalty for AI-generated culture. As tool use normalizes, “Was AI used?” may become less informative than “How was it used, and who stands behind the result?”

## Chapter 15 — Friction as a Feature

Separate protective friction from ceremonial friction. A pause, checklist, confirmation step, second signature, waiting period, or manual review is valuable only when it changes the probability or cost of a consequential error, improves learning, creates a useful commitment point, or supplies information that automation would otherwise erase.

Use **reversibility** as the practical discriminator. When an action is cheap to reverse and errors are easy to detect, extra friction is harder to justify. When an action is difficult to reverse, creates externalities, or can silently propagate harm, deliberate friction can be economically rational even if automation could remove it.

Counterevidence to preserve: friction is often defended after it has stopped serving its original purpose. Checklists can become box-ticking; approval chains can diffuse rather than concentrate responsibility; waiting periods can simply ration access; manual review can become a costly rubber stamp. The chapter should not romanticize inconvenience.

## Chapter 16 — The Status of Difficulty

Distinguish three kinds of difficulty before making any status argument:

1. **Training difficulty** builds capability through practice, prediction, reconstruction, or feedback.
2. **Protective difficulty** slows or constrains actions whose mistakes are costly or hard to reverse.
3. **Status difficulty** signals resources, discipline, taste, exclusivity, or identity because the activity is costly to perform.

These categories can overlap, but they should not be treated as interchangeable. A difficult task can build skill without conveying status; a status practice can be difficult without building socially useful skill; protective friction can be tedious rather than prestigious.

The effort-heuristic literature can support only a bounded claim: under some conditions, observers use perceived effort as one cue when evaluating outcomes. Replications and extensions do not justify a universal law that harder work is always valued more. Expertise, outcome quality, norms, observability, and whether the difficulty was voluntarily chosen all matter.

Preserve the class boundary. Chosen difficulty can be recreation, craft, or status consumption for someone with abundant time and money while imposed difficulty remains ordinary scarcity for someone else. A premium on handmade, slow, local, live, or arduous experience is not evidence that hardship itself has become desirable.

Research anchor:
- Justin Kruger et al., “The effort heuristic,” *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 40(1), 91–98 (2004): https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-1031(03)00065-9

## Cross-chapter continuity

The four chapters should form one argument rather than four celebrations of humanity. Cheap generation raises the relative value of scarce reception; scarce reception increases the importance of trustworthy selection and accountable commitment; accountable systems sometimes preserve useful friction; and once convenience becomes abundant, selected forms of difficulty can acquire training, protective, experiential, or status value. Each step has a failure mode. Filters can manipulate, human labels can become marketing theater, friction can become bureaucracy, and difficulty can become exclusion.

The revision should keep those failure modes inside the argument rather than relegating them to disclaimers. The book’s claim is repricing under abundance, not moral purification of whatever remains scarce.
