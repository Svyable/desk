# Artificial Abundance Chapters 22–24 — politics, human value, and ending revision controls

Date: 2026-09-07

This note prepares the closing three-chapter revision pass. The ending should narrow rather than inflate the book's thesis: cheap cognitive production redistributes bargaining power and access unevenly; human provenance can carry value without making human production inherently superior; and the final scarce thing should remain a contingent object of choice rather than a protected list of uniquely human tasks.

## Chapter 22 — The Politics of Plenty

### Revision objective

Keep the chapter focused on distribution rather than technological verdicts. A capability can become cheaper and more widely available while gains, transition costs, infrastructure burdens, and bargaining power remain unevenly distributed.

### Primary evidence checked

**ILO–NASK, _Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure_ (20 May 2025).** The working paper estimates that one in four workers globally are in occupations with some GenAI exposure and 3.3% of global employment is in its highest exposure category. Exposure is higher in high-income countries, and the authors explicitly describe job transformation as more likely than wholesale replacement because most occupations still contain tasks requiring human input.

A useful 2026 update from the ILO on occupational segregation reinforces the distributional boundary: exposure differs substantially across groups and occupations. Use this as evidence that transition burdens are heterogeneous, not as a forecast of realized displacement.

### Additions worth making

1. Separate **access to capability** from **capture of surplus**. A worker can receive a better tool without automatically receiving the productivity gain as wages, leisure, autonomy, or job security.
2. Distinguish exposure from outcome. Occupational exposure is a map of tasks potentially affected by GenAI, not an observed job-loss statistic.
3. Treat transition capacity as scarce. Training time, savings, mobility, bargaining power, childcare, credentials, and local job alternatives affect whether a productivity shock feels like opportunity or coercion.
4. Preserve the consumer countercase. Lower prices and broader access can create genuine welfare gains even when producer income falls; distributional analysis should not erase those gains.
5. Make infrastructure incidence visible. The places that host power, data-centre, transmission, or other physical expansion may bear costs that are geographically different from where digital benefits are consumed.

### Falsification pressure

The chapter's distributional concern weakens where productivity gains are broadly shared through higher compensation, lower prices, shorter hours, new demand, ownership, or public provision, and where workers can move between tasks without large transition costs. The political claim should become stronger or weaker with those institutions rather than assuming one labor-market outcome.

## Chapter 23 — Stay Expensively Human

### Revision objective

Keep the title provocative while making the chapter impossible to read as a command to preserve human inefficiency. Human involvement earns a premium only where origin, relationship, responsibility, presence, formation, or process is itself part of what someone values.

### Evidence checked

**Grassini & Koivisto, _Scientific Reports_ 14, 4113 (published 19 February 2024).** In a study of 201 participants, people could not reliably distinguish the selected human-made from AI-generated artworks. The AI-generated images in the study were liked more on average, yet images participants *believed* were AI-generated were evaluated less favorably regardless of actual source. Treat this as evidence that believed provenance can affect valuation in this experimental setting, not as a universal law of art markets or human preference.

### Additions worth making

1. Separate **artifact quality** from **relationship value**. A machine-made artifact can be better on visible performance while a buyer still values knowing, supporting, meeting, or being accountable to a person.
2. Make willingness to pay empirical. Do not infer a durable human premium merely because respondents express a preference or source bias in an experiment.
3. Preserve substitution. Where buyers care only about function, cheap machine production may correctly eliminate a human premium.
4. Avoid moralizing provenance. Human-made goods can be exploitative, careless, fraudulent, or mediocre; machine assistance can expand access and let human workers spend more time on the relational part of a service.
5. Distinguish chosen human contact from imposed human labor. A premium paid for a relationship is different from preserving low-productivity work because institutions refuse useful tools.

### Falsification pressure

The chapter's human-premium claim weakens in markets where buyers remain indifferent to provenance after quality, price, and disclosure are controlled, or where machine-mediated relationships satisfy the valued function equally well. That result would not make human work worthless; it would show that the value was in the outcome rather than the origin.

## Chapter 24 — The Last Scarce Thing

### Revision objective

Make the ending resist a final protected category of human superiority. The chapter should close on commitment under finite life: abundance can expand options, but an individual or institution still has to allocate time, attention, authority, physical capacity, and irreversible action among them.

### Additions worth making

1. Treat scarcity as moving, not disappearing. If judgment, verification, creativity, or planning become cheaper, the book should welcome that rather than redefine those capacities as secretly scarce.
2. Distinguish option abundance from lived multiplicity. A person can generate ten thousand plausible lives and still inhabit one sequence of days.
3. Keep commitment costly for structural reasons. Choosing one project, relationship, location, institution, or course of action often excludes alternatives because time and physical consequences are finite.
4. Preserve reversibility where it is useful. Cheap generation can make experimentation less costly; the argument should not glorify irreversible choices merely because they are scarce.
5. Let the final scarce thing remain conditional: what matters is the capacity to decide what receives finite consequence when production of possibilities is cheap.

### Counterevidence to preserve

- Better agents can reduce selection and verification costs as well as generation costs.
- Recommendation and delegation can make abundance manageable rather than overwhelming.
- Some commitments can become more reversible through simulation, modularity, insurance, and better information.
- Digital goods can expand real experience rather than merely create fake options.
- People differ in how much choice they want; abundance does not imply universal choice overload.

### Ending discipline

Do not end by claiming love, meaning, consciousness, taste, or judgment is permanently beyond machines. Do not turn finitude into a motivational slogan. The ending is strongest if it returns to the economic argument: abundance changes the price of another possibility, while consequence still has to land somewhere.

## Coherent manuscript pass

Keep the closing sequence distinct:

- **Chapter 22:** Who receives the gains and bears the transition when cognition gets cheaper?
- **Chapter 23:** When does human origin or presence remain part of the product rather than a production inefficiency?
- **Chapter 24:** What remains scarce when the system can keep producing another plausible option?

The connective tissue is allocation under abundance. Avoid adding a new named framework. Preserve counterevidence and allow favorable institutional outcomes to weaken the strongest scarcity claims.

## Scope discipline

This research pass does not justify claims that:

- one in four jobs will disappear because of GenAI;
- occupational exposure is equivalent to observed displacement;
- AI productivity gains necessarily accrue to capital rather than labor or consumers;
- people universally prefer human-made art;
- human provenance guarantees quality, ethics, or authenticity;
- physical human presence is always preferable to machine mediation;
- choice overload is universal;
- any particular cognitive capacity is permanently protected from automation.

The stronger closing claim is narrower: artificial abundance can make capability cheap faster than institutions redistribute its gains, can make provenance one dimension of value without making it a quality certificate, and can multiply possible actions faster than finite actors can give those possibilities consequence.
