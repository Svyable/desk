# Chapter 13 — generated reviews and the receiving-side tax

Rechecked September 6, 2026.

## Why this belongs here

Chapter 13 argues that cheap generation can move costs onto the receiving side: an item that is nearly free to produce may still consume time, verification, ranking, or trust before a recipient can safely ignore or use it. Consumer reviews provide a concrete institutional case because the scarce resource is not review text. It is confidence that the review represents a real experience.

## Primary-source anchor: FTC Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule

The Federal Trade Commission's final Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule took effect October 21, 2024. Among other practices, it prohibits specified fake or false reviews and testimonials, including reviews that misrepresent that they came from a person who does not exist, such as an AI-generated fake reviewer, or from someone who did not actually experience the product or service. The rule also addresses purchased sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled review sites presented as independent, certain review suppression, and fake social-media indicators.

The useful mechanism for this chapter is narrower than "AI causes fake reviews." Reviews are a compression device: consumers use other people's reported experience to reduce the amount of product evaluation they must perform themselves. When fabricated review volume becomes cheap, the receiver's filtering problem becomes more expensive and the review system can lose some of its compression value.

## Counterevidence and current-policy boundary

Do not turn the rule into evidence that generative AI has already destroyed online reviews, or that every platform now faces an unmanageable verification burden. The FTC rule covers deceptive review practices that predate generative AI as well as AI-enabled ones. Platforms can use purchase verification, provenance, moderation, reputation systems, anomaly detection, and enforcement to reduce the problem. A successful filter can preserve the value of reviews even as generation gets cheaper.

There is also an important current enforcement wrinkle. In 2024 the FTC brought a separate case against Rytr over an AI review-generation service, alleging that subscribers could generate detailed false reviews at scale. But on December 22, 2025 the Commission reopened and set aside that consent order in response to the administration's AI Action Plan. The underlying Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule remains a separate rule; the rescinded Rytr order should therefore not be cited as if it were current proof of an active AI-review-specific enforcement position.

## Manuscript implication

If this case enters Chapter 13, use it beside the existing spam analogy, not as a new detour. The sharper sentence-level claim is that abundance can degrade a filtering signal: when plausible testimony is cheap to manufacture, recipients and platforms must spend more effort deciding which testimony deserves belief.

The strongest counterargument should remain in the prose. Cheap generation can also improve the filter. The chapter's durable claim is therefore not that attention systems inevitably collapse under AI-generated volume, but that the economics of reception matter: whoever can cheaply create another item can externalize part of the cost onto whoever must rank, authenticate, review, or reject it.
