## Chapter 17 — Reality Gets Expensive

Current strength: the chapter already establishes the central distinction between cheap representation and constrained transformation, and it correctly keeps physical scarcity conditional rather than claiming that all embodied goods must become more expensive.

Depth to add:

- Make **capacity rights** more explicit. A scarce physical endpoint is not only a quantity of land, power, labor, or seats; it is often a right to use capacity at a particular place and time. A megawatt at the wrong node, a hospital bed without the relevant staff, or a permit after the financing window closes is not an equivalent substitute. This gives the chapter a stronger explanation of why aggregate abundance can coexist with local shortage.
- Develop **queues as prices paid in time**. When money prices are fixed, regulated, reputationally constrained, or simply slow to adjust, scarcity appears as waiting, lotteries, booking windows, priority rules, or administrative triage. Cheap discovery and planning can lengthen these non-price queues even when nominal prices barely move.
- Add the **option-to-execution ratio**. Generative systems can raise the number of plausible proposals per unit of implementation capacity. The useful operational metric is therefore not drafts per hour but the fraction of generated options that can be tested, approved, funded, built, or deliberately rejected without consuming disproportionate downstream attention.
- Deepen the distinction between **substitution and complementarity**. A digital meeting can remove a flight; a better travel planner can increase demand for flights. A simulation can reduce prototypes; cheap design can also increase the number of prototypes worth considering. The direction of physical demand is empirical and task-specific.
- Preserve the political boundary already present: some bottlenecks are engineering problems, while others are conflicts over incidence, land use, risk, timing, or consent. Better cognition can clarify a tradeoff without dissolving it.

Avoid:

- New numerical electricity, grid, construction, or data-centre claims unless separately verified and added to the source trail.
- Treating queues as inherently bad; rationing rules can protect access or safety when expansion is impossible or undesirable.
- Suggesting that physical capacity is permanently fixed. Automation, investment, substitution, and institutional reform can expand it.

## Chapter 18 — The Physical Premium

Current strength: the chapter already avoids romanticizing matter and shows that physical advantage includes maintenance, liability, installed capability, and exposure as well as scarcity.

Depth to add:

- Separate **purchase price from carrying cost** more sharply. Physical ownership creates a stream of future obligations: storage, inspection, maintenance, downtime, insurance, replacement, disposal, and coordination. The premium is therefore partly a balance-sheet commitment to future work, not merely a higher marginal production cost.
- Develop **utilization versus resilience**. Software can make scarce assets run closer to capacity, but a system optimized for average utilization can become brittle when arrivals cluster, equipment fails, or high-severity cases take longer than expected. Some apparent slack is option value: the ability to absorb variance without turning every disruption into a queue.
- Add **complement failure** as a source of stranded capacity. A facility can have floor space but no grid connection; a clinic can have rooms but insufficient qualified staff; a factory can have machines but lack one certified input. Physical output is often governed by the scarcest complement rather than the average abundance of inputs.
- Clarify **who captures the premium**. Scarcity can accrue to landowners, workers with scarce credentials, infrastructure owners, intermediaries controlling booking/access, or customers through waiting time. A rising physical premium does not imply that the operating business automatically earns higher margins.
- Deepen the countercase: better prediction, modularity, robotics, remote operation, standardization, and repairability can make physical systems more elastic. The chapter's claim should remain relative: as informational components cheapen, the remaining embodied constraints become more visible, not necessarily more expensive in absolute terms.

Avoid:

- Repeating Chapter 17's representation/transformation argument at length.
- Treating deliberate inefficiency as authenticity.
- Inferring aggregate energy demand from per-task efficiency in either direction without evidence.

## Chapter 19 — The Meaning Shortage

Current strength: the chapter already rejects the simplistic claim that more choice is inherently harmful and correctly frames the problem as valuation and commitment rather than a clinical shortage of meaning.

Depth to add:

- Develop **commitment as an information-producing act**. Keeping options open preserves flexibility but withholds feedback. Choosing a path exposes a preference to customers, colleagues, a city, a craft, a relationship, or a calendar; reality can then return information that simulation cannot fully provide. This is why optionality has diminishing value when no option is exercised.
- Distinguish **reversible exploration from identity-scale commitment**. Cheap experiments are especially valuable when they let people test paths without prematurely locking themselves in. The problem is not experimentation; it is remaining indefinitely in polished simulation when the next useful evidence requires contact with consequence.
- Add **organizational carrying cost of options**. Every plausible initiative creates review, comparison, memory, coordination, and opportunity-cost burdens even if generation itself is free. A portfolio can become expensive before any project is funded because somebody must keep alternatives legible and decide when they are dead.
- Deepen **delegated preference formation**. An assistant that remembers prior choices can reduce search costs, but repeated optimization against inferred preferences can also narrow what becomes salient. Preserve both possibilities: memory can protect stated priorities from novelty, or it can harden yesterday's preferences into tomorrow's menu.
- Give **stopping rules** more weight. In an abundant option environment, a valuable institution or individual practice is often a rule for when search ends: enough evidence, a deadline, a budget, a reversible trial, or an explicit opportunity cost. The rule does not identify the correct end; it prevents the search process from consuming the resource it was meant to allocate.
- Preserve the strongest counterargument already in the chapter: cheaper generation can improve meaning when it makes experimentation accessible to people previously blocked by money, skill, language, disability, or time. If abundant experiments improve commitment, the shortage metaphor should remain modest.

Avoid:

- Clinical claims about depression, loneliness, addiction, or mental health without dedicated evidence.
- Turning commitment into moral heroism or making reversal shameful.
- Treating generated language as inherently hollow. Meaning can survive assistance when the person remains responsible for the intention and consequence.

## Coherence for the three-chapter pass

The sequence should tighten rather than repeat:

1. Chapter 17: cheap cognition reaches scarce external capacity.
2. Chapter 18: embodied capacity carries costs, complements, resilience requirements, and future obligations.
3. Chapter 19: once both production and option generation cheapen, selection and commitment become the scarce internal act.

A good revision should make each handoff explicit while avoiding recap paragraphs. The shared movement is from possibility to consequence: proposal → physical execution → chosen purpose.
