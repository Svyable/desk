# Chapters 21–24 — research controls

This note prepares the closing four chapters as one argument. Artificial abundance does not remove institutions or politics; it changes what they must allocate and govern. Human-origin goods may acquire premiums in some contexts, but that premium is neither universal nor proof of superior quality. The book should end with finite human time, attention, commitment, and consequence rather than with a claim that some cognitive task will remain permanently beyond machines.

## Chapter 21 — Institutions for Plenty

The chapter should resist the idea that abundant generation makes institutions obsolete. Institutions coordinate rules, rights, accountability, common records, shared infrastructure, and distribution. Cheap production can increase the number of options they must govern faster than it reduces these coordination costs.

Useful evidence comes from the OECD's 2025 survey of more than 5,000 small and medium-sized enterprises across seven countries. Generative AI was already in use in 31% of surveyed SMEs. Firms reported performance benefits and help with skill or labour shortages, but adoption was constrained by suitability, legal and regulatory concerns, information handling, and employee skills. Twice as many SMEs reported that generative AI increased their need for highly skilled workers as reported that it decreased that need. This is a useful institutional point: a general-purpose capability can diffuse rapidly while increasing demand for complementary rules, training, and expertise.

NIST's Generative AI Profile to the AI Risk Management Framework provides a governance counterweight to product-centric narratives. Its purpose is not to freeze a particular organizational chart around AI, but to make risk management part of design, deployment, measurement, and oversight. Use it to support the claim that organizations must build durable processes around systems whose outputs are cheap and variable.

Falsification / counterevidence: institutions can become bloated, defensive, and slower than the technologies they govern. Some coordination can itself be automated. Do not imply that every existing institution deserves to survive or that more governance is always better. The claim is that abundance does not abolish coordination problems; it can move them upstream into objectives, permissions, standards, verification, and access.

## Chapter 22 — The Politics of Plenty

The chapter should define politics broadly as collective conflict over distribution, rules, legitimacy, and who bears transition costs. It should not advocate a partisan program or pretend that productivity gains arrive evenly.

The ILO's 2025 global exposure index estimates that one in four jobs worldwide is in an occupation with some potential exposure to generative AI, while only 3.3% of global employment falls into the highest exposure category. The ILO emphasizes transformation rather than wholesale replacement as the more likely near-term pattern. Exposure also differs materially by income level and gender because occupations are distributed unevenly.

The OECD's current generative-AI adoption statistics make the distribution problem visible on the user side. More than one-third of individuals across OECD countries used generative AI tools in 2025, but usage gaps were large by age and meaningful by education and income. This is not evidence that access gaps will persist forever. It is evidence that a technology can become broadly available while still diffusing unevenly.

The chapter can also use OECD modelling of possible G7 productivity gains as a bounded example: projected aggregate gains differ materially across countries because sector mix, exposure, and adoption differ. Productivity is therefore not a single technological dividend waiting to be divided; institutions and economic structure shape its size before distribution begins.

Falsification / counterevidence: AI may compress some within-occupation productivity or wage differences, and cheap digital tools can broaden access to capabilities that were previously expensive. Do not write the politics chapter as though inequality can only worsen. The durable claim is that abundance creates distributive choices because gains, exposure, bargaining power, complementary assets, and adjustment costs are heterogeneous.

## Chapter 23 — Stay Expensively Human

The chapter title is intentionally provocative and must be bounded. It should not claim that human-made work is inherently superior, more authentic in every context, or economically protected simply because a person made it.

A 2024 Scientific Reports experiment found that participants could not consistently distinguish human-made from AI-generated images. The selected AI-generated images were in fact liked more on average, yet participants showed a negative bias toward works they believed were AI-generated. This is useful because it separates output quality from source belief: human origin can affect valuation even when people cannot reliably infer origin from the artifact.

Research on the handmade effect supplies an older analogue. Consumers can attach value to perceived human effort, care, personality, or producer responsibility even when machine production would be cheaper or more consistent. Use this as a model of symbolic value, not a universal law. Some buyers want the handmade chair; others want the precisely machined chair at one-tenth the price.

The chapter should distinguish several human premiums that abundance may make more legible: real-time human attention, physical presence, accountable authorship, craft process, witnessed effort, and a relationship with a particular person. These are different goods and should not be collapsed into a vague authenticity premium.

Falsification / counterevidence: consumers may prefer AI-generated outputs in many settings, especially when source is hidden or when speed, cost, consistency, or personalization dominate. Human labels can also become empty luxury signals. A market for human-origin work can coexist with mass preference for machine-assisted work.

## Chapter 24 — The Last Scarce Thing

The ending should not nominate intelligence, judgment, creativity, or meaning as permanently human. The book has repeatedly argued that scarcity migrates. Any task-level sanctuary would contradict that logic.

A stronger ending is finite human life. A person has limited time, attention, relationships, commitments, and irreversible choices even in a world of abundant generated options. Artificial systems may expand what can be done during that life, but they do not make the person's own hours infinitely repeatable.

Research on time and well-being provides a bounded empirical anchor. Across several studies, people who prioritized time over money reported greater subjective well-being, even controlling for available time, income, and other factors. This is correlational in important parts and should not become a prescription that everyone should always trade money for leisure.

A second study using large U.S. data sets and experiments found a nonlinear relationship between discretionary time and subjective well-being: too little time was associated with lower well-being, but more free time did not increase well-being without limit. In some cases, very large amounts of discretionary time were associated with lower well-being when people lacked a sense of productive purpose. This is useful counterevidence against the simple claim that the final scarce thing is merely "more free time."

The chapter should therefore close on allocation rather than quantity. The last scarce thing is a life that must be spent: what receives attention, what becomes a commitment, which relationships receive unautomated presence, which risks a person accepts, and which goals become worth pursuing after production is no longer the dominant constraint.

Falsification / counterevidence: future systems may extend healthy life, reduce time spent on unwanted work, and dramatically enlarge human capability. None of this makes individual time literally infinite. Avoid metaphysical claims about consciousness or mortality that the book has not established. The ending works economically and existentially without them.

## Revision boundaries for the closing run

- Do not treat institutions as intrinsically good or bureaucracy as a synonym for governance.
- Do not turn heterogeneous AI exposure into a prediction of mass unemployment.
- Do not claim that AI necessarily raises inequality; preserve evidence that some tools compress performance differences.
- Keep current adoption and exposure statistics dated and source-bounded.
- Do not claim that people can reliably identify human-made work from the artifact alone.
- Do not equate human origin with quality, virtue, or copyright status.
- Do not end by declaring a permanent list of uniquely human cognitive tasks.
- Keep the final scarcity attached to finite human allocation and consequence, not to mystical exceptionalism.
