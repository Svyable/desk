# Artificial Abundance Chapters 19–21 — meaning, agency, and institutional-plenty revision controls

Date: 2026-09-07

This note prepares one coherent revision pass across Chapters 19–21. The sequence moves from abundant possibility and scarce commitment, to assistance that can either expand or hollow practical agency, to institutions whose job changes when production becomes cheap. The pass should not claim that AI creates a universal crisis of meaning, that assistance necessarily deskills people, or that institutions are valuable merely because they are slow.

## Chapter 19 — The Meaning Shortage

### Revision objective

Keep the chapter's strongest boundary: “meaning shortage” names a scarcity of valuation and commitment, not a measurable social depletion of meaning. Sharpen the distinction between **option generation** and **option valuation**. Cheap generation can expand the menu dramatically while leaving the chooser's time, obligations, relationships, identity, and willingness to foreclose alternatives finite.

### Additions worth making

1. Separate **exploration value** from **commitment cost**. Generating ten prototypes can be useful because it makes preferences visible. The problem begins when cheap iteration becomes a reason never to close the option set.
2. Distinguish **polish** from **preference maturity**. A generated strategy, speech, life plan, or proposal can become externally complete before the person or institution has formed an equally mature commitment to it. The artifact's finish should not be treated as evidence that the underlying choice is settled.
3. Make downstream scarcity explicit. Cheap proposals can increase demand on review time, implementation capacity, legal attention, capital, political legitimacy, and calendar space. The marginal artifact may be nearly free while the marginal decision is not.
4. Preserve the countercase that generative systems can improve valuation rather than merely multiply options. They can elicit criteria, expose contradictions, summarize tradeoffs, simulate consequences, and help people discover preferences they could not easily articulate unaided.
5. Give the thesis a falsification test: if systems become reliably good at helping people clarify values, narrow options, and commit without producing persistent decision churn or downstream review overload, the “meaning shortage” mechanism weakens materially.

### Existing evidence boundary

The chapter already cites the Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman choice-overload meta-analysis and correctly avoids the slogan that more choice is always worse. Preserve that nuance. The useful condition is preference uncertainty and decision difficulty, not assortment size by itself.

Do not extend that literature into claims about clinical well-being, loneliness, depression, or social purposelessness. Those would require different evidence.

## Chapter 20 — Agency After Assistance

### Revision objective

Make agency operational rather than romantic. Agency should not mean unaided performance or maximal manual approval. The useful question is whether a person or accountable institution can form or adopt goals, understand consequential alternatives well enough to choose among them, revise those goals, contest important actions, and change or revoke delegation when circumstances change.

### Additions worth making

1. Separate **capability gain** from **authorship drift**. Assistance expands agency when it gives a person access to actions previously out of reach. It can weaken agency when inferred past preferences quietly become standing rules that continue acting after the person would have changed them.
2. Treat preference models as expiring hypotheses, not identity. A system that learned “decline evening meetings” or “prefer this supplier” should have a way to surface material drift, changed context, and unusual choices rather than optimizing indefinitely against stale behavior.
3. Distinguish **approval frequency** from **contestability**. Hundreds of approvals can produce less control than a small number of well-placed opportunities to set objectives, bound authority, inspect exceptions, revoke delegation, and reverse consequences.
4. Preserve pre-authorized automation as a strong countercase. Emergency braking, automated insulin delivery, networking, security response, and other machine-speed functions show that meaningful human agency can be exercised upstream through rules and authorization rather than transaction-by-transaction clicking.
5. Add an “exit cost” test. Delegation remains more genuinely optional when a person can change tools, retrieve relevant state, withdraw permissions, and continue the underlying activity without punitive switching costs or institutional retaliation.

### Evidence and governance anchors

The chapter's automation-bias discussion is historical human-factors evidence and should remain bounded to the mechanism: reliable automation changes monitoring behavior, especially under divided attention. Do not present aviation, clinical decision support, and generative AI as interchangeable technologies.

NIST's Generative AI Profile, published 26 July 2024 and updated on the NIST site in 2026, is useful as a governance counterweight. It treats risk management across the lifecycle and assigns responsibilities across AI actors rather than equating control with a single human approval point. Use it only to support the institutional design claim that oversight is distributed across governance, measurement, management, and deployment—not as evidence for a psychological theory of agency.

### Falsification pressure

The agency-erosion concern weakens where assistants reliably surface preference drift, preserve meaningful exit and contest rights, improve user understanding of consequences, and make goal revision easier rather than simply predicting and executing past behavior more efficiently.

## Chapter 21 — Institutions for Plenty

### Revision objective

Tighten the distinction between **institutional production** and **institutional authority**. Cheap generation can remove document work, translation work, search costs, and some coordination work without eliminating jurisdiction, authoritative records, appeal, responsibility, continuity, or legitimate allocation of scarce resources.

The chapter should not defend bureaucracy as such. Its stronger claim is that institutions need to identify which frictions are carrying a governance function and which are merely historical delay.

### Fresh primary evidence checked

**OECD, _Generative AI and the SME Workforce_ (2025).** The OECD surveyed more than 5,000 SMEs in Austria, Canada, Germany, Ireland, Japan, Korea, and the United Kingdom in late 2024. Generative AI was reported in use by 30.7% of surveyed SMEs. Among users, 65% reported improved employee performance; 83% reported no change in overall staff need. Twenty percent said generative AI increased the relative need for highly skilled workers, compared with 9% saying it decreased it. The survey also notes that only a minority of using firms had adopted measures such as staff training or internal guidance.

Use those figures as survey findings, not universal prevalence estimates. The OECD itself notes definitional, timing, and participation differences from national statistical-office measures.

**ILO–NASK, _Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure_ (20 May 2025).** The index estimates that about one in four jobs worldwide sits in an occupation with some potential GenAI exposure, while 3.3% of global employment falls in the highest exposure category. The ILO emphasizes transformation of tasks and jobs rather than treating exposure as equivalent to replacement.

Keep that distinction explicit. Occupational exposure is not an observed layoff rate and does not establish how quickly firms will redesign jobs.

**NIST AI RMF: Generative AI Profile (26 July 2024; NIST page updated 8 April 2026).** This supports the narrower institutional claim that trustworthy deployment requires defined roles, lifecycle governance, measurement, monitoring, and risk-management processes. It does not imply that NIST endorses any specific staffing model or level of bureaucracy.

### Additions worth making

1. Make the **authoritative-state problem** concrete. A conversational answer can be cheap and useful, but institutions still need to know which policy, record, entitlement, credential, or decision is binding when generated answers conflict.
2. Separate **latency that protects a function** from **latency left over from old process**. Appeals, separation of duties, identity checks, evidentiary records, and recovery paths can remain valuable. Re-keying data, waiting for one person who owns a password, or routing every trivial draft through senior approval may not.
3. Add a distributional test. An institution that removes professional scarcity can broaden access, but it can also shift verification burden onto users who are least able to challenge errors. “Self-service abundance” is not automatically equitable if appeal, correction, and authoritative interpretation become harder to reach.
4. Preserve institutional decomposition. Schools, firms, hospitals, courts, and public agencies bundle production, coordination, legitimacy, protection, continuity, identity, and allocation. AI can transform one function while leaving several others scarce.
5. Treat transformation as a design obligation. If jobs lose routine production tasks, the institution must decide which judgment, relationship, verification, accountability, or operational tasks remain and whether workers are actually equipped to perform them.

### Counterevidence to preserve

- Cheap intelligence can remove genuine gatekeeping and broaden access to explanation, analysis, translation, and professional-quality tools.
- Some institutional delay has no protective function and should disappear.
- Automation can make authoritative state more legible by improving records, provenance, consistency, and monitoring.
- Exposure estimates are not destiny; adoption, regulation, complementary investment, worker learning, and organizational redesign all mediate outcomes.
- Institutions themselves can preserve scarcity for rent-seeking reasons and should not be presumed legitimate simply because they are established.

### Falsification pressure

The institutional-scarcity thesis weakens where generative systems make authoritative state, appeal, responsibility, continuity, and access cheaper at the same rate as they make ordinary production cheaper. If legitimacy and contestability become easier to provide rather than residual bottlenecks, value may not migrate toward institutions in the way the chapter predicts.

## Coherent manuscript pass

Keep the chapters distinct while preserving the progression:

- **Chapter 19:** abundant possibility does not remove the need to rank and commit;
- **Chapter 20:** assistance expands agency when people can still revise the objective and contest consequential delegation;
- **Chapter 21:** institutions for plenty must convert cheap possibility into legitimate, authoritative, contestable action without preserving obsolete scarcity.

The shared connective tissue is **closure**. Chapter 19 asks who closes an option set. Chapter 20 asks who can revise or revoke a delegated choice. Chapter 21 asks which institutional boundary turns abundant advice or generated artifacts into a binding decision.

Do not add a branded framework. Prefer concrete tests: what becomes binding, who can revise it, what resource is actually scarce, what happens when two generated answers disagree, where appeal lives, and whether the institution is preserving a function or merely preserving delay.

## Scope discipline

This research pass does not justify claims that:

- AI creates a universal or clinical “meaning crisis”;
- more options are inherently harmful;
- assistance necessarily reduces competence or agency;
- meaningful human control requires approving every machine action;
- occupational exposure estimates are job-loss forecasts;
- the OECD SME survey is a universal measure of business adoption;
- all bureaucracy carries legitimate governance value;
- institutions should preserve professional scarcity after production costs fall.

The stronger argument is conditional: artificial abundance can make generating possibilities and artifacts much cheaper than choosing among them, revising delegated objectives, and converting recommendations into legitimate institutional action. Whether those become the new bottlenecks depends on preference clarity, contestability, authoritative state, distribution of switching costs, institutional design, and how quickly complementary human and organizational capabilities adapt.
