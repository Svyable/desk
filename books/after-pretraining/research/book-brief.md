# Book Brief — After Pretraining

## Reader promise

By the end of the book, a technically curious general reader should understand why frontier AI is shifting from learning mainly by imitation toward learning from action and consequence; what reinforcement learning, reward models, verifiers, critics, groups, replay, exploration, and environments actually do; why the shift changes the economics of training; and where the approach breaks when the thing humans care about cannot be cleanly scored.

The book should make the machinery legible without turning into a textbook.

## Central question

What happens when the most valuable training data is no longer found but generated by an agent acting in an environment?

## Thesis

Pretraining converted existing human artifacts into general-purpose predictive capability. The next major source of improvement is increasingly experiential: systems act, receive outcome signals, preserve trajectories, and update from what happened. As learning algorithms reduce the cost of extracting signal from each trajectory, the strategic bottleneck migrates toward environments, verifiers, permissions, instrumentation, and the right to collect and reuse experience.

The economic consequence is that the best training asset may become a closed loop rather than a static corpus.

The technical consequence is that reward design, exploration, and off-policy reuse become central.

The social consequence is that organizations will be tempted to make more of the world machine-verifiable, because what can be scored can be optimized.

## Strongest competing explanation

The apparent "post-pretraining" shift may be overstated. Most gains could still come from larger and better pretrained models, higher-quality curated data, inference-time search, distillation, and benchmark-specific optimization. Reinforcement learning may primarily expose or reweight capabilities that were already latent rather than create fundamentally new reasoning. Environment engineering may become expensive bespoke supervision by another name.

The book has to take that possibility seriously. Its thesis should not depend on declaring pretraining obsolete.

## Distinct contribution

Nearby books about AI typically organize around scale, alignment, agents, intelligence, or labor. This book is organized around the source of the learning signal after a capable base model already exists.

The unit of analysis is the loop:

action → consequence → evaluation → update → new action.

That allows one narrative to connect AlphaGo, RLHF, DeepSeek-R1, RLVR, critic-free and group-free methods, coding agents, adaptive environments, robotics, scientific automation, reward hacking, data rights, and the business value of proprietary operational feedback.

## Chapter architecture

### 1. One Rollout
Open with the 2026 KLPO diagram. Translate what is being removed: learned critic, prompt groups, extra full trajectories. Establish the economics of extracting more learning from each experience.

### 2. The Imitation Machine
Explain pretraining as compression and prediction. Show why static imitation is astonishingly powerful and where it becomes insufficient for long-horizon action.

### 3. Reward
Build reinforcement learning from first principles through concrete games and control problems. Separate reward from value, objective from behavior, and sparse terminal reward from dense feedback.

### 4. The Critic
Explain actor-critic methods, value estimation, variance reduction, and why critics are useful. Then show why modern LLM training keeps trying to simplify or eliminate them.

### 5. Games Without Teachers
Follow TD-Gammon, AlphaGo, AlphaZero, MuZero, and self-play. The chapter's job is not triumphalism; it is to show when an environment can manufacture unlimited labeled experience.

### 6. Human Feedback
Move into preference learning and RLHF. The machine cannot score a summary or conversation on its own, so humans become the reward infrastructure. Follow the costs and distortions.

### 7. Verifiable
Explain RLVR through mathematics, code, tests, theorem checking, and rule-governed tasks. Show why correctness signals are so valuable and why they favor some domains over others.

### 8. The Group Disappears
Trace PPO → GRPO → newer critic-free and single-rollout methods. Use KLPO as one live example while preserving uncertainty about which algorithmic branch wins.

### 9. Experience Factories
Investigate companies and research groups building synthetic task generators, simulations, cyber ranges, coding environments, and interactive curricula. Experience becomes manufactured inventory.

### 10. Replay
Explain off-policy learning and why old trajectories can remain valuable after the model changes. The economic question: how many times can one expensive experience be productively reused?

### 11. The Environment Is the Dataset
Shift from algorithms to assets. A static benchmark is copied easily; a live operational environment may be proprietary, regulated, embodied, or difficult to reproduce.

### 12. What Cannot Be Scored
Medicine, strategy, diplomacy, scientific taste, management, and other domains resist clean verification. Examine judge models, rubrics, process rewards, and irreducible ambiguity.

### 13. Reward Hacking
Follow specification gaming from classic RL examples to modern model behavior. An optimizer learns the measurement channel. Show why "better reward" is not synonymous with "better outcome."

### 14. The Cost of Exploration
Exploration produces failures. In a game they are cheap; in production they can destroy data, money, reputation, or safety. Investigate sandboxing, simulation, and risk budgets.

### 15. Agents in the Wild
Coding, browsing, customer support, security, robotics, and computer use. Ask which deployed agents actually close the loop from action to learning and which merely execute.

### 16. Who Owns the Experience?
Training rights move beyond copyright. Examine customer data, generated trajectories, telemetry, employee work, proprietary repositories, consent, trade secrets, and contractual restrictions.

### 17. After Human Demonstrations
Test the strongest version of the thesis. How far can models progress without expert traces? Where does human knowledge remain the fastest or only path? Treat human supervision as an allocation problem, not a vanishing species.

### 18. The Second Education
Return to the opening diagram with changed meaning. The final question is not whether machines will keep learning after pretraining. It is who gets to build the worlds they learn from, what those worlds reward, and which parts of reality resist becoming a score.

## Narrative discipline

The book should not become an alphabet soup of optimization algorithms. Each technical method enters because it changes one of four pressures: cost per experience, quality of the learning signal, reuse of past experience, or the ability to explore.

Use a small number of recurring objects deeply: a Go board, a code repository, a reward-model comparison screen, a verifier, an agent trajectory, and the KLPO flow diagram.

Avoid the easy "AI teaches itself" line. The system is always embedded in an environment someone built or granted access to, under an objective someone specified or accepted.

## Commercial frame

The broad audience hook is that the AI industry's most valuable resource may be changing. The first boom treated the internet as ore. The next may treat reality as a laboratory.

Two forces should carry the book.

**The experience moat:** if a capable model becomes widely available, advantage can move toward the organizations that possess difficult-to-copy environments where agents can act, fail, receive credible feedback, and retain the resulting trajectories. A proprietary feedback loop can matter even when the underlying model is rented.

**The measurement trap:** the same pressure that makes verifiable environments valuable gives organizations an incentive to redefine messy goals as scores. What can be scored becomes trainable. What becomes trainable attracts optimization. The danger is that the score can become easier to improve than the underlying reality.

These forces create the commercial tension. Better learning from consequences can make agents dramatically more useful while increasing the value of operational access and the temptation to turn ambiguous human purposes into machine rewards.

The book is therefore about technology, but also about ownership, work, institutions, measurement, and power.

## Ending test

The final chapter should leave the reader able to look at any new agentic system and ask five practical questions without needing the book's vocabulary: What can it actually do? What tells it that the action worked? Who owns the environment in which it learns? What does failure cost? What important part of the goal is missing from the score?

If the manuscript reaches the end without changing how those questions feel, it has explained algorithms but failed as a book.
